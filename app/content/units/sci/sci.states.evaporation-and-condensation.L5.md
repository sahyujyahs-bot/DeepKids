# Evaporation and condensation with particles: what speeds up drying, and how the water cycle works
`sci.states.evaporation-and-condensation.L5`

**Summary.** A tumbler of water at room temperature has no flame under it, yet its level drops a little every day. The particles of the water move at many different speeds. The fastest ones at the surface escape into the air as water vapour. That is evaporation. Warmth, a wider surface, moving air and dry air all speed it up. When particles of vapour slow down on something cool, they join again as drops. That is condensation. Your child measures how the size of the surface changes evaporation. They find out why evaporation cools the water left behind, and they explain the water cycle with particles.

**Intro.** *After a head bath, hair left loose dries in about an hour. The same hair tied in a tight plait can still be damp at bedtime. The hair, the water and the air are the same. So why does the plait stay wet?* Water can leave only from a surface that touches the air. Loose hair has a very large surface, and a plait hides most of that surface inside. In this unit you find out what happens at the surface of water. You measure how much more water leaves a wide plate than a narrow tumbler. You find out why hot milk cools faster in a davara than in a tumbler. Then you follow particles of water from the sea up to a cloud and back down as rain.

## Terms
- **Particle**: One of the tiny pieces that every substance is made of. Particles are far too small to see, even with a school microscope. A particle of water is also called a water molecule. *e.g.* One teaspoon of water holds more particles than there are grains of sand on all the beaches of the Earth.
- **Particle model**: A way of explaining what substances do by picturing their particles. In a solid the particles are held in place. In a liquid they touch and slide past each other. In a gas they are far apart and fly freely. *e.g.* The particle model explains why a puddle dries: particles leave the liquid one by one and fly off as a gas.
- **Evaporation**: A liquid turning into a gas at its surface. It happens at any temperature, because some particles at the surface are always moving fast enough to escape. *e.g.* A wet floor dries after mopping. The water has evaporated into the air of the room.
- **Water vapour**: Water as a gas. Its particles are far apart and mixed in with the particles of the air, so it cannot be seen. *e.g.* The air in your room holds water vapour now. You see it only when it turns back into drops on a cold bottle.
- **Condensation**: A gas turning into a liquid. Particles of water vapour slow down on something cool and stick together as drops. *e.g.* The inside of a pot lid is covered in drops when rice is cooking. The vapour condensed on the cooler lid.
- **Change of state**: A change from solid to liquid, liquid to gas, or back again. The particles stay the same. Only how they move and how close they are changes. *e.g.* Melting, freezing, evaporation and condensation are the four changes of state of water at home.
- **Reversible change**: A change that can be undone, so that you get the same substance back. *e.g.* Water evaporates from the sea, condenses in a cloud and falls as rain. It is the same water all the way.
- **Surface area**: The size of the surface of a thing. For water in a vessel, it is the size of the top of the water. That is where the water touches the air. It is measured in square centimetres. *e.g.* Water in a wide plate has a much bigger surface area than the same water in a tumbler.
- **Temperature**: A number that tells how fast the particles of a thing are moving on average. The faster they move, the higher the temperature. It is measured in degrees Celsius. *e.g.* The particles in hot rasam move faster on average than the particles in cold buttermilk.
- **Average**: One number that stands for a whole group of numbers. To find it, add all the numbers and divide by how many there are. *e.g.* Four particles have speeds 2, 3, 3 and 6. The total is 14, so the average speed is 14 ÷ 4 = 3.5.
- **Rate of evaporation**: How much water evaporates in a given time, such as millilitres in a day. *e.g.* On a hot, windy day the rate of evaporation from a wet towel is high. The towel dries in under an hour.
- **Humidity**: How much water vapour is in the air. Humid air already holds a lot of vapour, and dry air holds little. *e.g.* Mumbai in July is very humid, so clothes on the line stay damp. Jaipur in May is dry, so they dry fast.
- **Saturated air**: Air that holds as much water vapour as it can at that temperature. In saturated air, as many particles land back on a wet thing as leave it, so nothing dries. *e.g.* The air inside a closed, half-full water bottle is saturated. The water level in the bottle does not fall.
- **Dew point**: The temperature at which the vapour in the air starts to condense. The more vapour the air holds, the higher its dew point. *e.g.* On a humid day in Kochi the dew point may be 25 degrees. A glass of cool water at 20 degrees mists over at once.
- **Boiling**: Fast evaporation that happens all through a liquid, with bubbles of gas forming inside it. Water boils only when it is hot enough. *e.g.* Water for tea boils in the pan. The big bubbles that rise from the bottom are full of water vapour.
- **Boiling point**: The temperature at which a liquid boils. For pure water at sea level, it is 100 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Water in an open pan in Chennai stays at 100 degrees however long it boils.
- **Evaporative cooling**: The cooling of a liquid, or of whatever it is on, as the liquid evaporates. The fastest particles leave, so the ones left behind are slower on average. *e.g.* Sweat evaporating from your skin cools you after a game of kabaddi.
- **Water cycle**: The round journey of water. Water evaporates from the sea and the land and condenses into clouds. It falls as rain or snow and flows back to the sea. *e.g.* Rain that falls on the Western Ghats may have evaporated from the Arabian Sea a few days before.
- **Precipitation**: Any water that falls from clouds to the ground: rain, drizzle, snow or hail. *e.g.* Shimla gets snow in January, and Hyderabad sometimes gets hail in April. Both are precipitation.
- **Variable**: Anything in a test that could be changed. In a fair test you change one variable and keep all the others the same. *e.g.* In the Wide or Narrow test, the variable you change is the size of the water surface. The amount of water stays the same.

## Explanations
### Evaporation with particles: the fastest ones at the surface escape · *defined*
**Definition:** Evaporation is a liquid turning into a gas at its surface. The particles of a liquid move at many different speeds. A particle at the surface that is moving fast enough breaks away from its neighbours. It flies off into the air.

The particles of liquid water touch each other and hold on to each other. They also move all the time, bumping and sliding. After each bump a particle may be moving faster or slower than before. So at any moment a few particles are very fast, most are in the middle, and a few are slow. Deep inside the water a fast particle only bumps into more water. At the surface a fast particle can break free. It becomes a particle of water vapour, a gas, and mixes with the air. This goes on at every temperature, with no flame and no sunshine. That is why a tumbler of water left on a shelf slowly goes down.
- e.g. In the Shake the Plate activity in this unit, whole chana, which are chickpeas, in a steel plate stand for particles of water. Only the chana that reached the rim with enough speed jumped out. Most of the chana stayed in the plate.
- e.g. A wet steel plate left on the drying rack is dry by evening, though nobody heated it.
- e.g. Ink left in an open bottle dries up over the summer holidays.
### Water leaves only at the surface, so a bigger surface loses more · *defined*
**Definition:** Evaporation happens where water meets air. The more surface the water has, the more particles are at the surface at any moment. So more of them leave each second.

Pour the same water into a wide plate and a narrow tumbler. The plate has many more particles at its surface, so many more leave each minute. Double the surface, and about double the water leaves in the same time. The amount of water underneath makes no difference to how fast it leaves. A deep lake and a shallow puddle in the same weather lose about the same depth of water each day. The puddle is gone first only because it had little depth to lose. A tall vessel loses a little less than its surface suggests. Damp air collects above the water inside its walls, and some particles land back.
- e.g. In the Wide or Narrow experiment in this unit, the plate's surface was about four times the bowl's. The plate lost about four times as much water.
- e.g. Wet hair left loose dries much sooner than the same hair in a plait.
- e.g. Farmers in Guntur spread red chillies in a thin layer on the ground, and never dry them in a heap.
- e.g. Salt makers in Thoothukudi let sea water into wide, shallow pans, not deep tanks.
### Why warmth speeds up evaporation · *defined*
**Definition:** Warming water makes its particles move faster on average. So more of the particles at the surface are fast enough to escape each second.

Heat is energy, and it goes into the movement of the particles. In cool water only a few particles at the surface are fast enough to leave. In warm water many more are. So warm water evaporates much faster than cool water, long before it boils. The Sun warms the top of the sea, a wet road and a wet shirt in this way. Warmth helps in a second way too. Warm air can carry more water vapour before it is saturated. So fewer particles land back on the wet thing.
- e.g. Clothes on a terrace in Madurai dry in an hour at noon in May. In December they take half a day.
- e.g. In the Fast Ones Leave game in this unit, adding 1 to every speed for the Sun round let more particles reach the escape speed.
- e.g. A puddle on a tar road vanishes sooner than a puddle of the same size under a tree.
### Why moving air and dry air speed up evaporation · *defined*
**Definition:** Particles that have escaped can land back on the water. Moving air carries them away, and dry air has few of them to begin with. In both cases fewer particles land back, so the water goes down faster.

Evaporation is a two-way traffic. Particles leave the surface, and particles of vapour from the air land back on it. What we see is the difference between the two. In still air the escaped particles collect just above the surface, and many land back. A fan or a breeze sweeps them away and brings air with less vapour. In humid air, such as Kolkata's in the monsoon, the air already holds a lot of vapour. Nearly as many particles land as leave. Inside a closed bottle the two become equal. The water level stays the same, although particles still leave and land every moment.
- e.g. In the Tilting Hanger experiment in this unit, the cloth under the fan lost more water than the same cloth in still air.
- e.g. In the Wide or Narrow experiment in this unit, the closed jar lost no water at all in a whole day.
- e.g. A towel hung in a closed bathroom after a bath stays wet. The same towel on the balcony dries.
### Why evaporation cools what is left: temperature is an average · *defined*
**Definition:** The temperature of water tells how fast its particles move on average. Evaporation takes away the fastest particles. The ones left behind are slower on average, so the water is cooler.

A thermometer gives one number for a whole tumbler of water. But the particles in the tumbler have many different speeds. The number stands for their average. When the fastest particles escape, the average of the rest goes down. A class's average height goes down in the same way if the tallest children leave the room. The water then takes in heat from whatever it touches: your skin, a clay pot, the air. That is why evaporation cools things. Evaporating one teaspoon of sweat takes away enough heat to cool a litre of water by nearly 3 degrees.
- e.g. In the Oil Lid experiment in this unit, the open bowl of hot water cooled faster than the bowl sealed with oil.
- e.g. In the Fast Ones Leave game in this unit, the average speed in the pot fell whenever the 5s and 6s at the surface escaped.
- e.g. Hot coffee poured back and forth between a davara and a tumbler cools in a minute.
- e.g. A dog pants on a hot day. Water evaporates from her tongue and cools her blood.
### Condensation with particles: the reverse of evaporation · *defined*
**Definition:** Condensation is a gas turning into a liquid. Particles of water vapour that touch something cool lose energy and slow down. They stick to each other and to the surface. Many of them together make a drop.

Particles of water vapour fly about at around 600 metres a second, faster than a passenger jet. When one hits a cold tumbler, it gives some of its energy to the steel and slows down. A slow particle cannot break away again, so it stays. More particles join it, and soon there is a drop big enough to see. Condensation is evaporation in reverse. Evaporation takes heat in, and condensation gives the same amount of heat back out. That is why steam burns the skin more badly than boiling water. In 1958 the weather scientists Joanne Simpson (then Malkus) and Herbert Riehl argued that this heat powers the tall towers of rain cloud in the tropics.
- e.g. Mist forms on a steel tumbler of cold buttermilk, but only as high as the buttermilk inside.
- e.g. Your warm breath makes a misty patch on a cold window, and the patch evaporates again in seconds.
- e.g. The outside of a cold water bottle taken from the fridge is wet within a minute in Chennai in August.
### Four changes of state, and what each needs · *defined*
**Definition:** Water changes state in four everyday ways. Melting and evaporation need heat to go in. Freezing and condensation happen when heat goes out. All four can be undone.

Ice melts when heat goes in and its particles shake loose from their places. Water freezes when heat goes out and the particles lock into place again. Water evaporates when particles at the surface take in enough energy to fly off. Vapour condenses when its particles lose that energy. In every change the particles themselves stay the same, so no water is made and none is destroyed. Seal a little water in a bottle. The bottle has the same mass before and after the water evaporates and condenses inside it.
- e.g. Melting: an ice cube in a tumbler of nimbu pani.
- e.g. Freezing: water in an ice tray in the freezer.
- e.g. Evaporation: a wet swimming costume drying on a line.
- e.g. Condensation: drops under the lid of a tiffin box packed with hot idlis.
### Boiling is not the same as evaporation · *defined*
**Definition:** Evaporation happens only at the surface, at any temperature. Boiling happens all through the liquid, only at the boiling point, with bubbles of vapour forming inside the liquid.

Heat a pan of water and watch. Tiny bubbles appear on the bottom and walls while the water is only warm. These are air that was dissolved in the water. Much later, at 100 degrees Celsius, big bubbles form at the hot bottom and rise. These are water vapour: water that has turned into a gas inside the liquid. Once water is boiling, more heat does not make it hotter. The heat goes into turning more water into vapour. The white cloud above the pan is the vapour condensing into tiny drops as it meets cooler air. Real water vapour is invisible.
- e.g. In the What Is in the Bubbles? activity in this unit, the first small bubbles came out long before the water boiled.
- e.g. Rasam left simmering with no lid slowly gets less. The missing rasam left as vapour.
- e.g. A wet floor dries at 30 degrees without ever boiling.
### The water cycle explained with particles · *defined*
**Definition:** The water cycle is evaporation and condensation on the scale of the whole Earth. Sunlight gives particles at the surface of the sea enough energy to leave. High up, where the air is cold, they slow down and join into the drops of a cloud.

Sunlight warms the top of the sea. The fastest water particles escape, and the salt stays behind. The vapour mixes into the air, and warm, damp air rises. As the air rises it cools, by about 5 to 10 degrees for every kilometre. When it is cooled to its dew point, the vapour particles slow down. They stick to specks of dust, salt or smoke. Each speck grows into a cloud drop about a fiftieth of a millimetre wide. About a million cloud drops must join to make one raindrop. Drops that are heavy enough fall as rain. In very cold clouds the water falls as snow or hail. Rain runs into rivers and soaks into the ground, and most of it reaches the sea again.
- e.g. The south-west monsoon carries vapour from the Arabian Sea. It condenses as the air is pushed up the Western Ghats.
- e.g. The sea loses more than a metre of depth to evaporation every year, about 3 millimetres a day. Rain and rivers put it back.
- e.g. A particle of water stays in the air for about 9 days, on average, before it falls.
- e.g. A tree is part of the cycle. A large oak tree can send about 150,000 litres of water into the air from its leaves in a year.
### Dew point: how to tell how much vapour is in the air · *defined*
**Definition:** The dew point is the temperature to which air must be cooled before its vapour starts to condense. Humid air has a high dew point, and dry air has a low one.

Vapour particles in warm air are too fast to stick. Cool the air, or bring it against a cold surface, and at some temperature they begin to stick. That temperature is the dew point. You can find it with a steel tumbler, a thermometer and ice. Cool the water slowly and read the thermometer when the first mist appears. Weather offices report the dew point every day. Clouds form at the height where rising air has cooled to its dew point. That is why the bottoms of many clouds are flat.
- e.g. In the Find the Dew Point activity in this unit, the mist appeared at a higher temperature in a steamy bathroom than in the bedroom.
- e.g. Dew forms on parked scooters in Bengaluru on clear winter nights, when the metal cools below the dew point.
- e.g. On a humid summer day in Chennai the dew point can be about 25 degrees. On a cold, dry winter morning in Delhi it can be 5 degrees or lower.
### How living beings use evaporation · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings cool themselves by letting water evaporate from their bodies. The water takes heat away with it.

Humans cool mainly by sweating. A person working hard in the heat can lose more than a litre of sweat in an hour. The sweat cools the body only if it evaporates. On a very humid day it drips off, and the body stays hot. A dog has few sweat glands, so she pants and lets water evaporate from her tongue and mouth. Honeybees carry water into the hive on hot days. They spread it in thin films and fan it with their wings, and the hive cools. Plants lose water vapour through tiny holes in their leaves, and this cools the leaf. The plant opens and closes these holes, and so controls how much water leaves.
- e.g. After running, you cool faster under a fan, because the moving air carries away the vapour from your sweat.
- e.g. A crow on a hot terrace in Nagpur holds her beak open and pants.
- e.g. A leaf in full sunshine stays cooler than a stone beside it.
### Should lakes and canals be covered to stop evaporation? · *debated*
India's reservoirs and canals lose a great deal of water to evaporation each summer. Covering the surface cuts the loss. In 2012 Gujarat roofed a stretch of a Narmada canal with solar panels. In 2015 the city of Los Angeles floated 96 million black plastic balls on one reservoir. Floating solar panels now sit on reservoirs in Telangana and Kerala. But a cover changes the water under it. People who study this do not agree on how far to go.
- e.g. Canal-top solar panels in Gujarat shade the water and make electricity at the same time.
- e.g. A study in 2018 found that making the Los Angeles balls used a lot of water too. The balls must float for years to save more than they cost.
- e.g. Farmers cover the soil round young plants with dry leaves or straw. This is called mulch, and it slows evaporation from the soil.
- **A water engineer:** Every litre that evaporates from a reservoir is a litre lost to a town or a field. Covers and shade save water where it is most scarce, so we should use them widely.
- **A lake ecologist:** A cover blocks light and slows the mixing of air into the water. Water plants, fish and birds lose their home. Cover canals and tanks, but leave living lakes open.
- **A climate scientist:** Evaporation cools the land and feeds the rain. Covering a few reservoirs changes little. But the saved water is small beside what better pipes and less waste would save.
### Can light knock water particles out without heating the water? · *idea*
For two hundred years, evaporation has been explained by heat alone: warm particles move faster, and the fastest leave. In 2023 a group led by Gang Chen at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology reported something odd. Under certain light, their wet materials lost water faster than the heat in the light could explain. Green light worked best. They suggested that light can knock small clusters of water particles straight out of the surface. They named this the photomolecular effect. Other laboratories are still testing whether it is real. If it is, it may happen in clouds and fog as well.
- e.g. Some measurements suggest that clouds take in a little more sunlight than theory predicts. This effect might explain why.
- e.g. If the effect is real, it could make machines that dry things or clean salty water using less energy.

## Mostly agreed
- Evaporation happens only at the surface of a liquid, and at any temperature. *How we know:* The same water goes down faster in a wide dish than in a narrow one. Wet things dry in a cool room, far below the boiling point.
- The particles of a liquid at one temperature move at many different speeds. *How we know:* James Clerk Maxwell worked this out for gases in 1860. In the 1920s, experiments with beams of atoms tested the spread of speeds he had predicted.
- Evaporation cools the liquid that is left, and condensation gives the same heat out again. *How we know:* Water wrapped in a wet cloth falls below the temperature of the room. Steam that condenses on skin gives out enough heat to burn.
- Warmth, a bigger surface, moving air and dry air each make evaporation faster. *How we know:* Each can be tested alone, with everything else kept the same. Weather stations across India measure daily evaporation from open pans.
- Water vapour is a gas that mixes with the air. The air does not dissolve the water. *How we know:* John Dalton found this in 1801. Water gives off the same amount of vapour into an empty space as into air.
- The water cycle neither makes nor destroys water. *How we know:* More than a metre of depth leaves the sea each year. The rain and river water that return to it match that loss.

## How we found out
**John Dalton, a teacher and weather recorder in Manchester, England, The experiments were done in 1801 and printed in 1802. His first printed table of atoms followed in 1805..** In the 1700s most scientists explained evaporation like this: air dissolves water, just as water dissolves sugar. If that were right, water could not evaporate where there is no air. John Dalton was a teacher in Manchester, England, who wrote down the weather every day for 57 years. In 1801 he tested the idea. He let water evaporate into a glass tube that held almost no air. He did the same in a tube full of dry air, at the same temperature. Then he measured how hard the water vapour pushed in each tube. The push was the same. So the air was not dissolving the water. Dalton concluded that water vapour is a gas of its own. Its particles move about among the particles of the air without joining them. The air only gets in their way and slows them down. Thinking about these mixed gases led Dalton, a few years later, to his theory that all matter is made of atoms.  
*What people believed before:* That water evaporates because air dissolves it, as water dissolves sugar. Dalton showed that water gives off the same amount of vapour into an almost empty tube as into air. So air is not needed for evaporation.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The bubbles in boiling water are air.** (A very common belief among children and adults; *rejected*)  
  For: Bubbles in a fish tank and in soda are gas that came from outside the water. Small bubbles do appear in a pan while the water is only warm, and those really are air.  
  Against: The big bubbles at the boil keep coming for as long as the pan is heated. By then the dissolved air has long gone. If they were air, the water would not get less. They are water vapour, and the water level falls as they leave.
- **More water evaporates from a full bucket than from a wet floor, because the bucket holds more water.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: A bucket holds far more water than a thin wet film. It seems that a bigger amount should give off more.  
  Against: Water leaves only from its surface. A mopped floor has a surface hundreds of times bigger than a bucket's. So it loses water hundreds of times faster. The floor is dry in minutes, and the bucket stays full for days.
- **At one temperature, every particle of water moves at the same speed.** (What a single thermometer reading seems to say; *rejected*)  
  For: A thermometer gives one number for the whole tumbler. Stir the water and the number is the same everywhere.  
  Against: If all the particles had the same speed, either all of them could escape or none could. Water would vanish in an instant or never dry. Slow, steady evaporation shows that only a few particles are fast enough at any moment.
- **A cloud is a bag of water vapour that bursts when it is full.** (A picture many children have, and the phrase 'cloudburst'; *rejected*)  
  For: Clouds look like solid shapes with edges. Heavy rain can start very suddenly, as if something had split.  
  Against: A cloud has no skin. It is air full of tiny liquid drops, too small to fall fast. Rain starts when drops join until they are heavy enough to fall. A cloudburst is very heavy rain from a tall cloud, and nothing bursts.
- **Light can free water particles from a surface directly, without heating the water.** (Gang Chen and his group at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, in a paper from 2023; *debated*)  
  For: Their wet samples lost water faster under light than the heat in the light could explain. The effect changed with the colour of the light, which plain heating would not do.  
  Against: The results are new, and other laboratories have not yet repeated them fully. Some scientists suggest other explanations, such as water in a gel evaporating more easily than plain water.

## Roots
We have learned that the fastest particles of water escape at its surface. We have also learned that slow particles of vapour join into drops. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In The Oil Lid, the open bowl of hot water cooled faster than the bowl sealed with oil. Water particles move at many different speeds, and only the fastest ones at the surface escape. The particles left behind are slower on average, so the water is cooler. So one temperature stands for many different speeds, and evaporation lowers it by removing the fastest.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the water is at 30 degrees' as if every bit of the water were the same. A thermometer gives one number, so we picture every particle moving in the same way. The number is an average of particles that differ widely. What happens to the water depends on the few that are far from the average.  
*Evidence:* In The Oil Lid, two bowls began equally hot, and the bowl that could evaporate ended several degrees cooler. In Fast Ones Leave, the average speed of the pot fell whenever the 5s and 6s at the surface left. In Wide or Narrow, water kept leaving at room temperature, so some particles were always fast enough to escape.

- *who am i:* Your body cools itself in this way. When sweat evaporates, its fastest particles leave your skin and take heat with them. You have one body temperature, held steady by losing fast particles all day.
- *what is life:* A neem tree, a panting dog and a hive of bees all lose water to the air to stay cool. Each of them controls how much: the tree by opening leaf holes, the dog by panting, the bees by fanning.
- *what is this world:* The sea loses its fastest particles every second, more than a metre of depth each year. Those particles become every cloud, every river and all the rain. The fresh water on land is water that left the sea as vapour and came back as rain.

A tumbler of water looks like one still thing with one temperature. It is a vast crowd of particles, each moving at a speed of its own. The one temperature comes from all of them together. The few fastest leave, and from them come vapour, clouds and rain. Rain feeds the rivers, the trees and you. So the water cycle begins with the differences in speed between the particles inside the water.

- **Lucretius, On the Nature of Things, Book 1 (Rome, about 55 BCE):** Clothes hung by the sea grow damp, and spread in the sun they dry. Nobody sees the water settle on them or leave them. So, Lucretius says, the water must come and go in pieces too small for the eye to see. (This is evaporation explained with particles, two thousand years before anyone could test it. Lucretius reasoned from washing on a line, as this unit does.)
- **Tirukkural, verse 16, in the chapter on rain, by Thiruvalluvar:** Unless drops fall from the sky, not even a blade of green grass will lift its head. (Every one of those drops was made by condensation, from particles that evaporated somewhere else. The verse tells us how much all living things depend on rain. The science tells us where the drops come from.)
- **Milindapanha (The Questions of King Milinda), the chariot passage:** The monk Nagasena asks the king whether the chariot is its wheels, its axle or its pole. It is none of them. 'Chariot' is a name for all the parts working together. (Temperature is like this. No single particle has it. It is a name for what a great many particles are doing together. The science does not prove the Buddhist teaching about the self, which Nagasena goes on to draw.)
- Science (settled): The particles of a gas or a liquid at one temperature move at a wide spread of speeds. James Clerk Maxwell worked out the spread in 1860.
- Science (settled): Evaporation removes the most energetic particles, so it cools what is left. Physicists use the same method to cool gases to within a millionth of a degree of the coldest possible temperature.
- Science (debated): Light may be able to knock water particles out of a surface without heating it first.
- Other view, Many physicists and philosophers of science: An average is not less real than the particles. Temperature burns a finger, melts ice and drives the weather, and it can be measured without knowing anything about particles. A crowd has real features that none of its members has. So the temperature of the water is as real a thing as any particle in it.
- Practice, Sit with a warm tumbler (3 min): Let's hold a tumbler of warm water in both hands and sit still. Feel the warmth in your palms. Now look at the surface of the water. You cannot see anything leaving. But the fastest particles are leaving every moment. Take ten slow breaths. Feel the tumbler grow a little less warm. Where has that warmth gone?
- Ask: If you could follow every particle in a tumbler of water, would you still need the word 'temperature'?
- Ask: The fastest particles leave the sea and become rain for the land. Is the rain still part of the sea?
- Ask: Your body is warm because of how your particles move on average, and some of them are much faster and some much slower. So what is you: one particular particle, or all of them together?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* No single particle of water has a temperature of its own. So what does the temperature of a tumbler of water describe? (hint: Think about the average height of a class. Does any one child have to be exactly that tall?)
- *deep:* Nobody chooses which particles leave. Yet an open bowl of hot water cools every time. How can chance give the same result each time? (hint: Think about the Fast Ones Leave game in this unit. What happens to the average when only high numbers are removed?)
- *deep:* Evaporation and condensation are the same change, run in opposite directions. After a round trip, is the water the same water? (hint: Think about what changes in a change of state, and what stays.)
- *big:* The Sun lifts more than a metre of the sea into the sky each year, for free. A city pays for pumps to lift water to a tank. Who does the work in each case? (hint: Think about where the energy comes from each time.)
- *big:* A potter slows evaporation to save her pots. A farmer speeds it up to save her grain. Is fast drying good or bad? (hint: Think about what is drying, and what happens inside it.)
- *big:* Covering a canal saves water for a town. Evaporation from that canal would have become rain somewhere else. Whose water is it? (hint: Think about where the vapour goes, and whether anyone can say where it will fall.)

## Story: The Pot That Dried Too Fast
*Yazhini, Ponni Paatti and their shed are made up. The science of drying clay is real. Wet clay shrinks as its water evaporates. Water leaves only from the surface, so the outside and the thin rim dry and shrink first. If they shrink much faster than the wet clay inside, the pot cracks. Potters in many parts of India dry their pots slowly in shade and out of the wind. They often cover them with damp cloth, and they turn them. A dry, unbaked pot can be soaked in water and turned back into soft clay.*

Ponni Paatti made water pots in a tiled shed in Villianur, a short bus ride from Puducherry. In the summer holidays her granddaughter Yazhini, who was ten, came to stay and to get clay under her fingernails.

That May the heat was fierce, and a marriage hall had ordered thirty pots for its drinking water. "In six days," Paatti said. "One day for the kiln, one day to cool. So the pots have four days to dry."

By Monday noon thirty pots stood in rows at the back of the shed, dark and wet and smelling of rain. Paatti hung damp gunny sacks across the open side of the shed. She laid a strip of wet cloth round every rim.

"Paatti, that is backwards," said Yazhini. "You want them dry. Why are you making them wet?"

"Watch them for four days and tell me," said Paatti. Then her phone rang. Her sister in Cuddalore had fallen and hurt her knee. Paatti packed a bag. "I will be back on Wednesday evening. Sarasu akka next door will give you your meals. Leave the pots exactly where they are."

Yazhini meant to. But on Tuesday morning she pressed a finger on a pot, and it was as cold and damp as the day before. Four days! At home in Tiruchi she hung the washing every evening. She knew what dried a shirt: hot sun, a good wind, and plenty of room. The shed had none of the three.

So she carried six pots, one at a time, into the middle of the yard. She took the wet cloths off their rims. The sun stood overhead, and a hot west wind was blowing. By four o'clock the six pots had turned from dark brown to pale grey. She knocked on one with her knuckle. It felt hard. She was so pleased that she carried them back in and set them in the front row for Paatti to see.

On Wednesday morning she looked at the front row, and her stomach turned over. A thin line ran down from the rim of the first pot, like a hair. The second had two. The third had split right across its base.

Paatti came home at dusk. She looked at the six pots for a long time. She did not shout. She picked up the third pot and handed Yazhini a knife. "Cut through the wall and tell me what you see."

Yazhini sawed through the clay. The outside of the wall was pale and hard, like a biscuit. The middle of the wall was still dark, and it felt cool and damp on her thumb.

"Two colours," she said. "It is dry outside and wet inside."

"Where does the water leave a pot from?" Paatti asked.

Yazhini thought of her shirts. "From the outside. From where it touches the air."

"Only from there. The water in the middle of the wall has to creep out to the skin first, and that is slow. Now, clay shrinks when its water goes. Measure a wet pot and a dry pot, and the dry one is a finger narrower. Your sun and your wind took the water from the skin in one afternoon. So the skin shrank. The inside was still wet and still fat."

"A small shirt on a big body," said Yazhini slowly. "It tore."

"It tore at the rim first, because the rim is thin and has air on both sides." Paatti put the pot down. "So. Why do I hang wet sacks?"

Yazhini looked round the shed as if she had never seen it. The roof kept off the sun. The walls kept off the wind. The sacks made the air damp. The cloths covered the rims. "Everything that dries a shirt fast, you take away," she said. "You are not drying them slowly because you are slow. You hold the outside back, so that the inside can keep up."

"Then you know my whole trade," said Paatti, and she went to wash her feet.

On Thursday Paatti had to fetch firewood for the kiln. At noon the west wind rose again, hotter than before, and blew straight in at the open side. The gunny sacks had dried stiff. Yazhini saw the rims of the back rows beginning to turn pale, while the bellies of the pots were still dark.

She did not wait. She dragged the sacks down, soaked them in the trough and hung them up again, dripping. She splashed water over the earth floor. She wet the rim cloths and laid them back. Then she gave every pot a quarter turn, so that the side facing the wind became the side facing the wall.

When Paatti came back with the cart, the shed smelt of rain again. She put her palm on a rim and then on a belly, and nodded.

By Friday evening the twenty-four pots were one even grey from lip to base. Paatti rapped one, and it rang.

"Now they have finished shrinking," she said. "Now you may give them all the sun you like." Together they carried the pots out into the yard to bake dry before the kiln.

The six cracked pots went into the soaking pit. By Sunday they had slumped into soft brown clay again. Yazhini pushed her hands into it, up to the wrists.

"Six more pots," she said. "And this time I will dry them like a potter."

**For the parent:** For the parent: water evaporates only from a surface that touches the air. Sun, wind, dry air and a large exposed surface all speed this up. Clay shrinks as it loses water. So a pot dried fast shrinks on the outside while the inside is still wet and full-sized, and it cracks. Yazhini knew what makes washing dry quickly. The turn comes when she sees that her grandmother removes each of those things on purpose: shade for the sun, walls for the wind, wet sacks for the dry air, cloths over the thin rims. To slow evaporation, you need to know exactly what speeds it up.

- Ask: Which three things did Yazhini know would dry a shirt quickly?
- Ask: The cut wall of the cracked pot had two colours. What did that show about where water leaves a pot?
- Ask: Why did the rims crack first?
- Ask: On Thursday Yazhini did four things in the shed. For each one, say which cause of fast evaporation it worked against.
- Ask: At the end, the pots were put in full sun. Why was that safe then, and not on Tuesday?

### g2 · Fast Ones Leave: a dice game about cooling (20 min)
- **rules:** You need the pot drawn on paper, a pencil, a score sheet and one die.; Roll the die once for each of the 12 squares, and write the number in the square. The number is that particle's speed.; Add up the 12 speeds and divide by 12. This average stands for the temperature of your pot. Write it on the score sheet.; The water stirs itself, so each row takes a turn at the surface. In round 1 the top row is at the surface. In round 2 it is the middle row, and in round 3 the bottom row.; In each round, every particle in the surface row with a speed of 5 or 6 escapes. Cross out its square and write its speed in the AIR cloud.; After each round, add up the speeds still in the pot. Divide by the number of particles left. Write the new average, to one decimal place.; Then play the Sun round. Add 1 to every speed still in the pot. Now any particle with a speed of 5 escapes. Work out the last average.; With friends, each player has a pot and plays the three rounds. The coolest pot, with the lowest average after round 3, wins. Alone, guess your round 3 average before you roll, and see how close you get.
- **Printable board: A pot to draw: 3 rows of 4 squares:** Draw a rectangle with 3 rows of 4 squares, each square about 3 cm wide. This is the pot.; Each square is one particle of water. Leave room in each square for a number.; Write ROUND 1 beside the top row, ROUND 2 beside the middle row and ROUND 3 beside the bottom row.; Draw a cloud shape above the pot and write AIR in it.
- **Printable score sheet: A score sheet:** My guess: will the average go up, go down or stay the same?; Start: 12 particles. Total of the speeds: ___. Average: ___.; After round 1: particles left ___. Total ___. Average ___.; After round 2: particles left ___. Total ___. Average ___.; After round 3: particles left ___. Total ___. Average ___.; After the Sun round: particles left ___. Total ___. Average ___.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the pot and the AIR cloud. 2. Roll the die 12 times and write a speed in each square. 3. Add up the speeds and divide by 12. 4. Ask what will happen to the average when the fastest particles leave, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Each number is the speed of one particle. The average is the temperature of the pot. When the fast ones at the surface leave, what will happen to the average? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the pot. Roll the die 12 times. Write a speed in each square. Find the average. Guess what will happen to it.
  - Note: A phone calculator is fine for the division. For example, a total of 42 gives 42 ÷ 12 = 3.5.
- **s2 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play round 1. Cross out every 5 and 6 in the top row and write them in the AIR cloud. 2. Work out the new average. 3. Play round 2 with the middle row and round 3 with the bottom row. 4. Write the average after each round.  
  *Parent says:* Which particles escaped? Look at the ones left behind. Is the pot warmer or cooler than before? Did the average ever go up?  
  *Child mode:* Cross out each 5 and 6 in the top row. Find the new average. Do the middle row next, then the bottom row.
  - Note: The average falls whenever a particle leaves, and it never rises. After three rounds it is usually between 2 and 3. If no 5 or 6 is in a row, nothing leaves in that round.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Play the Sun round. Add 1 to every speed still in the pot. 2. Cross out every particle that now has a speed of 5. 3. Work out the last average. 4. Ask what the Sun round shows about warm water.  
  *Parent says:* The Sun has warmed the pot, and every particle is faster by 1. How many more escaped? What does that tell you about warm water and evaporation?  
  *Child mode:* Add 1 to every speed left in the pot. Cross out each 5. How many escaped this time? Find the last average.
  - Ask: Why did the average fall each time particles left? (good: Only the fastest ones left, so the ones left behind were slower on average.)
  - Ask: What did the Sun round show? (good: Warming makes more particles fast enough to escape, so warm water evaporates faster.)
  - Check: In Fast Ones Leave, the average speed fell whenever particles escaped. What does this show about real water? → **Evaporation cools the water left behind**

  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened. What did the cold plate show?
  - Ask: Both bowls lost heat through the steel. What else was the open bowl losing? (good: Water vapour. The plate misted above it.; Its fastest particles, which escaped from the surface.)
  - Note: The particles of the hot water move at many different speeds. In the open bowl the fastest ones at the surface kept escaping. The particles left behind were slower on average, and slower particles mean cooler water. Under the oil no particle could escape, so that bowl lost heat only through the steel and the oil.
  - Check: In The Oil Lid, the open bowl cooled faster than the bowl under oil. Why? → **Its fastest particles kept escaping**
  - Think: Why does a thin skin of cream on hot milk keep the milk hot for longer?
### e3 · The Tilting Hanger: spread, fan or sunshine, which dries a cloth fastest? (45 min)
- **question:** Three things can speed up drying: spreading the cloth out, moving air and warmth. In your home today, which of the three makes the biggest difference in 8 minutes?
- **predict:** Guess first, and write your guess. You will run three tests. The first is a spread cloth against a folded cloth. The second is a cloth under the fan against a cloth in still air. The third is a test that you choose. In which test will the two cloths end up most different? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Start each test with two cloths that balance on the hanger, so they hold the same water. In each test change only one thing. In the first test both cloths are under the fan, and only the folding differs. In the second both are spread out, and only the moving air differs. Give both cloths the same 8 minutes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each test, showing how far the lighter cloth's end rose. Then write what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Test | What was different | Which cloth became lighter? | How many centimetres did its end rise?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hang the coat hanger on a door handle or hook, away from the fan. 2. Fix one clothes clip to each end. 3. If one end is lower, stick bits of tape on the higher end until the hanger is level. 4. Tape the paper behind one end and mark where that end sits.  
  *Parent says:* This hanger is our balance. If two cloths hold the same water, it sits level. If one has lost more water, which way will it tilt?  
  *Child mode:* Hang the hanger on a door handle. Put a clip on each end. Make it level with tape. Mark where one end sits.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dip both cloths in the bowl. 2. Hold them together and squeeze until the dripping stops. 3. Bunch each cloth and clip one to each end of the hanger. 4. If one end is lower, drip water from the teaspoon onto the other cloth until the hanger is level. 5. Ask for the guess and its reason.  
  *Parent says:* Now the two cloths hold the same water. We will dry them in different ways. In which of our three tests will they end up most different? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Wet both cloths. Squeeze them together. Clip one to each end. Add drops until the hanger is level. Now guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Spreading, because a folded cloth hides most of its surface.; The fan, because moving air carries the vapour away.; The sun, because warmth makes the particles faster.)
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take both cloths off. 2. Spread cloth A open over the back of a chair, under the fan. 3. Fold cloth B in four and lay it on a plate on the seat of the same chair. 4. Wait 8 minutes. 5. Clip both back on the hanger. Measure how far the lighter cloth's end has risen above the mark.  
  *Parent says:* Both cloths were under the same fan. Only the folding was different. Which end rose? How many centimetres?  
  *Child mode:* Spread one cloth on a chair back. Fold the other in four on a plate. Wait 8 minutes. Clip them back on. Which end rose?
  - Note: The spread cloth is lighter, often by a few grams, and its end rises 1 to 4 cm. If the mark is on the other end, measure how far that end has dropped. It is the same distance.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wet both cloths again and balance them as before. 2. Spread cloth A over the chair under the fan. 3. Spread cloth B over another chair in a room with the fan off. 4. Wait 8 minutes. 5. Clip both back on and measure the rise.  
  *Parent says:* This time both cloths are spread out. Only the moving air is different. Which end rose, and how far?  
  *Child mode:* Wet and balance the cloths again. Spread one under the fan. Spread one in still air. Wait 8 minutes. Measure again.
  - Note: Choose a still room that is about as warm as the room with the fan. Do not use the bathroom, because its air is damp.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to plan a third test that changes one new thing. It could be a sunny spot against a shady one, or a bathroom after a bath against a bedroom. 2. They say what will differ and what will stay the same. 3. Wet and balance the cloths, run the test for 8 minutes, and measure.  
  *Parent says:* Now plan your own test. What one thing will you change? What must stay the same for both cloths?  
  *Child mode:* Plan your own test. Change one thing only. Say what stays the same. Run it for 8 minutes. Measure the rise.
  - Note: If your child wants to change two things at once, ask how they would know which one made the difference.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the three results side by side and draw the bar chart. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Which change made the biggest difference in our home today?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened. Which change mattered most today?
  - Ask: Explain each result with particles. (good: Spread out: more of the water is at a surface, so more particles can leave.; Fan: moving air carries escaped particles away, so fewer land back.; Sun: warm particles move faster, so more of them can escape.)
  - Note: The order changes from home to home and from day to day. On a humid day the fan may matter most. On a dry, hot day spreading may matter most. All three work by letting more particles leave than land back.
  - Check: In The Tilting Hanger, a cloth under a fan lost more water than the same cloth in still air. Why does moving air speed up evaporation? → **It carries escaped particles away**
  - Think: Would the order of your three results be the same in the monsoon as in May? How could you find out?

### a2 · Shake the Plate: a model of particles leaving water (15 min)
- **record:** Make a table with three rows: gentle shake, hard shake, hard shake with a lid. Write how many chana left the plate each time.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the bedsheet on the floor. 2. Put the plate in the middle with 40 chana in it. 3. Tell your child what stands for what. Each chana is a particle of water. The rim stands for the hold that the particles have on each other. Shaking stands for warmth. 4. Ask for a guess: how many will jump out with a gentle shake, and with a hard shake?  
  *Parent says:* Each chana is a particle of water. The rim stands for the hold they have on each other. When I shake gently, how many will get out? And when I shake hard? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Put 40 chana in the plate. Each chana is a water particle. Guess how many will jump out. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: A hard shake makes them move faster, so more will get over the rim.; Only the ones near the edge can get out.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Slide the plate gently from side to side on the sheet for 10 seconds. 2. Count the chana that left the plate, and write the number. 3. Put them back. 4. Shake harder for 10 seconds, without lifting the plate. 5. Count and write again.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the chana that get out. Where in the plate were they just before they left? Were they slow ones or fast ones?  
  *Child mode:* Slide the plate gently for 10 seconds. Count the chana that jumped out. Put them back. Now shake harder. Count again.

- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put all 40 chana back. 2. One person holds the second plate upside down, about 3 cm above the first. 3. The other shakes hard for 10 seconds. 4. Count the chana that left.

  *Child mode:* Hold a second plate just above the first. Shake hard again. How many chana got out this time?

- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the three numbers. 2. Ask what the model shows about real water. 3. Ask where the model is unlike real water.  
  *Parent says:* What does this model show about evaporation? And in what way is a plate of chana not like real water?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your three numbers. What does the model show about water? How is real water different?
  - Ask: How is real water different from the chana? (good: Nobody shakes real water. Its particles move by themselves all the time.; Real particles are far too small to see, and there are far more of them.; In real water, particles from the air also land back on the surface.)
  - Check: In Shake the Plate, which chana jumped out of the plate? → **Fast ones that reached the rim**

### c1 · More Drops per Hour: build a better fresh-water maker (if you have a sunny balcony or window) (40 min)
- **problem:** Build two fresh-water makers and stand them side by side in the sun for 3 hours. One is the plain design. The other is your improved design. Each starts with 1 tumbler of salty, coloured water. Make your improved one collect more fresh water than the plain one.
- **safety:** Do not drink the collected water. The film and the bowls may not be clean enough.
- **your turn:** Now design your own improvement. Change one thing at a time if you can. How many teaspoons did each maker collect? Which change helped, and how do you explain it with particles?
- **constraints:** Both start with the same water: 1 tumbler of water with 1 teaspoon of salt and a spoonful of black tea for colour.; Both stand in the same sunshine for the same 3 hours.; Use only the Sun for heat. No stove and no hot water.; The collected water must be clear. If it is brown, salty water has splashed in.
- **general:** Stand the bowl on a black cloth, or use a dark bowl. Dark things take in more sunlight, so the water gets warmer.; Use a wider bowl or tray, so that the salty water has a bigger surface.; Put a heavier stone on the film. The film then slopes more steeply, and the drops run to the middle sooner.
- **creative:** Prop a shiny steel plate behind the bowl, to throw extra sunlight onto it.; Lay a ring of wet cloth round the edge of the film. A cooler film condenses vapour faster.; Hang a strip of black cloth with one end in the salty water. The wet cloth adds more surface for evaporation.

## Applications
- Farmers dry paddy, pepper, coffee beans and red chillies in the sun. They spread them in a thin layer and turn them. A heap would stay wet inside and grow mould.
- Salt makers in Thoothukudi and in Kutch let sea water into wide, shallow pans. The big surface lets the Sun and the wind take the water and leave the salt.
- An air cooler blows room air through wet pads. Water evaporating from the pads cools the air. Coolers work well in dry Nagpur and poorly in humid Chennai.
- Builders keep new concrete covered with wet gunny sacks for a week or more. Concrete needs its water to harden, so they slow the evaporation.
- Sports clothes are woven to spread sweat into a thin film over a large surface. The sweat evaporates fast and cools the runner.
- Attar makers in Kannauj heat rose petals in water in a copper pot. The vapour carries the scent along a bamboo pipe and condenses in a cooled vessel.
- Power stations cool their hot water in tall cooling towers. The white plume above a tower is water vapour that has condensed into tiny drops.

## Open questions
- **Can light free water particles from a surface without heating the water?** A group in the United States reported this in 2023. Other laboratories have not yet repeated it fully, and some scientists suggest other explanations.
- **Why has evaporation from weather-station pans fallen in many places, while the world has warmed?** Records from many countries, including India, show less water lost from open pans than fifty years ago. Weaker winds and hazier skies explain a part of it. Scientists still argue about the rest.
- **How do the first few particles of water join into a drop when there is nothing to stick to?** The first cluster has only a handful of particles and lasts a tiny part of a second. It is too small and too quick to watch, and hard to calculate.
- **Can drinking water be taken cheaply from dry desert air?** New sponge-like materials can catch vapour at night and give it up as water in sunlight. They work in the laboratory. Nobody yet knows if they can be made cheaply enough for a village.
- **Can evaporation be used to make electricity?** Small engines driven by evaporating water have been built in laboratories since 2015. Lakes and reservoirs lose a huge amount of energy this way. Nobody knows yet whether it can be collected in a useful amount.

## Connected maths
- In Wide or Narrow, a bowl had a water surface of 75 square cm. It lost 7 mm of water in a day. A tray with a surface of 450 square cm stood beside it. About how much water did the tray lose? **About 42 mm.**. The tray's surface is 450 ÷ 75 = 6 times as big as the bowl's. - The water in a plate is 16 cm across. Use the three-quarters rule to find the area of its surface. **192 square cm.**. Multiply the width by itself: 16 × 16 = 256. A circle fills about three-quarters of the square round it. Three-quarters of 256 is 192. So the area is about 192 square cm. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L5`)
- In Wide or Narrow, a plate of 300 square cm lost 28 mm of water. A tumbler of 37 square cm lost 2 mm. If the tumbler had lost water as fast as the plate for its size, how much would it have lost? Why might it have lost less? **About 3.5 mm. The tumbler's tall walls shelter its water.**. The plate's surface is about 300 ÷ 37 = 8 times the tumbler's. So the tumbler should lose about 28 ÷ 8 = 3.5 mm. It lost only 2 mm. Its walls stand above the water and keep the fan's air out. So damp air collects there, and more particles land back. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- In a game of Fast Ones Leave, the top row holds the speeds 6, 2, 5 and 3. The middle row holds 4, 1, 6 and 3. The bottom row holds 2, 4, 5 and 1. What is the average speed at the start? What is it after round 1, when the 6 and the 5 in the top row have escaped? **3.5 at the start, and 3.1 after round 1.**. At the start the total is 16 + 14 + 12 = 42, and 42 ÷ 12 = 3.5. In round 1 the 6 and the 5 in the top row leave. The total is now 42 − 11 = 31, and 10 particles are left. 31 ÷ 10 = 3.1. The average has fallen, so the pot is cooler. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In The Oil Lid, readings were taken every 5 minutes, from 0 to 20 minutes. The open bowl was cooler than the oil bowl by 0, 3, 5, 6 and 7 degrees. In which 5 minutes did the gap grow most? Predict the gap at 25 minutes, and give a reason. **The gap grew most in the first 5 minutes. At 25 minutes it would be about 7 or 8 degrees.**. The gap grew by 3, then 2, then 1, then 1 degree. So it grows more slowly as time passes. One more step of about 1 degree, or less, gives 7 to 8 degrees. The reason is that the open bowl is cooler by then. Cooler water has fewer fast particles, so it evaporates more slowly. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
- Wide or Narrow was set up at 8:40 on Monday morning. The water was measured at 7:25 on Tuesday morning. For how long did the water stand? Was that more or less than one full day? **22 hours and 45 minutes, which is 1 hour and 15 minutes less than a full day.**. From 8:40 on Monday morning to 8:40 on Tuesday morning is 24 hours. 7:25 is 1 hour and 15 minutes before 8:40. 24 hours − 1 hour 15 minutes = 22 hours 45 minutes. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- In Fast Ones Leave, a particle in the surface row escapes if its speed is 5 or 6. What is the chance that one surface particle escapes? Of the 4 particles in a surface row, about how many escape in a round? **The chance is 2 in 6, which is 1 in 3. About 1 or 2 of the 4 escape.**. A die has 6 faces, and 2 of them are a 5 or a 6. So the chance is 2 in 6, or 1 in 3. One-third of 4 is a little more than 1. So in most rounds 1 or 2 particles escape, and sometimes none. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
