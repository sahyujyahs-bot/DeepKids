# Breathe and stretch: what slow breathing and stretching do inside you
`sci.health.breathe-and-stretch.L1`

**Summary.** Hold your breath and count. Very soon your body makes you breathe again. So who is in charge of your breath, you or your body? In this lesson you feel your breath go in and out, slow it down, and notice what changes inside you. You stretch like a cat and a cobra, measure how far you can reach, and meet yoga, kabaddi and other breath-and-stretch play from India.

**Intro.** *Put one hand on your tummy and one hand on your chest. Sit still and do nothing at all. Which hand moves?* You did nothing, and still a hand moved. Air went into you and came out again. Your body did that by itself. Now take one big slow breath on purpose. This time you did it. Today you find out what breath and stretching do inside you, and why people in India have practised both for a very long time.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can feel my breath go in and out, and show which parts of my body move.
1. I can say that my body breathes by itself, and that I can also change my breath when I choose.
2. I can breathe slowly and say how my body feels afterwards.
3. I can do a gentle stretch and point to where I feel the pull.
4. I can tell a gentle pull from pain, and I stop when a stretch hurts.
5. I can show two yoga poses or games from India that use breath and stretching.
6. I can say why my body needs both moving and resting.

## Terms
- **Breath**: A breath is one time air goes in and one time it comes out. *e.g.* Put your hand under your nose. Each warm puff you feel is the end of one breath.
- **Breathe in**: To breathe in is to take air into your body through your nose or mouth. *e.g.* You breathe in when you smell a hot dosa.
- **Breathe out**: To breathe out is to let air leave your body. *e.g.* You breathe out when you blow on hot kanji to cool it.
- **Air**: Air is the invisible mix of gases all around us. You cannot see it, but you can feel it when it moves. *e.g.* A fan pushes air onto your face.
- **Lungs**: Your lungs are two soft bags inside your chest. Air goes into them when you breathe in. *e.g.* Your lungs fill up like slow balloons when you take a big breath.
- **Nose**: Your nose is the part of your face you breathe through. It warms and cleans the air as it goes in. *e.g.* After a dusty bus ride, your nose is grey inside, because it caught the dust.
- **Mucus**: Mucus is the wet, sticky layer inside your nose. Dust sticks to it. *e.g.* When you blow your nose after a dusty ride, the dust comes out in your mucus.
- **Diaphragm**: The diaphragm is a flat muscle under your lungs, shaped like an upturned bowl. You say it 'dye-a-fram'. It pulls down to bring air in. *e.g.* When you breathe in, your tummy rises because the diaphragm pushes down on it.
- **Hiccup**: A hiccup is when your diaphragm pulls down by itself, quickly, and you did not ask it to. *e.g.* You eat a laddoo too fast and 'hic' comes out.
- **Automatic**: Automatic means it happens without you choosing it. *e.g.* Your heart beats automatically, even when you are asleep.
- **On purpose**: On purpose means you choose to do it. *e.g.* You wave to a friend on purpose.
- **Heart**: Your heart is a muscle in your chest that pushes blood all round your body. *e.g.* Put your hand on your chest. The thump you feel is your heart.
- **Heartbeat**: A heartbeat is one push of your heart. It happens again and again, all your life. *e.g.* Your heartbeat is faster after you run and slower when you rest.
- **Pulse**: Your pulse is the tap you can feel on your wrist or neck each time your heart pushes blood. *e.g.* Press two fingers on the inside of your wrist and you feel the tap, tap, tap.
- **Sigh**: A sigh is one long, loud out-breath. *e.g.* You sigh when you finally find your lost chappal.
- **Calm**: Calm means quiet and settled inside, with no hurry. *e.g.* After five slow breaths, your shoulders drop and you feel calmer.
- **Nerve**: A nerve is a thin thread inside your body that carries messages to and from your brain. *e.g.* A nerve in your finger tells your brain that the tea glass is hot.
- **Brain**: Your brain is the soft part inside your head that gets messages and sends messages. One small part of it starts every breath. *e.g.* Your brain gets the message that your foot is cold.
- **Muscle**: A muscle is a part of your body that can pull. When a muscle pulls, a bone moves. *e.g.* Bend your arm and feel the bump above your elbow. That is a muscle pulling.
- **Bone**: A bone is a hard part inside your body that holds you up. Muscles pull on bones to move you. *e.g.* You can feel the bones of your fingers when you press them.
- **Stretch**: To stretch is to move slowly until a muscle is pulled gently longer, and to hold it there. *e.g.* You stretch your arms up high when you wake up.
- **Gentle pull**: A gentle pull is the mild tugging feeling of a good stretch. It does not hurt. *e.g.* Reaching for your toes can feel like a kite string pulling at the back of your legs.
- **Pain**: Pain is a hurting feeling, such as a sharp pinch or a burn. It is your body saying 'stop'. *e.g.* A sharp pinch behind the knee in a stretch means ease off.
- **Sensor**: A sensor is a tiny part of the body that feels something and sends a message about it. Your body has thousands of kinds. *e.g.* Sensors in your fingertips feel if something is hot, rough or smooth.
- **Body-position sense**: Body-position sense is how you know where your arms and legs are without looking. Scientists call it proprioception, said 'pro-pree-oh-SEP-shun'. *e.g.* With your eyes shut, you can still touch your nose with one finger.
- **Warm up**: To warm up is to move gently for a few minutes before you stretch or play, so your muscles are ready. *e.g.* You march on the spot before you stretch.
- **Bendy**: Bendy means your body can move a long way at a joint. The word scientists use is flexible. *e.g.* Some children can fold flat over their legs and others reach only their knees. Both are fine.
- **Yawn**: A yawn is a long, deep breath in with a wide-open mouth, and then a breath out. *e.g.* You yawn when you are sleepy, and then your friend yawns too.
- **Yoga**: Yoga is a practice from India. People hold steady shapes, breathe quietly and pay attention. *e.g.* A neighbour does yoga on a mat in the park at dawn.
- **Asana**: An asana is one yoga shape that you hold for a few breaths. You say it 'AH-suh-nuh'. *e.g.* The tree is an asana.
- **Vrikshasana (tree pose)**: Vrikshasana is the tree pose. You stand on one leg and bring your hands together above your head. *e.g.* You stand like a neem tree, steady in the wind.
- **Bhujangasana (cobra pose)**: Bhujangasana is the cobra pose. You lie on your tummy and lift your chest a little, like a cobra rising. *e.g.* You lift your head and chest while your hips stay on the mat.
- **Marjariasana (cat pose)**: Marjariasana is the cat pose. You go on hands and knees and round your back like a cat. *e.g.* A cat arches her back on a wall in the same shape.
- **Tadasana (mountain pose)**: Tadasana is the mountain pose. You stand tall and still with your feet together. *e.g.* You stand like the Himalayas, quiet and strong.
- **Shavasana (resting pose)**: Shavasana is the resting pose. You lie on your back, still, and let your breath go slow. *e.g.* You lie down at the end of yoga and rest.
- **Surya Namaskar**: Surya Namaskar is a chain of twelve yoga shapes done one after another with the breath. The words mean 'greeting to the Sun'. *e.g.* Many families in India do it in the morning.
- **Kabaddi**: Kabaddi is a team game from India. A raider runs into the other half and must keep chanting 'kabaddi' on one breath. *e.g.* Children play kabaddi in a sandy ground in the evening.
- **Raider**: A raider is the player in kabaddi who runs into the other team's half to touch someone and get back. *e.g.* The raider keeps chanting while she tries to touch a player and run home.
- **Rest**: Rest is when the body is quiet and mends. Your heart and lungs still work. *e.g.* You rest after a game, and your breath slowly goes slow again.
- **Active**: Active means moving your body around. *e.g.* Running, dancing and kabaddi are active.
- **Oxygen**: Oxygen is a gas in the air. Your body takes it from every breath and uses it to get energy from food. *e.g.* The air you breathe in has oxygen in it.
- **Carbon dioxide**: Carbon dioxide is a gas your body gives out in every out-breath. Plants take it in. *e.g.* A tulsi plant takes in the carbon dioxide you breathe out.
- **Gills**: Gills are the parts a fish breathes with. They take oxygen from the water. *e.g.* A fish opens and closes her gills as she swims.
- **Prana**: Prana is a Sanskrit word for breath and also for the life in a living being. *e.g.* In India, thinkers used one word, prana, for breath and for life.

## Explanations
### Breathing is air going into you and coming out of you · *defined*
**Definition:** Breathing is taking air into the lungs and pushing it out again. The lungs are two soft bags inside the chest.

Air is all around you, though you cannot see it. When you breathe in, air goes through your nose, down a tube in your throat, and into your lungs. Your lungs are two soft bags inside your chest. They fill up like slow balloons, so your chest and tummy get bigger. When you breathe out, the air leaves and your chest gets smaller again. In and out together make one breath.
- e.g. Hold your palm near your nose. You feel warm air touch your hand each time you breathe out.
- e.g. Breathe out on a steel plate or a mirror. A patch of mist shows the air that came out of you.
- e.g. Lie down with a paper boat on your tummy. The boat rides up when you breathe in and down when you breathe out.
### You take about 20 breaths every minute, and more than 20,000 every day · *defined*
**Definition:** A child of six at rest takes about 20 breaths each minute. A grown-up at rest takes about 12 to 20.

Ask a grown-up to time one minute while you sit still. Count each time your tummy rises. Most six-year-olds count about 20. Now think of a whole day. Twenty breaths a minute comes to more than 20,000 breaths between one morning and the next. You did not plan even one of them. Smaller bodies breathe faster. A new baby takes about 40 breaths a minute, and a grown-up takes fewer than you.
- e.g. A sleeping baby's tummy goes up and down about twice as fast as yours.
- e.g. A resting dog takes about 15 to 30 breaths a minute, close to your own count.
- e.g. A mouse takes more than 100 breaths a minute. An elephant at rest takes about 4 to 12.
### Your body breathes by itself, and you can also breathe on purpose · *defined*
**Definition:** Breathing is automatic, which means it happens without your choosing. It can also be done by choice for a short time.

You breathe when you sleep. You breathe when you forget all about it. A small part at the bottom of your brain sends the signal for every breath. But you can also step in. You can breathe fast, breathe slowly, or hold your breath. Try to do that with your heartbeat. You cannot tell your heart to stop for a moment. Breath is one of the few things inside you that works both ways. And when you hold your breath too long, your body takes over again.
- e.g. Hold your breath and count. After some seconds the wish to breathe grows too strong to refuse.
- e.g. You blink without thinking, and you can also blink on purpose. Blinking works both ways too.
- e.g. A singer in a Carnatic concert plans every breath for a long line of song. The same singer does not plan a single breath while asleep.
- **Doctors who study breathing:** A group of nerve cells low in the brain keeps the rhythm of breathing going. Other parts of the brain, the ones you use for choosing, can change the rhythm for a while. So two parts of you share one job.
- **Yoga teachers, following Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, written about 2,000 years ago:** Breath is the one thing inside the body that a person can easily reach by choice. So yoga uses the breath as a way to reach what cannot be reached directly, such as a restless mind.
- **Scientists who study dolphins and whales:** A dolphin lives in water and must come up to breathe air. Many scientists think dolphins choose every breath. Dolphins rest one half of the brain at a time, and this may be why.
### A flat muscle under your lungs does most of the work of breathing · *defined*
**Definition:** The diaphragm is a flat muscle that lies under the lungs. When it moves down, air is drawn into the lungs.

A muscle is a part of the body that can pull. Under your lungs lies a flat muscle shaped like an upturned bowl. Its name is the diaphragm, said 'dye-a-fram'. When the diaphragm pulls down, your lungs get more room and air comes in. Your tummy gets pushed out of the way, so it rises. When the diaphragm lets go, it goes back up and the air leaves. That is why your tummy moves when you breathe, though no air goes into your tummy.
- e.g. A hiccup is the diaphragm pulling down suddenly when you did not ask.
- e.g. Babies breathe mostly with the tummy. Watch a sleeping baby and you see the tummy rise, not the chest.
- e.g. After running, the muscles between your ribs and in your neck help too, so your chest and shoulders lift.
### Your nose warms, wets and cleans the air before it reaches your lungs · *defined*
**Definition:** The nose is the body's air filter. Hairs and a sticky wet lining catch dust, and the air is warmed and dampened on the way in.

The inside of your nose is wet and warm. Small hairs stand near the opening. Further in, a sticky wet layer called mucus coats the walls. Dust and smoke stick to the hairs and the mucus. Cold air warms up as it passes, and dry air picks up water. So the air that reaches your lungs is cleaner, warmer and wetter than the air outside. Your mouth can also take in air, and that helps when you run hard. But the mouth does not clean the air as well.
- e.g. After a dusty bus ride, the inside of your nose is grey when you blow it. That dust did not reach your lungs.
- e.g. On a cold morning in Shimla or Ooty, the air feels sharp in your nose and softer in your chest.
- e.g. A camel can close the nostrils to keep desert sand out.
### Your heart beats a little faster as you breathe in, and a little slower as you breathe out · *defined*
**Definition:** In a healthy body the heartbeat speeds up slightly during each in-breath and slows slightly during each out-breath.

Your heart is a muscle in your chest that pushes blood round your body. You can feel each push as a tap on your wrist or neck. This tap is called your pulse. Now breathe in slowly for four counts and out slowly for six. A grown-up who feels your pulse can notice a change. The taps come a little closer together on the in-breath. They spread out on the out-breath. This happens in every healthy child, and it is stronger in children than in grown-ups. So a long, slow out-breath gives the heart a small rest.
- e.g. The German doctor Carl Ludwig first recorded this in 1847, in a dog whose pulse he traced on paper.
- e.g. Dogs, horses and seals show the same pattern, often more strongly than people do.
- e.g. A sigh is one long out-breath. Many people sigh without planning to after something hard is over.
### Slow breathing often makes people feel calmer, and scientists are still working out why · *debated*
**Definition:** Many studies find that breathing slowly, about six breaths a minute for a grown-up, leaves people feeling calmer. The reason is not settled.

Try five slow breaths when you feel cross or jumpy. Breathe out as if you are cooling hot kanji on a spoon. Many people feel quieter afterwards. Their heartbeat is a little slower and their shoulders drop. Tests on thousands of people have found the same. But why does it work? Scientists have several ideas and they do not agree. It also does not work for everyone, every time. That is worth knowing, so you do not feel you have done it wrong.
- e.g. A review of many studies in 2018, led by Andrea Zaccaro in Italy, found that slow breathing was linked with feeling more at ease and more alert.
- e.g. Divers who collect clams from Vembanad lake in Kerala breathe slowly before going under, because a quiet body uses up its air more slowly.
- **Patanjali, Yoga Sutras 1.34, about 2,000 years ago:** The mind becomes steady by breathing out fully and holding the breath gently. Patanjali lists this as one of several ways to calm the mind, and does not say it is the only one.
- **Svatmarama, Hatha Yoga Pradipika 2.2, written in about the 15th century CE:** When the breath moves, the mind moves. When the breath is still, the mind is still. This book treats breath and mind as two ends of one rope.
- **Kevin Yackle, Mark Krasnow and their team in California, in 2017:** In mice they found a small group of nerve cells inside the breathing centre of the brain. These cells pass news of the breathing rhythm to the part of the brain that makes a mouse alert. Slow breath, less alarm. This was shown in mice, and people have not been tested in the same way.
- **Doctors who study the vagus nerve:** A long nerve called the vagus runs from the brain to the heart, lungs and stomach. A long out-breath makes this nerve slow the heart. A slower heart may then tell the brain that all is well.
- **Psychologists who study attention:** Counting breaths gives the mind one small job. While it does that job, it stops going over the thing that upset it. On this view any quiet task that holds your attention could help, and breath is simply always at hand.
### A stretch gently pulls a muscle longer for a little while · *defined*
**Definition:** To stretch is to move a part of the body so that a muscle is pulled gently towards its full length and held there.

Muscles are the parts that move you. Each muscle is tied to bones at both ends. When a muscle pulls, it gets shorter and fatter, and a bone moves. Bend your arm and feel the bump above your elbow. That muscle has got shorter. Now reach your arm straight up as high as you can. The muscles along your side are pulled long. That is a stretch. A muscle that has sat short for a long time feels tight, and a stretch feels good to it.
- e.g. After a long car ride your legs feel stiff, and you want to stretch them the moment you get out.
- e.g. Reach both arms up after waking and you feel a pull from your hips to your fingertips.
- e.g. Sit with your legs straight and reach for your toes. The pull is at the back of your legs.
### Sensors inside your muscles tell you where your body is, even with your eyes shut · *defined*
**Definition:** Muscles and joints hold tiny sensors that report how long each muscle is and how hard it is pulling. This sense of body position is called proprioception.

Close your eyes and touch your nose with one finger. You found it without looking. How? Inside every muscle are tiny sensors that feel how long the muscle is. More sensors sit where the muscle joins the bone. They send messages to your brain all day. So you know where your arms and legs are without seeing them. When you stretch, these sensors send the feeling of pull. Your body has thousands of kinds of sensors, far more than the five that people usually count.
- e.g. You can clap your hands behind your back, though you cannot see them there.
- e.g. Stand on one leg with your eyes shut. It is harder, because your eyes were helping your muscle sensors.
- e.g. A cat walking along a wall places the back paws exactly where the front paws stepped, without looking back.
- **Charles Sherrington, an English scientist, in 1906:** He gave this sense the name proprioception, which means 'feeling one's own self'. He showed that the body senses itself as well as the outside world.
- **Yoga and kalaripayattu teachers:** Kalaripayattu is a fighting art from Kerala. Its teachers, like yoga teachers, train students to notice exactly where each limb is. They treat this noticing as a skill that grows with practice, like reading.
### A good stretch feels like a gentle pull, and pain means stop · *defined*
**Definition:** A safe stretch is slow, is held without bouncing, and gives a mild pull. Sharp pain is the body's signal to ease off.

Reach slowly until you feel a gentle pull. Stop there. Keep breathing. Stay for about three slow breaths, and then come back slowly. If you feel a sharp pain, a burning or a pinch, you have gone too far. Come back a little. Pain is a message from your body, and it is worth listening to. Warm muscles stretch more easily than cold ones. So walk about or play for a few minutes before you stretch.
- e.g. A stretch done after playing reaches further than the same stretch done just after waking.
- e.g. Bouncing in a stretch makes the muscle sensors pull the muscle tight again, so you reach less, not more.
- e.g. Each body is different. One child folds flat over her legs, another reaches his knees, and both are stretching well.
- **Patanjali, Yoga Sutras 2.46, about 2,000 years ago:** A posture should be steady and comfortable. Patanjali gives no list of difficult shapes. On his view, a pose that hurts or wobbles is not yet a pose.
- **Doctors who treat sports injuries:** A muscle pulled too hard or too fast can tear. They advise slow stretches held for about 15 to 30 seconds for grown-ups, and less for young children, who are usually bendy already.
### If you stretch every day you can reach further, and scientists argue about why · *debated*
**Definition:** Regular stretching increases how far a person can reach. Whether the muscle becomes longer, or the person simply gets used to the feeling, is not settled.

Touch your toes every day for three weeks and you will reach further. Almost everyone does. For a long time people thought the reason was plain: the muscle had grown longer. Then scientists measured the muscle itself, and many found something odd. The muscle was about as stiff as before. What had changed was the point where the person said 'enough'. So is your muscle longer, or are you braver about the pull? Both ideas have evidence.
- e.g. In your own test with a ruler, you may reach a few centimetres further after three gentle stretches. Ten minutes is too short for a muscle to grow.
- e.g. Dancers and gymnasts who train for years do show longer muscle fibres in some studies.
- **Peter Magnusson, a Danish scientist, with Cynthia Weppler, in a review of 2010:** In studies lasting a few weeks, the muscle pulls back just as hard as before. People reach further because they can bear a stronger feeling of stretch. The change is in the sensing, and not in the length.
- **Scientists who study long training, such as Anthony Blazevich in Australia:** Over many months of hard stretching, pictures taken with sound waves show the muscle fibres getting longer. So the muscle does change, but slowly, and only with a lot of stretching.
- **Yoga teachers:** Reaching further is not the aim. The aim is to be steady, to breathe easily and to notice the body. On this view the question of length misses the point of the practice.
### Dogs, cats and many other animals stretch after resting, and nobody taught them · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals with backbones stretch and yawn after sleep or long stillness. Scientists call this waking stretch pandiculation.

Watch a street dog wake up. The front legs go forward, the chest goes down, and the back makes a long slope. A cat arches up like a bridge. A hen stretches one wing and one leg on the same side. A baby stretches before birth, inside the mother. Nobody taught any of them. Animals, including us, are born knowing how to stretch. After long stillness, muscles sit short and blood moves slowly. The stretch wakes the muscle sensors and gets everything ready to move.
- e.g. The yoga pose called 'the dog facing down' has the same shape as a dog's waking stretch.
- e.g. A crow on a wire stretches one wing down past the foot, and then the other.
- e.g. You often stretch your arms without planning to when you wake up or stand up from a long sit.
- **Scientists who study animal behaviour:** The waking stretch is built in. It resets the muscles after rest so the animal is ready to run or climb at once. An animal who wakes up stiff may be caught.
- **The makers of yoga postures, as recorded in the Gheranda Samhita, a yoga book of about 1700 CE:** This book describes 32 postures and names many after other beings: the cobra, the tree, the peacock, the frog, the camel, the lion, the fish. The names record whom people watched and learned from.
### Why do we yawn? Nobody is sure · *debated*
**Definition:** A yawn is a long, deep in-breath with the mouth wide open, followed by a shorter out-breath. Its purpose is not known.

A yawn stretches the jaw, the face and the chest. People long believed that we yawn to pull in more air when the body runs short. It sounds right. But in 1987 the American scientist Robert Provine tested it. He gave people air with extra oxygen, the part of air our bodies use. They yawned just as often. So that idea did not pass the test. Scientists have other ideas now, and none is agreed. Yawns also spread. Seeing a yawn, or even reading about one, can make you yawn.
- e.g. Fish, snakes, birds, dogs and people all yawn.
- e.g. Dogs sometimes yawn after a person they know yawns. Ramiro Joly-Mascheroni and his team in London showed this in 2008.
- e.g. You may have yawned while this card was read to you.
- **Andrew Gallup, an American scientist, since 2007:** A yawn cools the brain. The big in-breath and the stretched jaw bring cooler blood to the head. People yawn less with a cold pack held to the forehead, and animals with bigger brains yawn for longer.
- **Scientists who see the yawn as a wake-up stretch:** A yawn is a stretch for the face and chest. It comes when the body is changing from rest to action or from action to rest, and it helps the change along.
- **Scientists who study animals living in groups:** A yawn that spreads puts a whole group into the same mood at the same time, sleepy together or alert together. That may be why yawns pass most easily between friends and family.
### Yoga began in India, and its poses are named after trees, animals and mountains · *defined*
**Definition:** Yoga is a practice from India that joins steady postures, called asanas, with attention to the breath and the mind.

In yoga you hold your body in a shape, breathe quietly and pay attention. Each shape is called an asana. Stand on one leg with your hands joined above your head, and you are a tree: Vrikshasana. Lie on your tummy and lift your chest, and you are a cobra: Bhujangasana. Go on hands and knees and arch your back, and you are a cat: Marjariasana. Yoga is very old. Patanjali's Yoga Sutras were written about 2,000 years ago. They speak mostly about the mind and say little about poses.
- e.g. Tadasana, the mountain: stand tall and still with your feet together. It looks like doing nothing, and it takes attention.
- e.g. Baddha Konasana, often called the butterfly: sit with the soles of your feet together and let your knees move like wings.
- e.g. The Hatha Yoga Pradipika, from about the 15th century CE, describes 15 postures. The Gheranda Samhita, about 1700 CE, describes 32.
- **Mark Singleton, a historian, in his book 'Yoga Body', 2010:** Many standing and flowing poses done in yoga classes today took their present form in the early 1900s. Teachers such as T. Krishnamacharya in Mysuru, in the 1930s, mixed older yoga with wrestlers' exercises and gymnastics of that time.
- **Jason Birch, a scholar of Sanskrit, in 2018:** He studied yoga manuscripts from the 1600s and 1700s CE that few had read. They describe well over a hundred postures, some of them moving and standing. So much of what looks new has older roots in India than Singleton's book allowed.
- **Yoga teachers in the line of Patanjali:** The age of a pose matters less than what it is for. Postures are one of eight parts of yoga. The others include how you treat other beings, how you breathe and how you steady your mind.
### Kabaddi is a game played on one breath, and India has many games that stretch and bend the body · *defined*
**Definition:** In traditional kabaddi a raider crosses into the other team's half and must keep chanting 'kabaddi' on a single out-breath until back home.

In kabaddi one player, the raider, runs into the other team's half. The raider tries to touch someone and get back. In the old way of playing, the raider chants 'kabaddi, kabaddi, kabaddi' without taking a new breath. If the chant stops, the raid is over. So the game is a test of breath as well as of speed. Try it. Most children manage about six to ten 'kabaddis'. India has many more such games, and other countries have their own.
- e.g. Kho-kho and langdi, played across Maharashtra and beyond, need quick bends, lunges and hops on one leg.
- e.g. Mallakhamb, from Maharashtra, is yoga and wrestling moves done on a tall wooden pole or a hanging rope.
- e.g. Surya Namaskar is a chain of twelve poses done with the breath. The Raja of Aundh, Bhawanrao Pant Pratinidhi, made it widely known in the 1920s.
- e.g. In China, people of every age practise tai chi and qigong in parks at dawn: slow movements matched to slow breaths.
- e.g. Thang-ta from Manipur and kalaripayattu from Kerala begin every lesson with long, deep stretches.
### Your body needs both moving and resting, and rest is not the same as doing nothing · *defined*
**Definition:** A healthy body moves a lot each day and also rests. During rest the heart, lungs and brain keep working, and muscles mend.

After a game of catch your breath is fast and your face is warm. Sit down, and in a few minutes your breath is slow again. Your body swings between these two all day. Both are needed. When you move, muscles and bones are asked to work, and they grow stronger. When you rest, the small wear from that work is mended. Even at rest your heart beats and your lungs fill. Slow breathing and gentle stretching sit in between. You are awake and working, and quiet at the same time.
- e.g. The World Health Organization advises that children from 5 to 17 years move energetically for about 60 minutes a day in total.
- e.g. A lioness rests or sleeps for most of the day, and then runs at full speed for a short hunt.
- e.g. A swift, a small bird, can stay in the air for months and rests while gliding.
- **Doctors and the World Health Organization, in guidelines of 2020:** Children today sit too long. More movement of any kind, including play, cycling and walking to school, helps the heart, bones, sleep and mood.
- **Teachers of yoga's resting pose, Shavasana:** Lying completely still while staying awake is a skill, and is listed as a posture in the Hatha Yoga Pradipika. On this view rest is something you practise, the way you practise a stretch.
### The air you breathe out is used by plants, and the air you breathe in was made by them · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals take a gas called oxygen from the air and give out a gas called carbon dioxide. Plants and tiny green beings in the sea take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen.

Air is a mix of gases you cannot see. One of them is oxygen. Your body takes some oxygen from every in-breath. It uses the oxygen to get energy out of your food. Your out-breath carries a waste gas called carbon dioxide. Here is the surprise. The tulsi on the balcony and the neem by the road take in that carbon dioxide. They use it, with sunlight and water, to build their bodies. And they give out oxygen. About half the oxygen in the air comes from tiny green beings floating in the sea.
- e.g. The air in your lungs now was in the room a moment ago, and will be outside the window soon.
- e.g. A fish breathes the oxygen that is mixed into water, using gills. An earthworm breathes through their damp skin.
- e.g. A leaf breathes through thousands of tiny openings, mostly on the underside, too small to see.
- **Scientists, since Joseph Priestley's test of 1771:** Priestley found that a mouse in a sealed jar soon grew weak, and that a sprig of mint in the jar kept the air fit for the mouse. Later scientists named the gases and showed how animals and plants pass them back and forth.
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, a Buddhist teacher from Vietnam, in his talks on 'interbeing':** When you breathe, the tree is in you and you are in the tree. He asked people to notice that no clear edge can be found where the breather ends and the air begins.
### Some animals hold their breath for hours, and some never breathe with lungs at all · *defined*
**Definition:** Every animal needs to exchange gases with the surroundings, but bodies do this in different ways: lungs, gills, skin or tiny air tubes.

You can hold your breath for perhaps half a minute. A sea turtle resting under water can stay down for hours. A Cuvier's beaked whale was once timed staying under for more than three hours. These animals have lungs like yours, and they have learned to make one breath last. Other animals do it another way. An ant has no lungs. Air goes in through small holes along the sides of the body and travels down tiny tubes. A frog breathes with lungs and also through the skin.
- e.g. The Bajau people of Indonesia and nearby seas dive for fish on one breath, some for several minutes, and have done so for many generations.
- e.g. A bar-headed goose flies over the Himalayas, where the air is so thin that a person would struggle to walk.
- e.g. A dolphin sleeping at the surface comes up for each breath with half the brain awake.
### Prana: the old Indian idea that breath carries life · *idea*
**Definition:** Prana is a Sanskrit word that means both breath and the force of life. Many old traditions used one word for breath and for life or spirit.

A living body breathes. When breathing stops for good, life ends. People everywhere noticed this long ago. In India, thinkers used the word prana for the breath and also for the life in a being. The Chandogya Upanishad tells a story. The eye, the ear, the mind and the breath argue about who is the most important. Each leaves the body for a year. The body manages without sight, without hearing, without thinking. When the breath starts to leave, all the others are pulled out with it. So they agree that breath comes first.
- e.g. In Chinese the word qi, in Greek the word pneuma, in Hebrew the word ruach, and in Latin the word spiritus all mean breath or wind, and also life or spirit.
- e.g. The Zhuangzi, a Chinese book of about the 4th century BCE, says in chapter 6 that the wise breathe from their heels, while most people breathe from their throats.
- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.1, and Prashna Upanishad, second question:** Prana is the chief of the powers in the body, the one the others depend on. In these texts prana is more than air. It is the life that moves in every being.
- **Most scientists who study the body:** Breath carries oxygen in and carbon dioxide out. No separate life force has been found by any instrument. A body with a machine doing its breathing stays alive, so life depends on the gases, however they are moved.
- **Scientists and thinkers who ask what life is:** Science has no final definition of life, only working ones. Scientists can list what living bodies do, such as breathe, grow and mend. They cannot yet say what makes a set of chemicals alive. So the old question behind the word prana is still open.

## Mostly agreed
- People who cannot breathe well through the nose, because of a cold or large adenoids, sleep less well. *How we know:* Sleep doctors record breathing during the night. Children with a blocked nose wake more often and are more tired the next day, and this improves when the blockage is treated.
- A body that stays still for many days loses muscle quickly. *How we know:* People resting in bed in hospital, and astronauts floating in space, lose muscle within a week or two. Space agencies make astronauts exercise for about two hours every day for this reason.
- Children are more bendy than grown-ups. *How we know:* Measurements of joints at different ages show that the range of movement is greatest in young children and falls slowly through life, faster in people who move little.
- Smoke and dirty air harm the lungs of children more than those of grown-ups. *How we know:* Children breathe faster for their size and their lungs are still growing. Studies in Delhi and other cities have found smaller lung capacity in children who grow up in badly polluted air.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, Galen, Ibn al-Nafis, Joseph Priestley, Antoine Lavoisier, Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, Pierre-Simon Laplace, The 4th century BCE to 1783.** For about two thousand years, doctors believed that we breathe to cool the heart. The Greek thinker Aristotle taught this in the 4th century BCE, and the doctor Galen repeated it in the 2nd century CE. It fitted what people felt. The chest is warm, the air coming in feels cool, and a person who runs gets hot and breathes harder. Around 1242 CE in Cairo, the doctor Ibn al-Nafis worked out that blood passes from the heart through the lungs and meets the air there. His finding was little known for centuries. In 1771 in England, Joseph Priestley found that a mint plant could restore air that a burning candle had spoiled, so that a mouse could live in it again. In Paris, Antoine Lavoisier and Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier tested breathing with care. In 1783, with Pierre-Simon Laplace, Antoine measured the heat given off by a guinea pig and the gas the animal breathed out. The amounts matched those of a slow fire. Marie-Anne translated the English reports, kept the records and drew the experiments. Their conclusion was that breathing does not cool the body. It feeds a slow, gentle burning of food that keeps the body warm and working.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that breathing exists to cool the heart. It was believable because the chest is warm, the in-breath feels cool, and a hot, running person breathes harder.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Stretching before a game prevents injuries.** (Generations of sports coaches. Tested by Rodney Pope and colleagues in Australia in 2000, and by Rob Herbert and Michael Gabriel in a review of 2002; *debated*)  
  For: A tight muscle seems more likely to tear, and players feel readier after stretching.  
  Against: Pope's team followed more than 1,500 army recruits. Half stretched before training and half did not. The two groups were injured about equally often. Warming up by moving, such as jogging, has better support.
- **Breathing through the mouth instead of the nose changes the shape of a growing child's face.** (Egil Harvold, an orthodontist, from tests on monkeys in the 1970s and 1980s, and some dentists today; *debated*)  
  For: Young monkeys whose noses were blocked grew longer faces and narrower jaws. Some children who breathe through the mouth for years show similar shapes.  
  Against: Studies of children give mixed results. A narrow face may be the cause of mouth breathing and not the result. Many doctors say the evidence in people is too weak to be sure.
- **People should breathe much less than they do, and deep breathing is harmful.** (Konstantin Buteyko, a doctor in the Soviet Union, from the 1950s; *minority*)  
  For: Some people with asthma, an illness that narrows the air tubes, report fewer symptoms after practising his method of small, quiet breaths.  
  Against: Careful trials found that the method did not improve how well the lungs work, though some people used less medicine. His idea that many illnesses come from breathing too much is not accepted.
- **Yawning brings extra oxygen to the body.** (A belief held widely for centuries, and still printed in some books; *rejected*)  
  For: A yawn is a very deep breath, and it comes when we are sleepy or bored, when breathing is shallow.  
  Against: Robert Provine's test in 1987 found that breathing extra oxygen did not reduce yawning, and breathing extra carbon dioxide did not increase it.

## Roots
You have felt your breath go in and out, and you have slowed it down. Now we ask what the breath tells us about who we are.

**Deep truth.** You held your breath, and after some seconds your body took the next breath without asking you. Then you breathed slowly on purpose, and your body followed you. The same breath belongs to the part of you that chooses and to the part that runs by itself. No line can be found in the breath where one stops and the other starts.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'I did it' for some things and 'my body did it' for others, as if a chooser sat inside a machine. The breath does not fit on either side of that split, because the same act is both.  
*Evidence:* In the lesson the child holds a breath until the body breaks the hold, and then slows the breath by choice for five counts. Both happen with the same lungs within one minute.

- *who am i:* You are not only the part that decides. You are also the part that keeps you breathing while you sleep. When you watch your breath, the chooser and the body meet, and you can see that they were never two.
- *what is life:* Every living being trades something with the world around them, all the time. You trade gases with the air. A fish trades with the water and a leaf trades with the sky. A life is this trading that never stops, and not a thing sealed in a skin.
- *what is this world:* The oxygen in your chest was given out by a leaf or by a tiny green being in the sea. The gas you breathe out will become part of a plant. The world is passing through you with every breath, and you are passing through the world.

Breath is the easiest place to notice that you are not sealed off. Air that was outside becomes part of your blood within seconds, and air that was part of you is outside again. The breath also shows that the one who chooses and the body that runs by itself are a single being. Old teachers in India used the breath as the first step in looking inward for exactly this reason. It is always present, and it is always on the border between you and everything else.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 5.1:** The powers of the body, sight, hearing, mind and breath, argue about who is greatest. Each leaves for a year, and the body lives on. When the breath begins to leave, all the others are pulled up with it, as a strong horse pulls up the pegs of the ropes. They agree that breath is the first among them. (The story makes a point a child can test. You can shut your eyes or block your ears for a minute and carry on. You cannot stop your breath for long. The text goes further than science does, and calls breath the life in all beings.)
- **Anapanasati Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 118:** The Buddha teaches: breathing in a long breath, know that you are breathing in a long breath. Breathing out a short breath, know that you are breathing out a short breath. He does not ask for the breath to be changed, only known. (This is the lesson's noticing, done with care. The body breathes by itself and the mind watches. The Buddha taught that this simple watching steadies the mind.)
- **Patanjali, Yoga Sutras 2.46 and 1.34:** A posture should be steady and comfortable. And the mind can be calmed by breathing out and holding the breath gently. (The child's rule for stretching, a gentle pull and no pain, is Patanjali's rule in other words. The link between out-breath and calm is the one scientists are still testing.)
- Science (settled): The oxygen that animals breathe is made by plants, algae and tiny green beings in the sea, and the carbon dioxide that animals breathe out is taken in by them.
- Science (open): Slow breathing changes the heartbeat and often changes how a person feels. The pathway from breath to feeling is being traced, and is not fully known.
- Other view, Most scientists who study the brain, and the old Indian Charvaka school: Breathing is a rhythm kept by nerve cells low in the brain, and choosing is the work of other nerve cells. Both are parts of one body made of matter. On this view there is nothing more to find in the breath than gases and signals, and the feeling of a self who watches is something the brain makes.
- Practice, Whose breath is this? (3 min): Sit or lie down comfortably. Put one hand on your tummy. For five breaths, do nothing. Only feel your hand go up and down. Now for five breaths, make the out-breath long and slow, as if cooling hot kanji. Now stop choosing, and let your body breathe again. Notice the moment when your body takes over. Tell me what you noticed.
- Ask: When your body took the breath back from you, did it feel like someone else, or like you?
- Ask: The air in your chest came from a leaf. Is that air part of you now, or part of the leaf, or both?
- Ask: A cat stretches without thinking about it, and you can stretch on purpose. Are these the same stretch?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When you are asleep, your body keeps breathing. When you hold your breath, you are in charge. So who is the one that breathes: you, or your body? (hint: Ask yourself if 'you' and 'your body' are two things or one. Try holding your breath and notice who wins.)
- *big:* A dog was never taught to stretch, and you were never taught to breathe. What else do you know how to do that nobody taught you? (hint: Think about what a new baby can already do on the first day.)
- *deep:* After five slow breaths you feel calmer. Did the breath change the feeling, or did you change the feeling by paying attention? (hint: How could you test it? What could you do instead of breathing slowly that also needs attention?)
- *deep:* The air you breathe out goes into a tree, and the air the tree gives out goes into you. Where does your breath stop being yours? (hint: Try to point to the exact place where the air becomes 'you'.)
- *deep:* If stretching every day does not make the muscle longer, but only makes you feel the pull differently, has your body changed or has your mind changed? (hint: Think about where the feeling of a stretch is made: in the leg, in the brain, or in both.)
- *big:* A whale chooses every breath. You choose almost none. Would your day feel different if you had to choose each one? (hint: Imagine reading, eating and sleeping while remembering to breathe.)
- *big:* Many old languages use one word for breath and for life. Were those people right that breath is life, or did they mix up two things? (hint: Think of a seed, who does not seem to breathe, and of a person breathing with the help of a machine.)
- *big:* Yoga poses are named after the cobra, the cat and the tree. Is a person in tree pose learning something about being a tree, or only making a shape? (hint: Stand in the pose for ten breaths on a windy day, and then say what you noticed.)
- *deep:* Is sitting still and breathing slowly resting, or is it doing something? (hint: Compare it with watching a cartoon. Which one takes more effort from you?)

## Story: Who Taught the Dog?
*A made-up story set in Alappuzha, Kerala. The people and the dog are imagined. What is real: the yoga pose named after a dog's stretch, the one-breath chant in traditional kabaddi, and the clam divers of Vembanad lake, who work on a held breath. Nobody knows what a stretch feels like to a dog.*

Ammini was six, and she woke before the crows. She sat on the back step with her chin on her knees. Under the jackfruit tree a brown street dog named Kari was still asleep.

Then Kari woke. She did not jump up. First she pushed her front legs far forward. Her chest went down to the ground and her back made a long slope. She held it. Then she opened her mouth in a huge yawn and shook herself from nose to tail.

On the veranda next door, Shyla aunty was on her mat. Shyla aunty played kabaddi for the district team. Her hands were on the mat, her hips were high, and her back made a long slope.

'Aunty!' said Ammini. 'You are copying Kari!'

Shyla aunty laughed upside down. 'I am. This pose is called the dog facing down. Long ago, people watched dogs and thought, that looks useful.'

'Then who taught Kari?'

'Nobody taught Kari. She was born knowing.'

Ammini went over and tried it. The backs of her legs pulled. 'It hurts,' she said.

'Is it a hurt, or is it a pull?' asked Shyla aunty. 'A hurt is sharp. A pull is like a kite string in the wind.'

Ammini thought. 'A pull.'

'Then stay for three breaths. If it turns into a hurt, come back.'

In the evening Ammini went with her aunt to the sand ground. The team was practising raids. A raider ran across the line saying 'kabaddi-kabaddi-kabaddi' without stopping. She touched a player's ankle, twisted free and dived home, still chanting.

'Why does she keep saying it?' asked Ammini.

'To show she has not taken a new breath,' said Shyla aunty. 'One raid, one breath. Try.'

Ammini filled her chest. 'Kabaddi-kabaddi-kabaddi-kabaddi-kabaddi-kabaddi-kab...' She gasped. Six and a half.

Then the small children played a real game. Ammini was caught on her first raid. A bigger boy named Sunil held her foot, and she was out. Her face went hot. Her breath came fast and high in her chest. She sat down hard on the sand and would not look at anyone.

Shyla aunty sat beside her. She did not say 'never mind'. She said, 'Put your hand on your tummy. Breathe out as if you are cooling hot kanji. Slowly. Again.'

Ammini did not want to. She did it anyway. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. The hot feeling was still there, but it had become smaller, like a wave going back down the beach.

'Did I make the angry go small?' she asked. 'Or did my breath do it?'

'I have played this game for twenty years,' said Shyla aunty, 'and I still ask that.'

On the way home they passed Beevi umma, who sat mending a net. Beevi umma had dived for clams in Vembanad lake for thirty years.

'Umma,' said Ammini, 'how long can you hold your breath?'

'Longer when I am quiet,' said Beevi umma. 'Before a dive I sat in the boat and breathed slowly. A quiet body uses up its air slowly. A frightened body eats it in no time. The lake taught me that. No teacher did.'

'Like Kari,' said Ammini.

'Like Kari,' said Beevi umma. 'But Kari cannot choose to do it when she is angry. You can. That part people had to find out.'

'How do you know Kari cannot?' asked Ammini.

Beevi umma put down the net. 'I do not know,' she said slowly. 'I have never asked her.'

That night Kari slept under the jackfruit tree. Her side went up and down, up and down. Ammini lay by the window and made her own breath go with Kari's. In with Kari. Out with Kari.

Somewhere in the middle she stopped trying. She was asleep. And her breath went on all night without her, as it had every night since she was born.

**For the parent:** The body already knows how to stretch and how to breathe. What people added was noticing it, and choosing to do it when it is needed.

- Ask: Ammini asked if she made the angry feeling small, or if her breath did. What do you think?
- Ask: Kari stretches without being taught. Beevi umma learned to breathe slowly before a dive. Is one of these cleverer than the other?
- Ask: When Ammini fell asleep, who was breathing?

## Experiments
### e1 · How Far Can I Reach? A stretch test with an eraser and a ruler (22 min)
- **question:** Does a few gentle stretches change how far I can reach towards my toes?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will you reach further after the stretches, the same, or less far? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Only the stretching changes. We use the same floor, the same eraser, the same wall and the same slow reach. We reach three times before and three times after, because the first try is often the stiffest and one try can be a fluke. We never bounce. We warm up first. This test cannot tell stretching apart from warming up and practice, and the last step shows how to check.
- **safety:** Stretch only to a mild tug. A sharp pinch or a burn means stop and ease off. Never bounce. If your child has an injury, skip the stretch and just do the notice-and-count parts.
- **record:** The results table with six gaps. Circle the smallest gap before and the smallest after. Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw and why.
- **results table:** Try | Gap before (cm) | Gap after (cm)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table together. 2. Ask your child to guess and say why. 3. Show the wall, the eraser and the ruler.  
  *Parent says:* We will measure how far you can reach. Then you will do some gentle stretches. Will you reach further, the same, or less far? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess if you will reach further after the stretches. Say why.
  - Ask: Will you reach further after the stretches? Why? (good: Yes, because the stretch makes my legs looser.; The same, because it is only ten minutes.; I do not know. Let us find out.)
  - Note: Write the guess down without saying if it is right. Every guess is welcome. Some children say a muscle cannot change so fast. That is a good thought.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. March on the spot for one minute together. 2. Sit on the mat with straight legs and the soles of the feet flat on the wall. Knees stay straight but not pushed down. 3. Put the eraser on the floor between the hands, level with the hips.  
  *Parent says:* First we warm up, because warm muscles stretch more easily. Now sit with your feet against the wall. Put the eraser by your hips.  
  *Child mode:* March for one minute. Then sit with your feet on the wall.
  - Note: If a smooth floor is not available, put the eraser on a sheet of paper. If the child's legs are not quite straight, that is fine. Keep the same seat for every try.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to breathe out and slide the eraser slowly forward along the floor, beside the legs. 2. They stop at a gentle pull. 3. Measure the gap from the eraser to the wall with the ruler. A smaller gap means a longer reach. 4. Write it down. 5. Do this three times, with a few breaths of rest between tries.  
  *Parent says:* Breathe out, and slide the eraser slowly. Stop at a gentle pull. No bouncing. I will measure how far the eraser is from the wall.  
  *Child mode:* Breathe out and slide the eraser slowly. Stop when you feel a gentle pull.
  - Note: Measure from the front of the eraser to the wall, along the floor. If the gap is longer than the ruler, measure twice and add. Say the number aloud and write it down. Do not say 'good' or 'not good'. Every gap is just a number.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit with straight legs again. 2. Ask your child to reach gently towards the toes with both hands and stop at a gentle pull. 3. Hold for three slow breaths while you count them aloud. 4. Come back slowly. 5. Do this three times.  
  *Parent says:* Reach towards your toes until you feel a gentle pull. Stay there for three slow breaths. In... out... one. In... out... two. In... out... three. Come back slowly. Where did you feel the pull?  
  *Child mode:* Reach for your toes until you feel a gentle pull. Stay for three slow breaths. Point to where you feel it.
  - Ask: Where do you feel the pull? (good: At the back of my legs.; Behind my knees.)
  - Note: If the pull turns into a pinch or a burn, come back a little. A child who only reaches the knees is stretching just as well as one who reaches the toes. Never press the child's back.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the eraser back by the hips. 2. Measure three reaches again in the same way as before. 3. Write the gaps in the After column.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same slow reach as before, three times. I will measure each one.  
  *Child mode:* Slide the eraser slowly, like before. Three times.
  - Note: Keep everything the same as in the first reaches. Same floor, same eraser, same slow reach.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the smallest gap before and the smallest gap after. 2. Ask the questions. 3. Then tell your child that this test cannot yet say why a gap changed.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think? What did you see? Why do you think it changed, or did not change?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What could have changed in ten minutes: the muscle, or the feeling? (good: The muscle got looser.; I got used to the pull.; I was warmer from marching.)
  - Note: Most children gain 1 to 4 centimetres, and some gain nothing. Both are real results. A muscle needs far longer than ten minutes to grow. The change may come from warm muscles, from getting used to the pull, or from practice. Scientists argue about this. To check, on another day, rest for three minutes instead of stretching, and measure again. If the gap changes only after stretching, the stretch did it.
  - Think: If you stretched every day for a month, what would you expect to happen? How would you find out?
### e2 · How Many Kabaddis on One Breath? A breath test (15 min)
- **question:** Does a minute of slow breathing change how many 'kabaddis' I can say on one breath?
- **predict:** Guess first. After slow breathing, will you say more, the same, or fewer? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The chant is the same each time: one steady 'kabaddi' about every second, on a single out-breath, with no new breath. Only the minute of slow breathing changes. We try three times before and three times after, with a rest between tries, because one try can be a fluke. This test cannot tell slow breathing apart from practice, so the numbers may rise, stay the same or fall, and every one of them is a real result.
- **safety:** Chant only as long as it is easy. Stop at the first wish to breathe. Never chant until you feel dizzy, and sit down if you feel light-headed. Do only three tries each time. A child with asthma or a cold can chant for fewer kabaddis and does not need to match anyone.
- **record:** The results table. Circle the biggest number before and after. Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw and why.
- **results table:** Try | Kabaddis before slow breathing | Kabaddis after slow breathing
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the game: the raider chants on one out-breath and may not take a new breath. 2. Show it yourself first, with a steady 'kabaddi' about once a second. 3. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* In kabaddi, the raider says 'kabaddi' again and again on one breath. She cannot breathe in until she is back home. How many can you say? After a minute of slow breathing, will you say more, the same, or fewer? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the raider's chant. Then guess if slow breathing will help you say more.
  - Ask: Will slow breathing help you say more? Why? (good: More, because I will be calm.; The same, because I have the same lungs.; I do not know. Let us find out.)
  - Note: Write down the guess without saying if it is right.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to stand, breathe in comfortably, not as much as possible, and chant on one out-breath. 2. Tap your finger once a second so the speed stays the same. 3. Count each 'kabaddi' and stop the moment a new breath is taken. 4. Rest for 30 seconds. 5. Do it three times in all and write down each count.  
  *Parent says:* Take a comfortable breath in. Now chant, one 'kabaddi' for each tap. I will count. Stop when you need to breathe.  
  *Child mode:* Take a comfortable breath. Chant 'kabaddi' with each tap. Stop when you need to breathe.
  - Note: Most children manage about six to ten. Count only whole chants. Do not cheer a bigger number or sigh at a smaller one.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to sit comfortably and put a hand on the tummy. 2. Time one minute. 3. Together, breathe in for a count of three and out for a count of five, again and again.  
  *Parent says:* Sit and put a hand on your tummy. Breathe in, two, three. Breathe out, two, three, four, five. We will do this for one minute.  
  *Child mode:* Sit. Hand on your tummy. Breathe in for three counts and out for five counts. Do it for one minute.
  - Note: If the long out-breath feels too long, use in for two and out for four. Nobody should strain.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand again. 2. Do three more chants in the same way as before, with a rest between. 3. Write each count in the After column.  
  *Parent says:* Now chant again in the same way. Same speed, same tap, one out-breath.  
  *Child mode:* Chant 'kabaddi' with each tap, like before. Three times.
  - Note: Keep the tap speed the same. Stop any try that makes your child dizzy.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the biggest number before and after. 2. Ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think? What did you see? What could have made the numbers change, or stay the same?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What else, besides slow breathing, could change your count? (good: Getting better at the game with practice.; Being more tired.; Saying it faster or slower.)
  - Note: The numbers might go up, stay the same or go down. All three are real. The test cannot tell slow breathing apart from practice and from how fast each chant went. A scientist would ask, what could we test next? One idea is to try on another day with a minute of sitting still instead of slow breathing, and compare.
  - Think: Why might a raider breathe slowly before a raid? What would you test to find out?

## Activities
### a1 · Boat on the Tummy: watch your breath (10 min)
- **record:** Draw the boat when you breathe in and when you breathe out. Or tell your grown-up what the boat did each time.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to lie on their back with knees bent or straight, whichever is comfortable. 2. Place the boat on the tummy, just above the belly button. 3. Say nothing for a few breaths and let your child watch.  
  *Parent says:* Lie down and put the boat on your tummy. Do nothing special. Just watch what the boat does. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Lie down. Put the boat on your tummy. Watch it.
  - Ask: What does the boat do? (good: It goes up and down.; It rides up when I breathe in.)
  - Note: Loose clothes work best. If the boat tips off, use a flatter lid.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to breathe slowly. Then fast, for a few breaths only. Then slowly again. 2. Next, ask them to try to keep the tummy flat and lift only the chest. 3. Ask what is harder.  
  *Parent says:* Breathe slowly and watch the boat. Now breathe fast for a few breaths. Now slowly again. Now try to breathe in without moving the boat. Is that easy?  
  *Child mode:* Breathe slowly. Then fast for a few breaths. Then try to breathe without moving the boat.
  - Ask: Is it easy to breathe without moving the boat? (good: It is hard.; My chest went up instead.)
  - Note: Fast breathing only for about five breaths. Stop if your child feels dizzy. Children can often breathe in using the chest alone. It is not wrong, and the boat just stays still.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit up. 2. Tell your child that air does not go into the tummy. Air goes into the lungs, which are in the chest. A flat muscle under the lungs, the diaphragm, pulls down and pushes the tummy out. 3. Ask the question.  
  *Parent says:* Air goes into your lungs, up in your chest. But the boat is on your tummy. Why do you think the tummy moves?  
  *Child mode:* Think about why your tummy moves when the air goes into your chest.
  - Ask: Why does the tummy move? (good: Something pushes it down there.; Something under the lungs moves.)
  - Note: The diaphragm is a flat muscle shaped like an upturned bowl, below the lungs. It pulls down to bring air in and pushes the tummy out of the way.
  - Think: What would the boat do on a sleeping baby's tummy?
### a2 · Four Poses Named After Beings (15 min)
- **safety:** Stay in each pose only while it is a gentle pull. For the tree, rest the foot on the calf or ankle, never on the knee. For the cobra, lift only the chest a little and keep the hips down. A sharp pinch or a burn means come out of the pose.
- **record:** Draw your favourite pose, and tell your grown-up where you felt the pull.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the mountain (Tadasana): feet together, arms by the sides, standing tall. 2. Stay for three slow breaths. 3. Ask your child to feel their feet on the floor.  
  *Parent says:* Stand like a mountain. Feet together, arms down, tall and still. Breathe in slowly, out slowly. Three breaths. Where do you feel the floor?  
  *Child mode:* Stand tall like a mountain. Feel your feet on the floor. Breathe slowly three times.
  - Note: It looks like doing nothing. It takes attention. Keep the shoulders soft.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand near a wall. 2. Show the tree (Vrikshasana): rest one foot on the other ankle or calf, never the knee. 3. Bring the hands together in front, or up above the head. 4. Hold for three slow breaths, then change legs.  
  *Parent says:* Now be a tree. Stand on one leg, with the other foot on your ankle. Hands together. Breathe slowly three times. Then the other leg.  
  *Child mode:* Stand on one leg like a tree. Rest your hand on the wall if you wobble. Breathe three times. Then change legs.
  - Ask: Is a tree steady, or does she sway a little? (good: She sways a little.; She stays still.)
  - Note: Wobbling is normal. A tree sways in the wind and stays rooted. Put a hand on the wall for steadiness.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the cat (Marjariasana): on hands and knees, hands under shoulders, knees under hips. 2. Breathe out and round the back up like an angry cat. 3. Breathe in and let the back dip down gently. 4. Do this three times, slowly.  
  *Parent says:* Go on hands and knees. Breathe out and round your back up like a cat. Breathe in and let it dip down a little. Do it slowly, three times.  
  *Child mode:* Go on hands and knees. Round your back like a cat as you breathe out. Do it three times.
  - Note: This is the same shape a cat makes when she arches on a wall. Keep the movement small and slow.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the cobra (Bhujangasana): lie on the tummy, hands under the shoulders, elbows close to the body. 2. Breathe in and lift only the head and chest, a little. 3. The hips and legs stay on the mat. 4. Hold for three slow breaths. 5. Come down slowly.  
  *Parent says:* Lie on your tummy. Hands by your shoulders. Breathe in and lift your head and chest a little, like a cobra rising. Stay for three breaths. Come down slowly. Do you feel a gentle pull in your back?  
  *Child mode:* Lie on your tummy. Lift your chest a little like a cobra. Breathe three times. Come down slowly.
  - Ask: Where do you feel the pull? (good: In my tummy and my back.; In my chest.)
  - Note: Lift only a little. The hands do not push. If the lower back pinches, lift less.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to lie on their back, arms and legs loose (Shavasana, the resting pose). 2. Give one minute of quiet while they breathe. 3. Ask how the body feels now.  
  *Parent says:* Lie on your back and let your arms and legs go loose. Breathe slowly. Be still for one minute. How does your body feel now?  
  *Child mode:* Lie on your back. Let everything go loose. Breathe slowly for one minute.
  - Ask: How does your body feel after the poses? (good: Quiet.; Warm.; A bit tired.)
  - Note: Some children feel calmer. Some do not. Both are true results, and slow breathing does not work the same way for everyone every time.
  - Think: Which pose did you like best? What being would you like to be a pose of?
### a3 · Watch an Animal Stretch (12 min)
- **safety:** Watch street dogs and other animals from a distance. Never touch, feed or approach an animal you do not know.
- **record:** A drawing of the animal's stretch, with arrows showing which parts are pulled long.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a place to watch a resting animal. A dog under a parked auto, a cat on a wall, a hen, a crow, or your own pet. 2. Wait quietly. Animals often stretch just after they wake or stand up. 3. If you cannot find one, look at your own body when you wake up tomorrow and watch yourself stretch.  
  *Parent says:* Look for an animal that is resting. Watch quietly. When she wakes up, what does she do first?  
  *Child mode:* Find a resting animal. Watch her wake up. What does she do first?
  - Ask: What did the animal do when she woke up? (good: She stretched her legs forward.; She arched her back.; She yawned.)
  - Note: This may take a few minutes. Do not wake or disturb the animal. If no animal wakes up, use your own stretch tomorrow morning, or a pet or baby you know.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to draw what the animal did, and mark with arrows which parts were pulled long. 2. Then copy the shape on a mat with the body.  
  *Parent says:* Draw her stretch. Which parts got long? Now try the same shape yourself.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the stretch. Then copy it with your body.
  - Note: Dogs often put the front legs forward with the chest low. Cats arch up like a bridge. A hen or a crow may stretch a wing out, one side at a time.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask the questions below. 2. Tell your child that animals stretch without being taught, and scientists call this waking stretch pandiculation.  
  *Parent says:* Did anyone teach her to stretch? How does she know how? Do you think she feels something good?  
  *Child mode:* Think about how the animal knows how to stretch.
  - Ask: Did anyone teach the animal to stretch? (good: No, she knew already.; She was born knowing how.)
  - Note: Nobody knows exactly what a stretch feels like to an animal. It is fair to say we do not know.
  - Think: You stretch without planning to when you wake up. Is it you doing it, or your body?
### a4 · Count My Breaths in One Minute (10 min)
- **record:** Your count and the grown-up's count, as tally marks in groups of five.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to sit still and relaxed. 2. Time one minute. 3. Your child makes a tally mark each time the tummy rises, or you watch the tummy and count for them. 4. Write the total.  
  *Parent says:* Sit still. When I say go, make one mark each time your tummy rises. Go. Stop. How many marks?  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Make one mark every time your tummy rises. Stop after one minute.
  - Ask: How many breaths did you take in a minute? (good: About 20.; About 18 to 25.)
  - Note: Most six-year-olds count about 20. Counting changes breathing a little, so do not worry about the exact number. If your child finds it hard to count and breathe, watch the tummy and count for them.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Now count your own breaths for one minute while your child counts the marks, or watches the tummy. 2. Compare the two numbers. 3. Ask the question.  
  *Parent says:* Now I will breathe and you count. Who took more breaths, you or me? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Count a grown-up's breaths for one minute. Who took more?
  - Ask: Who breathes faster, you or a grown-up? Why? (good: Me, because I am smaller.; I do not know.)
  - Note: A resting grown-up takes about 12 to 20 breaths a minute. Smaller bodies breathe faster. A new baby takes about 40 a minute, and an elephant at rest about 4 to 12. Counts are different each day, and a number a little over or under is normal.
  - Think: Your breath does not stop all night. How many breaths do you take while you sleep?
### a5 · Who Is in Charge of My Breath? (6 min)
- **safety:** Hold your breath only for a few easy seconds, and breathe again the moment you want to. Never hold until you feel dizzy. Never hold your breath under water.
- **record:** Say what you felt when you held your breath, and what you felt when you slowed it.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to take a comfortable breath and hold it. 2. Count the seconds aloud, and stop at 10 at most. 3. Tell them to breathe again the moment they want. 4. Do it twice.  
  *Parent says:* Take a normal breath and hold it. I will count. Breathe again as soon as you want to. What do you feel in your body?  
  *Child mode:* Take a breath and hold it. Breathe again when you want to. What do you feel?
  - Ask: What did your body do? (good: It wanted to breathe again.; My chest got tight.; It breathed by itself.)
  - Note: Stop at 10 seconds even if your child wants to continue. Never ask for a longer hold or a contest. The body asks for air, and that is the point.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to put a hand on the tummy. 2. Breathe in through the nose for a count of three. 3. Breathe out slowly for a count of five, as if cooling hot kanji. 4. Do five breaths.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are in charge. Breathe in for three. Breathe out slowly for five, like cooling hot kanji. Five times. Who is breathing now, you or your body?  
  *Child mode:* Breathe in for three counts and out for five, like cooling hot kanji. Do it five times.
  - Ask: Who is breathing, you or your body? (good: Me.; My body.; Both of us.)
  - Note: There is no right answer. Many people feel it is both. The breath goes on by itself, and we can step in.
  - Think: Can you make your heartbeat stop for a moment, as you can with your breath?

## Challenges
### c1 · Build a Model of the Muscle That Pulls Air In (25 min)
- **problem:** Make a model where pulling something at the bottom makes a balloon, the 'lung', fill with air by itself, and letting go makes the air leave.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the bottle with scissors and covers the cut edge with tape, because a cut plastic edge is sharp. Keep balloons away from children under five and never put a balloon in the mouth.
- **your turn:** Now change something. Make the model fill up more, or with less pulling. Which change helped most? How does your model differ from a real body?
- **constraints:** No blowing into the balloon.; The balloon must fill and empty again and again.; Use only things from the list.
- **general:** Cut the bottom off the bottle. Push a balloon into the bottle and stretch its neck over the mouth, so it hangs inside. Cover the open bottom with a cut-open second balloon, tied on with a rubber band. Pull the rubber down and the inside balloon fills. Push it up and the balloon empties.
- **creative:** Use a plastic glove for the bottom, and fill it with a pinch of air to show the 'diaphragm' moving.; Use a bigger bottle and make two balloon 'lungs' with a straw joined to both.; Draw the real diaphragm on the bottle and label the parts: lungs, diaphragm, nose.
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts the bottle about one-third of the way up from the bottom, and tapes the cut edge. 2. Cut the neck off the second balloon. 3. Keep the stretchy part of that balloon. It will cover the open bottom. You will pull it down by pinching the middle.  
  *Parent says:* We will build a model of the muscle that pulls air in. The bottle is the chest. The balloon inside is the lung. The stretchy rubber at the bottom is the diaphragm.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the parts. The bottle is the chest. Which part do you think is the lung?
  - Note: A grown-up does all the cutting. Cover the whole cut edge with tape. Keep the neck of the bottle open.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push the first balloon into the bottle. 2. Stretch its neck over the bottle mouth, so it hangs inside. 3. Stretch the cut balloon over the open bottom and fix it with a rubber band. 4. Test: pull the bottom rubber down and watch the lung.  
  *Parent says:* Now pull the bottom rubber down slowly. What happens to the balloon inside? Now let go. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Pull the bottom rubber down. Watch the balloon inside. Then let go.
  - Ask: What happens to the balloon when you pull the bottom down? (good: It gets bigger.; It fills up by itself.)
  - Note: If the balloon does not fill, air is leaking. Press the rubber band tighter, or add tape round the bottom, then try again. The seal has to be airtight.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to try small pulls and big pulls, and to see what changes. 2. Ask what is different from a real body.  
  *Parent says:* Try a small pull and a big pull. Which fills the balloon more? In a real body, what pulls down?  
  *Child mode:* Try a small pull and a big pull. Which fills the lung more?
  - Ask: What is the 'diaphragm' in your model? (good: The stretchy rubber at the bottom.; The balloon at the bottom.)
  - Note: Real lungs are not balloons in a bottle, and the real diaphragm is a muscle that curves under the lungs. A model shows an idea, and it is not the real thing.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child try the creative ideas, or invent their own. 2. Ask which design worked best.  
  *Parent says:* Which change filled your lung the most? What would you change next?  
  *Child mode:* Try a change. Did it fill more?
  - Ask: What did you change, and what happened? (good: I pulled further and it filled more.; I used a bigger bottle.)
  - Note: There are many good designs. Do not rank them. Ask your child to say why theirs works.
### c2 · Make a Reach Measurer That Gives the Same Answer Every Time (18 min)
- **problem:** Make a tool that measures how far a child reaches along the floor. The same reach must give the same number each time.
- **your turn:** Make your own measurer. Test it with two people on the same day and again on another day. Does it give a number you can trust?
- **constraints:** No bought material.; It must work on a floor next to a wall.; Measuring twice must give numbers within one centimetre.
- **general:** An eraser slid along the floor, and a ruler laid from the wall, so the gap can be read.; A paper strip with marks every centimetre, taped to the floor.
- **creative:** A colour-coded strip: green, yellow and orange zones, so a young child can read the zone instead of a number.; A sliding block in a groove made from two rulers, so it stays in a straight line.; A strip that a friend can read from the side, without bending over.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to think of a way to measure the reach. 2. Share the first solution if they get stuck. 3. Mark a paper strip in centimetres with a ruler.  
  *Parent says:* How can we measure your reach so it gives the same number every time? What do we need to keep the same?  
  *Child mode:* Think of a way to measure your reach. What must stay the same each time?
  - Ask: What must stay the same for a fair measure? (good: The wall.; The start line.; The floor.)
  - Note: A fair tool has the same start and the same slow reach each time. A tool that moves when you touch it is not fair.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Build the strip and test it. 2. Measure the same child three times. 3. Do the numbers match? If not, what moved?  
  *Parent says:* Test your measurer three times with the same reach. Are the numbers close? What could make them different?  
  *Child mode:* Test your measurer three times. Are the numbers close?

- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child try the creative ideas, or their own. 2. Ask which design worked best, and why.

## Worksheets
### Breath and Stretch (core) (core, 11 questions)
1. (mcq) You hold your breath. After a few seconds, what does your body do?  
   Options: It stops breathing for good / It makes you breathe again / It falls asleep  
   **Answer:** It makes you breathe again. *Why:* Your body asks for air and takes over again. Breathing goes on by itself, and you can only step in for a short time.
2. (truefalse) You can tell your heart to stop for a moment, just like your breath.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. You can change your breath on purpose for a short time. You cannot tell your heart to stop, so breath works both ways and the heart does not.
3. (sort) Is it a gentle pull or a pain? Put each feeling in a box.  
   **Answer:** a slow pull at the back of my legs → Gentle pull; a sharp pinch in my knee → Pain, so stop; a warm stretchy feeling in my side → Gentle pull; a burning feeling in my back → Pain, so stop. *Why:* A gentle pull is mild and you can breathe through it. A sharp pinch or a burn is pain. Pain means ease off.
4. (match) Draw a line from each being to its pose.  
   **Answer:** Tree → Vrikshasana; Cobra → Bhujangasana; Cat → Marjariasana; Mountain → Tadasana. *Why:* Yoga poses are named after beings and places. The tree is Vrikshasana, the cobra is Bhujangasana, the cat is Marjariasana and the mountain is Tadasana.
5. (fill) Fill the gap: When a stretch gives a sharp pain, you ____.  
   **Answer:** stop. *Why:* A sharp pain is a message from the body. Ease off until it is only a gentle pull.
6. (order) Put the steps of one stretch in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Reach slowly until you feel a gentle pull', 'Stay for three slow breaths', 'Come back slowly']. *Why:* You reach slowly first, so the pull stays gentle. Then you stay and breathe. Then you come back slowly, because a fast change can hurt.
7. (write) Put a hand on your tummy and breathe slowly. Say what moves, and what you think moves it.  
   **Answer:** My tummy goes up when I breathe in and down when I breathe out. Something under my lungs pushes it.. *Why:* A good answer says the tummy rises with an in-breath. A strong answer says that a flat muscle under the lungs, the diaphragm, pulls down.
8. (draw) Draw yourself breathing in. Show with arrows where the air goes.  
   **Answer:** A child with arrows going in at the nose and down into two lung shapes in the chest.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the air going into the lungs in the chest, and not into the tummy.
9. (mcq) Which of these stretch when they wake up?  
   Options: Only cats / Dogs, cats, birds and people / Only people  
   **Answer:** Dogs, cats, birds and people. *Why:* Many animals with backbones stretch after resting, and nobody taught them. People do too.
10. (perspective) Two friends talk about breathing.  
   **Answer:** No single right answer. Breathing is both.. *Why:* Both friends are partly right. Your body breathes by itself, and you can also change it. Where the one stops and the other starts is hard to say.
11. (mcq) Which is a good warm-up before a stretch?  
   Options: Sitting still for a long time / Walking about for a few minutes / Holding your breath  
   **Answer:** Walking about for a few minutes. *Why:* Warm muscles stretch more easily than cold ones. Walking about warms them.
### Breath, Heart and Muscles (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) What does your nose do to the air before it reaches your lungs?  
   Options: It warms, wets and cleans it / It makes it colder / It turns it into water  
   **Answer:** It warms, wets and cleans it. *Why:* The inside of your nose is wet and warm, with hairs and mucus that catch dust.
2. (truefalse) Bouncing in a stretch helps you reach further.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bouncing makes the muscle sensors pull the muscle tight again, so you reach less.
3. (count) You take about 20 breaths each minute. How many breaths in 5 minutes?  
   **Answer:** 100. *Why:* 20 + 20 + 20 + 20 + 20 = 100. Five minutes of the same 20 gives 100 breaths.
4. (fill) A hiccup is when the ____ pulls down by itself.  
   **Answer:** diaphragm. *Why:* The diaphragm is the flat muscle under the lungs. A hiccup is it pulling down suddenly, when you did not ask.
5. (sort) Which ideas do scientists agree on, and which are still being worked out?  
   **Answer:** The heartbeat slows a little on the out-breath → Scientists agree; Why we yawn → Still being worked out; Why slow breathing leaves people calmer → Still being worked out; Muscles pull on bones to move us → Scientists agree. *Why:* Scientists agree on what happens in the body. They do not agree on why we yawn or why slow breathing often calms people.
6. (write) Your friend says, 'Only people stretch.' What do you say back? Use an animal you know.  
   **Answer:** Dogs, cats, hens, crows and many other animals stretch when they wake up, and nobody teaches them.. *Why:* A good answer names a real animal and what she does. A strong answer says the animal was not taught.
7. (label) Mark L on the lungs and D on the flat muscle below them.  
   **Answer:** L on the two soft bags, D on the flat curved muscle under the lungs.. *Why:* The lungs fill with air. The diaphragm lies under them and pulls down.
8. (mcq) You measure your reach, stretch, then measure again and reach further. What else could explain it?  
   Options: Only the stretch / Warming up and practice too / The ruler changed  
   **Answer:** Warming up and practice too. *Why:* In a short test the stretch, the warm-up and practice happen together. We cannot tell which one made the change.
9. (perspective) Two scientists look at why daily stretching lets people reach further.  
   **Answer:** Both ideas have some evidence.. *Why:* Some studies find longer muscle fibres after years of training. Others find the muscle as stiff as before, and the person only feels the pull less. Scientists have not settled it.
10. (order) Put these in order from the most breaths a minute at rest to the fewest.  
   **Answer:** ['Mouse', 'You', 'Elephant']. *Why:* Smaller bodies breathe faster. A mouse takes more than 100 a minute, you about 20, and an elephant at rest about 4 to 12.
### Think Like a Scientist of Breath (challenge) (challenge, 11 questions)
1. (mcq) Which is a fair way to compare your reach before and after a stretch?  
   Options: Use a different floor each time / Same floor, same slow reach, three tries / Try once and trust it  
   **Answer:** Same floor, same slow reach, three tries. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same. Three tries guard against one lucky or unlucky reach.
2. (truefalse) A whale that dives for three hours has gills.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A Cuvier's beaked whale has lungs like yours. She has learned to make one breath last for hours.
3. (count) A child takes 20 breaths a minute. About how many breaths in one hour?  
   **Answer:** 1,200. *Why:* There are 60 minutes in an hour. 20 × 60 = 1,200 breaths.
4. (mcq) A tulsi plant is next to you. What does she do with the air you breathe out?  
   Options: She uses the oxygen in it / She uses the carbon dioxide in it / She does nothing with it  
   **Answer:** She uses the carbon dioxide in it. *Why:* Plants take in carbon dioxide and use it, with sunlight and water, to build their bodies.
5. (sort) Match each being to the way she gets air.  
   **Answer:** A dolphin → Lungs; A fish → Gills; An earthworm → Damp skin; An ant → Tiny air tubes; A cow → Lungs. *Why:* Every animal needs to swap gases with the air or water. Bodies do it in different ways.
6. (write) Why does a stretch feel easier after playing than just after waking up?  
   **Answer:** Playing warms the muscles. Warm muscles stretch more easily than cold ones.. *Why:* A strong answer says that muscles are warmer after playing, and that warm muscles stretch more easily.
7. (draw) Draw a street dog's waking stretch. Next to it, draw the yoga pose named after a dog.  
   **Answer:** A dog with front legs forward and chest low, and a person with hands and feet down and hips high.. *Why:* The yoga pose called the dog facing down copies a dog's stretch. Both have the back sloping long.
8. (write) Five slow breaths make you feel calmer. How could you test if the slow breaths did it?  
   **Answer:** Rate how calm I feel, do slow breaths, rate again. On another day sit still for the same time without slow breaths, and compare.. *Why:* A fair test compares slow breaths with a quiet rest for the same time. It might be the rest that helps, and not the breaths.
9. (perspective) What does a raider need most in kabaddi?  
   **Answer:** No single right answer. All three matter.. *Why:* A raider uses speed to get across, strength to twist away, and breath to keep chanting. Which matters most depends on the game. Players and coaches disagree.
10. (mcq) Meena stretches every day for a month and reaches 4 centimetres further. Which reply is best?  
   Options: Her muscles must be longer. That is proven. / Maybe longer muscles, maybe a braver stretch / Stretching never changes anything.  
   **Answer:** Maybe longer muscles, maybe a braver stretch. *Why:* Scientists have measured both. The muscle may become longer, or the feeling of the stretch may change. Nobody has settled it.
11. (mcq) Which sentence about a yawn is true?  
   Options: A yawn brings in extra oxygen / Scientists agree on why we yawn / Nobody is sure why we yawn  
   **Answer:** Nobody is sure why we yawn. *Why:* In 1987 Robert Provine gave people extra oxygen, and they yawned as often as before. So that idea failed, and no other idea is agreed.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A paper boat lies on your tummy. You breathe in. What does the boat do?  
   Options: It rises / It sinks / It stays still  
   **Answer:** It rises. *Why:* Your diaphragm pulls down and pushes your tummy out, so the boat rises. A child who says 'sinks' is thinking the tummy goes flat as air comes in.
2. (truefalse) When your tummy rises as you breathe in, the air goes into your tummy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The air goes into your lungs in your chest. The tummy rises because the diaphragm pushes it out of the way.
3. (mcq) You are asleep. Who makes you breathe?  
   Options: Only you, by choosing each breath / Nobody, so breathing stops until you wake / A small part of your brain, without you choosing  
   **Answer:** A small part of your brain, without you choosing. *Why:* A small part of the brain sends the signal for every breath, asleep or awake. You can also step in and change your breath when you are awake.
4. (mcq) Which one can your body do by itself and you can also do on purpose?  
   Options: Your heart beating / Breathing / Digesting dinner  
   **Answer:** Breathing. *Why:* You can slow, speed or hold your breath for a short time, and your body also does it for you. You cannot tell your heart to stop.
5. (mcq) You feel cross. You breathe out slowly five times. What is most likely?  
   Options: You feel a bit calmer, though not everyone does / You will always feel completely calm / Nothing at all happens inside you  
   **Answer:** You feel a bit calmer, though not everyone does. *Why:* Many people feel calmer after slow breathing, and the heartbeat slows a little. It does not work for everyone every time. Scientists still argue about why.
6. (mcq) During a slow out-breath, what does your heartbeat do?  
   Options: Speeds up a lot / Stays exactly the same / Slows down a little  
   **Answer:** Slows down a little. *Why:* In a healthy body the heartbeat speeds up a little on the in-breath and slows a little on the out-breath.
7. (mcq) You sit and reach for your toes with straight legs. Where is the gentle pull?  
   Options: In your arms / At the back of your legs / In your ears  
   **Answer:** At the back of your legs. *Why:* The muscles along the back of your legs are pulled long. That is where the stretch feels like a gentle pull.
8. (mcq) Kiran stretches just after waking. Rani stretches after playing. Who reaches further?  
   Options: Kiran / Rani, because her muscles are warm / Both the same, always  
   **Answer:** Rani, because her muscles are warm. *Why:* Warm muscles stretch more easily than cold ones. After waking, muscles are cooler and often stiffer.
9. (mcq) You feel a sharp pinch in a stretch. What do you do?  
   Options: Push harder / Hold on and count to twenty / Come back a little  
   **Answer:** Come back a little. *Why:* A sharp pinch is pain, and pain means ease off. Come back until it is only a gentle pull.
10. (mcq) Priya reaches her toes. Dev reaches his knees. Who is stretching well?  
   Options: Only Priya / Both of them / Only Dev  
   **Answer:** Both of them. *Why:* Each body is different. A gentle pull that stays gentle is a good stretch, however far it reaches.
11. (mcq) A kabaddi raider chants on one breath. What does the chant show?  
   Options: That she is very strong / That she is tired / That she has not taken a new breath  
   **Answer:** That she has not taken a new breath. *Why:* If she stops chanting, it means she has taken a breath, and the raid is over.
12. (mcq) You lie on your tummy and lift your chest a little. Which pose are you?  
   Options: Cobra / Tree / Cat  
   **Answer:** Cobra. *Why:* The cobra pose is Bhujangasana, and many poses are named after beings.
13. (mcq) Why does your body need rest as well as moving?  
   Options: Moving is bad for you / Rest does nothing / Rest is when muscles mend  
   **Answer:** Rest is when muscles mend. *Why:* Moving asks your muscles and bones to work and grow stronger. Rest is when the small wear is mended, while the heart and lungs go on working.
14. (mcq) A friend says rest means doing nothing. What is a good reply?  
   Options: Yes, the body stops / Your heart and lungs keep working while muscles mend / You must stay awake  
   **Answer:** Your heart and lungs keep working while muscles mend. *Why:* Even at rest the heart beats and the lungs fill. The body is busy mending.

## Applications

- Clam divers on Vembanad lake in Kerala sit quietly and breathe slowly before a dive, because a quiet body uses its air more slowly.
- A PE teacher begins with marching and gentle stretches before a game, because warm muscles stretch more easily.

- A grown-up who sighs after a hard day is giving the heart a slow out-breath, without planning it.
- A kabaddi coach trains the raiders to breathe in a steady way, so their chant on one breath lasts through the raid.

## Open questions
- **Why does slow breathing make many people feel calmer?** Scientists have found a nerve pathway in mice that links breathing rhythm to alertness, and they know the out-breath slows the heart. They have not shown which of these, if any, produces the feeling of calm in people, or why it fails for some.
- **What is a yawn for?** The oxygen idea failed its test. The brain-cooling idea, the wake-up-stretch idea and the group-signal idea each explain some findings and not others. No single test has yet separated them.
- **When stretching lets you reach further, what has changed?** Short studies point to a change in how the pull is felt. Long studies find some change in the muscle. Scientists do not yet know how much each matters, or how it differs between children and grown-ups.
- **Do dolphins and whales really choose every breath?** Their sleep with half the brain awake suggests it, and they do not breathe under deep anaesthesia. But nobody can ask a dolphin, and the nerve cells that control their breathing are hard to study.
- **What is life, if it is more than breathing?** Science has only working definitions of life. A dry seed and a frozen water bear do not breathe in any way we can measure, and they can become active again. What exactly was there the whole time is not agreed.

## Connected maths
- You take about 20 breaths in a minute. Count in twenties to find how many breaths you take in 5 minutes. **100 breaths.**. 20, 40, 60, 80, 100. Each minute adds another 20, and 5 minutes is 5 steps. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L1`)
- A mouse takes 100 breaths in a minute. You take 20. How many of your minutes of breathing fit in one mouse minute? **5 of your minutes. 20, 40, 60, 80, 100: 5 steps of 20 make 100.**. 100 divided into steps of 20 gives 5 steps. The mouse breathes 5 times as fast as you. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L1`)
- A new baby takes 40 breaths a minute. You take 20. How is your count related to the baby's? **You take half as many breaths as the baby. 20 is half of 40.**. Halve 40 to get 20. The baby breathes twice as fast as you, and you breathe half as fast as the baby. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L1`)
- A slow breath is 2 counts in and 4 counts out. After 3 slow breaths, how many counts of out-breath have you done? How many counts of in-breath? **12 counts out and 6 counts in.**. Out-breath: 4, 8, 12. In-breath: 2, 4, 6. The out-breath is twice as long as the in-breath each time. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L1`)
- Three kabaddi tries give 6, 8 and 7 kabaddis. Which try was biggest? How many more is it than the smallest? **The second try, with 8. It is 2 more than 6.**. 8 is bigger than 7 and 6. 8 take away 6 is 2. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L1`)
- Ria counts her breaths for one minute on three days and gets 19, 21 and 20. Her friend says the 19 and 21 are wrong. Is the friend right? Why or why not? **No. The counts are close to each other, around 20. A little change from day to day is normal.**. 19 is 1 less than 20 and 21 is 1 more. A small change each day is normal, because breathing is never exactly the same and counting changes it a little. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
