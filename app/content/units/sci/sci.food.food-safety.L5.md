# Storing food safely: how microbes spoil it, and when to take extra care
`sci.food.food-safety.L5`

**Summary.** A small pot of dal cooked at night is usually fine in the morning. A wedding pot of the same dal, boiled just as well, can make a hundred guests ill. The big pot stays warm in the middle for many hours, and warm food is where microbes multiply fastest. Your child heaps hot rice in a bowl and spreads the same rice on a plate. They time how long the middle of each stays hot. They watch which microbes spoil bread, rice and lemon in sealed bags. They test whether rice that looks dry is dry enough to store. They also learn where each food keeps best, and which people, foods and days need extra care.

**Intro.** *Two pots of dal are boiled for the same time and covered. By morning the small pot is safe and the big pot may not be. What did the size of the pot change?* Most people think that food is safe if it was cooked well and covered. Cooking kills most microbes, but a few kinds survive boiling as tough spores. When the food cools to a warm temperature, the spores wake up and multiply. How long the food stays warm then matters more than how well it was cooked. In this unit you time how long the middle of a heap of rice stays hot. You work out how many bacteria ten bacteria become in six hours at three temperatures. You find out which microbes spoil which foods, and how damp grain grows a mould that makes a poison. You also plan a meal for a baby, a grandmother and a pregnant aunt at one table.

## Terms
- **Spoilage**: A change that makes food unfit to eat. Microbes cause most spoilage of moist food. *e.g.* Dal that has turned sour and frothy has spoiled.
- **Microbe**: A living being too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria, yeasts and moulds are microbes. *e.g.* One drop of sour milk holds millions of microbes.
- **Bacteria**: Microbes made of one tiny cell each. They multiply by splitting in two. One of them is called a bacterium. *e.g.* Bacteria turn wet cooked rice slimy within a day or two in summer.
- **Yeast**: A fungus made of one cell. Yeasts eat sugar and make gas and alcohol. *e.g.* Yeasts make old fruit juice fizzy and puff up the packet.
- **Mould**: A fungus that grows as fine threads over food and makes powdery spores. Moulds can grow on drier and sourer food than bacteria can. *e.g.* The green patch on an old lemon is a mould.
- **Hyphae**: The fine threads that make up the body of a mould. They grow into the food, deeper than the patch you see. *e.g.* Under the fuzzy spot on bread, hyphae have already spread through the slice.
- **Spore**: A tiny, tough, resting cell. Mould spores float in air and start new moulds. The spores of some bacteria survive boiling. *e.g.* Spores of the bacterium Bacillus cereus survive in cooked rice and wake when the rice turns warm.
- **Enzyme**: A substance made by living cells that cuts or joins other substances. Microbes pour enzymes on to food to digest it outside their bodies. *e.g.* Enzymes from bacteria cut the protein in fish, and the cut pieces smell bad.
- **Toxin**: A poison made by a living being. Some microbes leave toxins in food, and some toxins stay after cooking. *e.g.* Aflatoxin is a toxin made by a mould on damp groundnuts and maize.
- **Danger zone**: The range of temperature from about 5 to 60 degrees Celsius. Bacteria multiply in this range, fastest between 20 and 45 degrees. *e.g.* Dal on the counter in May sits in the danger zone.
- **Doubling time**: The time a group of bacteria takes to become twice as many. *e.g.* In warm food some bacteria have a doubling time of 20 minutes.
- **Lag time**: The waiting time before bacteria in a new place begin to multiply. They use it to get ready for the new food. *e.g.* Bacteria that land in fresh dal may wait an hour or more before they start doubling.
- **Perishable**: Food that spoils within hours or days unless it is kept cold. Perishable foods are moist and not very sour, salty or sweet. *e.g.* Milk, cooked rice, fish and cut fruit are perishable.
- **Shelf life**: The length of time a food stays good when it is stored as the label says. *e.g.* Unopened carton milk has a shelf life of about six months.
- **Cold chain**: An unbroken line of cold places that carries food from the maker to the home. *e.g.* Milk is chilled at the village, in the tanker, at the dairy, in the van and in the shop fridge.
- **Pasteurise**: To heat a food for a short time to kill the microbes that cause disease. Pasteurised milk is heated to 72 degrees Celsius for 15 seconds. *e.g.* Pouch milk is pasteurised, so it still needs the fridge.
- **UHT**: Short for ultra-high temperature. The food is heated to about 140 degrees Celsius for a few seconds and packed in a germ-free carton. *e.g.* UHT milk keeps for months on a shelf until the carton is opened.
- **Airtight**: Closed so well that no air or damp can get in or out. *e.g.* Atta in an airtight dabba stays dry through the monsoon.
- **Rancid**: The sharp, stale smell and taste of fat or oil that air, light and warmth have changed. Microbes are not needed for this. *e.g.* Groundnuts kept for a year in a warm, open jar smell rancid.
- **Storage pest**: An insect or other animal who eats stored food. Rice weevils, flour beetles, moths and rats are storage pests. *e.g.* A rice weevil lays her eggs inside grains of rice.
- **Reheat**: To heat cooked food again before eating. Safe reheating makes the food steaming hot all the way through. *e.g.* Reheat leftover sambar until it bubbles, and stir the middle.
- **Vulnerable**: More easily harmed than most people. Babies, pregnant women, old people and ill people are more vulnerable to unsafe food. *e.g.* A baby of eight months is vulnerable, so her food is made fresh.

## Explanations
### Five ways food is lost in storage · *defined*
**Definition:** Stored food is lost in five ways. Microbes eat it, or the food's own life goes on, or pests get in. Air and light change it, or water moves in or out.

Microbes are the first way. They eat moist food and leave it sour, slimy, smelly or mouldy. The second way is the food's own life. A potato or an onion is alive, so they sprout. A banana goes on ripening until it is soft and black. The third way is pests. Weevils, moths, ants and rats eat stored grain and leave droppings. The fourth way needs no living being. Air, light and warmth slowly change oil and fat until they smell rancid. The fifth way is water on the move. A biscuit takes in damp from the air and turns soft. A chapati loses water and turns hard. Each way needs its own answer, so a good storer first asks which way threatens this food.
- e.g. Sour dal: microbes ate the dal.
- e.g. A sprouting onion: the onion's own life went on.
- e.g. Small beetles in the atta: storage pests got in.
- e.g. Old groundnuts with a sharp smell: air and warmth turned the oil rancid.
- e.g. A soft biscuit in the monsoon: water moved in from the air.
### How microbes spoil food: they digest it from outside · *defined*
**Definition:** Microbes pour enzymes on to food. The enzymes cut the food into small pieces, and the microbes soak the pieces up. What they leave behind is the spoilage we see and smell.

A microbe has no mouth. To eat, they release enzymes into the food around them. The enzymes cut starch into sugars, proteins into smaller pieces and fats into acids. The microbe takes in what they need. The rest stays in the food, with the wastes the microbe makes. Acids make the food sour. Gases make froth and puff up a packet. Pieces of cut protein give the smell of bad fish or bad eggs. Enzymes that cut the glue between plant cells turn a firm vegetable to mush. Many bacteria also make a sticky coat around themselves, and this is the slime on old food. So each sign of spoilage is the trace of one kind of feeding.
- e.g. Sour milk: bacteria turned milk sugar into acid.
- e.g. A swollen juice packet: yeasts turned sugar into gas.
- e.g. The smell of bad fish: bacteria cut the proteins into smelly pieces.
- e.g. A soft, wet patch on a tomato: microbes cut the glue between the cells.
### Bacteria, yeasts and moulds: which spoils which food · *defined*
**Definition:** Bacteria win on wet food that is not sour. Yeasts win on sweet or slightly sour liquid. Moulds win on food that is drier, sourer or saltier.

All three kinds of microbe are nearly everywhere, so the food decides who grows first. Bacteria multiply fastest, but they need plenty of free water and little acid. Cooked rice, dal, milk, fish and meat suit them. Yeasts stand more sugar and more acid than most bacteria, so they spoil fruit juice and old fruit. Moulds grow slowly, but they stand dryness, salt and acid better than the others. They also need air. So moulds take the food that bacteria cannot use: bread, chapati, lemons, jam and the top of a pickle. This is why wet rice turns slimy in a day, while bread grows fuzz after several days. Knowing who will come tells you how to store the food.
- e.g. Wet cooked rice in a bag: slimy and watery within two days, from bacteria.
- e.g. Bread in a bag: fuzzy white, green or black spots after three to six days, from moulds.
- e.g. A lemon: a blue-green mould patch after a week or more, and almost never slime.
### One microbe after another: how milk spoils in turns · *defined*
**Definition:** Spoiling is often a line of different microbes. Each kind changes the food, and the change suits the next kind.

Leave raw milk in a warm place and watch what scientists have seen many times. First, bacteria that eat milk sugar multiply and make acid. The milk turns sour and sets. The acid then stops those same bacteria. Next come yeasts and moulds, who can live in acid. They slowly use the acid up. When the sourness falls, a third group arrives: bacteria who digest protein. Now the milk smells rotten. Ecologists call such a line of changes a succession. The same word is used when bare ground turns to grass, then bushes, then forest. A bowl of milk passes through its own succession in a week or two.
- e.g. Day 1: milk sugar bacteria multiply, and the milk turns sour.
- e.g. Days 3 to 7: yeasts and moulds grow on the sour curd.
- e.g. After that: protein-eating bacteria take over, and the smell turns rotten.
### Spoiled food and unsafe food are not the same · *defined*
**Definition:** Spoilage microbes change how food looks and smells. Many microbes and toxins that make people ill change nothing you can see or smell.

Your nose is good at finding spoilage. It picks up the acids and gases that large numbers of spoilage microbes make. But the bacteria that cause the worst illness often work in small numbers and make no smell. Some leave a toxin in the food. Reheating kills the bacteria, but some toxins stay. A mould on damp groundnuts or maize makes a toxin called aflatoxin, which cooking does not fully destroy. So there are two separate questions about any stored food. Has it spoiled? And has it been kept in a way that lets harmful microbes multiply? Only the second question protects you, and you answer it from the food's history: how warm, how long, how damp.
- e.g. Sour curd has changed a lot and is safe.
- e.g. Rice kept warm all night can look and smell fine and still hold a toxin.
- e.g. Groundnuts that are shrivelled, mouldy or bitter should be thrown away.
### Time and temperature: why two hours · *defined*
**Definition:** Bacteria multiply by doubling. How often they double depends on the temperature, so warmth and time together decide how many bacteria a food holds.

In warm food at about 35 degrees Celsius, some bacteria double every 20 minutes. Ten bacteria become 640 in two hours and more than two million in six hours. In a cool room at 18 degrees they may double only every two hours. In a fridge at 4 degrees most food bacteria stop. Bacteria also wait before they start. This waiting time is called the lag time, and it often lasts an hour or more in fresh food. The two-hour rule comes from these facts. Cooked food can stay in the danger zone, from 5 to 60 degrees, for about two hours in all. After that, eat it, chill it or keep it hotter than 60 degrees. On days hotter than about 32 degrees, food safety agencies shorten the time to one hour.
- e.g. Sambar served at 1 o'clock should be in the fridge by 3 o'clock.
- e.g. A flask that keeps soup hotter than 60 degrees Celsius keeps the soup out of the danger zone.
- e.g. The two hours add up: one hour on the table at lunch and one hour at dinner make two.
### Why a big pot stays warm in the middle · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat leaves food only through the surface. The middle of a deep or big pot is far from the surface, so it stays warm for many hours.

Heat is the jiggling of the tiny particles in a thing. Hot food cools when its particles pass their jiggling on to the air and the vessel. That can happen only at the surface. Heat from the middle must first travel to the surface, grain by grain. Thick food such as rice, upma or thick dal cannot flow, so this is slow. When food is spread thin, every part is near the surface. In a heap, the middle is far away. Size makes this worse. In a pot twice as wide, the heat has twice as far to go. There is also far more food for each bit of surface. Thick food in a pot twice as wide takes about four times as long to cool. So the middle of a wedding pot stays in the danger zone all night.
- e.g. Hot kanji poured on to a wide plate is cool enough for a baby in a minute or two.
- e.g. A full pressure cooker of rice is still warm in the middle two hours later.
- e.g. Cooks who make food for hundreds divide it into shallow trays to cool.
### Cooling, storing and reheating cooked food · *defined*
**Definition:** Cool cooked food fast in shallow vessels and chill it within two hours. Reheat it until it steams all the way through.

Five habits follow from the science. First, cook only a little more than the family will eat. Second, put leftovers into shallow vessels, with the food no deeper than about three finger-widths. Third, get them into the fridge within two hours of cooking. A vessel standing in a tray of cold water cools faster still. Fourth, reheat only the amount you will eat. Heat it until it bubbles or steams in the middle, and stir, because the middle heats last too. Fifth, reheat leftovers only once, and eat fridge leftovers within about two days. Rice needs the most care. Spores of the bacterium Bacillus cereus survive cooking. In warm rice they wake, multiply and make a toxin that reheating does not destroy.
- e.g. Leftover rice spread in a flat steel box cools faster than rice left in the cooker.
- e.g. A big vessel of hot sambar put straight into the fridge is still warm in the middle hours later.
- e.g. Soup from the fridge is safe to serve when it bubbles all over, and the middle is hot too.
### A place for each food · *defined*
**Definition:** Each food keeps longest in the place that blocks the way it is most likely to be lost.

Dry foods such as rice, dal, atta, rava, sugar and salt are lost to damp and pests. Keep them dry and airtight, away from the stove and the sink. Potatoes, onions and garlic are alive. Keep them cool, dark and airy, in a basket and never in a closed plastic bag. Light turns a potato green, and the green part holds a bitter poison called solanine. A very cold fridge turns some of a potato's starch to sugar. Bananas turn black in the fridge. Most other fruit and vegetables keep longer in the fridge, loosely wrapped so they do not dry. Milk, curd, eggs, cooked food, cut fruit, fish and meat are perishable and belong in the fridge. Oils and ghee keep best closed, cool and dark, because air and light turn them rancid. Bread grows mould more slowly in the fridge, though it turns stale faster there.
- e.g. Atta: an airtight steel dabba, because damp atta grows mould and draws beetles.
- e.g. Potatoes: a dark, airy basket, because light turns them green.
- e.g. Cooked dal: a shallow covered vessel in the fridge, because bacteria multiply in warm dal.
- e.g. Cooking oil: a closed bottle away from the stove, because warmth and light turn oil rancid.
### Keeping grain and dal for a year · *defined*
**Definition:** Grain keeps for a year or more if it is dry enough, sealed from damp air and protected from insects.

Rice, wheat and dal are harvested once or twice a year and eaten every day. So someone must store them for months. Two enemies wait. The first is damp. Grain with more than about 14 parts of water in 100 lets moulds grow, though it looks dry. Some of these moulds make aflatoxin. So farmers dry grain in the sun before storing it, and families sun their rice and dal after the monsoon. The second enemy is insects. A rice weevil lays her eggs inside the grains. Families add dried neem leaves or dried chillies, which keep some insects away. A truly airtight container works better. The insects and the grain use up the oxygen inside it, and the insects stop feeding and die. Farmers now use sealed three-layer bags that work this way, with no chemical added.
- e.g. Rice spread on a terrace in the October sun before it goes back into the bin.
- e.g. A tall clay or steel grain bin with a tight lid, in a village home in Tamil Nadu or Bihar.
- e.g. A sealed bag of cowpeas in which the weevils stopped feeding within days.
### Pasteurised milk, UHT milk, tins and the cold chain · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat kills microbes in food, and a sealed pack or an unbroken line of cold keeps new growth away.

Pouch milk is pasteurised. It is heated to 72 degrees Celsius for 15 seconds, which kills the microbes that cause disease. Some harmless microbes and spores survive, so the pouch must stay cold from the dairy to your kitchen. That line of cold places is called a cold chain. An American chemist, Mary Engle Pennington, worked out many of its rules after 1905. She tested railway wagons that carried food, to see how cold they kept it. Carton milk is treated by UHT, which is heating to about 140 degrees for a few seconds. This kills the spores too, and the carton is filled without letting any microbe in. So it keeps for months on a shelf. A tin of food is heated after it is sealed. A tin or packet that has swollen holds gas made by living microbes. Never open or taste its food.
- e.g. India's milk cold chain begins at a village cooler. There the milk is chilled to 4 degrees Celsius within hours of milking.
- e.g. An opened UHT carton is like pouch milk: keep it in the fridge and finish it in two days.
- e.g. Ice cream that has melted and frozen again has ice crystals in it, a sign that the cold chain broke.
### When to take extra care · *defined*
**Definition:** Extra care is needed for vulnerable people, for perishable foods and in hot or damp weather. It is also needed when food is cooked in large amounts or carried far.

Some people fall ill from fewer microbes than others. A baby has less acid in their stomach, and a small body loses water fast. A pregnant woman's defences are turned down a little, and a bacterium called Listeria can reach the baby inside her. Listeria is unusual because they can multiply slowly in a fridge. Old people and ill people also have weaker defences. For all of them, cook food fully and serve it fresh and hot. Avoid raw sprouts, raw milk and soft-boiled eggs. A baby under one year must not have honey. Honey can hold spores of a bacterium that grow in a baby's gut. Some foods need care for everyone: cooked rice, dal, milk sweets, cut fruit, eggs, fish and meat. Some days need care: the hot weeks of summer and the damp weeks of the monsoon. And some situations need care: feasts, lunch boxes, journeys and power cuts.
- e.g. A lunch box packed at 7 and eaten at 1 has spent six hours in the danger zone.
- e.g. A fridge with its door kept shut stays cold for about four hours in a power cut.
- e.g. For a feast, food cooked the night before is the usual cause of trouble.
### Hot food: straight into the fridge, or cool it first? · *debated*
**Definition:** Cooks and food safety agencies agree that cooked food must be chilled within two hours. They disagree about whether it must cool on the counter first.

Many families wait until food reaches room temperature before it goes in the fridge. Food safety agencies now say that waiting is the bigger risk. The answer depends on the amount of food and on the fridge.
- e.g. A katori of warm dal hardly changes the temperature of a fridge.
- e.g. A five-litre pot of hot sambar can warm everything on its shelf for hours.
- **Many home cooks:** Hot food warms the fridge and the milk and curd beside it, and steam turns to drops and frost. The fridge then works harder and uses more electricity. So food should cool on the counter first.
- **Food safety agencies, such as the Food Safety and Inspection Service of the United States:** Food left to cool on a counter can spend many hours in the danger zone. Small amounts in shallow vessels can go into the fridge while still warm. A modern fridge can remove that heat.
- **People who cook for hundreds:** Both are right about a different pot. A big pot must never go in whole, because its middle stays warm in any fridge. Divide it, stand the trays in cold water for half an hour, and then chill them.

## Mostly agreed
- Microbes cause most spoilage of moist food. *How we know:* Food that is heated in a sealed tin stays good for years, because no living microbe is inside. The same food spoils within days once the tin is opened.
- Bacteria multiply fastest in warm food, and slowly or not at all in a cold fridge. *How we know:* In 1982 David Ratkowsky and his team in Tasmania measured how fast many kinds of bacteria grew at different temperatures. Growth fell steadily as the temperature fell, and stopped for most kinds near fridge temperature.
- Large amounts of food that cool slowly cause many outbreaks of food poisoning. *How we know:* Health officers who trace outbreaks at feasts and canteens often find food that was cooked hours ahead in big vessels. In laboratory tests, spores of the bacterium Clostridium perfringens multiplied in cooked food that was cooled slowly. They did not multiply in food that was cooled fast.
- Moulds on damp grain and nuts can make poisons that cooking does not fully destroy. *How we know:* In 1960 more than 100,000 young turkeys in England died after eating mouldy groundnut meal. In 1974 mouldy maize poisoned people in more than 100 villages of Gujarat and Rajasthan. Rain had wet the maize.
- Babies under one year should not be given honey. *How we know:* In 1979 Stephen Arnon and his team in California studied babies with an illness called infant botulism. Many of the ill babies had been fed honey. The team found the spores of the bacterium in samples of that honey.
- Fresh fruit and vegetables are still alive after they are picked. *How we know:* They go on taking in oxygen and giving out carbon dioxide and water. In 2013 Janet Braam's team in Texas studied cabbages bought from a shop. The cabbages still kept a daily rhythm of day and night.

## How we found out
**William Blount, a poultry adviser in England. Kenneth Sargeant and his team of scientists in England. K. A. V. R. Krishnamachari, R. V. Bhat and their team in India., The turkeys died in 1960. The mould was found in 1961, and the poison was named soon after. The outbreak in western India was in 1974..** In 1960, young turkeys began to die on farms in England. More than 100,000 birds died. Farmers and vets thought a new germ was passing from bird to bird. They named the illness Turkey X disease. But no germ could be found, and sick birds did not make healthy birds ill. A poultry adviser, William Blount, looked at what the sick flocks had eaten. Their feed all held groundnut meal that had come from Brazil. Scientists fed that meal to healthy ducklings, and the ducklings fell ill. In 1961 scientists in England, including Kenneth Sargeant, found a mould growing in the meal. The mould is called Aspergillus flavus. They showed that the mould makes a poison, which was named aflatoxin. The groundnuts had grown mouldy. In 1974 the same poison harmed people in more than 100 villages of Gujarat and Rajasthan. Rain had wet their maize, and the maize had grown mouldy. Doctors in India traced the illness to the maize.  
*What people believed before:* That the turkeys had an infectious disease, caused by a new germ that passed from bird to bird. Tracing the feed showed that only birds fed a certain groundnut meal fell ill. Feeding that meal to healthy ducklings made them ill too.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Rice must never be reheated.** (A warning that is often passed around.; *rejected*)  
  For: People have fallen ill after eating reheated rice. Spores of Bacillus cereus survive cooking, and the toxin survives reheating.  
  Against: The harm is done before the reheating, while the rice sits warm for hours. Rice that was cooled fast, chilled within an hour or two and reheated once until steaming is safe.
- **Rice left overnight in water is unsafe, because it breaks the two-hour rule.** (Some food safety teachers.; *debated*)  
  For: Cooked rice at room temperature for twelve hours is exactly what the two-hour rule forbids. Bacillus cereus spores are common on rice.  
  Against: Millions of people eat this soaked rice every summer. It is called pazhaya sadam in Tamil Nadu, pakhala in Odisha and panta bhat in Bengal. The water turns sour as lactic acid bacteria grow, and sourness holds back many harmful bacteria. Few careful studies have measured how safe it is.
- **Freezing kills microbes, so frozen food is safe for ever.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Frozen food does not spoil, even after months.  
  Against: Freezing stops microbes from multiplying, but most of them stay alive. They start again when the food thaws. So thawed food is as perishable as fresh food.
- **Eggs must always be kept in the fridge.** (Shops and health agencies in some countries, such as the United States.; *debated*)  
  For: Cold slows any bacteria on or in the egg, and eggs keep several weeks longer in a fridge.  
  Against: In much of Europe eggs are sold from open shelves. An unwashed shell has a natural coat that keeps bacteria out. In an Indian summer, a fridge is the safer place.
- **One rotten fruit spoils the whole basket.** (An old saying in many languages.; *accepted*)  
  For: Mould from one fruit makes millions of spores, and juice from a rotting fruit wets the fruit beside it. Ripe fruit also gives off a gas that ripens the others.  
  Against: The fruit must touch or share still air for this to happen. Sorting the basket each day stops it.
- **If food was boiled and then covered, nothing can grow in it.** (Many cooks.; *rejected*)  
  For: Boiling kills most bacteria, and a lid keeps flies and dust out.  
  Against: The spores of some bacteria survive boiling. They wake when the food cools to a warm temperature, and a lid does not stop them. Only time and temperature do.

## Roots
In this unit you timed how long the middle of a heap of hot rice stayed hot. Here we ask what that test says about food, about microbes and about your own body.

**Deep truth.** In Deep or Shallow, the spread rice cooled in minutes, but the middle of the heap stayed hot. Heat leaves food only through the surface, and the middle of a heap is far from the surface. Microbes multiply fastest in food that stays warm for hours. So the shape and size of a heap of food help decide how safe the food stays.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think that safety is inside the food: it is clean or dirty, fresh or stale, cooked or raw. But two helpings of the same rice from the same pot can end up different. What happens to food also depends on its shape, its size and the hours it has spent warm.  
*Evidence:* In Deep or Shallow, rice from one pot was put on a plate and in a bowl. The plate rice fell below 43 degrees Celsius in about ten minutes. The middle of the bowl took about an hour. Outbreaks at feasts are often traced to food that cooled slowly in big vessels.

- *who am i:* You are a warm body with a surface too. A baby is small, with a lot of skin for their size, so they cool fast. You make heat all day and lose it through your skin.
- *what is life:* A bacterium is so small that every part of them is close to their surface. Food gets in quickly and waste gets out quickly. That is one reason the smallest living beings multiply the fastest.
- *what is this world:* The same rule works for a planet. The Earth is so big that its middle is still hot after four and a half billion years. A small asteroid cooled long ago.

The rice on the plate and the rice in the bowl were the same rice, with the same few spores. Nothing about the rice made one safer than the other. The difference lay in how far each grain was from the air. So what happens to a thing depends on what it is made of. It also depends on how the thing meets the world around it. Many sages taught that nothing exists by itself, apart from everything else. The science here shows one exact case. The middle of a heap is what it is because of all the rice around it.

- **Bhagavad Gita 17.10:** The verse lists the foods that a dull and heavy mind prefers. One well-known commentary reads the first food on the list as food cooked more than one watch ago. A watch is about three hours. (On that reading, the verse sets a time limit on cooked food. The limit is near the two-hour rule of food safety. The Gita is speaking about the mind and its qualities. It does not speak about microbes, which nobody had seen.)
- **J. B. S. Haldane, the essay 'On Being the Right Size' (1926):** A change in size changes what a body can do. An elephant has far less skin for each kilogram of body than a mouse has. So the elephant loses heat slowly, and the mouse must eat all day to stay warm. (Haldane wrote about mice and elephants. The same rule makes a wedding pot cool more slowly than a katori. Haldane later lived and worked in Kolkata and Bhubaneswar.)
- **Charaka Samhita, Vimana Sthana, chapter 1, the rules for eating:** Eat food while it is warm, in the right amount, and only after the last meal is digested. (Food that is still warm from the fire has had no time in the danger zone. Charaka gave the rule for good digestion. The link to microbes is ours.)
- Science (settled): Heat leaves a body only through its surface. A body twice as wide has four times the surface and eight times the volume. So it holds its heat longer.
- Science (debated): The Earth's middle is still hot. Part of the heat is left from the Earth's making, and part is made inside by radioactive atoms. Scientists still argue about how much comes from each.
- Science (open): How a resting spore in cooked food senses that it is time to wake is only partly understood.
- Other view, Many food safety teachers: Shape and size matter only for big pots. In a home kitchen, most illness from food comes from dirty water, unwashed hands and raw food touching cooked food. Clean food eaten fresh is safe in any vessel. Putting the shape of the heap at the centre makes a small part of food safety look like the whole.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A covered pot of dal looks the same at night and the next morning. What can change in food without leaving any sign? (hint: Think of the doubling table. Which of your senses could count bacteria?)
- *big:* When a safety rule works, nobody falls ill and nothing happens. How can anyone know that the rule worked? (hint: Compare many kitchens that follow the rule with many that do not.)
- *big:* A cook throws away 30 kilograms of dal that might have been safe. Is that waste, or is it care? (hint: Who pays if she is wrong one way? Who pays if she is wrong the other way?)
- *deep:* Milk spoils in turns, as each microbe changes it for the next. Is spoiling an ending, or a line of many lives? (hint: A forest also arrives in turns: grass, then bushes, then trees.)
- *big:* The two-hour rule is the same for a baby and for a wrestler. Should a rule be written for the strongest person, or for the weakest? (hint: Who sits at a wedding lunch? Who is harmed if the rule is too loose?)

## Story: The Middle of the Pot
*Poongodi, Mallika, Paatti, Kanmani and the two weddings are made up. These facts are real. Kumbakonam, Thirunageswaram and Swamimalai are towns in Thanjavur district in Tamil Nadu. Wedding cooks there feed hundreds of guests from very large vessels. A big vessel of thick cooked food stays warm in the middle for many hours. The spores of some bacteria survive boiling and multiply in food that stays warm, without changing its look or smell. Cooks who feed large numbers cool food in shallow trays for this reason.*

Poongodi was eleven, and this was her first wedding as a worker.

Her aunt Mallika cooked for weddings in and around Kumbakonam. Tomorrow six hundred guests would sit down to lunch on banana leaves. Tonight, in the shed behind the wedding hall, three wood fires burned under vessels big enough to bathe in.

Poongodi had three jobs. She peeled garlic. She carried water. And she minded Kanmani, her aunt's baby, who slept in a cloth cradle tied to a beam.

At nine o'clock Kanmani woke and howled. Her ragi kanji had come straight off the fire. Poongodi blew on a spoonful and touched it to her lip. It was far too hot. Kanmani howled louder.

So Poongodi did what Paatti always did at home. She poured the kanji out of the tumbler on to a wide steel plate. She tilted the plate this way and that, until the kanji lay as thin as a dosa. She counted slowly to sixty. Then she touched it with her little finger. It was only warm. Kanmani ate every spoonful and went back to sleep with ragi on her chin.

By then the dal was done. Thirty kilos of toor dal had boiled for an hour in the biggest vessel, for tomorrow's sambar.

"Boil the dal tonight and we save two hours in the morning," Mallika said. Two women lifted the lid, which was as wide as an open umbrella, and set it on the vessel. Everyone washed. Then they lay down on mats in the hall, and one by one they slept.

Poongodi could not sleep. The hall smelled of jasmine and the fans were loud. Some time after three she got up for a drink of water. The shed was dark. The fires were grey ash.

She passed the dal vessel and laid her palm on its side, the way you pat a cow in the dark. Near the top the metal was cool. Lower down it was warm.

That was strange. The fire had been out for six hours.

She lifted the edge of the lid and took the long ladle. She pushed it down into the very middle of the dal and brought it up, and held her finger over it. The dal from the middle was as warm as bath water.

Poongodi stood there with the ladle. The baby's kanji had cooled while she counted to sixty. This dal had been cooling for six hours, and its middle was still warm. It would be warm at sunrise.

She thought of what Paatti said about milk, every summer. Hot is safe and cold is safe. Warm is where it turns.

She did not want to wake her aunt. Mallika had been on her feet since four the morning before. And who was Poongodi? She was the girl who peeled the garlic. She woke her all the same.

Mallika came with her hair loose and her eyes half shut. She bent over the vessel and smelled the dal. She held the lamp close and looked at it.

"It boiled for an hour," she said. "It was covered. It smells sweet. I have done it this way for twenty years."

"It has been warm all night, Athai. It is still warm in the middle. Paatti says warm is where it turns."

"Your Paatti says that about milk."

"Is dal so different?"

Mallika did not answer. Rain had begun on the tin roof. She put her own palm on the vessel, high and then low. She took the ladle and brought up dal from the middle, and felt it, and stood still for a long time.

"Thirunageswaram," she said at last, not to Poongodi. "Four years ago. Forty people were ill after a lunch of mine, and I never knew which dish. I blamed the well."

She looked at the vessel. "Thirty kilos. That is four thousand rupees. And nobody can tell me that it has turned."

"Nobody can tell you that it has not," Poongodi said, and was frightened by her own mouth.

Mallika woke the women at four. The old dal went into the pit behind the hall, where the used banana leaves would go. New dal went on two fires in two smaller vessels, and it was soft before six. The sambar was late by nobody's clock but Mallika's.

Six hundred people ate. They praised the sambar and asked for more of it. Nobody fell ill. Nobody knew there had been a first dal.

Poongodi ate last, sitting on an upturned bucket. Would anyone have fallen ill? She would never find out. The pit had the dal, and the pit was not going to tell her.

A month later there was a wedding in Swamimalai. Poongodi came into the cooking shed at night with her basket of garlic, and stopped.

The big vessel stood empty. The dal for the morning lay in twelve wide trays, two fingers deep. The trays stood in a stone trough of well water, each under a square of thin cloth. A woman was moving along the row with a palm-leaf fan.

Mallika did not look up from the fire.

"Like your kanji," she said. "Now go and peel the garlic."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Poongodi cooled the baby's kanji in a minute by spreading it thin on a plate. The dal in the huge vessel was still warm in the middle after six hours. Heat leaves food only at the surface. So a big, deep pot holds its middle in the warm range for a whole night. That warm range is where surviving spores multiply, and nothing in the look or smell shows it. Mallika's twelve shallow trays at the end are the baby's plate again, made large.

- Ask: The kanji cooled in a minute, and the dal was still warm after six hours. What was different about them?
- Ask: The dal looked and smelled fine. Why could Mallika not find out whether it was safe?
- Ask: Nobody fell ill at the wedding. Does that show that Poongodi was right? What would you need to know?
- Ask: At the second wedding the dal lay in twelve trays in water. Why does that work better than one big vessel?

  - Ask: The rice turned slimy and the bread turned fuzzy. What does that tell you? (good: Different microbes grew on them.; Bacteria grew on the wet rice.; Moulds grew on the drier bread.)

  - Note: The reason: bacteria multiply fastest, but they need plenty of free water and little acid. The wet rice suits them, and they make slime, liquid and gas. Bread is too dry for most bacteria, so slower moulds get the bread. A mould looks fuzzy because they grow as fine threads called hyphae. The coloured dots hold their spores. Lemon is too sour for almost all bacteria, so only a few slow moulds can use it. Dry toast gives no microbe enough water. If your lemon shows nothing by day 6, keep that bag sealed for another week and look again.
  - Think: Idli batter is wet and warm, yet it turns sour and safe. Which microbes got there first?
  - Wait: Do this on day 6
### e3 · Dry Enough? The salt jar test for stored rice (35 min)
- **question:** Rice from the dabba is dry. A second lot of rice has been wetted a little and now looks dry again. Can dry salt in a closed jar show which rice is too damp to store?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After the wetted rice looks dry again, will it behave like the dabba rice? What will the salt do in each jar? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use three jars of the same size, all completely dry. Put the same amount of rice, half a cup, and the same amount of salt, one tablespoon, in each. Shake each jar for the same time and wait the same time. Only one thing changes: the rice in the jar.
- **safety:** A grown-up roasts the salt and lets the pan cool. Do not put the test rice back in the dabba. Wash it and cook it the same day.
- **record:** Draw the three jars. Under each write what is in it, and what the salt did: ran freely, formed lumps, or stuck to the glass.
- **results table:** Jar | What is in it | The salt after 15 minutes: runs freely, forms lumps, or sticks to the glass
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stir 1 teaspoon of water into half a cup of rice. 2. Spread the rice on a plate. 3. Leave it for 45 minutes, until no grain looks wet. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* I have wetted this rice a little. In 45 minutes it will look dry again. Will it then be the same as the rice in the dabba? How could we find out? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* This rice got a little wet. Soon it will look dry. Will it be the same as before? Why?
  - Note: The rice soaks up the teaspoon of water and its surface dries. It then looks and feels almost like dabba rice. Do not say whether it is dry inside.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* While the rice dries, a grown-up roasts 4 tablespoons of salt in a dry pan. Use a low flame for 3 minutes. Tip the salt on to a dry plate, and let it cool for 5 minutes. Dry the three jars with a clean cloth.  
  *Parent says:* I am roasting the salt to drive out any damp. The salt must be bone dry for this test. Why do you think that matters?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up roasts the salt. It must be very dry. Why does that matter?
  - Note: In the monsoon, salt from the jar is often damp already. Damp salt would stick in every jar and spoil the test.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Label the jars A, B and C. Put half a cup of dabba rice in A. Put the wetted rice in B. Put the third dry food in C. Add 1 tablespoon of cooled salt to each jar. Close the lids. Your child shakes each jar for 1 minute.  
  *Parent says:* Jar A has rice from the dabba. Jar B has the rice we wetted. Jar C has a food you chose from the shelf. Add the salt, close the lids and shake each jar for one minute.  
  *Child mode:* Fill jars A, B and C. Add salt. Close them. Shake each for one minute.
  - Check: In Dry Enough, why are the lids kept closed during the wait? → **So only the grain's damp reaches the salt**
- **s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the jars for 15 minutes. Then tilt and turn each jar slowly. Look at the salt on the glass and among the grains. Write what you see. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Tilt each jar slowly. Does the salt run like dry sand, or does it stick to the glass? Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Tilt each jar. Does the salt run or stick? Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: The wetted rice looked dry. What did the salt show? (good: It still held extra water inside.; Water came out of the rice into the air of the jar.; Looking dry is not the same as being dry.)
  - Ask: Why does a farmer need this test before filling a grain bin? (good: Damp grain grows mould in the bin.; Some moulds make a poison.; A whole year's grain could be lost.)
  - Note: The reason: grain is always passing water to the air around it, and taking water back. In a closed jar, damp grain makes the air damp. Dry salt takes water out of damp air, turns sticky and clings to the glass. If the salt runs freely, the grain holds less than about 14 or 15 parts of water in 100. That is dry enough to store. Above that, moulds can grow on grain that looks perfectly dry. Wash the test rice and cook it today.
  - Think: Why do many families spread their rice and dal in the sun after the monsoon?

  *Child mode:* Ten bacteria double every 20 minutes. Guess how many there are after six hours.
  - Note: The three rules. Warm kitchen, about 35 degrees Celsius: the number doubles every 20 minutes. Cool room, about 18 degrees: it doubles every 2 hours. Fridge, 4 degrees: it stays at 10. - **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child fills in the table. In the warm kitchen, three doublings an hour is times 8. In the cool room the number doubles at hours 2, 4 and 6. The fridge column stays at 10.  
  *Parent says:* Fill in the warm kitchen first. Three doublings in an hour is the same as times eight. Then fill in the cool room and the fridge.  
  *Child mode:* Fill in the table. Warm kitchen: times eight each hour. Cool room: double every two hours.

  - Ask: Real bacteria wait about an hour before they start to double. How does that change the table? (good: Everything happens an hour later.; At two hours there would be only 80.; It gives us a little more time, which is why the rule is two hours.)
  - Note: The waiting time is called the lag time. With a lag of one hour, ten bacteria become about 80 by two hours. That is why two hours in the danger zone is the limit, and one hour on very hot days.
  - Check: Ten bacteria double every 20 minutes. How many are there after one hour? → **80**

### c1 · Cool the Feast Pot: thick hot food below 43 degrees in 15 minutes (35 min)
- **problem:** A cook has thick, hot food that must be kept for tomorrow. The middle must fall below 43 degrees Celsius within 15 minutes, so that it can go into the fridge quickly. Design a way to cool 2 cups of hot cooked rice, or thick upma, that fast.

### How food is lost, and where it keeps (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A biscuit left open in the monsoon turns soft. In which way was it lost?  
   Options: Water moved in from the air / Microbes ate the biscuit / The biscuit's own life went on / Pests got in  
   **Answer:** Water moved in from the air. *Why:* A dry biscuit takes in water from damp air and turns soft. No microbe is needed. Microbes would leave mould, slime or a bad smell.
2. (match) Match each sign to the way the food was lost.  
   **Answer:** Slime on old rice → Microbes ate the food; A shoot on a potato → The food's own life went on; Small beetles in the atta → Pests got in; A sharp smell from old oil → Air and warmth changed it; A hard, dry chapati → Water moved out. *Why:* Each way of losing food leaves its own sign. Reading the sign tells you which kind of storage would have saved the food.
3. (fill) The fine threads that make up the body of a mould are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['hyphae']. *Why:* Hyphae grow into the food, much deeper than the fuzzy patch on top. This is why soft food with one mould spot is thrown away whole.
4. (truefalse) Food that looks and smells fine is always safe to eat.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Spoilage microbes change the look and smell. Many harmful bacteria and toxins change nothing. Safety depends on how warm the food was kept, and for how long.
5. (mcq) Which microbes are most likely to spoil a bowl of wet cooked rice first?  
   Options: Bacteria / Moulds / Yeasts / Weevils  
   **Answer:** Bacteria. *Why:* Bacteria multiply fastest on wet food that is not sour. Moulds are slower, and they win only on drier or sourer food. Weevils are insects, and they eat dry grain.
6. (sort) Sort these foods by the place where each keeps best.  
   **Answer:** cooked dal → Fridge; milk → Fridge; rice → Dry, airtight dabba; atta → Dry, airtight dabba; potatoes → Cool, dark, airy basket; onions → Cool, dark, airy basket. *Why:* Cooked dal and milk are perishable, so they need cold. Rice and atta are lost to damp and pests, so they need a dry, sealed place. Potatoes and onions are alive, so they need dark and moving air.
7. (truefalse) Freezing kills the microbes in food.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Freezing stops microbes from multiplying, but most stay alive. They start again when the food thaws.
8. (mcq) Why is cooked food cooled in shallow vessels?  
   Options: Every part is near the surface / Shallow vessels are cleaner / Thin food tastes better / The fridge holds more of them  
   **Answer:** Every part is near the surface. *Why:* Heat leaves food only through the surface. In a shallow layer no part is far from the surface, so the whole of the food cools fast.
9. (fill) The range of temperature from about 5 to 60 degrees Celsius is called the ____ zone.  
   **Answer:** ['danger']. *Why:* Bacteria multiply in the danger zone, fastest between 20 and 45 degrees Celsius. Cooked food should spend no more than about two hours there.
10. (write) In Deep or Shallow, the plate rice cooled long before the middle of the bowl. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Heat leaves rice only through the surface. On the plate every grain is near the surface. In the bowl, heat from the middle must pass from grain to grain to reach the outside. That is slow.. *Why:* A strong answer says where heat leaves. It compares how far the heat must travel on the plate and in the bowl.
11. (write) A family cooks dal at 1 o'clock and eats it again at 8. Write what they should do in between.  
   **Answer:** Put the leftover dal in a shallow covered vessel. Get it into the fridge by 3 o'clock, which is within two hours. At 8, reheat only what they will eat until it bubbles, and stir the middle.. *Why:* A strong answer has three parts: cool it fast, chill it within two hours, and reheat it until steaming.
12. (draw) Draw a slice of bread cut through, with a mould growing on it. Label the hyphae and the spores.  
   **Answer:** A fuzzy patch on top of the slice. Fine threads, the hyphae, run from the patch down into the bread. Small coloured dots on stalks above the patch hold the spores.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the hyphae reach deeper than the patch you can see.
13. (perspective) Should hot leftover dal go straight into the fridge?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For the officer: bacteria double every 20 minutes in warm food. For the grandmother: a big hot pot does warm a fridge. A strong answer may say that the amount of food decides.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement between kitchens and food safety agencies. A strong answer uses the two-hour rule and says what a big pot does to a fridge.
14. (order) Put these in order, from the food that keeps for the shortest time to the longest.  
   **Answer:** ['Cooked dal on the counter', 'Curd in the fridge', 'Onions in an airy basket', 'Dry rice in an airtight bin']. *Why:* Warm, wet dal lasts about two hours. Cold, sour curd lasts about a week. Onions last about two months, and dry rice about two years. Dryness protects longest.
15. (mcq) Who needs the most care with food at a family meal?  
   Options: A baby of 8 months / A healthy girl of 10 / A healthy man of 30 / A football player of 20  
   **Answer:** A baby of 8 months. *Why:* A baby has less stomach acid, defences that are still learning, and a small body that loses water fast. The other three are healthy and well past babyhood.
### Time, temperature and extra care (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (count) Ten bacteria double every 20 minutes. How many are there after 2 hours?  
   **Answer:** 640. *Why:* Two hours hold six lots of 20 minutes. 10 doubles to 20, 40, 80, 160, 320 and 640.
2. (mcq) Bacteria wait before they start to multiply in new food. What is this wait called?  
   Options: Lag time / Doubling time / Shelf life / Danger zone  
   **Answer:** Lag time. *Why:* The lag time is the wait before doubling begins. Doubling time is how long one doubling takes once they have started.
3. (write) Dal sat out for one hour at lunch and one hour at dinner. Why do the two hours add up?  
   **Answer:** The bacteria that multiplied at lunch are still in the dal. The fridge only stopped them for a while. At dinner they go on doubling from where they stopped.. *Why:* A strong answer says that cold pauses bacteria and does not remove them, so the count carries on.
4. (truefalse) Boiling food and covering it stops every microbe from growing in it later.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The spores of some bacteria survive boiling. They wake when the food cools to a warm temperature. A lid keeps flies out, but it does not cool the food.
5. (odd) Three of these foods are perishable. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Milk / Cut papaya / Cooked rice / Dry toor dal  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Milk, cut papaya and cooked rice are wet and not sour, so they spoil within hours in the warmth. Dry toor dal has no free water and keeps for about a year.
6. (match) Match each person to the reason they need extra care with food.  
   **Answer:** A baby of 8 months → Little stomach acid and a small body; A pregnant woman → Some bacteria can reach her baby; A grandmother of 80 → Defences slow down with age; A cousin just over typhoid → The body is still weak from illness. *Why:* Each of these people can fall ill from fewer microbes than a healthy child or adult. So their food is cooked fully and served fresh.
7. (mcq) A power cut lasts 3 hours and the fridge door stays shut. What about the milk inside?  
   Options: It is still cold enough to keep / It must be thrown away / It has turned to curd / It has frozen  
   **Answer:** It is still cold enough to keep. *Why:* A closed fridge stays cold for about four hours. Opening the door lets the cold air out, so the door stays shut.
8. (write) In Three Sealed Bags, wet rice turned slimy and bread turned fuzzy. Explain why they spoiled differently.  
   **Answer:** Different microbes won on each food. Bacteria need plenty of free water, and they made the slime on the wet rice. Bread is too dry for most bacteria, so slower moulds grew on it as fuzzy threads.. *Why:* A strong answer names the microbe for each food. It says why that food suits that microbe.
9. (sort) Sort these. Does each one kill most microbes, or only stop them from multiplying?  
   **Answer:** boiling → Kills most microbes; pressure cooking → Kills most microbes; UHT heating → Kills most microbes; a fridge → Only stops them multiplying; a freezer → Only stops them multiplying; drying → Only stops them multiplying. *Why:* Heat kills. Cold and dryness only take away what microbes need to multiply. When the food warms up or gets wet, the microbes start again.
10. (perspective) A wedding cook finds that 30 kilograms of boiled dal stayed warm all night. Should she serve it?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. Against serving: the middle of a big pot stays in the danger zone all night, and some toxins survive heat. For serving: food is precious, and nobody can show that this dal is unsafe. A strong answer weighs who would be harmed.. *Why:* This is a real choice that cooks face. A strong answer uses the science of cooling, and is honest that nobody can be sure about this one pot.
11. (fill) A tin with a swollen lid holds ____ made by living microbes.  
   **Answer:** ['gas']. *Why:* Microbes inside a tin make gas as they feed, and the gas pushes the lid out. The food in such a tin must never be opened or tasted.
12. (label) A deep pot of hot dal has stood for two hours. Label the hottest part and the coolest part.  
   **Answer:** ['Hottest part: the middle of the pot, far from every surface', 'Coolest part: the top and the sides, where heat leaves']. *Why:* Heat leaves only at the surface, so the outside cools first and the middle cools last.
13. (mcq) Why does pouch milk need a fridge, while unopened UHT carton milk does not?  
   Options: Pasteurising leaves some microbes alive / A pouch is thinner than a carton / UHT milk has a preservative / Pouch milk is fresher  
   **Answer:** Pasteurising leaves some microbes alive. *Why:* Pasteurising at 72 degrees Celsius kills the microbes that cause disease, but some others and their spores survive. UHT heating kills the spores too, and the carton lets no microbe in.
14. (truefalse) Reheating leftover food once, until it steams in the middle, is a safe habit.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True, if the food was chilled within two hours. Steaming heat in the middle kills the bacteria that grew slowly in the fridge. Reheating again and again gives them more warm hours.
15. (write) A lunch box is packed at 7 and eaten at 1. Give two ways to make that lunch safer.  
   **Answer:** Choose a dry or sour food, such as chapati with dry sabzi, lemon rice or curd rice. Cook it that morning, not the night before. Add a cold pack, or use a flask that keeps food very hot.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two changes. Each one cuts the warm hours, or makes the food harder for bacteria to grow in.
### Testing ideas about storing food (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) In Dry Enough, salt stuck to the glass in the jar of wetted rice. Explain where the water came from.  
   **Answer:** The water came out of the rice. The rice looked dry, but it still held extra water inside. In the closed jar that water passed into the air, and the dry salt took it up and turned sticky.. *Why:* A strong answer traces the water from the rice, to the air in the jar, to the salt.
2. (mcq) Why does a mould poison still matter after the mouldy food is cooked?  
   Options: Cooking does not destroy the poison / Cooking makes the mould grow / The mould hides from the heat / Cooked mould tastes bitter  
   **Answer:** Cooking does not destroy the poison. *Why:* Aflatoxin and some other mould poisons mostly survive roasting and boiling. The heat kills the mould, but most of the poison the mould made stays in the food.
3. (count) A bowl cools in 1 hour. At double the width it takes 4 hours. And at 4 times the width?  
   **Answer:** 16. *Why:* Four times as wide is twice as wide, and then twice as wide again. So the time is 4 times 4, which is 16 hours.
4. (truefalse) Rice that was cooled fast, chilled, and reheated once until steaming is safe to eat.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The danger with rice is the warm hours before it is chilled. Rice that was cooled fast gave the spores no time to multiply.
5. (order) Put these in order, as raw milk spoils in a warm place.  
   **Answer:** ['Milk sugar bacteria make acid', 'The milk turns sour and sets', 'Yeasts and moulds use up the acid', 'Protein-eating bacteria make the milk smell rotten']. *Why:* Each kind of microbe changes the milk, and the change suits the next kind. Scientists call such a line of changes a succession.
6. (perspective) Is cooked rice left overnight in water safe to eat?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer sees that the rule and the tradition may both be right. The sour water changes what can grow. A useful test: measure the sourness and count the harmful bacteria in many pots, at several room temperatures.. *Why:* Scientists have not settled this. A strong answer respects long experience, respects the rule, and says what evidence is missing.
7. (write) Birds on many farms fall ill. How could you find out whether a germ or the feed is the cause?  
   **Answer:** Find out what all the ill flocks have in common. If they share one batch of feed, and flocks on other feed stay well, the feed is suspected. Then give that feed to a few healthy birds and see whether they fall ill. If a germ were the cause, ill birds would pass the illness to healthy birds beside them.. *Why:* A strong answer compares ill and healthy flocks, and then tests the suspected cause directly.
8. (mcq) Weevils are sealed inside an airtight bin of grain. What happens over the next weeks?  
   Options: The oxygen runs low and they stop feeding / They multiply faster than before / The grain turns to flour / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** The oxygen runs low and they stop feeding. *Why:* The insects and the grain use up the oxygen in the sealed bin. With too little oxygen the weevils stop feeding and die. Farmers store grain in sealed bags for this reason.
9. (fill) Shops sell the oldest stock first. This rule is called first in, first ____.  
   **Answer:** ['out']. *Why:* First in, first out means the food that arrived first is used first. At home, older food goes to the front and new food behind it.
10. (draw) Draw the cold chain for a pouch of milk, from the animal to your fridge. Mark the weakest link.  
   **Answer:** A row of linked boxes: the cow or buffalo, the village cooler, the tanker, the dairy, the delivery van, the shop and the home fridge. The weakest link is often the crate outside the shop or the bag on the doorstep.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows every link, and gives a reason for the link marked as weakest.
11. (match) Match each observation to what it shows.  
   **Answer:** Salt sticks in a jar of rice that looks dry → Looking dry is not the same as being dry; The middle of a rice heap stays hot for an hour → Heat leaves only through the surface; Dry toast in a sealed bag does not spoil → Microbes need free water; A lemon slice grows mould but no slime → Acid stops most bacteria; Healthy ducklings fed mouldy meal fall ill → The meal carried the poison. *Why:* Each observation is evidence, and each conclusion says only what that evidence supports.
12. (write) Why does a nose warn you about spoiled food, but not about the most harmful microbes?  
   **Answer:** A nose picks up the acids and gases that huge numbers of spoilage microbes make. Many harmful bacteria make people ill in small numbers, and they make no smell. Some leave a toxin that has no smell either.. *Why:* A strong answer separates spoilage, which makes smells, from harm, which may make none.
13. (mcq) Which storage place suits potatoes best?  
   Options: A dark, airy basket / A sunny window sill / A sealed plastic bag / The freezer  
   **Answer:** A dark, airy basket. *Why:* Potatoes are alive. Light turns them green, and the green part holds a poison called solanine. A sealed bag traps damp, so they rot. Freezing breaks their cells.
14. (truefalse) On a day hotter than 32 degrees Celsius, cooked food should be chilled within one hour.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Bacteria double faster as food gets warmer. So food safety agencies shorten the two-hour rule to one hour on very hot days.
15. (count) A home throws away 2 katoris of food each day. How many katoris is that in 4 weeks?  
   **Answer:** 56. *Why:* Four weeks are 28 days. 28 times 2 is 56 katoris.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A packet of groundnuts kept for a year smells sharp and stale. What happened?  
   Options: Air and warmth turned the oil rancid / Bacteria ate the nuts / The nuts sprouted / Water moved into the nuts  
   **Answer:** Air and warmth turned the oil rancid. *Why:* The oil in nuts slowly changes in air, light and warmth, and smells rancid. Dry nuts hold too little water for bacteria.
2. (mcq) How do microbes eat solid food?  
   Options: They digest it outside their bodies / They bite off small pieces / They wait for it to melt / They breathe it in  
   **Answer:** They digest it outside their bodies. *Why:* A microbe has no mouth. Enzymes cut the food outside the microbe's body, and the small pieces pass in. The leftovers are the sourness, smell and slime of spoiled food.
3. (mcq) Which food is most likely to grow fuzzy mould, and not slime?  
   Options: Bread / Cooked dal / Milk / Fish curry  
   **Answer:** Bread. *Why:* Bread is too dry for most bacteria, so moulds spoil it. Dal, milk and fish curry are wet and not sour, so bacteria get there first.
4. (mcq) Why can cooked food stay out for only about two hours?  
   Options: Bacteria in warm food keep doubling / The food loses its vitamins / Flies need two hours to find it / The food dries out  
   **Answer:** Bacteria in warm food keep doubling. *Why:* In warm food some bacteria double every 20 minutes. After a short wait, their numbers rise faster and faster.
5. (mcq) Two cups of hot rice are pressed into a deep bowl. Which part cools last?  
   Options: The middle / The top / The side / The rim  
   **Answer:** The middle. *Why:* Heat leaves only through the surface. The middle is farthest from every surface, so its heat has the longest way to go.
6. (truefalse) A large pot of hot sambar should go into the fridge whole, with the lid on.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The middle of a large pot stays warm for hours, even in a fridge. Divide the sambar into shallow vessels first.
7. (mcq) Where should atta be stored in the monsoon?  
   Options: In an airtight dabba / In an open basket / In a paper bag near the sink / On the window sill  
   **Answer:** In an airtight dabba. *Why:* Atta takes in damp from monsoon air, and damp atta grows mould and draws insects. An airtight dabba keeps the damp out.
8. (mcq) Which of these must a baby under one year never be given?  
   Options: Honey / Mashed idli / Boiled and cooled water / Mashed banana  
   **Answer:** Honey. *Why:* Honey can hold spores that grow in a baby's gut and make a toxin. The other three are safe foods for a baby.
9. (mcq) Which meal needs the most care?  
   Options: Sambar for 200 guests, cooked the night before / Hot dosas eaten straight off the tawa / A banana peeled and eaten / Roasted chana from a dabba  
   **Answer:** Sambar for 200 guests, cooked the night before. *Why:* A large amount cooked hours ahead cools slowly, and many people will eat it. The other three are fresh and hot, or protected by a peel, or dry.
10. (mcq) In a fair test of how two vessels cool hot food, what must be different?  
   Options: Only the shape of the vessel / The amount of food / The room / The thermometer  
   **Answer:** Only the shape of the vessel. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing and keeps the rest the same. Then any difference in the result comes from that one thing.
11. (mcq) Groundnuts stored damp grow a mould. Why is roasting them not enough?  
   Options: The mould's poison survives the heat / Roasting adds water / Mould grows faster when hot / Roasted nuts turn rancid  
   **Answer:** The mould's poison survives the heat. *Why:* The mould makes a poison called aflatoxin. Roasting kills the mould, but most of the poison stays. Mouldy and shrivelled nuts are thrown away.

## Applications
- Cooks for weddings, temples, gurdwaras and school meals divide hot food into shallow trays. Or they cook it close to serving time.
- Dairy cooperatives chill milk in village coolers within hours of milking, so that it reaches the city fresh.
- Farmers dry grain in the sun and test its dampness before they fill a bin or a godown.
- Sealed three-layer grain bags let farmers keep grain and pulses for many months with no insect poison.
- Shops and home kitchens use first in, first out, so that the oldest food is used first.
- Hospitals and baby-care centres cook food fresh for patients and babies, and serve no raw sprouts or raw milk.
- Vaccines travel in a cold chain of their own, in ice boxes, to every village health centre.

## Open questions
- **How does a resting spore in cooked food sense that it is time to wake up?** Scientists know that warmth and certain nutrients start the waking. They do not fully know why some spores in the same food wake early and others wait for days.
- **Is cooked rice soaked overnight in water safe, and does it do the body good?** Millions of people in India eat it. The water turns sour, which should hold back many harmful bacteria. But few careful studies have counted the microbes in real kitchens.
- **Will hotter summers bring more illness from food?** Records from several countries show more food poisoning in warmer weeks. Nobody knows how far better fridges and better habits will cancel this out.
- **Can viruses that attack only bacteria be used to protect stored food?** Such viruses are called bacteriophages. A few are already used on some foods. Scientists are still testing how well they work and how fast bacteria become able to resist them.
- **Do vegetables in a fridge keep a daily rhythm, and does that change how good they are for us?** In 2013 cabbages from a shop were found to keep a rhythm of day and night. Nobody yet knows whether storing vegetables in light and dark would make them more nourishing.

## Connected maths
- In Deep or Shallow, the plate rice fell below 43 degrees Celsius after 10 minutes. The middle of the bowl took 60 minutes. How many times as long did the middle of the bowl take? **6 times as long.**. 60 divided by 10 is 6. So the middle of the bowl stayed hot 6 times as long as the rice on the plate. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Ten bacteria double every 20 minutes. How many are there after 3 hours? **5,120 bacteria.**. Three hours hold 9 lots of 20 minutes. Doubling 10 nine times gives 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640, 1,280, 2,560 and 5,120. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In a warm kitchen bacteria double every 20 minutes. In a cool room they double every 2 hours. Each place starts with 10. After 4 hours, how many times as many are in the warm kitchen? **1,024 times as many.**. In 4 hours the warm kitchen has 12 doublings, which gives 40,960. The cool room has 2 doublings, which gives 40. 40,960 divided by 40 is 1,024. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- Thick food in a pot twice as wide takes about 4 times as long to cool in the middle. The middle of a small vessel cools in 3 hours. A big vessel is twice as wide. Will its middle be cool 9 hours later? **No. It needs about 12 hours.**. 4 times 3 hours is 12 hours. After 9 hours the middle of the big vessel is still warm. (queues `math.multiplication.L5`)
- The power goes off at 1:30 in the afternoon and comes back at 7:10 in the evening. A closed fridge stays cold for about 4 hours. For about how long was the fridge too warm? **About 1 hour and 40 minutes.**. From 1:30 to 7:10 is 5 hours and 40 minutes. Take away the 4 cold hours. That leaves 1 hour and 40 minutes in the danger zone, which is inside the two-hour rule. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)

What percentage was forgotten? **45 per cent.**. 9 out of 20 is the same as 45 out of 100. So 45 per cent of the lost food was forgotten. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A home throws away 2 katoris of cooked food a day. One katori costs about 12 rupees to buy and cook. What does the lost food cost in a year of 365 days? **8,760 rupees.**. 2 katoris a day cost 24 rupees. 24 times 365 is 8,760 rupees in a year. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L5`)
