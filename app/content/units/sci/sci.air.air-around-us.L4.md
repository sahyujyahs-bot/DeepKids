# Air pushes: why a card holds up a glass of water, and nothing really sucks
`sci.air.air-around-us.L4`

**Summary.** Fill a glass to the brim, slide a card over it, and turn it upside down. The card stays put and the water stays in. Nothing is gluing the card on. The air is pushing it, with about one kilogram of push on every square centimetre. You will test a bottle that stops pouring when you screw the cap on, a straw that stops working when it has a hole, and a bottle that squashes itself. You find out why we never notice the push of air, why sucking is really air pushing, why a straw or a pump can lift water only about ten metres, and why your ears pop on a hill.

**Intro.** *Fill a glass to the brim and slide a card over it. Hold the card and turn the glass upside down. Now take your hand away. The card does not fall. The water does not fall. What is holding it up?* There is no glue, no magnet and no hand. Something you cannot see is pushing the card up from below, and it is strong enough to hold a glass of water. In this unit you find out what it is. You test a bottle with a hole, a straw with a hole and a bottle that crushes itself. You also find out how people first proved that air has a push, and why it lifts water only ten metres and no more.

## Terms
- **Atmosphere**: The deep layer of air that wraps around the whole Earth. Gravity holds it there. *e.g.* The atmosphere is the sea of air at the bottom of which we live.
- **Pressure**: How hard something pushes on each square centimetre of a surface. *e.g.* A drawing pin has a point so small that all your push is squeezed onto a tiny spot, and the pressure is very high.
- **Air pressure**: The push of air on every surface it touches. It comes from billions of air particles hitting the surface every second. *e.g.* Air pressure at sea level pushes with about 1 kilogram of weight on every square centimetre.
- **Area**: How much flat surface something has, counted in square centimetres. *e.g.* A card 10 centimetres long and 6 centimetres wide has an area of 60 square centimetres.
- **Balanced pushes**: Two pushes that are equal and opposite, so a thing does not move. *e.g.* When you press your palms together as hard as you can, neither hand moves.
- **Vacuum**: A space with no air in it, or almost none. *e.g.* Space between the planets is nearly a vacuum.
- **Suction**: The word for what happens when air takes the push away from one side, so the air on the other side pushes a thing towards the gap. *e.g.* When you sip through a straw, air pushing on your drink in the glass lifts the drink up the straw.
- **Barometer**: An instrument that measures air pressure. *e.g.* A barometer on a weather station falls when a storm is coming.
- **Altitude**: How high a place is above the sea. *e.g.* Ooty is at an altitude of about 2,200 metres, so its air pushes less than the air in Chennai.
- **Lift pump**: A hand pump with its working part down in the water, so it pushes the water up the pipe instead of waiting for the air to push it up. *e.g.* The India Mark II hand pump has its cylinder deep down inside the borehole.
- **Mercury**: A silvery metal that is liquid at room temperature and about 13.6 times heavier than the same amount of water. It is poisonous, so never play with it. *e.g.* Torricelli used mercury in his tube because a short tube of it balances the air.
- **Expand**: To spread out into more space. *e.g.* A sealed packet of chips puffs up when you take it up a hill, because the air inside expands.
- **Particle**: A tiny piece of matter, much too small to see. Air is made of particles that fly about in every direction. *e.g.* A single breath holds far more air particles than the usual estimate of the grains of sand on every beach on Earth.
- **Siphon**: A tube that moves liquid up and over a rim and down to a lower place, with no pouring or pumping once it starts. *e.g.* A farmer can empty a tank into a lower field through a pipe full of water.

## Explanations
### Air pushes on everything it touches, from every direction · *defined*
**Definition:** Air pressure is the push of air on a surface. The air pushes on the top, the bottom and the sides of every thing in it, all at once.

Air is made of particles that fly about and bump into whatever they meet. Each bump is a tiny push. A surface is hit billions of times every second, so the tiny pushes add up to one steady push. Because the particles fly in every direction, the push is the same in every direction. It pushes down on the top of the table. It pushes up on the underside. It pushes sideways on the wall. This is why the card on the glass holds when the glass is turned sideways too.
- e.g. Air pushes on your whole body all day, on your front, your back and your sides.
- e.g. A card held under an upside-down glass is pushed up by the air below it.
- e.g. A closed bottle taken to a hill station swells or squashes because the air outside pushes differently there.
### How big the push is: about 1 kilogram on each square centimetre · *defined*
**Definition:** At sea level, air presses on each square centimetre with about 1 kilogram of weight. On a table 1 metre by 1 metre, that is about 10 tonnes.

The air above you is a tall pile, going up about a hundred kilometres before it thins away. That air has weight, and the weight of the pile above a patch of ground pushes down on it. The pile above one square centimetre weighs about 1 kilogram. A flat child's hand is about 70 square centimetres. So the air pushes on each side of the hand with a weight of about 70 kilograms. Air pushes sideways and up just as hard, because the particles are bumping in all directions, not only because of the weight above.
- e.g. A packet of sugar on a thumbnail-sized patch is about the right weight.
- e.g. A door 2 metres high and 1 metre wide has about 20 tonnes of air pushing on each side of it.
- e.g. The push on a postcard is about 150 kilograms on each side.
### Why we do not notice the push · *defined*
**Definition:** We do not notice air pressure because it is balanced. The air pushes in from outside, and the fluids and air inside our bodies push out with the same strength.

A push that is balanced does not move anything, so we do not notice it. Inside your body there is air in your lungs, and your blood, your cells and your gut are full of water. They all push outward at the same strength as the air outside pushes inward. We feel the push only when it changes quickly, as in an aeroplane or a lift going up a tall building, because then the outside push changes and the inside one takes a little while to catch up. That is the pop in your ears.
- e.g. Your hand does not notice the weight of 70 kilograms of air on it.
- e.g. Your ears pop in a fast lift because the push outside has changed.
- e.g. A fish in the deep sea does not notice the huge push of water on her, because her body pushes back.
### Why a card holds the water in an upside-down glass · *defined*
**Definition:** The card stays on because the air pushes up on it much harder than the water pushes down on it.

A glass of water 10 centimetres tall pushes down on the card with about 10 grams of weight on each square centimetre. The air below pushes up with about 1,000 grams on each square centimetre. The air wins by about 100 times. For the water to fall, the card has to move away, and the water has to leave a gap above it. If the glass is full to the brim, there is no air in the glass to fill the gap, so the water cannot fall. The only way the water can go is if the card bends a little and lets some air in. A smooth, flat card stops that.
- e.g. A card held over a full tumbler turned upside down.
- e.g. A full bottle with a hole low down stops dripping when the cap is on.
- e.g. A drinks tin with one hole in the top pours slowly and in jerks, and with two holes it pours smoothly.
### Nothing sucks: suction is a push from the side that has more air · *defined*
**Definition:** A straw does not pull a drink. It takes some air out of the straw, so less air pushes on the drink inside it. The air pushing on the drink in the glass then lifts the drink up the straw.

When you sip, you make the space inside your mouth bigger, and the air in your mouth and straw spreads out and pushes less. The air outside is now pushing harder on the drink in the glass than the air in the straw is pushing on the drink inside the straw. The harder push wins, and the drink goes up. A hole in the straw lets air in, so the push inside the straw is as big as the push outside again, and the drink stays down. A vacuum cleaner, a suction hook and a dropper all work this way.
- e.g. A straw with a hole in it does not work. Cover the hole with a finger and it works again.
- e.g. A suction hook stays on a tile because the air outside pushes it flat against the tile.
- e.g. A dropper fills when you squeeze it, put it in water, and let go.
### Air pressure falls as you go up · *defined*
**Definition:** The higher you go, the less air is above you, so the air pushes less. At the top of Mount Everest, the push is about one third of the push at sea level.

Air is heaviest at the bottom, because it is squashed by all the air above it. As you climb, there is less air above you, and the air around you is thinner. At 5,500 metres, which is about as high as a base camp on Everest, the push is about half the push at sea level. A sealed bottle taken up a mountain swells, because the air inside still pushes at sea-level strength and the air outside pushes less. Taken down again, it squashes. Weather also changes air pressure a little each day, and a falling reading often means a storm is on its way.
- e.g. A packet of chips puffs up on the way to Ooty or Munnar.
- e.g. Aeroplane cabins are kept at the push you would feel at about 2,000 metres, not the push at the height the plane flies.
- e.g. Cooking takes longer high in the hills, because water boils at a lower temperature there.
### How far air can lift water: about ten metres · *defined*
**Definition:** The push of the air can hold up a column of water about 10 metres tall. This is why a straw or a pump at the top of a pipe cannot lift water from deeper than about 10 metres.

Imagine a tube standing in a tank of water, with all the air taken out of the tube. The air pushes on the water in the tank and forces water up the tube. It stops when the weight of the water in the tube equals the push of the air. At sea level that happens at about 10.3 metres. Torricelli used mercury, which is 13.6 times heavier than water, so the column was 13.6 times shorter, about 76 centimetres. In real pumps the limit is a bit lower, about 7 or 8 metres, because some air always leaks in. For deeper wells, the pump is put down in the water and pushes the water up.
- e.g. A mercury barometer stands at about 76 centimetres.
- e.g. A hand pump on top of a borewell stops working when the water falls below about 7 metres.
- e.g. An India Mark II pump has its cylinder deep down so it can work from much deeper.
### Pressure at the level of particles · *defined*
**Definition:** Pressure is made by particles hitting a surface. More particles in the same space, or faster particles, mean more hits and a bigger push.

Take a bicycle pump and press the plunger with your finger over the hole. The same air is squeezed into less space. The particles are closer and hit the walls more often, so the push grows. Warm the air and the particles move faster, so each bump is harder, and the push grows again. A sealed bottle of hot air that cools has fewer and slower bumps, and the pressure inside falls. This is what made the bottle crush itself. The particles are tiny and always moving, so even still air is a very busy place.
- e.g. A pumped-up tyre has more particles in the same space, so it pushes hard.
- e.g. A hot sealed bottle that is cooled pushes less on its walls.
- e.g. A pressure cooker raises the push inside, so water boils at a higher temperature.
### How a siphon works · *debated*
A siphon is a tube full of water that carries water up over a rim and down to a lower place. Everyone agrees on what a siphon does. Scientists still argue about the best way to explain why it works. The old textbook explanation says the push of the air on the water in the top tank is what drives the water up and over. Some experiments suggest that this is not the whole story.
- e.g. A farmer empties a tank into a lower field through a pipe.
- e.g. A fish tank cleaner moves dirty water into a bucket through a tube.
- e.g. Some experiments have kept a siphon running with much of the air pressure taken away.
- **The air pressure explanation, found in many older textbooks:** The air pushes on the water in the top tank and forces it up and over the bend. The siphon would stop in a place with no air.
- **The chain explanation, supported by some physicists who tested siphons at low pressure:** Water in the tube is a chain held together by the sticking of its particles to each other. The longer, heavier side of the chain falls down and pulls the rest after it. Some experiments report siphons running at much lower pressure, which suggests that air pressure is not what drives them. Others, such as Richert and Binder in 2011, tested this and found that pressure still matters.

## Mostly agreed
- Air has weight and pushes on everything it touches, from every direction. *How we know:* Torricelli's mercury tube, tried around 1643, and thousands of barometers since then show that the air's push holds up a column of mercury about 76 centimetres tall.
- At sea level, air pushes with about 1 kilogram of weight on each square centimetre. *How we know:* Measured with barometers in every country. The reading is about 1,013 millibars at sea level on an average day.
- Air pressure falls as you go higher, and the atmosphere has no sharp top. *How we know:* Périer's climb of the Puy de Dôme in 1648, and many balloon, aeroplane and mountain readings since.
- Drinking through a straw, using a suction hook and running a vacuum cleaner all work because air pushes from the side that has more of it. *How we know:* The straw stops working when a hole lets the air in, and starts again when the hole is covered.
- The push on a thing is the pressure multiplied by its area. *How we know:* A large flat surface feels a much bigger total push than a small one in the same air, as in the card and the door.

## How we found out
**Evangelista Torricelli and Vincenzo Viviani in Italy, and Blaise Pascal and Florin Périer in France, About 1643, and 1648.** About 2,300 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle taught that nature hates an empty space, so water has to follow a pump's piston to keep any gap from opening. People believed this for almost 2,000 years. Then, in the 1600s, well-makers in Italy found that their pumps would not lift water higher than about ten metres, and no one could say why. Around 1643, Evangelista Torricelli guessed that the weight of the air was holding the water up. His helper, Vincenzo Viviani, filled a glass tube about a metre long with mercury, a liquid metal 13.6 times heavier than water, and turned it over in a dish. The mercury sank to about 76 centimetres and stopped, leaving a space at the top. In 1648, Blaise Pascal's brother-in-law, Florin Périer, carried the tube up the Puy de Dôme, a mountain in France. The mercury stood lower, the higher he went.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle's idea that nature hates a vacuum and so fills every gap. The pump limit of about ten metres, Torricelli's empty space at the top of the tube, and the lower mercury on the mountain showed that the weight of air does the pushing, and that it pushes less with height.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Nature hates empty space, so water has to follow a pump's piston to fill the gap.** (Aristotle and many thinkers for nearly 2,000 years.; *rejected*)  
  For: Water does follow a piston up a pipe, and a gap never seemed to open.  
  Against: Pumps stop working above about 10 metres, so the 'hatred' has a limit. Torricelli's tube made a real empty space at the top, and nothing stopped it.
- **Suction pulls things towards an empty place.** (Many people, and most everyday speech.; *rejected*)  
  For: It feels like the straw pulls the drink, and the word is used everywhere.  
  Against: A hole in the straw stops the sucking, even though you pull just as hard. An empty space has nothing in it to pull with. The push comes from the air on the other side.
- **Air pressure pushes down, because the air is above us.** (Many children, and some adults.; *rejected*)  
  For: The weight of the air above does cause the pressure.  
  Against: The card held the water when the glass was tilted sideways. Air pushes sideways and up just as hard, because its particles fly in every direction.
- **A tall tree lifts water like a straw, using the push of the air.** (A natural guess.; *debated*)  
  For: Water goes up a tree just as water goes up a straw.  
  Against: The push of air can lift water only about 10 metres, but some trees are over 100 metres tall. Scientists think the leaves pull water up, with the water held together in a chain by the sticking of its particles. The details are still debated.
- **A fall in air pressure usually means that rain or a storm is on the way.** (Weather forecasters and sailors for over 300 years.; *accepted*)  
  For: In many places, low pressure goes with clouds, rain and wind.  
  Against: Pressure alone does not tell you the weather. A forecast also needs temperature, wind and water in the air.

## Roots
We have found that air pushes on everything, that you never notice it, and that sucking is really air pushing. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Nothing sucks. Through a straw, you take air out of the straw, so less air pushes on the drink inside it. The air pushing on the drink in the glass lifts it up. Suction is always a push from the side that has more air.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'suck' and picture the empty place pulling. But an empty place has nothing in it to pull with. The word makes us look at the gap, when the push comes from the other side.  
*Evidence:* A straw with a hole in it did not work, though the sip was just as hard. Covering the hole made it work again. Torricelli's tube stopped at 76 centimetres because the air's push, not a pull from the empty space, held the mercury up.

- *who am i:* About 70 kilograms of air pushes on each side of your hand, and you feel nothing. You are held in shape by pushes that balance.
- *what is life:* A fish, a bird and a tree each live at the push of their own home, and each body pushes back from inside. A body fits the pressure it grew up in.
- *what is this world:* We live at the bottom of a deep sea of air. Wind, weather, a straw and a pump all happen because the push is bigger in one place than in another.

Still air looks empty and calm. Zoom in and it is billions of particles, flying and hitting every surface every second. The calm is a balance of countless tiny pushes. A straw, a pump, the wind and a storm all begin when one push is a little bigger than another. Where we see nothing, there is a great deal going on, held in balance.

- **Rig Veda 10.168, the hymn to Vata, the wind:** The hymn says that the sound of the wind is heard but its form is not seen, and it calls the wind the self, or breath, of the gods (verse 4, as paraphrased). (We cannot see air, but we know it by what it does. It holds a card on a glass of water and lifts a drink up a straw.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment B51 (Diels and Kranz numbering), ancient Greece, about 500 BCE:** Heraclitus said that things hold together by pulling against each other in opposite directions, like the string and the wood of a bow or a lyre. (The card holds because the water pushes down and the air pushes up. The balance of two opposite pushes keeps it still.)
- Science (settled): Pressure comes from air particles hitting a surface billions of times every second. Their number and their speed set the size of the push.
- Science (settled): Empty space is never quite empty. Experiments have measured a tiny push between two metal plates in a vacuum, the Casimir effect.
- Other view, Many physicists: A difference in pressure can be described either way. 'The air pushes the drink up' and 'the straw sucks the drink up' are two ways of describing the same difference. The sums come out the same. So the word suction is fine for daily life, as long as we remember that nothing pulls from an empty place.
- Practice, Two pushes that cancel (3 min): Sit still and put your palms together in front of your chest. Push gently, then harder, then harder still. Notice that neither hand moves. Two strong pushes can look like nothing at all. Now relax your hands. Breathe in slowly three times. Feel the air coming in and going out. The air outside and the air in your chest are pushing on each other all day, in perfect balance.
- Ask: If a push is the same on every side, and you feel nothing, what else around you might be pushing without you noticing?
- Ask: A fish in the deep sea lives with a push on her body of a hundred times what we feel. Is that push big, or small, or just normal? Who decides?
- Ask: Is a vacuum cleaner sucking, or is the air pushing? Does it matter which word we use?
- Ask: The air in your lungs a second ago is now in the room. Where does the air end and you begin?

## Story: The Pump That Ran Out of Push
*A made-up story. The village, the people and the pump repair are imagined. The science in it is real. A pump at the top of a pipe can lift water only about ten metres at most, and for deeper water the working part of the pump is put down in the water, as in the India Mark II hand pump.*

In the last week of May, the hand pump beside the temple tank in Marungulam began to cough. Marungulam was a village near Thanjavur, and Selvi, who was nine, had pumped that handle every morning since she was six. Clang, clang, clang. Air hissed from the spout. Then one thin dribble came, and then nothing at all.

By evening half the village stood around the pump. Murugesan, the ward member, kicked the iron base. 'The pump is old,' he said. 'It has lost its pull. We need a stronger one.' Kumar, who drove the tractor, nodded. 'Bring the biggest pump from town,' he said. 'It will pull water up from anywhere.'

Next morning the block office sent Revathi, a hand pump mechanic with a canvas bag of spanners. She said very little. She opened the head of the pump, looked at the leather washer, and closed it again. 'The washer is fine,' she said. 'The pump is fine.'

'Then why is there no water?' asked Murugesan. Revathi tied a steel nut to a ball of string with a knot every metre. 'Selvi, hold the end and count the knots as I let it down the pipe.' The string ran into the dark. Selvi counted aloud. 'Eight. Nine. Ten. Eleven.' The nut stopped with a tiny plop. 'Twelve,' said Selvi, 'nearly twelve.' Only the last knot came up wet.

'The water has gone down twelve metres,' said Revathi. 'The rains were poor, and every house in the village has been taking water from the ground.'

'Then we need a stronger pull,' said Kumar again. Revathi smiled. She took a bottle of lime juice and a straw out of her bag. 'Kumar Anna, sip this.' Kumar sipped. 'Good,' he said. With a pin, Revathi made a hole in the straw, near the top. 'Sip again.' Kumar sipped and sipped, until his face was red. The juice stayed where it was. Somebody laughed.

'You pulled just as hard,' said Revathi. 'Nothing came.' She put her thumb over the hole. The juice rose at once. 'A straw does not pull the juice. A pump does not pull water. They only take the air out of the pipe above the water. Then the air outside pushes on the water in the bottle, and that push drives it up the pipe. The hole let air back in, so there was no difference in pushes.'

'Air pushing?' said Murugesan. 'Air is nothing.' 'On a patch as big as your thumbnail, it pushes with the weight of a packet of sugar,' said Revathi. 'On the whole top of a well, it is thousands of tonnes. But that push can hold up only about ten metres of water. Your water is at twelve. A bigger pump at the top would make no difference. Nothing could push water that high from there.'

Nobody spoke. A crow shouted from the tamarind tree. 'So what do we do?' asked Murugesan at last, quite softly.

'We stop asking the top of the pipe to do the lifting,' said Revathi. 'We put the working part of the pump down in the water. Then it pushes the water up the pipe from below, a bit at a time, and the air has no height to cover.'

It took two days. Murugesan, Kumar and six others pulled the heavy pipes out one by one, each as long as a man is tall. Revathi sat on a stool and gave orders. Selvi chalked a number on every pipe so that they would go back in the right order. At the end, the new part went down on long rods: a cylinder with a piston and a leather cup, which sat deep in the wet dark, twelve metres below the road.

On the second evening Revathi said, 'Selvi, you try.' Clang. Clang. A cough. Then water came, cold and clear, in a fat rope, into a steel pot. The whole street cheered. Murugesan filled a tumbler first and gave it to Revathi.

That night, at home, Selvi found a straw in the kitchen and poked it with her mother's pin. She poured a glass of buttermilk and sipped. Nothing. She put her thumb over the hole. Up came the buttermilk, cold and sour. She looked at the straw. 'Nobody pulled you,' she told the buttermilk. 'The whole sky pushed you.'

**For the parent:** A straw or a pump does not pull water. It takes the air away from one side, and the air on the other side pushes the water up. That push is strong, but it can lift water only about ten metres.

- Ask: Why can a pump at the top of a pipe never lift water from deeper than about ten metres, however strong the pump is?
- Ask: Why did the juice stop rising when there was a hole in the straw, even though the person sipped just as hard?
- Ask: If you could take a straw to the Moon, where there is no air, could you drink a drink with it? Why?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bottle with a Hole: when does the water stop? (40 min)
- **question:** A bottle of water has a small hole near the bottom. With the cap off, the water runs out. What happens with the cap screwed on tight? What happens if there is a second hole high up?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. With the cap on tight, will the water keep running out of the hole, or stop? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same bottle, the same hole and the same amount of water are used in every test. Only one thing changes at a time: the cap on or off, or a second hole. You count the seconds the water flows in the same way each time, and stop counting at 30. A test where the water does not stop is the control, because it shows what the hole does with nothing else changed.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the holes with the pointed tool, with the bottle on a folded towel, and pushes away from their hands. Do the test over a basin or a sink.
- **record:** Fill in the table for each test. Then draw the bottle with the hole, and draw arrows for the air at the hole and at the top of the water. Say which air is pushing in which place.
- **results table:** Test | Cap | Second hole high up | Did the water keep flowing? | Seconds of flow (stop at 30)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bottle, the needle and the basin. Say that you will make a hole in the bottle. Write down their guess and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will put a small hole near the bottom of this bottle and fill it with water. If I screw the cap on tight and let go of the hole, what will the water do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A bottle with a hole near the bottom. Cap on tight. Will the water run out? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen with the cap on, and why? (good: It will run out because it is heavy.; It will stop because the cap is shut.; It will dribble and then stop.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most people say it will run out. A guess with a reason is a good start. Write the guess at the top of the table.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up lays the bottle on a folded towel and twists the needle through the side, about 4 centimetres from the bottom. Make the hole as wide as a matchstick. Dry the bottle.  
  *Parent says:* I will make the hole. I push the point in with a twist, and I keep my other hand well away. The hole should be about as wide as a matchstick.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes a small hole near the bottom. Keep your hands away.
  - Note: A hole about 2 millimetres wide is best. A hole that is too big will keep dribbling with the cap on. If that happens, put a small piece of tape over half of the hole. A hole that is too small makes the flow too slow to see.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the bottle over the basin. Cover the hole with a finger. Fill the bottle with water to three quarters full. Screw the cap on very tight. Lift your finger and count the seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Finger on the hole. Fill the bottle three quarters full. Screw the cap on very tight. Now lift your finger. Count the seconds the water keeps coming out. Stop at thirty.  
  *Child mode:* Finger on the hole. Cap on tight. Lift your finger. Count the seconds.
  - Ask: What did the water do? (good: It came out for a moment and then stopped.; It dribbled and then stopped.; It stopped by itself.)
  - Note: Expect a few drops or a short squirt of one or two seconds, and then nothing. If it keeps running, the cap is not tight or the hole is too wide. Screw the cap harder first. A very thin bottle may squash a little and let a bit more out. That is fine, so write down what you see.
  - Check: The water stopped running with the cap on. Which of these should have changed inside the bottle? → **The air at the top had more room, so it pushed less on the water**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the bottle over the basin again, with the hole covered by a finger. Take the cap off. Lift your finger and count the seconds. After ten seconds, screw the cap back on while the water is still running.  
  *Parent says:* Same bottle. This time the cap is off. Lift your finger and count. After ten seconds, quickly screw the cap back on. What happens to the water?  
  *Child mode:* Cap off. Lift your finger. Count. After ten seconds, screw the cap on.
  - Ask: What did the water do with the cap off, and then when you put the cap back on? (good: It kept running with the cap off.; It stopped when the cap went back on.; The cap made it stop.)
  - Note: With the cap off, the water should keep running until the level drops to the hole. That takes a minute or two. With the cap screwed back on, the flow should stop in a second or two.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep the cap off and let the water run out until the bottle is nearly empty. Pour it back in until three quarters full. Cap on tight. Gently squeeze the sides, then let go.  
  *Parent says:* Fill it up again, cap on tight. Hold your finger on the hole. Lift it, then squeeze the sides gently. What does the water do? Now let go. Look at the hole.  
  *Child mode:* Cap on tight. Lift your finger. Squeeze the bottle. Let go. Watch the hole.
  - Ask: What did you see at the hole when you let go of the squeezed bottle? (good: Bubbles of air went in.; The water stopped squirting and a bubble went in.; Air went into the bottle.)
  - Note: When you squeeze, the water squirts out. When you let go, the bottle springs back, and a bubble of air is pushed in through the hole. It is the air outside pushing in.
- **s6 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up makes a second hole high on the bottle, 2 centimetres above the water line. Cap on tight. Lift your finger from the bottom hole and count the seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Now a second hole, high up, above the water. Cap on tight, and finger off the bottom hole. Will the water stop this time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A second hole, high up. Cap on. Finger off. Count. What happens?
  - Ask: The cap is on tight, but the water keeps running. Why? (good: Air comes in through the top hole.; The top hole lets air in.; The air on top is pushing again.)
  - Note: With the second hole open, the water should run steadily, as it did with the cap off. Air comes in through the top hole and pushes on the water at the top.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Fill in the table together. Then empty the bottle and dry everything.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? What did the air have to do with it?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought. Say what you saw. Say why. Fill in your table.
  - Ask: What does the bottle with a hole in the top show about air? (good: Air is pushing on the water.; Air needs to get in at the top for the water to run.; Air pushes on the water, and the water pushes back.)
  - Note: A strong answer says that the air pushed on the water at the hole, and that the water stopped when the air on top pushed less. After they have told you what they saw, explain: water running out gave the air at the top more room, so it pushed less. Soon the air outside, pushing in at the hole, was as strong as the push of the water and the air inside. Air at the top of the water is needed for water to keep running. A grown-up can show that a tin of juice with one hole runs in jerks, and with two holes runs smoothly.
  - Think: When the cap was on, what was missing at the top of the water?
### e2 · The Bottle That Squashes Itself (30 min)
- **question:** A bottle full of hot air and steam is closed and then cooled. Does it change shape? What if the cap is left off, or the bottle was never warmed?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After cooling, what will each of the three bottles look like? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Three bottles of the same kind are used. Bottle A gets hot water, a cap and then cold water. Bottle B has no hot water, but gets a cap and cold water. This is a control for the cold water. Bottle C gets hot water and cold water but no cap. This is a control for the closed cap. Everything else is the same. The only difference is whether the air inside was made warmer and then closed in.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water and handles the hot bottles with a thick cloth. The water must not boil in the bottle. Do not use glass bottles. Do not put the cap on loosely and then squeeze, because hot water can splash.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the three bottles at the end. Draw small arrows to show which air pushes harder on the walls of bottle A, inside or outside.
- **results table:** Bottle | What was done | Did it squash? (not at all, a little, a lot) | Seconds until it began to squash
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three bottles and say which is A, B and C. Explain what you will do to each one. Write down their guess and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will do something different to each bottle. A gets hot water, a cap and cold water. B gets only a cap and cold water. C gets hot water and cold water, but no cap. What will each bottle look like at the end? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Three bottles. A: hot water, cap, cold. B: cap, cold. C: hot water, no cap, cold. What happens? Why?
  - Ask: Which bottle do you think will change, and why? (good: A, because the hot air turns to something else.; A, because it was hot and closed.; All of them, because the cold will crush them.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write the guesses at the top of the table.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up pours about 100 millilitres of hot water into bottle A. Swirl it for 20 seconds, then pour it out. Right away, screw the cap on very tight. Do the same with bottle C, but leave its cap off.  
  *Parent says:* I will pour a little hot water into A and swirl it. Then I pour it out and put the cap on fast. I do the same with C but leave it open. Keep your hands away from the hot bottle.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up warms bottles A and C with hot water. Keep your hands away.
  - Note: Move quickly between pouring out and capping. The steam fills the bottle and pushes some air out. If the bottle goes soft or shrinks while you swirl, the water was too hot. Use water that has cooled for a few more minutes.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the cap on bottle B too, without any hot water. Then stand all three bottles in the bowl of cold water. Start the clock.  
  *Parent says:* Cap on B. Now all three bottles go into the cold water. Start the clock. Watch each bottle. Which one changes first?  
  *Child mode:* Cap on B. Put all three in cold water. Watch. Which changes?
  - Ask: What do you see happening to bottle A? (good: It is squashing.; It is crumpling by itself.; It is making a crackling noise.)
  - Note: Bottle A should start to crumple within 5 to 30 seconds, often with a crackling noise. It may help to pour cold water over the top of it. If nothing happens after a minute, use colder water, a thinner bottle or more hot water, and check that the cap is tight.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Watch for 2 minutes. Write down what happens to each bottle in the table. Then ask your child which air is pushing harder on bottle A, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all three bottles. Which squashed, and which did not? Which air is pushing harder on the walls of bottle A, the air inside or the air outside? How can you tell?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each bottle. Which one squashed? Which air pushes harder?
  - Check: Bottle A squashed, but bottle C did not. What is the main difference between them? → **Bottle A was closed, so the air inside could not be replaced as it cooled**
- **s5 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then pour the cold water away. Open bottle A's cap and listen. Dry everything.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened? Now open the cap on bottle A and listen. What do you hear?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought. Say what you saw. Say why. Open the cap and listen.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the air inside got cooler and pushed less on the walls, while the air outside kept pushing at the same strength, so it won. After they have said what they saw, add: the steam filled the bottle at first and pushed out some air. When it cooled, it turned back into drops of water. Cool air also pushes less. Opening the cap lets air rush in with a hiss, and the pushes match again. The bottle often stays crumpled, because the plastic has been bent. If bottle A did not squash, write that down. It is still a result, and you can try again with a thinner bottle.
  - Think: Bottle A did not get any lighter or heavier. What changed?

### a3 · The Suction Hook: how much can the air hold? (30 min)
- **safety:** Hang the bucket low and stand to the side, because it may fall. Do not go above 3 litres of water in the bucket. Put a towel on the floor.
- **record:** Write the number of litres the hook held before it fell. Measure across the cup of the hook. Draw the hook on the tile with arrows for the air pushing it flat.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the hook, the tile and the bucket. Ask for a guess and a reason about how many litres it can hold.  
  *Parent says:* This rubber hook will stick to the tile. How many litres of water in this bucket do you think it can hold before it falls? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A suction hook on a tile. How many litres can it hold? Why?
  - Ask: How many litres do you think it can hold, and why? (good: Not many, because it is only rubber.; A lot, because it sticks hard.; It depends on how well it is pressed on.)
  - Note: One litre of water has a mass of 1 kilogram. Write the guess in litres. Do not correct it.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wet the rim of the hook and press it flat on the tile for 5 seconds. Pull gently on the hook with your fingers to test it. Hang the empty bucket.  
  *Parent says:* Wet the rim, press the hook flat and count to five. Pull it gently. Does it hold? Now hang the empty bucket on it.  
  *Child mode:* Wet the hook. Press it flat. Hang the empty bucket.
  - Note: If the hook slides off, the tile may be rough or dusty. Wipe the tile, wet the rim again and use a smoother place, such as a mirror or a glass door.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour in one litre of water. Wait 10 seconds. Pour in another litre. Keep going up to 3 litres, or until the hook falls. Write the number of litres each time.  
  *Parent says:* Pour in one litre. Wait ten seconds. Is it still holding? Pour in another. Keep going, and write down how many litres it holds. Stop at three litres.  
  *Child mode:* Add one litre. Wait. Is it holding? Add another. Stop at three.
  - Ask: What do you think is holding the hook up? (good: The air is pushing it onto the tile.; The rubber is sticky.; There is no air under the cup.)
  - Note: Small hooks often hold 1 to 3 kilograms for a short time. The hook may slowly slide down over a minute or two. That is fine. Write the highest number of litres it held for ten seconds.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure across the hook's cup in centimetres. Ask your child to work out the area of the circle on the tile. Use half the width times half the width times three. Then work out the push of the air.  
  *Parent says:* Measure across the cup. Half of that is the radius. Multiply the radius by itself, and then by three. That is the area in square centimetres. About one kilogram of air pushes on each square centimetre. How big is the push?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the cup. Work out its area. Air pushes with 1 kilogram on each square centimetre.
  - Note: A cup 4 centimetres wide has a radius of 2. 2 times 2 times 3 is about 12 square centimetres. That is a possible push of about 12 kilograms. Real hooks hold much less, because some air gets under the rim and the rubber bends. The best-case number is the most a perfect hook could hold.
  - Think: The sum gives a very big number. The hook held far less. Why?

### c1 · Empty the Bucket Without Lifting It (35 min)
- **problem:** A full bucket of water stands on a table. A second, empty bucket stands on the floor beside it. Move all the water into the lower bucket without lifting, tipping, scooping or pouring, and without touching the water with a cup.

   **Answer:** About 1 kilogram on each square centimetre. *Why:* The air above one square centimetre weighs about 1 kilogram. A gram would be too small to hold up a glass of water. A tonne would crush us.
3. (mcq) Why do we not notice the push of air on us?  
   Options: The push is balanced by the push of the air and fluids inside our bodies / The push is too weak to matter / Our skin has no way to detect any push at all  
   **Answer:** The push is balanced by the push of the air and fluids inside our bodies. *Why:* Two equal pushes in opposite directions cancel, so nothing moves and we feel nothing. If you chose 'too weak', remember that it is about 70 kilograms on a hand.
4. (mcq) A capped bottle with a small hole low down has just stopped running. Why did it stop?  
   Options: The air at the top spread out and pushed less, so the air outside balanced the water / The cap pushed the water back / The hole closed up  
   **Answer:** The air at the top spread out and pushed less, so the air outside balanced the water. *Why:* A little water leaving gave the top air more room, so it pushed less. Then the air pushing in at the hole matched the push out. If you chose 'cap pushed', see what the cap-off test did.
5. (mcq) Why does a card stay on a glass that is full to the brim and turned upside down?  
   Options: The air pushes up on the card much more than the water pushes down / The water is too thick to fall / The card is magnetic  
   **Answer:** The air pushes up on the card much more than the water pushes down. *Why:* The air pushes up with about 100 times the weight of the water in a tumbler, so the card stays on.
6. (mcq) Why does a straw with a hole in it not work?  
   Options: The hole lets air in, so the push inside the straw matches the push outside / The hole makes the straw heavier / The hole lets the drink out  
   **Answer:** The hole lets air in, so the push inside the straw matches the push outside. *Why:* No difference in the pushes means nothing lifts the drink. Covering the hole brings the difference back.
7. (mcq) What really lifts the drink up a straw?  
   Options: Air pushing on the drink in the glass / The straw pulling / The empty space in the straw  
   **Answer:** Air pushing on the drink in the glass. *Why:* Sipping takes air out of the straw, and the air outside pushes the drink up. An empty space has nothing to pull with.
8. (mcq) What happens to air pressure as you climb a tall mountain?  
   Options: It gets smaller, because there is less air above you / It gets bigger, because you are nearer the sky / It stays the same everywhere  
   **Answer:** It gets smaller, because there is less air above you. *Why:* The higher you are, the less air is above you, so the push is smaller. At the top of Everest it is about a third of the push at sea level.
9. (mcq) Why can a pump at the top of a pipe not lift water from 15 metres down?  
   Options: The push of the air can hold up only about 10 metres of water / The pump is too small / Water is too heavy for any pump  
   **Answer:** The push of the air can hold up only about 10 metres of water. *Why:* The push of the air can lift water only about 10 metres at sea level. Deeper wells need the pump put down in the water.
10. (count) A card has an area of 80 square centimetres. About how many kilograms of air push on one side of it?  
   **Answer:** 80. *Why:* The air pushes with about 1 kilogram on each square centimetre. 80 times 1 is 80 kilograms.
11. (mcq) In a fair test with a hole in a bottle, which is the best way to see what the cap does?  
   Options: Use the same bottle and the same hole, and change only the cap / Change the cap and the size of the hole together / Use a new bottle for each test  
   **Answer:** Use the same bottle and the same hole, and change only the cap. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing at a time, so the change in the result must come from the cap.
12. (mcq) A sealed bottle is carried from the top of a hill down to the beach and it squashes. Which push is bigger on the bottle at the beach?  
   Options: The push of the air outside / The push of the air inside / They are the same  
   **Answer:** The push of the air outside. *Why:* The air at the beach pushes harder than the thin hill air that was shut in. The outside wins.

## Applications
- Drinking straws, droppers and syringes, which move liquid by lowering the push on one side.
- Vacuum cleaners, which lower the push inside so the air in the room pushes the dust in.
- Suction hooks, suction cups on tiles and the rubber cups that lift glass sheets in a factory.
- Pressure cookers, which raise the push inside so the water boils hotter and the food cooks faster.
- Barometers and weather stations, which watch the air's push to predict rain and storms.
- Hand pumps, such as the India Mark II, which work from the water up, below the 10-metre limit.
- Aeroplane cabins, kept at the push you would feel at about 2,000 metres so passengers are comfortable.
- Tyres, footballs and cycle pumps, which pack more air into a space so it pushes hard.
- The Ayrton fan, designed by the engineer Hertha Ayrton in 1915 to push poison gas out of trenches by moving air.

## Open questions
- **How exactly does a siphon work? Is it air pressure, or the pull of one side of the water on the other?** Everyone agrees on what a siphon does. Physics teachers and scientists still argue about the best explanation.
- **How do deep-sea animals keep their bodies working under a push of hundreds of times what we feel?** Scientists have found chemicals in deep-sea fish that seem to protect their proteins, but they do not yet know the whole story.
- **Is any space really empty, a perfect vacuum?** Even in the emptiest space, scientists measure tiny pushes and flickers of energy. What is really there is still being studied.
- **How do the tallest trees lift water more than 100 metres, when air can lift it only about 10?** Scientists think leaves pull water up in a chain, but the details are still debated and tested.
- **Can we forecast the weather perfectly if we measure the air pressure everywhere?** The air is made of billions of billions of particles, and tiny differences grow. Nobody knows how far ahead a forecast can ever be right.

## Connected maths
- A card is 12 centimetres long and 9 centimetres wide. What is its area? About how many kilograms of air push on one side of it? **108 square centimetres, and about 108 kilograms.**. 12 times 9 is 108 square centimetres. Air pushes with about 1 kilogram on each square centimetre, so the push on one side is about 108 kilograms. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
- The water in a glass 12 centimetres tall pushes down on the card with about 12 grams on each square centimetre. The air pushes up with about 1,000 grams on each square centimetre. About how many times bigger is the push of the air? **About 83 times bigger.**. 1,000 divided by 12 is about 83. The push of the air is about 83 times bigger than the push of the water, so the card stays on. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In the Bottle with a Hole, the water ran for 2 seconds with the cap on. With the cap off it was still running when you stopped counting at 30 seconds. At least how many times longer did it run with the cap off? **At least 15 times longer.**. 30 divided by 2 is 15. It ran at least 15 times longer, and probably much more, because it was still running when you stopped. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A village pump can lift water up to 7 metres. In the monsoon the water is 4 metres down. In summer it falls by 1 metre every 10 days. After how many days will the pump stop working? **After 30 days.**. The water must fall from 4 to 7 metres, which is 3 metres. At 1 metre every 10 days, 3 metres takes 3 times 10, which is 30 days. After that, the water is too deep for a pump at the top. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L4`)
- Look at the line graph of the air pressure readings. On which day was the pressure lowest, and by how many hectopascals did it fall between Monday and Thursday? **Thursday was lowest. It fell by 9 hectopascals.**. The line is lowest at Thursday, at 1,003. On Monday it was 1,012. 1,012 take away 1,003 is 9. A falling reading often goes with clouds and rain. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- A suction hook has a cup 4 centimetres wide. Its radius is 2 centimetres. Use area = 3 times radius times radius. What is the area, and what is the most that the air could hold, in kilograms? **The area is 12 square centimetres, and the air could hold at most about 12 kilograms.**. 2 times 2 is 4, and 4 times 3 is 12. With about 1 kilogram of push on each square centimetre, the most is about 12 kilograms. A real hook holds less. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- At sea level, the air can hold up 76 centimetres of mercury. On a hill where the push is three quarters as strong, how many centimetres of mercury could it hold up? **57 centimetres.**. A quarter of 76 is 19, so three quarters is 3 times 19, which is 57. A mercury barometer on that hill would stand at about 57 centimetres. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
