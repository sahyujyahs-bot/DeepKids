# Sorting living things: many good ways, and one that finds relatives
`sci.classification.sorting-living-things.L3`

**Summary.** A green chilli and a cucumber are both green, so a colour sort puts them together. Cut them open, and the chilli's seeds match a tomato's, not the cucumber's. There are many good ways to sort living things, and each one is right for some job. A cook, a farmer and a doctor each sort differently. But one question has a true answer that nobody chooses: which living things are relatives. Relatives share features because they came from the same ancestors. Scientists sort living things by these shared features, into groups inside groups, and people are in those groups too. The biggest groups have changed as scientists found new clues, as Carl Woese did in 1977.

**Intro.** *Put a green chilli, a cucumber and a tomato on a plate. Which two belong together? Most people say the chilli and the cucumber, because both are green. Now look at the tops. The chilli and the tomato both wear a small green cap. The cucumber does not. Which two really belong together?* Both answers are good sorts. Colour is a fine way to sort if you are making a green salad. But the caps are a clue to something else. Tomato and chilli plants are relatives, and the cucumber plant comes from a different family. In this unit you sort living things, people included, in many ways. Then you find out how to sort them by who is related to whom, using clues you can see and feel.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can sort the same living things in more than one way, and say the rule I used for each sort.
1. I can choose a sort that fits a job, and explain why a cook, a farmer and a scientist might sort the same plants differently.
2. I can say how people are similar and how they are different, and place people in groups inside groups: animal, backbone animal, mammal, primate.
3. I can find family clues in plants: the green cap and small flat seeds of the tomato family, and the square stems of the mint family.
4. I can explain that relatives share features because they came from the same ancestors, and that looks can mislead.
5. I can write a yes-or-no question that sorts living things, and use a key to name a leaf.
6. I can tell how Carl Woese found a new big group of living things, and say that scientists' biggest groups have changed over time.

## Terms
- **Sort**: To put things into groups by a rule. Scientists call sorting living things classification. *e.g.* Putting your fruit into 'has a stone inside' and 'has many small seeds' is a sort.
- **Classification**: The science of sorting living things into groups and naming the groups. *e.g.* Putting the cat, the tiger and the leopard in the cat family is classification.
- **Feature**: Something about a living thing that you can see, feel, smell or measure. *e.g.* A crow's feathers, a tulsi plant's smell and your height are all features.
- **Rule**: The question that decides which group a thing goes in. *e.g.* 'Does it have feathers?' is a rule. A crow goes in the 'yes' group and a cat in the 'no' group.
- **Similar**: Nearly the same, but not exactly. *e.g.* You and your cousin have similar hands. Both have five fingers, but their sizes differ.
- **Relative**: A living thing that shares ancestors with another. In science, plants and animals have relatives too. *e.g.* The tiger and the house cat are relatives. Both come from the same long-ago cat ancestors.
- **Ancestor**: A living thing from long ago that later living things came from: a parent, a grandparent, and so on, far back. *e.g.* Your grandmother's grandmother is one of your ancestors.
- **Inherit**: To get a feature from your parents, through the instructions in your body's cells. *e.g.* A kitten inherits its whiskers and claws from its parents.
- **Family (in science)**: A group of living things that are close relatives, such as the cat family or the tomato family. *e.g.* Tomato, brinjal, chilli and potato plants are all in the tomato family.
- **Calyx**: The ring of small green leaves, called sepals, that held a flower. On some fruits it stays on as a green cap. *e.g.* The green star on top of a tomato is its calyx.
- **Mammal**: An animal with hair, whose mother makes milk for her babies. *e.g.* Cats, cows, bats, whales and people are all mammals.
- **Primate**: The group of mammals with hands that grip, nails instead of claws, and eyes that face forward. Monkeys, apes and people are primates. *e.g.* A bonnet macaque on a temple wall in Tamil Nadu is a primate, and so are you.
- **Species**: One kind of living thing. Members of a species can usually have young together. *e.g.* All people are one species. Tigers are another species.
- **Key**: A list of yes-or-no questions that leads you, step by step, to the name of a living thing. *e.g.* 'Does the leaf smell strong? Yes. Is the stem square? Yes. It is tulsi.'
- **Venn diagram**: Two circles that overlap. Things that fit both rules go in the middle part. *e.g.* A bat goes in the middle of 'can fly' and 'has hair'.
- **Fungus**: A living thing that is neither a plant nor an animal. It feeds on dead or living things around it. Mushrooms, moulds and yeast are fungi. *e.g.* The green mould on old bread is a fungus.
- **Spore**: A tiny living cell that a fungus, a fern or a moss makes to start a new one. Spores are much smaller than seeds. *e.g.* A mushroom drops millions of spores from the thin flaps under its cap.
- **Bacteria**: Living things made of just one tiny cell, too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* Curd bacteria turn milk into curd.
- **Archaea**: Living things made of one tiny cell that look like bacteria but are very different inside. They are a big group of their own. *e.g.* Archaea live in hot springs, in the mud of rice fields and in a cow's stomach.
- **Kingdom**: One of the biggest groups of living things, such as the animals, the plants or the fungi. *e.g.* The crow, the ant and you are all in the animal kingdom.

## Explanations
### Sorting living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Sorting means putting living things into groups by a rule. The rule is a question about a feature, such as 'Does it have feathers?'

There are about two million kinds of living things with names, and many more without names. Nobody can remember them one by one. Sorting helps us find them, talk about them and compare them. A good rule is one that anyone can check, so two people get the same answer. 'Does it have six legs?' is a good rule. 'Is it pretty?' is not, because people disagree.
- e.g. A vegetable seller keeps leafy greens in one basket and roots in another, so they are easy to find.
- e.g. A doctor sorts snakes into those with dangerous bites and those without, because that decides the treatment.
- e.g. A bird book sorts birds by family, so you can find a new bird by its shape.
### More than one right way to sort · *defined*
**Definition:** The same living things can be sorted in many ways. Each sort is right if its rule is clear and checked carefully. Which sort is best depends on the job.

A cook sorts vegetables by how long they take to cook. A shopkeeper sorts them by price. A farmer sorts them by which pests attack them. All three sorts are correct, and they make different groups. A living thing belongs to many groups at once. A bat is in the 'can fly' group with crows, and in the 'has hair' group with cats. Neither group is wrong. When you sort, say your rule out loud, so others know which sort you made.
- e.g. Sort your family by height, and then by who likes rasam best. The groups change, but nobody changed.
- e.g. A crow and a mosquito are together in 'can fly', and apart in 'has a backbone'.
- e.g. A banana is a fruit to a cook, and to a botanist it is also a close relative of ginger and turmeric plants.
### People: similar and different · *defined*
**Definition:** All people are one species. We share the same body plan, and we differ in many small features, such as height, hair, voice and fingerprints.

Every person has a backbone, two arms, two legs, two eyes, a heart and lungs. Every person breathes, eats, grows and sleeps. These are the ways people are similar. People also differ. Some differences we are born with, such as the colour of our eyes. Some come from how we live, such as our languages and how far we can run. You can sort people by any of these features. Each feature makes different groups. No sort makes some people better than others. A sort only tells you how people answered one question.
- e.g. Count the fingers of everyone at home. Everyone has the same number, but the hands are different sizes.
- e.g. Sort your family by which hand they write with. Then sort them by the month they were born in.
- e.g. Two sisters can share a birthday month and still be in different groups for height.
### Groups inside groups · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists put living things into groups inside bigger groups. A small group shares many features. A big group shares only a few.

You are a living thing. Inside that big group, you are an animal. Inside the animals, you are an animal with a backbone. Inside those, you are a mammal, with hair and milk for babies. Inside the mammals, you are a primate, with gripping hands and nails. Inside the primates, you are an ape, with no tail. Each group sits inside the one before, like steel tiffin boxes stacked inside each other. A cat shares your first four boxes, but not the primate box. A macaque shares five, but it has a tail, so it is not an ape.
- e.g. A crow is a living thing, an animal and a backbone animal, but not a mammal.
- e.g. A tiger and a house cat share every box up to the cat family.
- e.g. A neem tree shares only the first box with you: living thing.
### Why relatives look alike · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things inherit features from their parents. So relatives, who share ancestors, share many features.

A kitten has whiskers because its parents had whiskers. Its parents got them from their parents, and so on, far back. Tigers, leopards and house cats all came from the same long-ago cat ancestors. So they all have whiskers, claws they can pull in, and rough tongues. Plants work the same way. Tomato, brinjal and chilli plants came from the same ancestor plants. So their fruits share a green cap and small flat seeds. When scientists sort by shared features like these, they find relatives. This sort has a true answer, because it comes from what really happened long ago.
- e.g. A tiger and a house cat both pull in their claws when they walk.
- e.g. Tulsi, mint and karpooravalli all have square stems and strong smells. They are in the mint family.
- e.g. Rice, wheat, ragi, maize, sugarcane and bamboo are all in the grass family. Their leaves are long and narrow, with veins running side by side.
### Family clues in the kitchen · *defined*
**Definition:** Botanists, scientists who study plants, sort plants into families. Many vegetables in an Indian kitchen belong to just a few families.

The tomato family includes tomato, brinjal, chilli, capsicum and potato. Their fruits keep a green cap, the calyx, and have small, flat, round seeds. The gourd family includes cucumber, pumpkin, bottle gourd, bitter gourd and watermelon. Their fruits have no green cap, and their seeds are bigger, flat and oval. The bean family includes moong, chana, rajma, peanut and methi. Their seeds grow in pods. The grass family includes rice, wheat, ragi and maize. Botanists use the flowers most of all, because flowers carry the clearest family clues. Caps and seeds are clues you can see in the kitchen.
- e.g. A potato has no cap, because it is a stem under the ground. But potato plants have flowers and small green fruits like tiny tomatoes.
- e.g. A pea pod and a moong pod are built the same way, with seeds in a row inside.
- e.g. Watermelon seeds are flat and oval, like cucumber seeds, though the fruits look very different.
### Looks can mislead · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things that are not close relatives can look alike, because they have solved the same problem in the same way.

A crow, a bat and a butterfly all have wings. Each of them had the same problem: how to reach food, and escape danger, through the air. Wings help each of them do that. But their wings are built differently. A crow's wing is an arm covered in feathers. A bat's wing is skin stretched between long fingers. A butterfly's wing is a thin sheet with no bones at all. These wings grew separately in three different families. So 'has wings' is a useful sort for some jobs, but it does not find relatives. To find relatives, scientists look at many features, especially ones that have no clear job, and now at DNA.
- e.g. A bat feeds its babies milk and has fur, so it is a mammal, like a cat.
- e.g. A dolphin has fins and lives in the sea like a fish, but it breathes air and feeds its babies milk. It is a mammal.
- e.g. A green chilli and a cucumber are both green, but they are in different families.
### Neither plant nor animal: fungi · *defined*
**Definition:** Fungi, such as mushrooms, moulds and yeast, are a kingdom of their own. They are neither plants nor animals.

For a long time, people sorted mushrooms with plants, because they grow in soil and do not walk. But a mushroom has no green leaves and cannot make food from sunlight. It feeds by soaking up food from the dead wood, leaves or soil around it. It makes spores, not seeds. Most of a fungus is a web of thin threads, hidden in the soil or wood. The mushroom is only the part that makes spores. When scientists compared their DNA, they found that fungi are closer relatives of animals than of plants.
- e.g. The mushrooms sold in Indian markets, such as button mushrooms and oyster mushrooms, are fungi.
- e.g. Yeast, which makes dosa batter and bread dough rise, is a fungus made of one cell.
- e.g. The white fluff on an old orange is a mould, which is a fungus.
### The biggest groups have changed · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists have changed the biggest groups of living things many times, as they found new clues. They still argue about them today.

In 1735, Carl Linnaeus, a Swedish scientist, sorted living things into two kingdoms: plants and animals. Microscopes then showed tiny one-celled living things that fit neither. In 1969, Robert Whittaker, an American ecologist, proposed five kingdoms: animals, plants, fungi, one-celled living things with a nucleus, and bacteria. Then in 1977 Carl Woese found that some 'bacteria' were very different inside. They are now called archaea. In 1990 he proposed three domains, groups even bigger than kingdoms: bacteria, archaea, and everything with a nucleus in its cells, including us. Newer studies have started another argument.
- e.g. Many textbooks still teach five kingdoms. Many scientists use three domains.
- e.g. Archaea live in the mud of rice fields and in a cow's stomach, where they make the gas methane.
- e.g. Plants, animals and fungi are all in the same domain, because their cells all have a nucleus.
- **Most biologists today:** Life has three domains: bacteria, archaea, and living things whose cells have a nucleus. This follows Woese's evidence.
- **Some newer studies of archaea, since about 2015:** Cells with a nucleus seem to have grown out of one branch of the archaea. If so, there are really two domains, bacteria and archaea, and we are a branch inside the archaea.
- **Some biologists who follow Ernst Mayr:** Sorting should also count how different living things look and live, not only their family tree. Mayr argued in 1998 that all one-celled living things without a nucleus belong together.
### What counts as one kind? · *debated*
**Definition:** A species is one kind of living thing. There is no single test for a species that works for every living thing.

The usual rule is that members of a species can have young together, and those young can also have young. Lions and tigers are two species. In zoos, a lion and a tiger have had cubs, called ligers. But lions and tigers do not mate in the wild. So are they one kind or two? Most scientists say two. For bacteria, the rule does not work at all, because bacteria do not mate to have young. They split in two. So scientists who study bacteria use other tests, such as how alike their DNA is.
- e.g. A horse and a donkey can have a mule, but mules almost never have young of their own. So horses and donkeys are two species.
- e.g. All dogs, from a tiny Chihuahua to a Rajapalayam hound, are one species.
- e.g. Curd bacteria are sorted into kinds by their DNA, not by who they mate with.
- **Ernst Mayr and many zoologists:** A species is a group that has young together in the wild, and does not mix with other groups.
- **Many scientists who study bacteria and plants:** That rule fails for living things that do not mate, and for plants that mix easily. A species is better defined by shared DNA and shared features.
### Every living thing senses and decides · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living thing senses the world around it and responds, from a person to a neem tree to a curd bacterium.

Whatever rule you use, one feature is in every group. A crow notices a cat and flies off. A tulsi plant senses light and turns its leaves towards it. A mushroom's threads sense food in the soil and grow towards it. A bacterium senses sugar and swims towards it. Each of them senses, remembers and decides in its own way, with its own body. Some scientists call the responses of plants and bacteria automatic. Either way, sensing and responding is something all living things share.
- e.g. A touch-me-not plant folds its leaves when touched.
- e.g. Ants change their path when you put your finger on their trail.
- e.g. A money plant on a windowsill grows towards the light.

## Mostly agreed
- Living things can be sorted in many correct ways. The best sort depends on the job. *How we know:* Cooks, farmers, doctors and scientists each use different sorts of the same living things, and each sort works for its job.
- All living things alive today are relatives. They all come from early cells that lived over 3.5 billion years ago. *How we know:* Every living thing uses the same kind of DNA instructions, and comparing DNA shows how they branch from shared ancestors.
- Relatives share features because they inherited them from shared ancestors. *How we know:* Comparing bodies, fossils and DNA gives the same family trees, again and again.
- People are animals: mammals and primates. Our closest living relatives are chimpanzees and bonobos. *How we know:* Our bodies share the features of mammals and apes, and our DNA is closer to theirs than to any other animal's.
- Fungi are a kingdom of their own, more closely related to animals than to plants. *How we know:* Fungi do not make food from sunlight, and DNA comparisons place them nearer to animals.
- Archaea are a separate big group of one-celled living things, not a kind of bacteria. *How we know:* Carl Woese and George Fox showed this in 1977 by comparing a molecule every cell has, and many later studies agree.

## How we found out
**Carl Woese, an American microbiologist, with George Fox, and later Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis, 1977 (archaea found) and 1990 (three domains proposed).** For most of the 1900s, scientists sorted every living thing into two groups. Some had cells with a nucleus, a small packet that holds the cell's DNA. Animals, plants and fungi were in this group. The rest had no nucleus, and they were all called bacteria. Carl Woese, an American scientist, wanted a better clue to who is related to whom. Every cell has a molecule called ribosomal RNA, which it uses to build proteins. Woese compared this molecule in many tiny living things. In 1977, he and George Fox found that some 'bacteria' that make methane gas were as different from other bacteria as bacteria are from us. They called them archaebacteria. Later Woese renamed them archaea, and in 1990 he proposed three domains of life. Many biologists did not accept it at first. The famous biologist Ernst Mayr was still arguing against it in 1998. But studies of many more archaea agreed with Woese. Today they live in textbooks, and in the mud of rice fields and in our own guts.  
*What people believed before:* That every living thing without a nucleus is a kind of bacteria. By comparing ribosomal RNA, Woese and Fox showed that archaea are as different from bacteria as bacteria are from animals and plants.

## Challenging perspectives
- **There is one right way to sort living things.** (A common idea, often from learning one sort at school; *rejected*)  
  For: Scientists do agree on family trees for most living things.  
  Against: Many sorts are correct for different jobs. Even the scientists' family tree sort has big groups that people still argue about.
- **Things that look alike must be close relatives.** (A natural guess, and how most people sorted living things for centuries; *rejected*)  
  For: Close relatives often do look alike, like tigers and house cats.  
  Against: Bats, crows and butterflies all have wings, but they are not close relatives. A dolphin looks like a fish but is a mammal.
- **Mushrooms are plants.** (Scientists for many centuries, including Linnaeus; *rejected*)  
  For: Mushrooms grow in soil and stay in one place, like plants.  
  Against: Mushrooms have no green leaves and do not make food from sunlight. DNA shows fungi are closer to animals.
- **There are three domains of life.** (Carl Woese, 1990, and most textbooks today; *debated*)  
  For: Bacteria, archaea and cells with a nucleus differ deeply inside, in the molecule Woese compared.  
  Against: Some newer studies suggest cells with a nucleus grew out of the archaea, which would make two domains.
- **People are not animals.** (A common way of speaking, and some old ways of sorting; *rejected*)  
  For: People talk, write, cook and build in ways no other animal does.  
  Against: People breathe, eat, grow, have hair and feed babies milk, like other mammals. Every scientific sort puts people with the apes.
- **Sorting people into groups shows that some groups are better.** (An old and harmful idea, used to treat people unfairly; *rejected*)  
  For: Nothing in science supports it.  
  Against: A sort shows only how people answered one question, such as height or eye colour. People are one species, and any two people are almost the same in their DNA.

## Roots
We have been sorting living things in many ways, and finding which ones are relatives. Now we ask what sorting tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Sorted by colour, the green chilli went with the cucumber. Sorted by caps and seeds, it went with the tomato and the brinjal. Those three plants share these features because they came from the same ancestor plants. So a living thing's body carries clues to its family, and careful sorting can find them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think every sort of living things is a choice we make, or that things which look alike must belong together. Many sorts are useful choices. But which living things are relatives was settled long ago, by who came from whom, and their bodies still show it.  
*Evidence:* In the vegetable test, the colour sort and the cap-and-seed sort made different groups. The cap and the seeds put the chilli with the tomato and the brinjal. Botanists, comparing flowers and DNA, put these three in one family too.

- *who am i:* You are a living thing, an animal, a mammal and a primate, all at once. Your body carries the clues: a backbone, hair, gripping hands.
- *what is life:* Every living thing came from parents, and they came from theirs. So all of life is one family tree.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of relatives. A neem tree, a crow, a mushroom and you share ancestors, very long ago.

Long ago there were only single cells. Over billions of years, their young changed a little in each generation, and the branches split again and again. Today there are millions of kinds of living things, from bacteria to banyan trees. When we sort by shared features, we trace those branches back towards where they join. One beginning became many kinds, and each kind still carries clues to where it came from.

- **Purananuru 192, a Tamil Sangam poem by Kaniyan Pungundranar:** Every town is our town. Everyone is our kin. (The tree of life shows that every living thing is a relative of every other. The poem says it about people. Biology finds it true of all life.)
- **Maha Upanishad 6.71 to 6.72 (the verse is also in the Hitopadesha):** 'This one is mine, that one is a stranger.' So people with small hearts count. For those with generous hearts, the whole world is one family: vasudhaiva kutumbakam. (We can sort the world into 'mine' and 'not mine' in many ways. The family tree of life is one sort nobody chose, and it puts everything in one family.)
- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), end of chapter 4:** The likenesses between all living things in one group have sometimes been pictured as a great tree. Its green twigs are the living kinds of today, and its branches the kinds that came before. (When you found that the chilli belongs with the tomato, you found two twigs on the same branch of Darwin's tree.)
- Science (settled): All living things alive today share ancestors. Their DNA, the instructions in every cell, is written in the same chemical code.
- Science (debated): How many domains of life there are, two or three, is still being argued.
- Science (open): Nobody knows how many kinds of living things there are. One estimate says about 8.7 million kinds of plants, animals and fungi.
- Other view, Many biologists: Being relatives is a fact about history, not a lesson about kindness. A tiger and a deer are relatives, and the tiger still hunts the deer. Whether we should treat all life as family is a question for people to decide, not something the family tree tells us.
- Practice, Count your groups (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. Feel your breath going in and out. You are a living thing. Now feel the bumps of your backbone. You are a backbone animal. Touch the hair on your arm. You are a mammal. Press your fingertips together. You are a primate. Now think of a crow outside, a tulsi plant and an ant. Somewhere far back, you share ancestors with each of them. Take three slow breaths for your relatives.
- Ask: If every living thing is a relative of every other, where does your family end?
- Ask: The chilli was in the green group and the tomato family at the same time. Which group is it really in, or is that the wrong question?
- Ask: Is there anything in the world that is not related to you in some way?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A bat is in the 'can fly' group and the 'has hair' group. Which group is the bat really in? (hint: Is a group something a bat has, or something we make by asking a question?)
- *big:* If every living thing is a relative of every other, how far back would you need to go to find the ancestor you share with a neem tree? (hint: Scientists think it lived well over a billion years ago, and was made of one cell.)
- *deep:* Is there a sort that nobody chose? (hint: Who chose that tomato and chilli are relatives? Did anyone?)

- *deep:* Your hand and a bat's wing have the same bones in them. What could that tell us? (hint: Feel the bones in your fingers. A bat's wing is held up by long finger bones.)
- *deep:* Where does a living thing end and the next kind begin, if ligers can be born? (hint: Is a 'kind' a fence in nature, or a line we draw in a smooth change?)

## Story: The Terrace Garden Sickness
*Bacterial wilt is real. It is a soil disease that attacks tomato, brinjal, chilli and potato plants, and Indian farmers avoid planting these crops one after another in the same soil. Kavya, her grandfather and this garden are made up.*

Kavya lived on the fourth floor of a building in Chennai. On the terrace, she and her grandfather, Thatha, kept a garden in twelve grow bags. Grow bags are thick cloth bags full of soil. They are like flower pots that you can fold away.

Kavya was nine, and she liked things in order. So she had sorted her seed packets into three boxes by the colour of the vegetable. The red box held tomato seeds. The green box held chilli, cucumber and bottle gourd seeds. The purple box held brinjal seeds.

That summer, the tomato plants grew tall. Small green tomatoes hung from them like marbles. Then, one hot afternoon, Kavya ran up to water them and stopped. Two tomato plants were drooping. Their leaves hung down, soft and floppy, though they were still green.

'They are thirsty,' she said, and she gave them a full mug of water each.

The next morning they were worse. The soil in the bags was wet, but the plants hung over like wet cloth on a line. By the end of the week, all four tomato plants had wilted and died.

Thatha pulled one out gently and cut its stem across with his knife. Inside, the stem was brown where it should have been pale green. 'This is a sickness in the soil,' he said. 'Tiny living things got into the plant and blocked the tubes that carry water. The leaves were wet at the roots and dry at the top.'

Kavya was sad, but she had a plan. 'The bags are empty now,' she said. 'I will plant my brinjal seeds in them.'

Thatha raised his eyebrows. 'Before you plant,' he said, 'look at your other bags.'

Kavya walked along the row. Next to the tomato bags stood her two chilli plants. One of them was drooping too, just like the tomatoes had. On the other side, the cucumber vine was climbing happily up its string. Its leaves were fat and green.

'That is strange,' said Kavya. 'The chilli and the cucumber are both green vegetables. They are in the same box. Why is one sick and the other one fine?'

'Good question,' said Thatha. 'Maybe the colour box is not the right box for this problem.'

Kavya went down to the kitchen and came back with a basket. In it were a tomato, a brinjal, a green chilli and a cucumber. She lined them up on the terrace wall.

First she looked at the tops. The tomato had a small green star where its stalk joined it. The brinjal had a big, spiky green cap that hugged its top. The chilli had a little green cup. The cucumber had no cap at all, only a plain stalk.

'Three of them have caps,' she said slowly. 'Tomato, brinjal and chilli.'

Thatha cut each one open on a steel plate. Kavya bent close. The tomato's seeds were small, flat and round, sitting in jelly. The brinjal's seeds were small, flat and round too, tucked into the white flesh. The chilli's seeds were small, flat and round, stuck to a pale core in the middle. The cucumber's seeds were different. They were longer, oval and pointed at one end.

'Tomato, brinjal and chilli match,' Kavya said. 'The caps match and the seeds match. The cucumber is the odd one.'

She looked at the row of grow bags. The tomatoes had died. The chilli next to them was sick. The cucumber was fine.

'Thatha,' she said, 'is the sickness choosing the plants with caps?'

Thatha smiled. 'Why do you think that would be?'

Kavya thought hard. 'Maybe they are a family,' she said. 'Like how Amma and Chithi have the same nose, because they had the same parents. If tomato, brinjal and chilli are relatives, maybe their bodies are alike inside too. Then a sickness that gets into one could get into the others.'

'That is exactly what the farmers in my village said,' Thatha told her. 'Tomato, brinjal, chilli and potato are one family. The same soil sickness attacks all of them. So we never planted brinjal where the tomatoes had died. We planted something from another family.'

Kavya looked at her purple box of brinjal seeds. If she had planted them in the old bags, they might have wilted too.

That evening she emptied her three colour boxes onto the floor. She made new boxes and wrote new labels. 'Tomato family: tomato, brinjal, chilli.' 'Gourd family: cucumber, bottle gourd.' Then she made a chart for the grow bags. Each bag had a row for every season. No bag would grow the same family twice in a row.

'You threw away your colour boxes?' asked Thatha.

'No,' said Kavya. 'The colours are still good for making a rainbow salad. But for the garden, I need the families.'

In the old tomato bags, she planted cucumber seeds. The brinjal seeds went into bags of fresh soil at the far end of the terrace. A month later, the cucumber vines in the old bags were climbing their strings, and the brinjal plants stood straight and green.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Kavya first sorted by colour, which is a fine sort for a salad. But the sickness did not follow colour. It followed family. Tomato, brinjal and chilli plants are close relatives, so their bodies are alike in ways we cannot see, and one soil disease can attack them all. The green caps and the small, flat, round seeds were clues to that family. Kavya found them by looking closely and comparing, the way botanists do. Neither sort was wrong. Each was right for a different job.

- Ask: Why did Kavya's colour boxes not help her with the sick plants?
- Ask: Which two clues showed that tomato, brinjal and chilli are relatives?
- Ask: Why did Thatha tell her to look at her other bags before planting?
- Ask: Kavya kept her colour sort for salads. When is a colour sort useful?
- Ask: Can you think of another time when the same things need to be sorted in two different ways?

## Experiments
### e1 · Family Clues in the Vegetable Basket (35 min)
- **question:** If we sort vegetables by their colour, and then by their caps and seeds, will we get the same groups?
- **predict:** Guess first. Look at the tomato, brinjal, green chilli, cucumber and capsicum. Which ones do you think have the same kind of seeds inside? Will the green ones match each other? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Look at every vegetable in the same way: the top first, then the seeds. Cut each one across its middle, so you can compare the insides. Put the seeds from each on white paper, so they are easy to see. Look at least five seeds from each, because one seed can be odd.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the cutting. Chilli juice burns the eyes, so do not touch your eyes after touching the chilli. Wash your hands with soap after the chilli. Cook and eat the vegetables afterwards, so nothing is wasted.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the two sorts as two pairs of circles: the colour sort and the cap-and-seed sort. Write the names in each circle.
- **results table:** Vegetable | Colour | Green cap at the top? (yes or no) | Seed shape and size | Drawing of one seed
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the five whole vegetables on the plate. 2. Ask your child to sort them into groups by colour.  
  *Parent says:* Let's start with an easy sort. Put these vegetables into groups by their colour.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the five vegetables. Put them in groups by colour. Which ones go together?
  - Ask: Which group has the most vegetables? (good: The green group: the chilli, the cucumber and the capsicum.)
  - Note: Your child will most likely make a green group (chilli, cucumber, capsicum), a red tomato and a purple brinjal. Write the colour groups down before you go on.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask for a guess and a reason. 2. Write the guess at the top of the page.  
  *Parent says:* Now guess. When we cut them open, which ones will have the same kind of seeds? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Which ones will have the same seeds inside? Say why you think so.
  - Note: Any guess is fine. Many children guess that the green ones will match, because they look alike. Don't hint.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the top of each vegetable, where the stalk joins. 2. Fill in the 'green cap' column.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the top of each one, where the stalk joins. Do you see a little green cap, like a star or a cup? This cap is called the calyx. It is made of the small green leaves that once held the flower.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the top of each one. Is there a green cap, like a little star? Write yes or no.
  - Ask: Which ones have a green cap? (good: The tomato, the brinjal, the chilli and the capsicum.; Not the cucumber.)
  - Note: Tomato, brinjal, chilli and capsicum all keep a green calyx. A cucumber has only a stalk. Pumpkin and bottle gourd slices usually have no stalk left, so mark them 'cannot see' if needed.
- **s4 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut each vegetable across its middle. 2. Put the halves on the plate. 3. Help your child pick out five seeds from each and put them on a paper square labelled with the vegetable's name.  
  *Parent says:* I'll cut each one across the middle. You pick out five seeds from each and put them on its paper.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts each one open. You pick out five seeds from each. Put them on the paper with its name.
  - Note: Cut the chilli last, and wash the knife after it. Tomato seeds sit in jelly, so pat them on a tissue. A tender cucumber has soft white seeds. They are still easy to see. If you have a pumpkin slice, its seeds are big and flat.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the seeds with the magnifying glass or the phone camera. 2. Fill in the seed column and draw one seed of each.  
  *Parent says:* Look closely. What shape is each seed? How big is it? Which seeds look like each other?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the seeds closely. What shape are they? How big? Which ones look the same? Draw one of each.
  - Note: Tomato, brinjal, chilli and capsicum seeds are small, flat and round, about 3 to 5 millimetres across. Cucumber, pumpkin and bottle gourd seeds are flat, oval and pointed at one end, and bigger.
  - Check: Which seeds look most like the tomato's seeds? → **The chilli's and the brinjal's seeds**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to sort the vegetables again, this time by cap and seeds. 2. Compare with the colour sort you wrote down.  
  *Parent says:* Now sort them again, by the caps and the seeds. Is it the same as the colour sort?  
  *Child mode:* Sort them again. Use the caps and the seeds this time. Are the groups the same as before?
  - Ask: Which vegetable changed groups? (good: The green chilli. It was with the cucumber, and now it is with the tomato and the brinjal.; The capsicum moved too.)
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: colour and seeds make different groups. What I saw: the chilli and the cucumber are both green, but the chilli has a green cap and small round seeds like the tomato and the brinjal. The cucumber has no cap and long seeds. Why: tomato, brinjal and chilli may be relatives. Now explain that they are. Botanists call them the tomato family, and the cucumber is in the gourd family.
### e2 · Square Stems: find the mint family (25 min)
- **question:** Which of our kitchen herbs have square stems like tulsi, and do those plants share other features too?
- **predict:** Guess first. Roll a tulsi stem between your finger and thumb. Which of the other herbs do you think will feel the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Test every stem the same way: roll a piece about as long as your finger, near the top of the plant, between your finger and thumb. Do it with your eyes closed, so you judge by feel, not by looks. Test two stems of each plant, because one stem can be bent or damaged.
- **safety:** Use only plants you know are food. Wash your hands afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a stem cut across: a square for square stems and a circle for round ones.
- **results table:** Plant | Stem shape (square or round) | Leaves in pairs facing each other? (yes or no) | Strong smell when rubbed? (yes or no)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out one sprig of each herb. 2. Let your child roll the tulsi stem between finger and thumb. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Roll this tulsi stem slowly between your finger and thumb. What does it feel like? Which of the others do you think will feel the same? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Roll the tulsi stem between your finger and thumb. What shape is it? Guess which other stems feel the same. Say why.
  - Note: A tulsi stem feels square, with four flat sides and four edges. Write down your child's guess.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cover your child's eyes with the cloth. 2. Hand them one stem at a time, in a mixed-up order. 3. They say 'square' or 'round'. 4. You write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Now I'll cover your eyes, so only your fingers decide. Square or round?  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Feel each stem. Is it square or round? Say it out loud.
  - Note: Mint and karpooravalli stems are square, like tulsi. Coriander, curry leaf and spinach stems are round. Give each plant twice, in a different order, to be sure.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take off the cloth. 2. Look at how the leaves join each stem. 3. Write yes if they grow in pairs facing each other.  
  *Parent says:* Now look at where the leaves join the stem. Do they grow in pairs, one on each side, facing each other?  
  *Child mode:* Look where the leaves join the stem. Are they in pairs, facing each other? Write yes or no.
  - Note: Tulsi, mint and karpooravalli leaves grow in pairs facing each other. Each pair is turned a quarter turn from the pair below. Coriander and curry leaves come off one at a time.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rub one leaf of each plant between your fingers. 2. Smell your fingers. 3. Write yes or no for a strong smell.  
  *Parent says:* Rub a leaf and smell your fingers. Is the smell strong?  
  *Child mode:* Rub a leaf. Smell your fingers. Is the smell strong? Write yes or no.
  - Note: Coriander and curry leaves also smell strong. That is a good lesson: one clue alone can mislead. Three clues together point to a family.
  - Check: Which plants had square stems, leaves in pairs and a strong smell? → **Tulsi and mint**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: tulsi and mint are in the same family. What I saw: both have square stems, leaves in pairs facing each other, and a strong smell. Coriander smells strong but has a round stem. Why: relatives share features they inherited. Botanists call it the mint family. It has thousands of kinds, and most of them have square stems.
### e3 · Mushroom Prints: does a mushroom make seeds? (20 min, 2 days)
- **question:** A plant makes seeds. Does a mushroom make seeds too, or something else?
- **predict:** Guess first. If we leave a mushroom cap face down on paper overnight, will anything come out of it? What will it look like? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two caps from the same packet. Put one on white paper and one on dark paper, so the print shows up whatever its colour. Cover both with a bowl, so a breeze cannot blow the spores away.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the stalks. Never eat a mushroom you find growing outside. Wash your hands after touching the mushrooms. Throw the used caps away afterwards.
- **record:** Draw the print. Write what colour it is. Draw a moong seed next to it at the right size.
- **results table:** Cap | Paper colour | What you saw in the morning | Colour of the print
- **schedule:** day 1: Cut the stalks off two mushroom caps. Put them face down on white and dark paper, and cover them. (10 min); day 2: Lift the caps and look at the prints. Compare a spore print with a seed. (10 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn a mushroom over. 2. Look at the thin flaps under the cap, called gills, with the magnifying glass. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Look under the cap. These thin flaps are called gills. If we leave the cap on paper overnight, will anything come out? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look under the mushroom cap. The thin flaps are called gills. Guess. Will anything come out overnight? Say why.
  - Note: Button mushrooms that are still closed underneath may not give a print. Choose ones where the cap has opened and the gills show. Older brown gills work best. Oyster mushrooms also work well.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut the stalk off level with the cap. 2. Put one cap, gills down, on white paper and one on dark paper. 3. Cover each with a bowl. 4. Leave them overnight in a still place.  
  *Parent says:* I'll cut off the stalks. You put each cap face down on the paper, and cover it with a bowl.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts off the stalks. Put each cap face down on the paper. Cover it with a bowl. Leave it till morning.
  - Wait: Leave the caps covered overnight, about 8 to 12 hours.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Next morning, lift the bowls and the caps gently. 2. Look at the paper.  
  *Parent says:* Let's lift the caps very gently. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Lift the bowl. Lift the cap very gently. What is on the paper?
  - Note: Button mushrooms usually give a dark brown print, easier to see on white paper. Oyster mushrooms give a pale print, easier to see on dark paper. If there is no print, the gills were still closed. Try again with a more open cap.
  - Check: The mushroom left a print made of fine powder in lines. What is the powder? → **Millions of spores from the gills**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a moong seed next to the print and compare.  
  *Parent says:* Let's put a moong seed next to the print. Can you see one single spore?  
  *Child mode:* Put a moong seed next to the print. Can you see one spore by itself?
  - Ask: How is a spore different from a seed? (good: A spore is far too small to see alone.; A seed is big and has a baby plant inside.)
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: a mushroom does not make seeds. It makes spores. What I saw: a print of fine powder in lines like the gills. I could not see a single spore. Why: mushrooms are fungi, not plants. Fungi start new fungi from spores.

### Sorting living things: core (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meena sorts a crow, a pigeon and a bat into 'can fly'. Which rule would put the bat in a different group from the birds?  
   Options: Lives in the city / Has hair / Has two eyes / Is alive  
   **Answer:** Has hair. *Why:* A bat has hair, and the crow and pigeon have feathers. The other rules put all three together.
2. (mcq) Which of these is a good sorting question that anyone can check?  
   Options: Is it cute? / Does it have six legs? / Is it my favourite? / Is it a good animal?  
   **Answer:** Does it have six legs?. *Why:* Anyone can count legs and get the same answer. Cute, favourite and good change from person to person.
3. (truefalse) There is only one right way to sort living things.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. There are many right ways. Each sort is right for some job, such as cooking, farming or finding relatives.
4. (truefalse) A bat can belong to the 'can fly' group and the 'has hair' group at the same time.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A living thing belongs to many groups at once. Each group comes from a different question.
5. (sort) Sort these by the green cap at the top. Which have a green cap?  
   **Answer:** tomato → Green cap; brinjal → Green cap; green chilli → Green cap; cucumber → No green cap; capsicum → Green cap; pumpkin → No green cap. *Why:* Tomato, brinjal, chilli and capsicum are in the tomato family, and they all keep a green calyx. Cucumber and pumpkin are in the gourd family.
6. (mcq) Arjun cuts open a tomato, a brinjal and a cucumber. Which one has seeds that look different from the others?  
   Options: Tomato / Brinjal / Cucumber  
   **Answer:** Cucumber. *Why:* Tomato and brinjal seeds are small, flat and round. Cucumber seeds are longer and oval, with a pointed end.
7. (fill) The green star on top of a tomato is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['calyx']. *Why:* The calyx is the ring of small green leaves that once held the flower.
8. (mcq) Which feature do tulsi and mint share?  
   Options: A round stem / A square stem / No smell / Leaves made of many leaflets  
   **Answer:** A square stem. *Why:* Tulsi and mint both have square stems, leaves in pairs and a strong smell. They are in the mint family.
9. (truefalse) People are animals.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. People breathe, eat, grow and move, have backbones and hair, and mothers make milk for babies. People are mammals and primates.
10. (order) Put these groups in order, from the biggest group to the smallest: mammal, living thing, primate, animal, backbone animal.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Each group sits inside the one before. Every primate is a mammal, every mammal has a backbone, every backbone animal is an animal, and every animal is a living thing.
11. (mcq) A crow has a backbone and feathers. Which group is it NOT in?  
   Options: Living thing / Animal / Backbone animal / Mammal  
   **Answer:** Mammal. *Why:* A crow is a bird. It has feathers, not hair, so it is not a mammal.
12. (match) Match each food to its plant family.  
   **Answer:** Rice → grass family; Moong → bean family; Brinjal → tomato family; Pumpkin → gourd family. *Why:* Rice is a grass, moong grows in pods, brinjal keeps a green cap like the tomato, and pumpkin grows on a vine like the cucumber.
13. (truefalse) A mushroom is a kind of plant.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A mushroom is a fungus. It has no green leaves, cannot make food from sunlight, and makes spores, not seeds.
14. (mcq) Why do a tiger and a house cat look alike?  
   Options: They live in the same places / They came from the same cat ancestors / They eat the same food / Someone painted them alike  
   **Answer:** They came from the same cat ancestors. *Why:* Tigers and house cats are relatives. They inherited whiskers, claws they can pull in and rough tongues from the same long-ago ancestors.
15. (draw) Draw two overlapping circles. Label one 'can fly' and one 'has hair'. Put a crow, a cat and a bat in the right places.  
   **Answer:** Crow in 'can fly' only, cat in 'has hair' only, bat in the middle.. *Why:* The bat can fly and has hair, so it goes where the circles overlap. This drawing is called a Venn diagram.
16. (fill) A living thing from long ago that later living things came from is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ancestor']. *Why:* Your grandmother's grandmother is one of your ancestors. All living things share very old ancestors.

## Applications
- Farmers change crop families from season to season, so soil diseases and pests that attack one family die out.
- Doctors sort snakes by their bites, so the right medicine, called antivenom, is given after a snakebite.
- Seed banks and botanical gardens sort seeds and plants by family, so scientists can find relatives of crops that resist disease.
- Plant breeders cross a crop with its wild relatives to give it new features, such as resistance to drought.
- Doctors sort bacteria into kinds, to choose the medicine that works against each one.
- Museums sort their collections of animals and plants so that any specimen can be found again.
- Libraries, shops and kitchens use the same idea: groups inside groups make things quick to find.
- Bird watchers and apps like iNaturalist use keys and families to name what people photograph.

## Open questions
- **How many kinds of living things are there on Earth?** About two million have names. One well-known estimate from 2011 says there may be about 8.7 million kinds of plants, animals and fungi, and there may be far more kinds of bacteria and archaea. Nobody knows.
- **Are there two domains of life, or three?** Some newer studies suggest that cells with a nucleus grew out of the archaea. Scientists are still collecting evidence.
- **What did the very first living thing look like?** It lived over 3.5 billion years ago and left no clear fossil. Scientists have ideas but no agreed answer.
- **Where exactly does one species end and the next begin?** Living things change slowly over many generations, and there is no single test for a species that works for all life.
- **How do the bodies of relatives stay so alike while each family changes?** Scientists understand a lot about how genes build bodies, but much about how features are kept or changed is still being worked out.

## Connected maths
- In the vegetable test, Kavya looked at 5 seeds from each of 5 vegetables. How many seeds did she look at altogether? **25 seeds.**. There were 5 vegetables, with 5 seeds each. 5 groups of 5 is 25. You can count in fives: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- Arjun has 12 living-thing cards. 7 can fly and 6 have hair. Only the bat card is in both groups. How many cards are in neither group? **0 cards.**. 7 can fly and 6 have hair, which makes 13. But the bat was counted twice, so there are 13 - 1 = 12 different cards in the circles. All 12 cards are in at least one group, so 12 - 12 = 0 are in neither. (queues `math.logic.sets-and-venn.L2`)
- In the people table, 6 people were measured. 4 were taller than 120 cm. 5 wrote with their right hand. What is the smallest number of people who could be both taller than 120 cm and right-handed? **3 people.**. There are 6 people. 4 are tall and 5 are right-handed, which makes 9. 9 is 3 more than 6. So at least 3 people must be in both groups. (queues `math.logic.sets-and-venn.L2`)
- Kavya has 4 grow bags. Each season, the tomato family moves one bag along. In season 1 it is in bag 1. Which bag is it in for season 4? **Bag 4.**. Season 1: bag 1. Season 2: bag 2. Season 3: bag 3. Season 4: bag 4. In season 5 it would go back to bag 1, like a circle. (queues `math.patterns.repeating-patterns.L2`)
- Each round of Make a Group, Meena finds 3 true rules and Ravi finds 2. After 6 rounds, how many more rules has Meena found than Ravi? **6 more rules.**. Meena finds 1 more rule than Ravi each round. Over 6 rounds that is 6 × 1 = 6 more. Check: Meena 3 × 6 = 18, Ravi 2 × 6 = 12, and 18 - 12 = 6. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
