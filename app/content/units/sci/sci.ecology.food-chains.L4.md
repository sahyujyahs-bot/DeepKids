# The Sun on your plate: where the energy of a food chain goes
`sci.ecology.food-chains.L4`

**Summary.** A cow eats grass all day, yet a kilogram of grass makes far less than a kilogram of cow. So where does the rest go? A food chain carries the Sun's energy from a plant to the animals that eat her, and on to the next eater. But the energy does not stay in any of them. At every step most of it leaves as heat, and only a small part is left to feed the next eater. In this unit you burn a peanut to see how much energy a bite holds, build three kinds of pyramid, follow a dead leaf through a hidden chain, and ask what a living body really is.

**Intro.** *Put the back of your hand on your forehead. Feel how warm you are? Where did that warmth come from, and where is it going now?* Your body is about as warm as a cup of tea you could sip. Nobody lit a fire inside you. The warmth comes from your last meal, which was a plant or an animal who ate plants, which caught light from the Sun. Now that warmth is leaving your forehead and spreading into the room. In this unit you will follow the Sun's energy along a food chain, and find out what happens to it at every step. You will also meet a chain in the mud that starts with a dead leaf.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that a food chain is a path for energy that begins with sunlight caught by producers, and name the trophic levels along it.
1. I can explain where the energy of a meal goes: into new body, into heat made by respiration, and into parts that are not eaten or not digested.
2. I can draw and read a pyramid of numbers, of biomass and of energy, and explain why the first two can be upside down but the energy pyramid cannot.
3. I can follow a dead-leaf chain, name detritivores and decomposers, and explain why so much of the energy in forests and mangroves passes through dead matter.
4. I can explain that atoms go round and round through living things, while energy goes through them once and ends as heat that leaves the Earth.
5. I can run a fair test, say what I think, what I saw and why, and explain why a measured number is always smaller than the energy that was really there.
6. I can say what scientists agree on, and what they still argue about: whether plants or hunters run a food web, and whether every food chain starts with sunlight.

## Terms
- **Trophic level**: A step in a food chain, counted from the producers. Producers are level 1, the animals that eat them are level 2, and so on. *e.g.* In rice, grasshopper, frog, snake, the frog is at level 3.
- **Producer**: A living being who makes her own food from light, or from chemicals, instead of eating other living things. *e.g.* A rice plant makes sugar from sunlight, water and carbon dioxide.
- **Primary consumer**: An animal who eats producers. She is at level 2. *e.g.* A cow, a grasshopper, a honeybee drinking nectar and a parrot eating seeds.
- **Secondary consumer**: An animal who eats primary consumers. She is at level 3. One who eats those is a tertiary consumer, at level 4. *e.g.* A frog eating a grasshopper is a secondary consumer. A snake eating the frog is a tertiary consumer.
- **Apex predator**: A hunter at the top of a food chain, whom no other animal hunts when she is grown. *e.g.* A tiger in a forest, or a big shark in the sea.
- **Detritus**: Dead leaves, dead wood, dung and dead bodies, broken into small pieces. *e.g.* The brown, soft mud under a mangrove tree is mostly detritus.
- **Detritivore**: An animal who eats detritus and breaks it into smaller pieces. *e.g.* An earthworm, a millipede, a dung beetle and a mangrove crab.
- **Decomposer**: A fungus or a bacterium who digests dead matter outside her body and soaks up the food. She sends the atoms back into the soil, water and air. *e.g.* The mould that grows on a damp chapati, and the white threads under a fallen log.
- **Respiration**: What every living cell does to free the energy in food. The cell uses food and oxygen, and makes carbon dioxide, water and heat. It is not the same as breathing. *e.g.* A compost heap gets warm because the microbes in it respire.
- **Biomass**: The mass of living matter, often counted after the water has been dried out. *e.g.* All the grass on a football field weighs far more than all the grasshoppers on it.
- **Pyramid of numbers**: A drawing with one bar for each trophic level, where the width of the bar shows how many living beings are at that level. *e.g.* One banyan tree can feed thousands of insects, so its pyramid of numbers is not a pyramid.
- **Energy pyramid**: A drawing with one bar for each trophic level, where the width of the bar shows how much energy flows through that level in a year. *e.g.* If grass takes in 1000 units of energy, the grasshoppers who eat her pass on about 100 and the frogs about 10.
- **Food web**: All the food chains of a place joined together, because most animals eat more than one kind of food. *e.g.* A crow eats grain, fruit, insects and scraps, so the crow sits in many chains at once.
- **Chemical energy**: Energy stored in the way atoms are joined together. Sugar, starch and fat hold a lot of it. *e.g.* A peanut holds chemical energy, which comes out as heat and light when it burns.
- **Chemosynthesis**: Making food from chemicals instead of from sunlight. Some bacteria do this. *e.g.* Bacteria near hot deep-sea vents use a smelly gas, hydrogen sulfide, to make food for tube worms.
- **Energy flow**: The one-way journey of energy through living things, from the Sun, through the food chain, and out as heat. *e.g.* Sunlight on a leaf, then a caterpillar, then a bird, with heat leaving at each step.

## Explanations
### Trophic levels: counting the steps from the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** A trophic level is a step in a food chain. Producers are level 1, animals who eat producers are level 2, animals who eat those are level 3, and so on.

The word trophic comes from a Greek word for food. Producers are plants, algae and some bacteria who make their own food. Everyone else is a consumer, and a consumer is counted by how many steps she is from the producers. An arrow in a food chain points from the eaten to the eater, because it shows which way the energy goes. Most food chains have only three to five levels. Decomposers are not on one level. They feed on the dead bodies and wastes from every level. A food chain is a simple picture, and real places are tangled into webs, but the counting is still useful.
- e.g. Rice plant (level 1), grasshopper (level 2), frog (level 3), snake (level 4).
- e.g. In a pond: tiny algae (level 1), water fleas (level 2), small fish (level 3), kingfisher (level 4).
- e.g. In the sea off Kerala: tiny algae (level 1), sardines or mathi (level 2 or 3), tuna (level 4).
### How little of the Sun a plant catches · *defined*
**Definition:** Photosynthesis changes light energy into chemical energy stored in sugars. Over a whole season, a green field stores only about 1 part in 100 of the sunlight that falls on it, or less.

Sunlight falls on a leaf. Some light bounces off, some passes through, and some warms the leaf and the water inside her. A small part is used to join carbon dioxide and water into sugar. That sugar holds chemical energy, which means energy stored in the way atoms are joined. Even sugarcane, one of the best crop plants, stores only a few parts in 100. A forest or the open sea stores less. So the first step of every food chain already starts with a small share of the Sun. All the animals on Earth are fed by that small share.
- e.g. A rice field in Thanjavur gets sunlight all day, and the rice grains and straw hold only a small share of it.
- e.g. Sugarcane stores more sunlight than most crops, which is why sugar mills can run on the cane left over after the juice is pressed.
- e.g. Coal is plant energy stored for about 300 million years. When coal burns, it gives back sunlight that fell on forests long before dinosaurs.
### Three exits: where the energy of a meal goes · *defined*
**Definition:** When an animal eats, the energy of the food goes three ways: some is not taken in, some is used up by respiration and leaves as heat, and a small part becomes new body.

Think of a grasshopper eating a leaf. First, she does not eat all of the plant, and she does not digest everything she does eat. The dung she leaves behind still holds energy, and decomposers will eat it. Second, her cells use most of the food she took in to move, to keep working and to repair themselves. That is respiration, and it ends as heat. Third, a small part is built into her body as growth, and into eggs. Only this third part, and her body when she dies, is left for the next eater. This is why a hunter at the top of a chain gets only a small part of what the plants first caught.
- e.g. A cow does not eat the roots of the grass, and she leaves dung. Dung beetles roll it into balls and eat it.
- e.g. A tiger leaves the bones, the skin and the stomach contents of a deer. Vultures, hyenas, beetles and fungi finish them.
- e.g. A child grows only a little each month. Most of the food she eats goes to run her body and to keep her warm.
### Respiration: why every living body gives off heat · *defined*
**Definition:** Respiration is how cells free the energy in food. A cell uses food and oxygen and makes carbon dioxide, water and heat.

Breathing and respiration are different. Breathing brings oxygen into your body. Respiration happens in every cell, and uses that oxygen to break down sugar and release its energy. Some of that energy is used to move, to build and to send signals. The rest becomes heat, and no cell can turn heat back into food. Plants respire too, day and night, and so do fungi and bacteria. That is why a compost heap can get hot enough to steam, why a crowded bus is warm, and why a bee colony keeps its nursery warm in winter. Where there is life, there is a little heat leaving.
- e.g. A pile of fresh grass cuttings or a compost heap can reach 50 degrees or more, because microbes respire.
- e.g. Your body stays near 37 degrees by making heat from food, all day and all night.
- e.g. A crowd of children in a small room warms the room, because each child is making heat.
### Why 'about a tenth', and why it changes · *defined*
**Definition:** About one tenth of the energy at one level reaches the next level. The real number is anywhere from less than 2 to more than 30 out of 100, depending on who is eating.

In 1942 Raymond Lindeman measured how much energy passed from one level to the next in a lake in Minnesota. In his lake it was around a tenth to a fifth. Later work showed the number is not a law. Warm-blooded animals, such as birds and mammals, spend most of their food on staying warm, so they turn only a few parts in 100 of their food into new body. Cold-blooded animals such as insects and fish keep their body temperature close to their surroundings, so they turn much more of their food into body. A chain through insects and fish can therefore pass up more energy than a chain through birds and mammals. About a tenth is a handy guess, and no more.
- e.g. A hen turns a few parts in 100 of her grain into body. A fish can turn ten times that share.
- e.g. A lizard can live for weeks on a few insects. A sparrow of the same weight needs food every day to stay warm.
- e.g. Plankton in the sea pass on a bigger share than a cow does, because plankton are not warm-blooded.
### Three pyramids: numbers, biomass and energy · *defined*
**Definition:** A pyramid of numbers counts the living beings at each level. A pyramid of biomass weighs them. A pyramid of energy measures how much energy flows through each level in a year.

Each pyramid answers a different question. Numbers ask: how many? Biomass asks: how much living matter is here right now? Energy asks: how much energy passes through this level in a year? In a grass field all three are wide at the bottom and narrow at the top. Energy is the fairest of the three, because one big animal and one tiny animal are compared by the same unit. The energy pyramid is always wide at the bottom, whatever the place, because a level can never pass on more energy than it receives. Numbers and biomass can behave oddly, as the next card shows.
- e.g. A grass field: 1000 grass plants, 100 grasshoppers, 10 frogs, 1 snake. The numbers shrink at each step.
- e.g. A pond: the weight of all the water plants is much more than the weight of all the fish.
- e.g. Any place: the energy bars always shrink upwards, because heat leaves at every step.
### Upside-down pyramids · *defined*
**Definition:** Pyramids of numbers and of biomass can be the wrong way up, but a pyramid of energy never is.

A single banyan tree is one producer, yet thousands of insects and many birds live from her. So the pyramid of numbers is thin at the bottom, wide in the middle and thin at the top. In the open sea, the tiny algae are eaten almost as quickly as they grow. At any one moment their total weight can be smaller than the weight of the tiny animals who eat them. The biomass pyramid is then upside down. Yet the algae divide every day or two, so over a year they make far more food energy than the animals do. The energy pyramid, which counts a whole year, stays the right way up.
- e.g. One banyan tree, 1000 insects living on her leaves, 20 birds, 1 hawk. The numbers are narrow, wide, narrow, narrow.
- e.g. One deer can carry thousands of ticks. The ticks are more numerous than the deer.
- e.g. In many open seas, the weight of tiny animals at one moment is more than the weight of the algae.
### Food webs and levels that are not whole numbers · *defined*
**Definition:** Most animals eat from more than one level, so an animal's trophic level is the average level of her food plus one, and is often not a whole number.

A crow eats grain and fruit, which are level 1, and also insects, which are level 2. Half her food is level 1 and half is level 2. The average is 1.5, so the crow sits at 2.5. Scientists use this idea to give every animal a number. They also measure it in the body. The nitrogen in an animal's body gets a little heavier at each step up a food chain, and the size of that change tells scientists the level. In 2013 a team estimated that people around the world, on average, are at about 2.2. That is near a pig or an anchovy. Most of what most people eat comes from plants.
- e.g. A cow eats only grass and straw. She is at level 2.
- e.g. A rat snake eats half frogs (level 3) and half rats (level 2). She is at 3.5.
- e.g. A person who eats mostly rice, dal and vegetables, with a little curd, is a little above 2.
### The dead-leaf chain · *defined*
**Definition:** A detritus food chain begins with dead plant matter, not with a living plant being eaten. Detritivores and decomposers feed on it, and other animals feed on them.

Most leaves are never eaten alive. In a forest, grazing animals eat less than a fifth of the leaves. The rest falls, dies and is eaten as detritus. In open water the balance is the other way, and most algae are eaten alive. In a mangrove, fallen leaves are pulled into burrows by crabs. Fungi and bacteria soften the leaf and grow on it, and tiny worms and shrimp-like animals graze on that film. Prawns, fish and herons then feed on those. So a dead leaf is not the end of a food chain. It is the start of another. Scientists are still measuring how much of a mangrove's food comes from its leaves, and how much from algae and plankton.
- e.g. Mangrove leaf, then crab, then fish, then heron, in a Kerala backwater.
- e.g. Fallen leaf, then fungus, then springtail, then centipede, in the soil of a plant pot.
- e.g. Cow dung, then dung beetle, then mynah, then a cat.
### Atoms go round, energy goes through · *defined*
**Definition:** The atoms in living things are used again and again. The energy is not. It enters as light or chemical energy and leaves as heat.

A carbon atom can be in the air as carbon dioxide, then in a leaf as sugar, then in a grasshopper, then in a frog, then back in the air when the frog respires. The same atom can go round many times. Energy is different. Sunlight arrives, plants catch a small part, and at every step much of it is turned into heat. That heat spreads out and finally leaves the Earth as invisible infrared light. The Earth sends out almost exactly as much energy as it gets from the Sun, which is why its temperature stays steady. A food chain is a small stretch of this one-way road.
- e.g. The carbon in a tiger's muscle was in the air as carbon dioxide only weeks or months earlier, before a plant took it in.
- e.g. A candle flame, a human body and a leaf all give out heat that ends in the sky.
- e.g. Satellites measure the light the Earth receives and the heat it gives out, and the two nearly match.
### Who runs a food web: the plants below or the hunters above? · *debated*
Scientists agree that plants feed everyone and that hunters can change what the grazers do. They disagree about which matters more, and the answer seems to depend on the place. This is one of the biggest open arguments in ecology.
- e.g. In the Serengeti, more rain means more grass, more wildebeest and more lions.
- e.g. Where sea otters were hunted, sea urchins ate the kelp forests. When otters returned, the kelp came back.
- e.g. In a pond, adding fish can change the number of algae, two levels below.
- **Bottom-up view:** Energy supply sets the limit. Plants decide how much food there is, so they decide how many grazers and how many hunters can live there. More rain or more nutrients means more of everything.
- **Top-down view:** In 1960 three American ecologists, Hairston, Smith and Slobodkin, argued that the world is green because hunters keep the grazers from eating all the plants. Take away the hunters, and the grazers can strip the land.
- **Both views together:** Many ecologists today say both are true. Which one is stronger depends on the place. Hunters seem to matter more in lakes and seas, and plants set the limits more on dry land.
### The hidden eaters: parasites, microbes and viruses · *idea*
Most food-web drawings leave out parasites, microbes and viruses. A parasite is an animal who lives on or in another and feeds from her. In some studies of estuaries, parasites took part in more than half of the feeding links. In the sea, bacteria feed on dissolved food, and viruses burst bacteria and algae open so that their contents return to the water for others. Scientists think this hidden loop carries a very large share of the sea's energy, but they are still working out how much. The tidy chain we draw is a small part of what is really going on.
- e.g. Ticks and worms on a deer pass energy from the deer to themselves.
- e.g. A tiny drop of seawater holds a million bacteria and about ten times as many viruses.
- e.g. A virus that bursts a sea alga lets bacteria feed on the alga's insides.

## Mostly agreed
- Nearly every food chain on Earth begins with producers who capture energy from sunlight. *How we know:* Satellites measure how much light green plants and algae take in, and scientists measure the oxygen and sugar that photosynthesis makes.
- Energy flows one way through a food chain and leaves as heat, while atoms are used again and again. *How we know:* In 1942 Raymond Lindeman measured the energy at each level of a lake and found less at every step. Satellites measure the heat leaving the Earth.
- At every step, most of the energy is not passed on. It is used in respiration, left uneaten, or left in dung. *How we know:* Scientists weigh the food an animal eats, her growth and her wastes, and measure the heat she gives off. In most animals the new body is only a small share of the food.
- Dead matter is one of the biggest pathways of energy in nature. *How we know:* A 1999 study by Just Cebrian of many places found that in forests grazers eat only a small share of what the plants make, and the rest goes to the dead-leaf chain.
- A pyramid of energy is never upside down, but pyramids of numbers and biomass sometimes are. *How we know:* Energy cannot grow on its way up a chain. Counts of banyan insects and weights of sea plankton show upside-down pyramids.
- Most animals feed at more than one trophic level. *How we know:* Scientists check this by looking inside stomachs and by measuring nitrogen in bodies. Nitrogen gets a little heavier at each step, as Minagawa and Wada found in 1984.

## How we found out
**Raymond Lindeman, with the help of his teacher G. Evelyn Hutchinson, 1941 to 1942.** In 1942 a paper appeared in the journal Ecology that changed how scientists look at nature. Its author, a young American called Raymond Lindeman, had studied a small lake in Minnesota called Cedar Bog Lake. Before him, a common way to describe a place was to list who lives there and count them. In 1927 Charles Elton had shown a pyramid of numbers, with many small animals and few big ones. Lindeman asked a different question: how much energy goes up from one level to the next? He measured the plants, the grazing animals and the hunters, and the dead matter in the mud, and found that each level held only a fraction of the one below. Reviewers at first refused his paper, saying it had too little data. His teacher, G. Evelyn Hutchinson, argued for it, and it was published. Lindeman died that summer, at the age of 27. It is now one of the most important papers in ecology.  
*What people believed before:* Many ecologists thought the way to understand a lake or a forest was to list and count who lives there. Counting can mislead, because one tree can carry thousands of insects. Lindeman showed that following the energy, and including the dead matter in the mud, tells you much more.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The 'one-tenth rule' is a law of nature.** (Many school books.; *rejected*)  
  For: Lindeman's lake in 1942 gave numbers near a tenth for some steps. A round number is easy to remember, and it makes a good first estimate.  
  Against: Measurements in many animals give anything from under 2 to over 30 parts in 100. Warm-blooded animals pass on much less than cold-blooded ones.
- **Eating plants always uses less of the Earth than eating animals.** (Many nutrition and climate studies, and many people.; *debated*)  
  For: A hen or a pig turns only a small share of her grain into meat, so feeding grain to animals wastes energy that people could eat directly.  
  Against: Cows, goats and sheep turn grass and straw, which people cannot digest, into milk and meat, often on land where crops will not grow. In India, milk is a key food for many families. Fish and shellfish can be raised on food that people do not eat.
- **Decomposers are the end of the food chain, and nothing eats them.** (Some older drawings of food chains.; *rejected*)  
  For: Decomposers come last in the drawing, after everything has died.  
  Against: Tiny worms called nematodes graze on fungi and bacteria, and springtails eat fungi. Decomposers are food for others, and their atoms and energy move on.
- **Every food chain starts with sunlight.** (Most textbooks.; *accepted*)  
  For: Almost everything on land and in the sea depends on photosynthesis.  
  Against: In 1977 scientists found animals living around hot vents at the bottom of the sea, in total darkness. In 1981 Colleen Cavanaugh and her colleagues showed that bacteria inside tube worms make food using a smelly gas, hydrogen sulfide, from the vents. Their chain starts with chemical energy from inside the Earth.
- **Energy flow is the best way to understand a food web.** (Raymond Lindeman, and ecologists who followed him.; *debated*)  
  For: Energy gives one unit that lets us compare a mouse, a tiger and a tree. It can be measured, added up and checked.  
  Against: Some ecologists say that energy counts hide who matters. A small predator who keeps a grazer in check can matter much more than her share of the energy. Disease, space and seasons matter too.

## Roots
We have followed the Sun's energy along food chains and watched it turn into heat. Now we ask what this tells us about what a living body is, and who we are.

**Deep truth.** Energy is not kept in a food chain. It passes through, and at every step most of it turns into heat. A living body is not a store of energy. It is a pattern that lasts only while energy keeps flowing through it, like a flame.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk as if a body 'has' energy and 'uses it up', like petrol in a tank, and as if the animal at the top has collected the most. But energy is never used up. It only changes form and spreads out as heat. What a body keeps is a way of letting energy pass.  
*Evidence:* The burning peanut gave its stored energy to the water, the air and the tin, and none of it stayed in the nut. Lindeman found less energy at every level of a lake, and heat leaves the Earth as fast as sunlight arrives. Living cells give off heat every second they are alive, and a body that stops taking in energy stops being alive.

- *who am i:* You are not a box that holds energy. You are a pattern that lasts while sunlight, through plants and food, keeps flowing through you.
- *what is life:* Life is energy passing through matter in a particular shape. Every being, from a bacterium to a tiger, keeps that shape going for a while by taking energy in and letting heat out.
- *what is this world:* The Earth is a place where the Sun's light is slowed down on its way back to space. Every green leaf, every grasshopper and every mangrove crab is part of that slowing down.

Sunlight arrives all as one stream. A leaf catches a small part, a caterpillar takes a small part of that, and a bird a small part of that again. At each step the stream turns into many bodies, each in a different shape, and at each step heat leaves and joins the sky. The Earth then gives back to space almost as much as it received. Nothing in the chain keeps the energy, and nothing in the chain could live without it. The many bodies are one flow, seen at different places along its way.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2 (the Bhrigu Valli):** A son, Bhrigu, asks his father Varuna to teach him what the ultimate is. The father says to look at food. Bhrigu finds that all beings are born from food, live by food, and go back into food. (This is the food chain and the dead-leaf chain in one line. A body comes from food, and when it dies it becomes food again for others.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 1:** The verse says that all this world is pervaded by one reality. It then says to enjoy what is given by letting go, and not to covet anyone's wealth. (In a food chain, no one holds the energy for long. Each eater receives, uses and lets go. The heat she lets go is what the next living thing, the sky, receives.)
- **Tattvartha Sutra 5.21, a Jain text by Umaswati:** The sutra says 'parasparopagraho jivanam', which is often translated as 'living beings support one another'. (A mangrove leaf feeds a crab, who feeds a prawn, who feeds a heron. Each being lives because others give their bodies, wastes and warmth.)
- Science (settled): Energy is never made or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. Sunlight becomes chemical energy in a leaf, and chemical energy becomes heat in every cell.
- Science (open): Some physicists, following Erwin Schrödinger in 1944 and Ilya Prigogine later, say a living thing is a pattern that keeps itself going only by taking in energy and giving out heat. How exactly life began as such a pattern is still open.
- Other view, Some ecologists: Energy flow is a useful way to count, but it is our way of drawing nature. A forest has no goal of passing on energy. They say it is better to study how species affect each other, because a tiny predator can change a whole web without moving much energy.
- Practice, One slow mouthful (3 min): Take a small piece of fruit or a spoon of rice. Before you eat, hold your other hand against your cheek and feel your own warmth. Now put the food in your mouth and chew it slowly for twenty seconds. Think about where the food was a few weeks ago. It was in a field, in the sunlight. Think about your warm hand. Some of that sunlight is now warming it. Then breathe out slowly and feel the warm air leave you.
- Ask: If none of the energy stays in you, what is it that stays?
- Ask: When you die, the atoms and the energy go on to others. What stops?
- Ask: Is the heat that leaves a body lost, or given?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* You eat rice, and the rice was sunlight. Then the energy leaves you as heat. If none of it stays, what exactly is it that you are made of? (hint: Think about the atoms that stay and the energy that passes. Which of the two is you?)
- *deep:* A candle flame stays the same shape while the wax burns away. Is a living body more like a thing, or more like a flame? (hint: What would have to stop for the flame to go out? What would have to stop for a body to stop being alive?)
- *deep:* An animal's trophic level changes with what she eats. Is a level something an animal has, or something she does? (hint: A crow at 2.5 eats more insects one week and more fruit the next. Where is the crow's level then?)
- *big:* Nine tenths of the energy at every step becomes heat. Is that waste, or is the heat the point? (hint: Think about the warmth that keeps you alive, and the heat that keeps the Earth from freezing.)
- *big:* If the tiger depends on the deer, and the deer on the grass, and the grass on the Sun, is anyone really at the top? (hint: Draw arrows for who needs whom, not for who eats whom. What does the picture look like?)
- *big:* When we choose what to eat, we also choose which beings get fed and which do not. How should we decide? (hint: A cow, a field of rice, a fishing boat and a farmer's family are all in the picture.)
- *deep:* Nobody can see energy. How do scientists know it moves from a plant to a grasshopper? (hint: Think of measuring heat from burning food, and the weights of what is eaten and what is left.)
- *big:* Is a mangrove forest one big being, or a crowd of beings who happen to live together? (hint: Think about the leaf, the crab, the prawn and the heron, and what each one needs from the others.)

## Story: The Leaves in the Net Bag
*A made-up story. The village is imagined, but pokkali fields near Kochi, where rice and prawns are grown in turn in the same flooded fields, are real, and so are Kudumbashree, the women's groups of Kerala. The science in it is real: in mangrove creeks, fallen leaves are eaten by crabs and softened by microbes, and a large share of the food that fish and prawns eat starts from them. Scientists are still measuring how large the share is, and algae and plankton matter too.*

Every November, after the rice was cut, Anjana's grandmother let the sea into the field. At high tide she opened the wooden gate, and salty water ran in from the canal and covered the stubble. By January the field was full of prawns. Anjana, who was in Class 4, loved the dawn net, when the whole field flashed silver and her grandmother, Muthassi, laughed like a girl.

This January the net came up nearly empty.

Muthassi pulled it in again and again. A few prawns, a few small fish, a lot of mud. By the third morning she did not laugh. She sat on the bund with the empty net across her knees and looked at the water as if it had told her a lie.

Their neighbour, Sebastian Uncle, came along the bund with his bicycle. He looked at the egrets standing in the shallows. 'There is your trouble,' he said. 'Herons and egrets. They are eating all your prawns. Put a net over the field, Muthassi. I will help you.'

Muthassi nodded slowly. But Anjana was counting. She had learned counting from her teacher, who said that if you want to know something, first count it.

She counted the birds on the bund that morning. Two herons and three egrets. She asked Muthassi how many there were last year. 'Oh,' said Muthassi, 'the whole bund was white. Twenty at least. You could not walk without frightening them.'

Anjana frowned. 'Muthassi, if the birds were eating the prawns, there would be more birds this year, not fewer. A bird that eats well stays, and brings her friends. Birds go where the food is.'

Sebastian Uncle scratched his head. 'Then why are the prawns gone?'

'I do not know,' said Anjana. 'But the birds are the last ones in the line. Something happened at the start of the line.'

That evening she asked Muthassi what prawns eat. Muthassi thought. 'The soft mud, my child. The brown mud at the bottom, near the kandal trees. The mud that smells sweet when you turn it.' The kandal were the mangrove trees that grew along the canal bend, with their stilt roots standing in the water like a hundred thin legs.

'And the mud comes from the kandal?'

'I never asked,' said Muthassi. 'It was always there.'

The next morning Anjana walked to the canal bend. Half the kandal trees were gone. Stumps stood in the mud like a row of teeth, and a new wooden landing had been built where the roots used to be. A man had cleared them in the dry months so that boats with tourists could come. Nobody had told Muthassi, because nobody thought it had anything to do with a rice field.

Anjana did not say anything. She picked up an old onion bag from the bank. She filled it with brown, fallen leaves from under the trees that were left. In a second bag she put green leaves, plucked from a branch. She tied both to a bamboo stake in the creek, where the tide would wash through them.

She went back every morning for four days.

On the first day the bags looked the same as when she left them. On the second day the brown leaves were slimy, with a thin brown skin on them. On the third day they were soft and torn at the edges. On the fourth day, when she lifted the bag, tiny pale things were wriggling on the brown leaves. A crab as big as her thumb was pulling a piece of leaf back into the mud.

The green leaves had hardly changed. They were hard and smooth, and nobody had touched them.

She borrowed Muthassi's reading glasses and held one lens over the brown leaf. It was alive. Worms as thin as a hair, and a swarm of creatures like tiny shrimps, were grazing on the soft skin of the leaf. It was like looking into a kitchen full of cooks.

'Muthassi,' she called. 'Come and look.'

Muthassi looked through her own glasses, which she had not known could do that. She was quiet for a long time.

'Nobody eats a green leaf,' said Anjana slowly. 'It has to fall first. It has to die and go soft, and the tiny things grow on it, and the crabs and the small worms eat them. Then the prawns eat the worms and the soft mud. Then the herons eat the prawns. The line does not start with a living plant being eaten. It starts with a leaf that has fallen.'

'And when the man cut the trees . . .' said Muthassi.

'No leaves fell. So no mud was made. No small things. No prawns. And then the herons went away to find a better creek. They did not steal anything, Muthassi. They were following the prawns, and the prawns were following the leaves.'

Sebastian Uncle came to see the net bag. He looked at the brown leaf, then at the stumps, then at his shoes. He did not say a word about herons again.

That month the women of the village, the ones who meet every Thursday in the Kudumbashree group, collected seeds from the kandal trees that were left. The seeds look like long green pencils. They pushed them into the mud at the canal bend, one by one, in a long line. Muthassi said it would take years.

'That is all right,' said Anjana. 'The prawns can wait. They are good at it.'

And she tied a fresh bag to the stake, to see what the new leaves would bring.

**For the parent:** The line of eaters did not start with a living plant. It started with a leaf that had fallen. Cut the trees, and the whole line goes hungry, even the birds at the end.

- Ask: Anjana did not believe that the herons were eating all the prawns. What fact made her doubt it?
- Ask: Why did the green leaves stay hard, while the brown leaves turned soft and full of life?
- Ask: If you wanted to bring the prawns back sooner, what could you do besides planting seeds?

## Experiments
### e1 · A Peanut Full of Sunshine: how much of a bite's energy can you catch? (45 min)
- **question:** When a peanut burns under a tin of water, how much does the water warm? And does a foil shield around the flame catch more of the heat?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many degrees will the water warm from one burning peanut? Will the foil shield make it warmer or not? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every trial uses 100 millilitres of water from the same jug, a peanut of about the same size, and a tin held 4 centimetres above the nut. The only thing that changes is the foil shield. Trials 1 and 2 have no shield. Trials 3 and 4 have the shield. We do each twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights the nut and holds the hot tin. The nut burns with a real flame and spits a little oil. Work on a steel tray, away from curtains, with a bowl of water and a wet cloth close by. Tie back long hair and loose dupattas. Open a window, because the smoke smells strong. Never eat a nut that has been burned. Do not leave a burning nut alone. A burned nut stays hot for a minute, so let it cool before touching.
- **record:** Write the start and end temperatures for each trial and the rise. Work out the average rise for bare and for shielded. Then write one sentence each for: what I think, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Trial | Set-up | Water at the start (degrees) | Water at the end (degrees) | Rise (degrees)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child a peanut and a tin of water. Say what you will do. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will burn a peanut under 100 millilitres of water. How many degrees will the water warm? Will a foil shield help? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will burn a peanut under a tin of water. How much will the water warm? Will foil help? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the water will warm by that much? (good: The peanut is hot when it burns.; A flame heats water.; A peanut has a lot of energy inside.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A guess with a reason is a good start. Most children guess 1 or 2 degrees, or 50. The real answer is somewhere between.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Work on a steel tray. A grown-up reads the safety rules first. Press the clay into the lid. Stand the nail in the clay. Press a peanut on to the nail point. Pour 100 millilitres of cold water into the tin. Read the start temperature and write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up is in charge of the flame. Press the nail into the clay and a peanut on to the nail. Pour 100 millilitres of water in the tin. Read the temperature and write it down.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up is in charge of the flame. Set the peanut on the nail. Pour 100 millilitres of water into the tin. Read the temperature.
  - Note: Bring the tin, the nut and the water to the same room, so the start temperature is the same each time. A peanut with a flat side stands best on the nail. If someone in your home has a nut allergy, use dry coconut for every trial.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Trial 1 and 2: no shield. A grown-up holds the peanut in the diya flame until it catches. Put it under the tin, held 4 centimetres above with tongs. Time the burn. When the flame goes out, stir the water gently and read the highest temperature. Refill with fresh water for trial 2.  
  *Parent says:* Light the nut. It may take up to 30 seconds. Hold the tin 4 centimetres above the flame until it goes out. Then stir the water gently and read the highest number.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up lights the nut. We hold the tin above it. When the flame dies, we stir and read the temperature.
  - Ask: What do you see and smell while the nut burns? (good: A yellow flame.; It crackles and spits oil.; Smoke.; It smells like roasted nuts.)
  - Note: The nut may take a few tries to light, because the skin is damp. Let the flame touch an edge. A good peanut burns for 20 to 60 seconds. Many families see a rise of 3 to 10 degrees. If the rise is under 2 degrees, use only 50 millilitres of water in every trial. Dry the tin before refilling. Do not reuse a half-burned nut.
  - Check: The water warmed by 6 degrees. What is the best explanation? → **Some of the energy stored in the peanut moved into the water as heat.**
- **s4 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Trial 3 and 4: with the shield. Roll the foil strip into a ring about 8 centimetres wide and 4 centimetres tall. Cut two small notches at the bottom for air. Stand the ring round the nail. Light the nut. Rest the tin on top of the ring. Read the water as before.  
  *Parent says:* Make a foil ring round the nut, with two notches for air. A grown-up lights the nut. The tin rests on the ring. Read the water when the flame goes out.  
  *Child mode:* Put a foil ring round the nut. A grown-up lights it. Rest the tin on the ring. Read the water.
  - Note: Keep the notches so the flame can breathe. Without air, the nut goes out. The ring is hot, so let it cool before touching it. Use a fresh nut and fresh water for trial 4.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out the average rise for the bare trials and for the shield trials. Then ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Read the nutrition label of the nuts together.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Work out the average rise for each set-up. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the shield trials warmed the water more, because less heat escaped into the room. After they have spoken, explain: the water caught only some of the heat. To find the share, weigh 10 nuts. One nut is about 0.6 grams. The label says about 24 kilojoules in a gram of peanut, so one nut holds about 14 kilojoules. The water caught 100 grams times 4.2 joules times the rise. For a rise of 8 degrees that is 3360 joules, or 3.4 kilojoules, about one quarter. The rest warmed the air, the tin and the foil, or left as smoke.
  - Check: Water in the shielded set-up warmed more than in the bare set-up. What does that show? → **The shield stopped some heat from escaping into the room.**
  - Think: Where did the rest of the heat go? How could you catch more of it?
### e2 · Warm Seeds: do living things give off heat? (55 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Does a box of sprouting moong become warmer than a box of boiled moong or an empty box, if all three sit in the same room?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which box will be warmest after a day? Will any box be warmer than the room? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All three boxes have the same size and the same lid. Box A holds soaked, living moong. Box B holds the same amount of moong that has been boiled, so the seeds are dead. Box C is empty. All sit side by side in one big cardboard box, wrapped in a towel. We read the same thermometer in each box at the same times. Box C shows what the room does to a box with nothing alive in it. Box B shows that heat does not come from the moong simply being there.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the moong and drains it. Hot water scalds. Do not eat any moong from this test, because it sits warm for a day. Wash your hands after touching the seeds.
- **record:** Write each temperature in the table. Draw a line graph with one line for each box. Say which box was warmest, and by how many degrees.
- **results table:** Time | Room (degrees) | Box A: living moong (degrees) | Box B: boiled moong (degrees) | Box C: empty (degrees)
- **schedule:** day 1: In the evening, soak the moong in plenty of water and leave it overnight. (10 min); day 2: In the morning, drain the moong, boil half, set up the three boxes, and read the first temperatures. Read again at noon and at evening. (35 min); day 3: Read the temperatures the next morning, about 24 hours after the start. Draw the graph and write what you think. (10 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 1, wash the moong. Put it in a bowl with plenty of water. Leave it overnight. Ask your child to guess the result and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will soak these seeds tonight. Tomorrow we will look for heat. Will living seeds make any heat? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Soak the moong in water tonight. Will living seeds make heat? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Do you think a living seed makes heat? Why? (good: Yes, because all living things make heat.; No, because a seed is asleep.; I am not sure.)
  - Note: A seed is alive and resting. Water wakes it up, and it begins to respire to grow. Do not correct the guess.
  - Wait: Leave the moong in water overnight.
- **s2 (20 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, drain the moong. Put half in a pan with fresh water and boil it for 10 minutes. A grown-up drains it and cools it fully to room temperature. Fill tub A with the living moong and tub B with the cooled, boiled moong. Leave tub C empty. Put the lids on loosely.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up boils half of the moong for ten minutes. This kills the seeds. When it is cool, put it in tub B. The living moong goes in tub A. Tub C stays empty.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up boils half the moong. Put the living moong in A and the boiled moong in B. C stays empty.
  - Note: The boiled moong must be cool before it goes in the tub, or it will start warm. Fill A and B to the same height. A deep tub, about two thirds full, holds heat best. If you have only a little moong, use smaller tubs.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three tubs in the cardboard box. Wrap the box in a towel. Push the thermometer into the middle of each tub. Wait one minute each and read it. Always go in the order room, A, B, C. Write the numbers in the first row.  
  *Parent says:* Put the tubs side by side in the box. Read the room first. Then read A, B and C the same way each time. Write the numbers down.  
  *Child mode:* Put the tubs in the box. Read the room, then A, B and C. Write the numbers.
  - Note: Wipe the thermometer between tubs. The first readings should be almost the same. If A and B differ by more than 1 degree, wait 20 minutes and read again.
  - Check: Why does the test include a box with boiled moong? → **To show that heat does not come from moong that is only sitting there.**
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the room and the three tubs again at noon and at evening. Always use the same order and wait the same time. Write the numbers. Do not open the tubs for longer than you need.  
  *Parent says:* Read the room, A, B and C again. Write the numbers. Keep the lids closed between readings.  
  *Child mode:* Read all four numbers again. Write them down. Close the lids.
  - Note: A rise of 1 or 2 degrees is real if the thermometer gave the same number in all three tubs at the start. Moong that is sprouting well will have small white tips by the evening. If A is no warmer than C, add more moong, use a smaller tub, or wrap the box with another towel.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 3, read all four numbers again. Draw a line graph, one line for each tub. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then throw the moong away.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line for each tub. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that tub A warmed more than B and C because the living seeds respire. After they have spoken, explain: respiration frees the energy in food, and some of it always turns into heat. Tub B may warm slightly after a day if microbes start to grow on the boiled moong. That is also respiration. If A is no warmer than C, say so. A small tub of seeds may not make enough heat to measure, and a larger heap would. A compost heap shows it clearly.
  - Check: Tub A is 2 degrees warmer than tub C. What does this show? → **Living seeds gave off heat as they respired.**
  - Think: A compost heap can get hot enough to steam. What do you think is happening inside it?

### From the Sun to the Snake (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a producer?  
   Options: A living being who makes her own food, using light or chemicals / An animal who eats plants / A fungus who feeds on dead leaves  
   **Answer:** A living being who makes her own food, using light or chemicals. *Why:* A producer starts the food chain by making food herself. A plant uses sunlight to make sugar. An animal who eats plants is a consumer, and a fungus who feeds on dead matter is a decomposer.
2. (mcq) In the food chain rice plant, grasshopper, frog, snake, which trophic level is the frog?  
   Options: Level 3 / Level 2 / Level 4  
   **Answer:** Level 3. *Why:* Counting from the producers, the rice plant is level 1, the grasshopper is level 2 and the frog is level 3. The snake is level 4.
3. (mcq) What does the arrow in a food chain show?  
   Options: The way the energy goes, from the food to the eater / Which animal is stronger / Which animal eats first  
   **Answer:** The way the energy goes, from the food to the eater. *Why:* The arrow points from the eaten to the eater, because it shows the way the energy moves. Some children read it as 'eats', which gives the arrow the wrong way round.
4. (mcq) An animal eats a lot of food. Where does most of the energy in that food end up?  
   Options: As heat, made when her cells use food to run her body / As new body, so she grows a lot / In the air as light  
   **Answer:** As heat, made when her cells use food to run her body. *Why:* Most of the energy is used by cells for movement, repair and keeping warm. That use is respiration, and it ends as heat. Only a small part becomes new body.
5. (truefalse) A mushroom is a decomposer who eats living plants.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A mushroom is a fungus who digests dead matter outside her body and soaks up the food. Most mushrooms in the home grow on rotting wood or soil.
6. (truefalse) Plants respire during the day and at night.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Every living cell respires all the time. In daylight a plant also makes food by photosynthesis, so the two happen together. At night only respiration goes on.
7. (fill) Cells free the energy in food by a process called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['respiration']. *Why:* Respiration uses food and oxygen and gives carbon dioxide, water and heat. It is not the same as breathing, which only brings oxygen in.
8. (fill) Most of the energy in a food chain finally leaves as ____.  
   **Answer:** ['heat']. *Why:* At every step cells make heat, and heat spreads into the air. That is why the energy cannot be used again by a plant.
9. (match) Match each living being to her trophic level in a rice field.  
   **Answer:** Rice plant → Level 1; Grasshopper → Level 2; Frog eating grasshoppers → Level 3; Snake eating frogs → Level 4. *Why:* Count the steps from the producer. The rice plant is the producer. Each eater is one step above the food she eats.
10. (sort) Sort each living being into producer, consumer or decomposer.  
   **Answer:** Neem tree → Producer; Rabbit → Consumer; Mushroom → Decomposer; Tiny algae → Producer; Hawk → Consumer; Bacteria in compost → Decomposer. *Why:* Producers make their own food. Consumers eat other living beings or their bodies. Decomposers digest dead matter and send the atoms back to the soil, water and air.
11. (count) Grass has stored 2000 units of energy in a season. About one tenth passes to the next level each time. How many units reach a frog who eats grasshoppers who ate the grass?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* The grasshoppers get a tenth of 2000, which is 200. The frog gets a tenth of 200, which is 20. This is a rough guess, because real numbers vary.
12. (order) Put these in the right order, for the dead-leaf chain in a mangrove.  
   **Answer:** ['A leaf falls into the mud', 'Fungi and bacteria soften the leaf', 'A crab eats the soft leaf', 'A prawn eats small animals in the mud', 'A heron eats the prawn']. *Why:* The chain starts with the fallen leaf. It must be softened by microbes before small animals can eat it. Each eater is eaten by the next.
13. (write) A vulture eats dead animals. Why is she a consumer and not a decomposer?  
   **Answer:** A vulture is an animal who eats the dead body in pieces and digests it inside her own body. A decomposer is a fungus or bacterium who digests dead matter outside her body and soaks it up. Both feed on dead things, but they do it differently.. *Why:* A strong answer names the difference: the vulture has a stomach and eats pieces, while decomposers soak up food from outside. It may also say that both help return atoms.
14. (write) A friend says: 'When I run, the energy from my rice is used up and gone.' What really happens to it?  
   **Answer:** The energy is not used up. It changes form. Some of it moves your muscles, and most of it becomes heat that spreads into the air around you. It cannot be used again by a plant.. *Why:* A strong answer says that energy changes form and spreads out as heat, and that it is not destroyed.
15. (draw) Draw a food chain of four links starting from a rice field. Put an arrow between each link, a level number under each link, and a small red arrow of heat leaving each one.  
   **Answer:** Rice plant to grasshopper to frog to snake, with arrows pointing to the eater, levels 1 to 4 beneath, and a red heat arrow from every one.. *Why:* A strong drawing points every arrow to the eater, counts the levels from 1, and shows heat leaving from all four links, not only the last.
16. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Mushroom, bread mould, grasshopper, bacteria in compost.  
   **Answer:** Grasshopper, because she eats living plants. The other three are decomposers who feed on dead matter.. *Why:* Mushrooms, moulds and compost bacteria digest dead matter. A grasshopper is a consumer who eats living leaves.
### Pyramids, Levels and Heat (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Why can the pyramid of numbers for a banyan tree be wide in the middle?  
   Options: One big tree can feed thousands of small animals / The tree is not a producer / The insects are bigger than the tree  
   **Answer:** One big tree can feed thousands of small animals. *Why:* One banyan has an enormous amount of leaf, so it can feed very many small animals. The count at the bottom is only one, so the numbers pyramid is not a pyramid. Biomass and energy would show a wide base.
2. (mcq) In the open sea, the tiny algae weigh less than the tiny animals who eat them at one moment. Why is the energy pyramid still wide at the bottom?  
   Options: The algae divide so quickly that they make far more food energy over a year / Algae are heavier than animals / The animals do not need food  
   **Answer:** The algae divide so quickly that they make far more food energy over a year. *Why:* The algae are eaten almost as fast as they grow, so at one moment their weight is small. But they replace themselves every day or two, so over a year they make much more energy than the animals above.
3. (mcq) A hen and a fish weigh the same. Why does the fish turn a larger share of her food into new body?  
   Options: The hen is warm-blooded and uses most of her food to keep warm / Fish eat more than hens / Hens do not respire  
   **Answer:** The hen is warm-blooded and uses most of her food to keep warm. *Why:* Warm-blooded animals make heat all the time to stay warm. That uses most of their food. A fish stays close to the water's temperature, so more of her food can become body.
4. (mcq) A crow eats half grain and fruit, which are level 1, and half insects, which are level 2. What is her level?  
   Options: 2.5 / 1.5 / 3  
   **Answer:** 2.5. *Why:* The average level of her food is 1.5. She is one step above her food, so her level is 2.5. Choosing 1.5 forgets to add the step for the crow herself.
5. (truefalse) An animal keeps the same trophic level all her life.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A level is an average of what she eats. If a crow eats more insects one season, her level goes up a little. If she eats more fruit, it goes down.
6. (truefalse) A pyramid of energy can be upside down.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A level can never pass on more energy than it received, and heat leaves at every step. So the energy pyramid is always widest at the bottom.
7. (fill) A banyan tree's pyramid of ____ is thin at the bottom and wide in the middle.  
   **Answer:** ['numbers']. *Why:* The numbers pyramid counts the beings at each level. One tree and thousands of insects makes a spindle shape.
8. (match) Match each kind of pyramid to what it measures.  
   **Answer:** Pyramid of numbers → How many beings are at each level; Pyramid of biomass → The weight of living matter at each level; Pyramid of energy → How much energy flows through each level in a year. *Why:* Each pyramid answers a different question. Only the energy pyramid is always the right way up.
9. (sort) Sort each of these into where a grasshopper's meal of leaf energy goes.  
   **Answer:** Keeping her cells working → Heat from respiration; Leaf pieces she did not digest → Dung or uneaten; Growing a longer leg → New body; Jumping away from a bird → Heat from respiration; Laying eggs → New body; Leaf edges left uneaten on the plant → Dung or uneaten. *Why:* Movement and keeping cells working use energy that ends as heat. Wastes and uneaten parts still hold energy for decomposers. Only growth and eggs become new body for the next eater.
10. (count) A grasshopper takes in 500 units of energy from leaves. 150 units come out as dung and 50 units become new body. How many units did her cells use in respiration?  
   **Answer:** 300. *Why:* She used what is left after the dung and the new body. 500 minus 150 is 350, and 350 minus 50 is 300 units. Most of the food went to run her body and left as heat.
11. (count) A peanut holds about 14 kilojoules of energy. The water over a burning peanut caught about 3.5 kilojoules. What fraction of the energy did the water catch?  
   **Answer:** one quarter. *Why:* 3.5 out of 14 is one quarter, because 3.5 times 4 is 14. The other three quarters warmed the air, the tin and the shield, or left as smoke.
12. (order) Put these in order, from the first link to the last, for a food chain in the sea.  
   **Answer:** ['Tiny algae', 'Zooplankton', 'Sardine', 'Tuna']. *Why:* Algae are the producers. Zooplankton graze on them, sardines eat the zooplankton, and tuna eat the sardines.
13. (odd) Which pyramid is the odd one out, and why? A banyan's numbers pyramid, ticks on a deer in a numbers pyramid, an open-sea biomass pyramid, an open-sea energy pyramid.  
   **Answer:** The open-sea energy pyramid, because it is always the right way up. The other three can be upside down.. *Why:* Energy cannot grow on its way up a chain. Numbers and biomass depend on how big and how fast-growing the beings are.
14. (perspective) Two friends argue about who runs a forest food web.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view, gives a reason, and names a test, such as counting plants, deer and hunters in forests with and without tigers.. *Why:* Scientists really argue about this. There is no single answer yet, and good thinkers use evidence from each place.
15. (write) A tiger is at level 3 but a tuna is at level 4, though both hunt at the top of their food chain. Why do you think they differ?  
   **Answer:** The sea chain has more steps. It begins with tiny algae, and many small animals lie between the algae and the tuna. On land the tiger eats deer, who eat plants, so there are fewer steps.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the number of steps differs, and may say that sea chains often have more links because the producers are tiny.
16. (draw) A deer carries 3000 ticks. Draw the pyramid of numbers for the grass, the deer and the ticks, and write what is odd about its shape.  
   **Answer:** A thin bar for the grass or a line, a thin bar for one deer, and a very wide bar for the ticks. The shape is upside down at the top, not a pyramid.. *Why:* Ticks live on one deer, so their count is far greater than the deer's. Counting beings makes the pyramid odd, and weighing or measuring energy would not.
### Be the Energy Detective (challenge) (challenge, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) Around hot vents at the bottom of the sea, animals live in total darkness. What do the bacteria in their bodies use to make food?  
   Options: Chemicals from the vent, such as hydrogen sulfide / Sunlight that sinks from the top / Leaves that fall from land  
   **Answer:** Chemicals from the vent, such as hydrogen sulfide. *Why:* The bacteria use the energy in chemicals from the vent, a process called chemosynthesis. No sunlight reaches that depth. Their food chain starts with chemical energy from inside the Earth.
2. (mcq) Why do ecologists measure heavy nitrogen in an animal's body?  
   Options: It becomes a little heavier at each step up the food chain, so it shows her level / It shows how old she is / It shows how fast she can run  
   **Answer:** It becomes a little heavier at each step up the food chain, so it shows her level. *Why:* The heavy kind of nitrogen builds up a little at every step. By measuring it, scientists can tell what level an animal has fed at over a long time, even if they never saw her eat.
3. (mcq) In a forest, grazing animals eat only a small share of the leaves while they are alive. What happens to the rest?  
   Options: It falls and is eaten as dead matter by detritivores and decomposers / It is lost and no one ever uses it / It turns straight into soil without any living being  
   **Answer:** It falls and is eaten as dead matter by detritivores and decomposers. *Why:* Most of the leaves enter the dead-leaf chain. Fungi, bacteria and small animals feed on them. Rotting is something living things do.
4. (truefalse) The Earth gives back to space almost as much energy as it gets from the Sun.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Earth's temperature stays steady because the heat leaving is almost equal to the sunlight arriving. Living things only slow the energy down on its way out.
5. (truefalse) Fungi and bacteria are never eaten by anyone.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Tiny worms graze on fungi and bacteria, and springtails eat fungi. Decomposers are food for others too.
6. (fill) In a food web, ____ go round and round, but energy goes through once.  
   **Answer:** ['atoms']. *Why:* A carbon atom can be in the air, in a leaf, in a grasshopper and back in the air again. Energy changes into heat and leaves.
7. (match) Match each scientist or team to what they showed.  
   **Answer:** Raymond Lindeman → Measured the energy at each level in a lake; Colleen Cavanaugh and her colleagues → Showed that bacteria in tube worms make food from chemicals; Hairston, Smith and Slobodkin → Argued that hunters keep grazers from eating all the plants; Minagawa and Wada → Found that nitrogen gets heavier at each step up a food chain. *Why:* These four findings are the base of how scientists study the flow of energy through food webs.
8. (count) A field receives 10,000 units of sunlight in a season and stores 1 in every 100 in its plants. A grasshopper then gets one tenth of the stored energy. How many units does the grasshopper get?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* One in 100 of 10,000 is 100 units stored in the plants. One tenth of 100 is 10. So the grasshopper gets 10 units out of the 10,000 that fell on the field. Most of the sunlight never became food.
9. (count) A hen eats feed holding 400 units of energy in a day. Her egg holds 80 units. What fraction of the feed's energy is in the egg?  
   **Answer:** one fifth. *Why:* 80 out of 400 is one fifth, because 80 times 5 is 400. The other four fifths went to keep the hen warm and moving, or came out as dung.
10. (sort) Sort each statement into what scientists agree on or what they still argue about.  
   **Answer:** Heat leaves at every step of a food chain → Agreed; Plants or hunters run most food webs → Argued about; Dead leaves feed many food chains → Agreed; The exact share of mangrove food that comes from mangrove leaves → Argued about; Atoms are used again and again → Agreed; How much of the sea's energy passes through viruses → Argued about. *Why:* Scientists agree on the flow of energy and the recycling of atoms. They are still measuring how much each pathway carries, and which group controls the web.
11. (order) Put these in order to follow a bit of rice energy from the Sun to the end.  
   **Answer:** ['Sunlight falls on a rice leaf', 'A plant makes sugar and stores energy', 'A grasshopper eats the leaf', "The grasshopper's cells make heat", 'The heat spreads into the air and leaves the Earth']. *Why:* Energy starts as light, is stored as chemical energy, passes to the eater, and finally spreads as heat out to space.
12. (odd) Which one does not belong, and why? Coal, rice, a peanut, a river pebble.  
   **Answer:** The river pebble, because it holds no stored energy from living things. Coal, rice and a peanut all hold energy that plants once caught from sunlight.. *Why:* Coal is plant matter that was buried for hundreds of millions of years. Rice and peanuts are plant food. A pebble is a rock.
13. (perspective) A village is arguing about what to farm on its new land.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a plan, gives a reason, and says what to measure, such as how many people each plan feeds and how much of the food comes from animals.. *Why:* People really disagree about this. The best answer for one place may not be best for another.
14. (write) A friend says energy is 'passed on' along a food chain, but you say it is not 'kept'. Use the burning peanut to explain what you mean.  
   **Answer:** When the peanut burned, its stored energy turned into heat and light. The water caught some of the heat, but most spread into the air. The nut did not keep its energy. In a food chain, each eater receives some energy and lets most of it go as heat.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the energy changes form and spreads out, and that at each step much of it becomes heat that is not kept.
15. (write) One scientist checks an animal's last meal by looking in her stomach. Another measures the heavy nitrogen in her body. Why might they get different levels?  
   **Answer:** The stomach shows only the last meal, which may be unusual. The nitrogen in her body builds up over weeks or months, so it shows what she has eaten over a long time.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the stomach shows one meal and the nitrogen shows the long-term diet.
16. (write) You want to test whether a compost pile makes heat. Describe a fair test.  
   **Answer:** Put a thermometer in the middle of the compost pile and another in a pile of dry sand of the same size next to it. Read both at the same times for three days. If the compost is warmer than the sand and the room, the heat comes from the living things in it.. *Why:* A strong answer has a comparison (sand or an empty pile), the same size and place, the same times of reading, and a clear idea of what would show heat made by life.
17. (draw) Draw the dead-leaf chain of a mangrove creek. Label the detritus, one detritivore, one decomposer and one animal who eats them.  
   **Answer:** A fallen mangrove leaf in the mud labelled detritus. A crab labelled detritivore. Fungus or bacteria on the leaf labelled decomposer. A prawn or fish eating small animals from the mud.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the leaf softening first, the crab and microbes feeding on it, and a bigger animal eating them.

2. (mcq) An animal eats 100 units of energy. Which statement is most likely true?  
   Options: Only a small part becomes new body that the next eater can use / Nearly all of it becomes new body / None of it becomes heat  
   **Answer:** Only a small part becomes new body that the next eater can use. *Why:* Most of the food is used by cells and ends as heat. A small part grows the body, and some leaves as dung. So the next eater gets only a small part.
3. (mcq) Why do hunters at the top of a food chain need a large area to live in?  
   Options: Little energy reaches the top, so many levels of plants and animals are needed beneath them / They like to walk far / Plants grow slowly near hunters  
   **Answer:** Little energy reaches the top, so many levels of plants and animals are needed beneath them. *Why:* Each step loses most of the energy. A hunter needs the food of many levels below her, so she needs a large area with a lot of plants.
4. (mcq) A pyramid of numbers for one oak tree and the insects living on her is wide in the middle. What does this show?  
   Options: Counting beings can hide how much a big producer gives / The oak is not a producer / Insects weigh more than the oak  
   **Answer:** Counting beings can hide how much a big producer gives. *Why:* A single tree can feed thousands of insects. Counting gives one at the bottom, so the shape looks odd. A pyramid of energy would be wide at the bottom.
5. (mcq) A warm-blooded hunter and a cold-blooded hunter eat the same weight of food. Who makes more new body?  
   Options: The cold-blooded hunter, because the warm-blooded one spends more food on keeping warm / The warm-blooded hunter, because she is bigger / They make the same amount  
   **Answer:** The cold-blooded hunter, because the warm-blooded one spends more food on keeping warm. *Why:* Keeping the body warm uses a lot of food and ends as heat. Cold-blooded animals do less of this, so more of their food can become body.
6. (mcq) What is detritus?  
   Options: Dead leaves, wood, dung and dead bodies, broken into pieces / Only the soil under a tree / A kind of living fungus  
   **Answer:** Dead leaves, wood, dung and dead bodies, broken into pieces. *Why:* Detritus is the dead matter that detritivores and decomposers eat. Soil is not only detritus, and a fungus is a living decomposer.
7. (mcq) In a mangrove creek, why would cutting the trees reduce the number of prawns?  
   Options: Fewer leaves fall, so there is less food at the start of the dead-leaf chain / Prawns eat the trees directly / The water becomes colder  
   **Answer:** Fewer leaves fall, so there is less food at the start of the dead-leaf chain. *Why:* Fallen leaves feed crabs and small animals, which feed prawns. Cutting the trees cuts the supply of leaves, so each level below the prawns has less food.
8. (mcq) What happens to the atoms in a dead leaf?  
   Options: Decomposers and other eaters pass them on, and they return to the soil, water and air / They disappear / They are turned into energy and leave as light  
   **Answer:** Decomposers and other eaters pass them on, and they return to the soil, water and air. *Why:* Atoms are not destroyed. They move from leaf to fungus to animal and back to the air and soil. They are used again and again.
9. (mcq) Which statement about energy and atoms is correct?  
   Options: Atoms go round and round, but energy flows through and leaves as heat / Energy goes round and atoms leave / Both stay inside living things for ever  
   **Answer:** Atoms go round and round, but energy flows through and leaves as heat. *Why:* Atoms are used again and again. Energy arrives as light and leaves as heat, so life needs a fresh supply of sunlight every day.
10. (mcq) Why must a fair test of burning peanuts use the same amount of water and the same height for the tin?  
   Options: So that only one thing changes and any difference comes from that one thing / So that the nuts burn faster / So that the flame is bigger  
   **Answer:** So that only one thing changes and any difference comes from that one thing. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing at a time. If the water or the height changes too, we cannot tell which change caused the different result.
11. (mcq) The water over a burning nut caught only a quarter of the nut's energy. Which statement is the best explanation?  
   Options: The rest of the heat warmed the air, the tin and the smoke / A nut holds less energy than the label says / Three quarters of the energy was destroyed  
   **Answer:** The rest of the heat warmed the air, the tin and the smoke. *Why:* Energy is not destroyed. The rest of the heat spread into the air and the tin, so the water could not catch it all. This is a small version of the loss at every step of a food chain.
12. (mcq) Scientists disagree about whether plants from below or hunters from above run a food web. What is the best way to settle it?  
   Options: Measure plants, grazers and hunters in many places and compare them / Ask the oldest scientist / Choose the view that most people like  
   **Answer:** Measure plants, grazers and hunters in many places and compare them. *Why:* A real argument in science is settled by evidence from many places. The answer may differ from one place to another.

## Applications
- Farmers choose crops and animals by asking how much food energy each field can really give. Rice and ragi give more energy per hectare than cattle fed on grain.
- Fish farmers in Kerala and Andhra Pradesh feed fish and prawns as low on the chain as they can, because every step up wastes energy.
- Biogas plants and composting turn the energy in dung and kitchen waste into gas and heat that families use, using the decomposer chain on purpose.
- Conservation planners count the energy at the bottom of the web to decide how many tigers a forest can support.
- Fishing rules, such as a break during the monsoon, protect the lower levels so that bigger fish can feed.
- Dairy farmers feed cows straw and grass that people cannot eat, and get milk, which is a way of turning energy people cannot use into food people can.
- Insulated lunch boxes and thermoses work by slowing the heat's escape, the same escape that every body in a food chain has.
- Scientists measure what fish and sea birds eat by the heavy nitrogen in their bodies, to learn whether a fishery is still healthy.
- Wetland and mangrove protection, such as at Pichavaram and in the Sundarbans, keeps the dead-leaf chain that feeds fish.
- Cooks and nutritionists measure food in kilojoules or kilocalories, the same energy units that scientists use to measure a food chain.

## Open questions
- **How much of the food in a mangrove creek really comes from the mangrove leaves, and how much comes from algae and plankton?** Studies in different places give different answers, and the answer seems to change with the tide, the season and the creek.
- **Do plants or hunters run most food webs?** Some places seem to be run from below and some from above. Scientists do not yet know why, or how it will change as the climate changes.
- **How much of the energy of the sea passes through viruses and microbes that we cannot see?** These tiny beings are hard to count and to watch. Scientists have estimates, but new studies keep changing the picture.
- **How many of the links in a food web are parasites, and what do they change?** Parasites are hard to find and hard to count. Only a few food webs have been studied with parasites included.
- **Could there be food chains that run on chemical energy on other worlds, such as under the ice of Europa, a moon of Jupiter?** We have found hot vents on Earth where life lives without sunlight. Nobody has yet looked beneath the ice of other worlds.
- **As the climate warms, will animals turn a smaller or a larger share of their food into body?** Cold-blooded animals depend on their surroundings for warmth, so warming changes how fast they use their food. Scientists are still measuring this in many animals.

## Connected maths
- In your peanut test, 100 grams of water warmed by 8 degrees. It takes about 4 joules to warm 1 gram of water by 1 degree. How many joules did the water catch? **3200 joules.**. Multiply the three numbers. 100 times 8 is 800. 800 times 4 is 3200. So the water caught about 3200 joules, or 3.2 kilojoules. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- The peanut held about 14,000 joules and the water caught 3500. A friend says most of the heat was lost. Is she right? What fraction was lost? **Yes. Three quarters was lost.**. 3500 out of 14,000 is one quarter, because 3500 times 4 is 14,000. So the water caught one quarter and three quarters went elsewhere. She is right. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In a grass field the grass holds 2000 units of energy, the grasshoppers 200 and the frogs 20. If the pattern continues, how many units does a snake who eats the frogs get? Why does the pattern stop being useful for very high levels? **2 units. The numbers get so small that there is not enough food for a large animal.**. Each level has a tenth of the one below. A tenth of 20 is 2. Two units are too little to support a big hunter, so food chains stop after a few levels. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- In the Peanut test, the bare set-up gave rises of 5 and 7 degrees. The shielded set-up gave rises of 9 and 11 degrees. The two trials of each set-up differ by 2 degrees. The averages differ by 4. Does the shield really make a difference? **Yes, probably. The gap between the set-ups is bigger than the gap between two trials of the same set-up.**. The averages are 6 and 10, so they differ by 4 degrees. Two trials of the same set-up differ by only 2. A difference bigger than the usual wobble between trials is more likely to be real. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A hen eats 3 parts grain, which is level 1, for every 1 part of insects, which is level 2. What is her level? Show how you work it out. **2.25.**. Out of 4 parts, 3 are level 1 and 1 is level 2. The total is 3 plus 2, which is 5. Five divided by 4 is 1.25. Add 1 for the hen herself, and she is at 2.25. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In Feed the Village, you plant 9 rice fields, 1 fish pond and 2 grass fields. How many people-months does the plan feed, and does it follow the rule that at least 8 come from animal foods? **81 people-months, and 9 come from animals. Yes, it follows the rule.**. Rice feeds 9 times 8, which is 72. The pond feeds 3 and the grass feeds 2. The straw from 9 rice fields adds 4, the most it can add. The total is 72 plus 3 plus 2 plus 4, which is 81. Animal foods are 3 plus 2 plus 4, which is 9. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
- In the Warm Seeds test, tub A read 28, 29, 30 and 31 degrees at four times. The room read 27, 27, 28 and 27. How much warmer than the room was tub A each time? What does the pattern say about the heat? **1, 2, 2 and 4 degrees warmer. The gap is growing, so the seeds keep making heat.**. Subtract the room from tub A each time. 28 minus 27 is 1, 29 minus 27 is 2, 30 minus 28 is 2, and 31 minus 27 is 4. The gap grows, which suggests living seeds keep making heat that builds up. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In the energy pyramid for a field, the grass holds 1000 units, and the frogs hold 10. What percent of the grass's energy reaches the frogs? Would a fish farm that feeds fish on algae directly pass on more or less? **1 percent. A fish farm that feeds fish on algae directly skips a level, so the farm passes on more of the original energy.**. 10 out of 1000 is 1 out of 100, which is 1 percent. If the fish eat algae directly, they are only one step from the producers. One fewer step means one fewer loss, so more of the algae's energy reaches them. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
