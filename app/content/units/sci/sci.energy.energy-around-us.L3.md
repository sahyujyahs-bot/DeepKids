# Energy: what makes things move, light up, heat up and make sounds
`sci.energy.energy-around-us.L3`

**Summary.** A fan turns, a torch shines, a cooker whistles and dosa batter turns crisp on a hot tawa. Each of these needs energy. Energy is what makes things happen: it makes things move, light up, heat up and make sounds. It is never made by the thing that uses it. It is passed on from somewhere else: from a cell, from the wall socket, from gas, from food or from sunlight. When you drop a marble into atta from higher up, it lands faster and digs a wider crater, because your arm gave it more energy by lifting it higher. Follow almost any energy back, step by step, and you reach the Sun.

**Intro.** *Try three things. Switch on a torch. Clap your hands hard. Hold your palm near, not on, a cup of hot tea. The torch gave light, the clap made a sound, and the tea warmed your hand. What do these three have in common?* Each one made something happen. Scientists say that whatever makes something happen needs energy. In this unit you go on a hunt for energy in your home. You drop a marble into a tray of atta from different heights to see what more energy does. And you find out where the energy for almost everything around you comes from.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what energy is: what is needed to make something move, light up, heat up or make a sound.
1. I can find energy at work in my home, and say whether it is making something move, light up, heat up or make a sound.
2. I can follow where the energy for a thing came from, step by step, often back to the Sun.
3. I can run a fair test that drops a marble into atta from different heights, and explain what the craters show about energy.
4. I can explain that energy is never used up: it is passed from one thing to another, and in the end much of it spreads out as heat.
5. I can tell how Willem 's Gravesande and Émilie du Châtelet used dents in clay to show that a faster ball carries much more energy.

## Terms
- **Energy**: What is needed to make something happen: to make it move, light up, heat up or make a sound. *e.g.* A ceiling fan needs energy from electricity to turn.
- **Movement energy**: The energy a thing has because it is moving. Scientists call it kinetic energy. The faster it moves, the more it has. *e.g.* A cricket ball bowled fast has more movement energy than one rolled gently.
- **Stored energy**: Energy that is held ready, waiting to make something happen later. Food, a cell, gas, a wound-up toy and a thing lifted up high all have stored energy. *e.g.* A coconut high up on a tree has stored energy. When it falls, that energy becomes movement energy.
- **Heat**: Energy that moves from a warmer thing to a cooler thing. It makes things warmer. *e.g.* Heat from the flame moves into the pan and cooks the dosa.
- **Light**: Energy that travels from things that shine, such as the Sun, a lamp or a screen. It lets us see. *e.g.* A torch turns energy from its cells into light.
- **Sound**: Energy carried by vibrations travelling through the air, or through water or solids, to our ears. *e.g.* A temple bell vibrates when it is struck, and the sound reaches you across the street.
- **Vibrate**: To move back and forth very fast. *e.g.* A stretched rubber band vibrates when you pluck it, and you hear a twang.
- **Electricity**: A way of carrying energy along wires, from a cell or a power station to the thing that uses it. *e.g.* Electricity carries energy from a wind farm in Tamil Nadu to a fan in a flat in Chennai.
- **Fuel**: Something that is burned to give out heat and light. Wood, cooking gas, kerosene and petrol are fuels. *e.g.* Cooking gas (LPG) is the fuel in most city kitchens.
- **Energy transfer**: Energy passing from one thing to another. Transfer means moving across. *e.g.* When you kick a ball, energy is transferred from your leg to the ball.
- **Source of energy**: Where the energy for something comes from. *e.g.* The source of energy for a solar lamp is sunlight.
- **Solar energy**: Energy that comes from the Sun as light and heat. Solar means 'of the Sun'. *e.g.* Solar panels on a roof turn sunlight into electricity.
- **Joule**: The unit scientists use to measure energy, named after James Joule, an English scientist. One joule is about the energy you need to lift a small apple one metre. *e.g.* Lifting a 100 gram apple from the floor to a table about 1 metre high takes about 1 joule.
- **Conservation of energy**: The rule that energy is never made and never destroyed. It only passes from one thing to another and changes form. *e.g.* When a falling marble stops in atta, its movement energy is not lost. It has gone into moving atta, a sound and a tiny bit of warmth.
- **Crater**: A bowl-shaped hole made when something hits a surface. *e.g.* The Moon is covered in craters made by rocks from space.

## Explanations
### What energy is · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy is what is needed to make something happen. Anything that moves, lights up, heats up or makes a sound is using energy.

Scientists usually say energy is what is needed to do work, which means to push or pull something and move it. For us, a simpler way works well: energy is what makes things happen. A still marble on a table does nothing. Lift it and drop it, and it falls, digs a crater and makes a thud. A switched-off bulb is dark. Let electricity reach it, and it lights up and gets warm. You cannot see or hold energy itself. You see what it does.
- e.g. A child running uses energy from food.
- e.g. A mixie grinding chutney uses energy carried by electricity.
- e.g. Sunlight warming a balcony floor brings energy from the Sun.
### Energy shows up in different forms · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy can be movement energy, heat, light, sound, electrical energy, or stored energy. These are called forms of energy.

The same energy can change from one form to another. In a torch, stored energy in the cells is carried by electricity to the bulb. The bulb gives out light and a little heat. In your body, stored energy from food becomes movement when you run, and heat that keeps you warm. A falling coconut's stored energy, from being high up, becomes movement energy as it falls. When it hits the ground, there is a thud: some energy has become sound.
- e.g. Cooker: the gas flame's heat makes steam, and the steam pushes up the whistle and makes a sound.
- e.g. Phone: stored energy in the battery becomes light on the screen and sound from the speaker.
- e.g. Fan: electrical energy becomes movement energy in the blades.
### Energy passes from one thing to another · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy transfer means energy passing from one thing to another. Things do not make energy. They get it from somewhere and pass it on.

When you throw a ball, energy passes from your arm to the ball. When the ball hits a window, energy passes to the glass, and the glass rattles. When you put a steel tumbler of cold water in the sun, energy passes from sunlight into the water, and the water warms up. Every time something happens, energy is passing from one thing to another.
- e.g. Your hand gives energy to a spinning top.
- e.g. A hot tawa gives energy to the dosa batter.
- e.g. Wind gives energy to the blades of a wind turbine.
### Why a higher drop digs a bigger crater · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing lifted higher has more stored energy. When it falls, that stored energy becomes movement energy, so it lands faster and can do more.

To lift a marble, your arm must push it up against the Earth's pull. Lifting it higher takes more energy. That energy is stored in the marble while you hold it up. When you let go, the marble speeds up as it falls. The stored energy becomes movement energy. A marble from 80 centimetres lands about twice as fast as one from 20 centimetres. When it hits the atta, its energy pushes atta aside, makes a thud, and warms the atta very slightly. More energy means a wider crater and a louder thud.
- e.g. A coconut falling from a tall tree lands much harder than one falling from a short tree.
- e.g. A hammer lifted high drives a nail in further than one lifted a little.
- e.g. Water falling from high up in a dam spins the turbines that make electricity, as at Idukki in Kerala.
### Energy is never used up · *defined*
**Definition:** Energy is never made and never destroyed. It only passes on and changes form. This is called the conservation of energy.

People often say a cell is 'used up' or a child has 'used up' their energy. The energy has not vanished. It has passed on to other things, and much of it has spread out as a little warmth in many places. When the marble stopped in the atta, its movement energy went into moving the atta, the thud and a tiny warming of the atta and the air. Spread-out warmth like this is still energy, but it is very hard to collect and use again. That is why we need new food every day, and why a cell stops working.
- e.g. After a long game of kabaddi, your energy from food has become movement, sound and warmth, and the warmth has spread into the air.
- e.g. A spinning top slows down because its movement energy passes into the floor and the air as a little warmth.
- e.g. A torch cell stops lighting the bulb when its stored energy has all been passed on.
### Where our energy comes from · *defined*
**Definition:** A source of energy is where the energy for something comes from. Almost all the energy we use on Earth came from the Sun.

Plants catch sunlight in their leaves and store its energy in the food they make. Your idli, rice and dal came from plants, so their energy is stored sunlight. Milk and eggs come from animals who ate plants. Coal, oil and cooking gas formed over millions of years from plants and tiny sea creatures who grew in sunlight long ago. Wind blows because the Sun warms some places more than others. Rain fills dams because the Sun lifts water into the sky as it evaporates. A few sources do not come from the Sun. Nuclear power stations, like Kudankulam in Tamil Nadu, use energy stored in uranium. Tides come mostly from the Moon's pull. Hot springs get their heat from deep inside the Earth.
- e.g. A ceiling fan in Chennai may run on electricity from the Muppandal wind farm near Kanyakumari. That wind was moved by the Sun's heat.
- e.g. Solar panels at Kochi airport turn sunlight straight into electricity.
- e.g. A dosa's energy came from rice and urad dal plants, which caught sunlight in a field.
### Energy in living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living thing needs energy to grow, move, repair their body and stay alive. Plants take it from sunlight. Animals, people, fungi and most microbes take it from food.

A plant spends the sunlight they catch on growing new leaves, roots, flowers and seeds. A crow at your window spends energy from their food on flying, keeping warm and finding more food. Your body uses energy even while you sleep, to keep your heart beating and keep you warm. A resting child gives out about as much heat as an old 60 watt bulb. Living things choose how to spend their energy. A plant in shade grows taller to reach the light. A plant in the open grows more leaves instead.
- e.g. A sprouting moong seed lives on the food stored inside them until their first leaves catch sunlight.
- e.g. Ants carry food back to their nest to feed the whole colony.
- e.g. You feel hungry after running, because your body has passed on a lot of energy.
### What is energy, really? · *idea*
Scientists can measure energy very exactly, in joules, and they have never found a case where it is made or lost. But what energy is, underneath, nobody can say. The physicist Richard Feynman said in his lectures that in physics today we have no knowledge of what energy is. We only know rules for counting it. Some scientists say energy is just a number that stays the same. Others think of it as something real that flows from thing to thing. This is still an open question.
- e.g. We count the energy in food in kilocalories, and the energy in electricity in units on the meter bill.
- e.g. Albert Einstein showed in 1905 that mass is a form of energy too, so even a still stone holds a huge amount.
- **Richard Feynman, physicist:** Energy is a number we can work out for any situation, and the total never changes. We do not know what it is, only that the count always comes out the same.
- **Many teachers and engineers:** It helps to think of energy as something real that is passed along, like water poured from one pot to the next. This picture works for almost every everyday problem.

## Mostly agreed
- Energy is never made or destroyed. It only passes on and changes form. *How we know:* In the 1840s, James Joule measured that the same amount of work always made the same amount of heat. Since then, every careful measurement has agreed.
- A moving thing's energy grows much faster than its speed. Twice as fast means about four times as much energy. *How we know:* Balls dropped into soft clay by Willem 's Gravesande in 1722 made dents that grew this way. Engineers use the same rule today to work out the force of crashes.
- Almost all the energy used by living things on Earth comes from sunlight. *How we know:* Plants and tiny sea life make food from sunlight, and every food chain studied starts with them, except a few deep-sea communities around hot vents.
- When energy changes form, some of it nearly always spreads out as heat. *How we know:* A bulb, a mixie motor, a running child and a stopping bicycle all get warm. Thermometers show the warming every time.
- The Sun's energy comes from hydrogen joining into helium in its centre. *How we know:* Physicists worked this out in the 1920s and 1930s. Tiny particles called neutrinos, made by these reactions, have been caught in detectors on Earth.

## How we found out
**Willem 's Gravesande, a Dutch scientist, and Émilie du Châtelet, a French scientist, testing an idea of Gottfried Leibniz, 1686 (Leibniz's idea), 1722 (the clay test) and 1740 (du Châtelet's book).** In the 1600s, scientists argued about what a moving ball carries with it. René Descartes, a French thinker, said it was the ball's size times its speed. So a ball moving twice as fast should hit twice as hard. In 1686, Gottfried Leibniz, a German thinker, disagreed. He said it was the ball's weight times its speed times its speed again. Then twice as fast would hit four times as hard. In 1722, Willem 's Gravesande, a Dutch scientist, tested it. He dropped brass balls into soft clay from different heights and measured the dents. A ball that landed twice as fast did not make a dent twice as deep. It made a dent about four times as deep. Émilie du Châtelet, a French scientist, saw what this meant. In her physics book of 1740, she explained that the clay dents agreed with Leibniz. Her book was read and argued about across Europe. In 1807, Thomas Young, an English scientist, began to call this quantity energy. Your Atta Crater Test is a small copy of the clay test.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists, following Descartes, thought a ball moving twice as fast carries twice as much and hits twice as hard. The clay dents showed that twice as fast does about four times as much.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Energy gets used up.** (A very common idea, used in everyday talk; *rejected*)  
  For: Cells go flat, petrol tanks run empty, and you feel tired after running. It really looks as if the energy is gone.  
  Against: The energy has passed on and spread out, mostly as warmth in the air. Careful measurements always find all of it. What is 'used up' is energy in a form that is easy to use.
- **Electricity is where our energy comes from.** (A common idea, because we get it from a socket; *rejected*)  
  For: Plug in a fan and it runs. Unplug it and it stops. So it seems that the socket is the source.  
  Against: Electricity only carries energy along wires. The energy comes from a power station, which got it from coal, gas, wind, sunlight, falling water or uranium.
- **A still thing has no energy.** (A common first idea; *rejected*)  
  For: A stone on the floor does nothing, makes no sound and is not hot or bright.  
  Against: Lift the stone onto a shelf and it has stored energy, ready to fall. And Einstein showed that the stuff of every thing is itself a huge store of energy. Even a still stone is full of it.
- **A clever machine could keep running for ever without any energy put in.** (Many inventors over hundreds of years, who built 'perpetual motion' machines; *rejected*)  
  For: Wheels with rolling weights and magnets look as if they should keep turning by themselves.  
  Against: Every one ever tested has slowed down and stopped. Some of the energy always spreads out as heat through rubbing, so a machine needs new energy to keep going.
- **The total energy of the whole universe may be exactly zero.** (Edward Tryon, an American physicist, in 1973, and others since; *debated*)  
  For: The energy of all the stuff in the universe might be exactly balanced by the negative energy of gravity pulling it together.  
  Against: Energy in a whole, expanding universe is hard to define, and some physicists say the question has no clear meaning.

## Roots
We have learned that energy makes things happen, and that it is passed on from one thing to another. Now we ask what energy tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** The marble dropped from higher dug a wider crater and made a louder thud. The extra energy came from your arm, which lifted it higher. Your arm's energy came from food, and the food's energy from sunlight. So that crater in the atta was dug, step by step, with sunlight.  
*Habit it breaks:* We speak of moving, lighting, heating and sounding as separate happenings, and we think each thing makes its own energy. But nothing makes the energy it uses. It is passed on from somewhere else, and you can follow it back, step by step.  
*Evidence:* From 80 cm the marble made a wider crater and a louder thud than from 20 cm. Lifting the water bottle above your head ten times tired your arm more than lifting it to your knee. More energy went in when you lifted higher, and more came out in the crater and the sound.

- *who am i:* Every move you make, and the warmth of your body, runs on energy from your food. That energy was sunlight caught by plants.
- *what is life:* A plant catches sunlight and stores the Sun's energy in food. Every animal, you too, lives on that store, passed from one living thing to the next.
- *what is this world:* Wind, rain, rivers and the food on your plate are all moved by the Sun's energy. Almost everything that happens on Earth starts with sunlight.

Moving, lighting, heating and sounding look like four different things. Underneath, they are one thing, energy, showing up in four ways. It is never made fresh by the thing that uses it. It passes from the Sun to a leaf, to a grain, to your arm, to a falling marble, to a thud, and the total never changes. So all the different happenings of a day are one supply of energy, passed from hand to hand.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2 (Bhrigu Valli):** Bhrigu asks his father Varuna what the source of all things is. His first answer is food: from food beings are born, by food they live, and into food they go when they die. (Your running, your warmth and the marble you lifted all ran on energy from food. When you die, your body becomes food for other living things, and the energy passes on again.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment 90 (Diels-Kranz numbering), about 500 BCE:** All things are an exchange for fire, and fire for all things, as goods are exchanged for gold and gold for goods. (Energy works like that gold. Sunlight is exchanged for rice, rice for movement, movement for a crater and a sound. The form changes, but the amount is kept.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding, 1988:** Look deeply into a sheet of paper and you can see the sunshine in it. Without sunshine, the tree could not grow, and there would be no paper. (Look deeply at the atta in your tray, or the idli on your plate, and you can see the sunshine in them too. The wheat and the rice grew from it.)
- Science (settled): Energy is never made or destroyed. It only passes on and changes form. This is the conservation of energy.
- Science (settled): Almost all the energy used by life on Earth comes from the Sun, caught by plants and tiny sea life. A few deep-sea communities live on chemical energy from inside the Earth instead.
- Science (open): What energy is, underneath, nobody can say. Richard Feynman said that we have no knowledge of what energy is, only rules for counting it.
- Other view, Many physicists: Energy is a number that scientists work out, and it stays the same when nothing gets in or out. Saying that sunlight dug the crater is a pleasant way of telling the story, but the number itself tells us nothing about meaning or oneness. It is simply a useful tool for predicting what will happen.
- Practice, Sunshine in my hand (3 min): Let's sit by a sunny window. Hold one palm in the sunshine and close your eyes. Feel it slowly warm up. That light left the Sun about eight minutes ago. Now put that hand on your chest. Feel your own warmth and your heartbeat. That warmth came from your breakfast, which grew in sunshine. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly, three times.
- Ask: If almost all your energy came from the Sun, is part of the Sun in you?
- Ask: When the marble stopped in the atta, its energy spread out as a tiny bit of warmth. Is it lost, or only harder to find?
- Ask: A plant decides how to spend the sunlight they catch: on roots, leaves or seeds. How does your body decide how to spend your energy?

## Story: Where the Pongal Came From
*Thai Pongal is a real harvest festival of Tamil Nadu, held in mid-January, and it thanks the Sun for the harvest. The milk and rice are boiled until they overflow, and people call out 'Pongalo Pongal!'. Tamil Nadu really gets electricity from wind farms such as Muppandal and from the Kudankulam nuclear power station. Kavya, Arjun, Paati and their flat are made up.*

It was the morning of Thai Pongal, and the flat on the fourth floor in Chennai smelled of jaggery and cardamom. Paati had drawn a kolam at the door before sunrise. Now a new clay pot sat on the gas stove, full of milk, with a turmeric plant tied around the pot's neck.

Kavya was ten. Her brother Arjun was six, and he was standing on a stool, too close to the stove. 'Back a step,' said Kavya, and she pulled the stool back. 'The pot gets very hot.'

'When will it boil over?' asked Arjun. 'Soon,' said Paati. 'The flame is giving its heat to the pot, and the pot is giving it to the milk. When the milk has taken enough, it will rise up.'

They waited. Tiny bubbles came, then bigger ones. Then the milk climbed up the sides of the pot and spilled over in a white wave. 'Pongalo Pongal!' they all shouted, and Arjun banged a steel plate with a spoon until Paati covered her ears.

Paati added the rice and the jaggery, and stirred. Arjun watched the flame. 'Akka,' he said, 'where does the fire come from?'

'From the gas,' said Kavya. 'The red cylinder under the counter is full of it. When it burns, it gives out heat and light.'

'And where does the gas come from?' Kavya thought. She had read about this in her science book. 'It comes from deep under the ground and the sea. It formed there over millions of years, from tiny living things in old seas. They grew using sunlight. So the gas is sunlight from long, long ago, stored up.'

Arjun looked at the blue flame as if it might wink at him. 'So the Pongal is cooked by old sunshine?' 'Yes,' said Kavya. 'And the rice in it grew in a field, in this year's sunshine. The plants caught the sunlight in their leaves and stored it in the grains.'

Arjun jumped off the stool and ran to the window, where the ceiling fan was turning. 'What about the fan? There's no sun in the fan.'

'The fan runs on electricity,' said Kavya. 'Electricity is like a road. It carries energy from a power station to our flat. Some of Chennai's electricity comes from the giant windmills near Kanyakumari. We saw them from the bus last year, remember?' Arjun nodded. He remembered their slow white arms.

'And the wind?' 'The Sun warms the land and the sea by different amounts,' said Kavya. 'Warm air rises, and cooler air rushes in to take its place. That rushing air is the wind. So the fan is pushed round by sunshine too, in a roundabout way.'

Arjun frowned. He liked catching his sister out. 'What about my plate banging? That was me. That wasn't the Sun.'

Kavya laughed. 'Where did you get the energy to bang it?' Arjun thought. 'From my idli,' he said slowly. 'And the idli came from rice and urad dal. And they grew in...' He stopped. 'In the sunshine,' he said, and he looked almost cross about it.

Paati turned off the stove and laughed too. 'That is why we say thank you to the Sun today,' she said. 'Pongal is the day we thank the Sun for the harvest. Our grandmothers knew the food came from the Sun. Now your sister tells you the fan does too.'

Arjun was quiet for a while, watching the sweet pongal steam. 'Is there anything that isn't the Sun?' he asked at last.

Kavya had to think hard. 'Some of our electricity comes from Kudankulam,' she said. 'That's a nuclear power station. It uses a metal called uranium, which has energy locked inside it. The uranium was made long before the Sun was born, in stars that exploded. So that energy is even older than the Sun.'

'Then what does the Sun run on?' asked Arjun. 'Hydrogen,' said Kavya. 'In the middle of the Sun, tiny particles of hydrogen are squeezed so hard that they join together. Joining gives out huge amounts of energy. That energy comes out as the light that grew our rice.'

Arjun took his steel plate and spoon to the window, where the morning sun lay in a bright square on the floor. He held the plate in the sunshine for a moment. Then he banged it once, very hard. 'Thank you, Sun,' he said. 'For the noise.'

**For the parent:** Almost everything that happened on Pongal morning, the boiling milk, the turning fan and Arjun's banging, ran on energy that came from the Sun and was passed on, step by step.

- Ask: Kavya said the gas is 'sunlight from long ago, stored up'. What did she mean?
- Ask: Arjun thought the banging was only his own energy. Where did it really come from?
- Ask: Which energy in the story did not come from the Sun?
- Ask: Pick one thing that happened in your home this morning. Can you follow its energy back to the Sun?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Atta Crater Test: does a higher drop do more? (35 min)
- **question:** Does a marble dropped from higher up make a wider crater in atta, and a louder thud?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will a marble dropped from 80 cm make a wider crater than one dropped from 20 cm? How much wider: a little, twice as wide, or four times as wide? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same marble, the same tray of atta and the same way of letting go. Only the height changes. Smooth the atta flat before every drop. Drop 3 times from each height, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Do it on the floor, on newspaper. Drop the marble, never throw it. Haldi stains clothes. Keep marbles away from children under 3, who may swallow them.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw one crater from each height, from above, at its real size.
- **results table:** Height of the drop | Crater width, try 1 (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | The thud: soft, medium or loud?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread newspaper on the floor and put the container on it. 2. Pour in atta about 3 cm deep. 3. Shake it gently from side to side, then smooth the top with the ruler. 4. Sprinkle a thin dusting of ragi flour over the top through the tea strainer.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to drop a marble into this atta from different heights. The dark dusting on top will show us where the atta is thrown.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the atta into the tub. Make the top flat with a ruler. Shake a thin dark dusting on top. Now it is ready.
  - Note: Use old atta, or atta you will later use for play dough, because the marble and the dark powder go into it. A layer of 3 cm or more matters: if it is too thin, the marble hits the bottom and the craters stop growing. Ragi flour on white atta shows the thrown atta best.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the marble and the three heights on the ruler: 20 cm, 40 cm and 80 cm. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will drop the marble from 20, 40 and 80 centimetres. Which crater will be widest? Will it be a little wider, or much wider? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will drop the marble from three heights. Which crater will be widest? How much wider? Say why you think so.
  - Ask: What do you think will happen, and why? (good: The highest drop will make the biggest crater, because it falls faster.; Four times the height might make it four times as wide.; It will be louder from higher up.)
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. Many children guess that four times the height will make a crater four times as wide. Don't correct it now: the atta will answer it.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the ruler upright on the atta. 2. Hold the marble with its bottom at the 20 cm mark. 3. Open your fingers and let it fall. Do not throw it. 4. Listen to the thud. 5. Lift the marble out with a spoon. 6. Measure the hole from edge to edge, not the dark splash lines. 7. Smooth and dust again. Do 3 drops.  
  *Parent says:* Let it drop, don't push it. Then measure across the hole, from one edge to the other. We smooth the atta every time, so each drop starts the same.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the marble at 20 cm. Let go. Don't throw! Listen to the thud. Take the marble out with a spoon. Measure across the hole. Smooth it and do it again. Three drops.
  - Note: Hold a ruler just above the crater, not pressing into it, to measure. Read to the nearest half centimetre. The three widths may differ by half a centimetre or so. That's normal, and is why we drop three times.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do 3 more drops from 40 cm, measuring and smoothing each time, in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now from twice as high. Watch the dark dusting. Does more atta get thrown?  
  *Child mode:* Now drop from 40 cm, three times. Measure each hole. Is it wider? Is the thud louder?
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do 3 more drops from 80 cm. 2. The child may need to stand while a grown-up holds the tape. 3. Measure and smooth each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now from 80 centimetres. That is four times as high as the first drop. Look at the crater, and at the white streaks of atta thrown out.  
  *Child mode:* Now drop from 80 cm, three times. Stand up if you need to. Measure each hole. Look at the white streaks too.
  - Note: From 80 cm the crater is wider and the dark dust is thrown further. But it is usually far less than four times as wide as the 20 cm crater. In our tries it was less than twice as wide. Talk about this in the next steps.
  - Check: The marble from 80 cm made a wider crater and a louder thud than from 20 cm. Why? → **It landed faster, so it carried more energy**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give your child a full 1 litre water bottle. 2. Ask them to lift it from the floor to their knee 10 times. 3. Rest. 4. Then from the floor to above their head 10 times. 5. Ask which tired their arm more.  
  *Parent says:* Where did the marble's extra energy come from? Let's find out with this bottle. Lift it low ten times, then high ten times.  
  *Child mode:* Lift the bottle from the floor to your knee, ten times. Rest. Now lift it from the floor to above your head, ten times. Which was harder work?
  - Ask: Which lifting was harder? What does that tell you about the marble? (good: Lifting high was harder, so it took more energy.; When I lifted the marble higher, I gave it more energy, and that energy made the bigger crater.)
  - Note: The marble is too light to feel the difference, so the bottle stands in for it. Lifting higher takes more energy from your muscles. That energy is stored in the lifted thing, and comes out when it falls.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a marble dropped from higher has more energy when it lands. What I saw: from 20 cm the crater was about 4 and a half cm wide, and from 80 cm about 6 and a half cm, with a louder thud. Why: lifting it higher took more energy from my arm. The marble stored that energy and it became movement as it fell. When it landed, the energy pushed atta aside and made the sound. Ask: why was the crater not four times as wide? A good guess is that a wider crater has to move much more atta.
### e2 · Sunshine in a Tumbler (25 min)

- **predict:** Guess first. After one hour, which tumbler of water will feel warmer: the one in the sun or the one in the shade? Will you be able to feel the difference with your finger? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two tumblers that are the same, with the same amount of water from the same tap, poured at the same time. Put both in the same room or balcony, one in direct sunshine and one in the shade. Only the sunshine changes.
- **safety:** Never look straight at the Sun. Keep the tumblers where they won't fall off the windowsill.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the two tumblers, with the Sun's rays reaching one of them.
- **results table:** Tumbler | Feel at the start (cool, same, warm) | Feel after 1 hour | Thermometer reading, if you have one
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill both tumblers with one cup of tap water each. 2. Ask your child to dip a finger in each. They should feel the same. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* These two tumblers are the same, with the same water. We'll put one in the sun and one in the shade. What will happen after an hour, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Dip a finger in each tumbler. Do they feel the same? Now guess. Which one will be warmer after an hour? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen, and why? (good: The sunny one will be warmer, because the Sun gives heat.; Both will warm up a little, but the sunny one more.)
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one tumbler where direct sunshine falls on it. 2. Put the other in the shade, in the same room or balcony. 3. Set a timer for 1 hour.  
  *Parent says:* One in the sun, one in the shade. Now we wait an hour.  
  *Child mode:* Put one tumbler in the sunshine. Put the other in the shade. Set a timer for one hour.
  - Note: Choose a time when sunshine falls on the spot for the whole hour: check where the Sun's patch moves. If the day is cloudy, try another day. The difference is easy to feel on a sunny day.
  - Wait: Leave both tumblers for 1 hour.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After an hour, bring the two tumblers side by side. 2. Your child dips one finger in each at the same time. 3. If you have a thermometer, put it in each for one minute and read it.  
  *Parent says:* Now feel them both at once, one finger in each. Which is warmer?  
  *Child mode:* Put one finger in each tumbler at the same time. Which one is warmer? Write it down.
  - Note: On a sunny day, the water in the sun is usually several degrees warmer after an hour. You can feel it clearly with both fingers in at once.
  - Check: The tumbler in the sunshine got warmer. Where did that energy come from? → **From sunlight, which carries energy from the Sun**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you feel that shows it? Why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I felt. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: sunlight carries energy. What I saw: after an hour, the water in the sun felt warmer than the water in the shade. Why: the Sun's light landed on the tumbler and the water, and its energy turned into heat.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about one more thing. Ask: on a hot afternoon, why does the balcony floor in the sun burn your feet, while the shady part does not?  
  *Parent says:* The same thing happens to the balcony floor. Why is the sunny part so hot under your feet?  
  *Child mode:* Think of the balcony floor in the afternoon. Why is the sunny part hot under your feet?
  - Ask: Why is the sunny floor hot? (good: Sunlight lands on it and its energy turns into heat.; The shady part gets less sunlight, so less energy.)

### Energy on the Move (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Priya drops a marble into atta from 20 cm, then from 80 cm, four times as high. The second crater is  
   Options: exactly four times as wide / wider, but much less than four times as wide / exactly the same width / narrower  
   **Answer:** wider, but much less than four times as wide. *Why:* The higher drop gives the marble more energy, so the crater is wider. But a crater twice as wide must push aside much more atta than twice as much. So the width grows much more slowly than the height. In made-up results, 4.5 cm grew to only about 6.5 cm.
2. (write) In the Atta Crater Test, we smoothed the atta and used the same marble every time. Why did that matter?  
   **Answer:** So that the only thing that changed was the height. Then any difference in the craters must come from the height.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea of a fair test: change one thing, keep everything else the same.
3. (truefalse) When a spinning top slows down and stops, its energy has been destroyed.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The top's movement energy passed into the floor and the air through rubbing, as a tiny bit of warmth. Energy is never destroyed, only passed on.

   **Answer:** ['the coconut hangs high up with stored energy', 'the coconut lets go of the tree', 'the coconut speeds up as it falls', 'the coconut lands, and the energy becomes sound and pushes the soil']. *Why:* Stored energy from height becomes movement energy as the coconut speeds up. When it lands, the energy passes into sound, the moving soil and a little warmth.
5. (write) A sleeping cat is not moving, lighting up or making a sound. Is she using energy? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Yes. She is breathing, her heart is beating, and her body stays warm. All of that uses energy from her food.. *Why:* A strong answer names at least one thing inside the cat's body that needs energy, such as her heartbeat or keeping warm.

7. (match) Match each energy change to where it happens.  
   **Answer:** electrical energy into movement → a mixie; stored energy in food into movement → you cycling; light into heat → a balcony floor in the sun; movement into sound → a marble landing on a steel plate; stored energy in gas into heat → a stove flame. *Why:* In each case, energy changes from one form to another. A strong answer could also name the heat that nearly always comes out too.
8. (fill) The rule that energy is never made and never destroyed is called the ____ of energy.  
   **Answer:** ['conservation']. *Why:* Conservation means keeping. The total amount of energy is always kept the same.
9. (draw) Draw an energy chain from the Sun to a lit diya. Draw at least 4 steps, with an arrow between each.  
   **Answer:** For example: the Sun, a sesame or groundnut plant, oil pressed from the seeds, the diya flame giving light and heat.. *Why:* A strong drawing has steps in the right order, and each step really gets its energy from the step before.

   Options: Wind / Coal / Uranium / Falling water in a dam  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Uranium was made in stars before the Sun was born. Wind is moved by the Sun's heat, coal came from ancient plants that grew in sunlight, and the Sun's heat lifted the water into the clouds before it fell as rain.

It gives Ishaan his due: we only see energy when something happens, and nothing shows while the ball sits still.. *Why:* Scientists call Tanvi's idea stored energy, or potential energy. Ishaan is right that we notice energy only by what it does.
12. (mcq) Nandini's mother gives her two bulbs that give the same light. One gets very hot and one stays only a little warm. Which one passes less energy on as heat?

Like the falling book in the Chain Reaction Bell, every lifted thing brings its own stored energy.

### Energy Thinker (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) In 1722, Willem 's Gravesande dropped brass balls into soft clay. A ball that landed twice as fast made a dent about four times as deep. Why was that a surprise to people at the time?  
   **Answer:** Many scientists thought a ball twice as fast would hit twice as hard. The dent four times as deep showed that a faster ball carries much more energy than they thought.. *Why:* A strong answer names the old idea (twice as fast, twice as hard) and says what the dents showed instead.
2. (perspective) Scientists in Europe argued about this for many years in the 1700s.  
   **Answer:** The dents support du Châtelet: twice the speed gave four times the dent. But the other side was not simply wrong. Weight times speed is also a real quantity, which scientists now call momentum. It tells how hard a thing is to stop in a given time. Both sides were measuring something true, but different.. *Why:* This was a real argument. A strong answer uses the evidence for du Châtelet, and also sees that the Descartes side had found a different true quantity.
3. (mcq) Kavya drops a marble from 20 cm. To make it land twice as fast, from what height should she drop it?  
   Options: 40 cm / 80 cm / 60 cm / 20 cm, but throw it  
   **Answer:** 80 cm. *Why:* To land twice as fast, a falling thing must fall from four times the height. 4 times 20 cm is 80 cm. This is because energy grows with speed times speed. Twice the speed needs four times the energy, and four times the energy needs four times the height.
4. (write) We say a cell is 'used up' when the torch stops working. Explain what really happened to its energy.  
   **Answer:** The energy was carried by electricity to the bulb. The bulb turned it into light and a little heat. The light landed on walls and turned into warmth. So the energy spread out into the room as a tiny bit of warmth. It is still there, but too spread out to use.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the energy step by step until it spreads out as warmth, and says why spread-out warmth is hard to use again.
5. (truefalse) A clever enough machine could keep running for ever without any new energy.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every real machine passes some energy on as heat through rubbing, so it slows down unless new energy is put in. Many inventors have tried for hundreds of years, and none has worked.
6. (draw) Design a machine that uses falling water to ring a bell. Draw it, and label where the energy starts and every change it goes through.  
   **Answer:** For example: a bottle of water on a high shelf, a pipe, a small paper wheel turned by the falling water, a string on the wheel that pulls a spoon against a tumbler. Labels: stored energy from height, movement, sound.. *Why:* A strong design starts with water high up, uses its fall to turn or push something, and ends in sound. Labels follow the energy in order.
7. (mcq) A resting child gives out about as much heat as an old 60 watt bulb. Where does this heat come from?  
   Options: From the air around the child / From the energy in the food the child ate / From the bed / From the Sun shining on the child right now  
   **Answer:** From the energy in the food the child ate. *Why:* Your body uses energy from food all the time, even while resting, to keep your heart beating and your body working. Much of it comes out as heat. That is why a room full of people gets warm.
8. (write) Revathi says, 'Since energy is never destroyed, we don't need to save electricity.' What would you tell her?  
   **Answer:** The total energy stays the same, but after we use it, it is spread out as warmth that we can't use again. Making new electricity often means burning coal or building power stations. So saving electricity means needing less of that.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the total amount of energy, which never changes, from useful energy, which runs down.
9. (match) Match each Indian power station to the source of its energy.  
   **Answer:** Muppandal, Tamil Nadu → wind; Idukki, Kerala → falling water; Kudankulam, Tamil Nadu → uranium; Pavagada, Karnataka → sunlight. *Why:* Muppandal has hundreds of wind turbines, Idukki uses water stored behind a dam, Kudankulam is a nuclear power station, and Pavagada is a huge solar park. All but Kudankulam trace back to the Sun.
10. (order) Put these in order, from the one that gives most of its energy as light to the one that gives the least. All three use the same electricity.  
   **Answer:** ['an LED bulb', 'an old filament bulb', 'an electric heater']. *Why:* An LED turns much of its energy into light. An old filament bulb turns only a small part into light and most into heat. A heater is made to turn nearly all of it into heat, with only a little red glow.
11. (label) Label the parts of a falling marble's journey: stored energy, speeding up, movement energy at the bottom, crater and sound.  
   **Answer:** stored energy at the top where it is held, speeding up along the fall, movement energy just before it lands, crater and sound at the atta.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the energy from stored, to movement, to the crater and sound.
12. (fill) The unit scientists use to measure energy is called the ____. It is about the energy needed to lift a small apple one metre.  
   **Answer:** ['joule']. *Why:* The joule is named after James Joule, who measured how work makes heat in the 1840s.
13. (write) The Sun will one day run out of hydrogen to join into helium. What would happen on Earth if the Sun stopped shining? Follow at least two energy chains.  
   **Answer:** Plants could not make food, so animals and people would run out of food. Without the Sun's heat, there would be no wind and no rain filling the dams. The Earth would get very cold.. *Why:* A strong answer follows at least two chains, such as food and wind or rain, and sees how much depends on sunlight. (The Sun has billions of years left.)
14. (sort) Sort these by whether the energy is stored or happening now.  
   **Answer:** a full gas cylinder → Stored energy; a cooker whistling → Energy happening now; a charged phone switched off → Stored energy; a coconut high on the tree → Stored energy; a marble falling → Energy happening now; a sunbeam on the floor → Energy happening now. *Why:* Stored energy waits until something lets it out. Moving, sounding, lighting and heating are energy happening now. A strong answer could say what would let each stored one out.
15. (mcq) Ishita runs up four floors of stairs in her building in Pune. At the top she feels hot and out of breath. Why does she feel hot?  
   Options: The stairs were hot / Her muscles passed on energy from her food, and much of it came out as heat / Running makes cold air leave her body / The building's lift was off

## Applications
- Power stations turn wind, falling water, sunlight, coal, gas or uranium into electricity for homes.
- Engineers work out how much energy a moving car or train carries, to design brakes and crash barriers.
- Doctors and dieticians measure the energy in food, to help people eat enough.
- Solar panels on roofs and at airports, such as Kochi, turn sunlight into electricity.
- LED bulbs are used to give the same light with less energy wasted as heat.
- Builders use energy stored in a lifted weight: a pile driver drops a heavy block to hammer posts into the ground.

## Spotted around us
- Rows of tall wind turbines at Muppandal and Aralvaimozhi, near Kanyakumari, turning in the wind that blows through the hills.
- Blue solar panels on the roofs of flats, schools and temples, and on the big solar fields at Cochin airport.
- The pressure cooker whistle in almost every Indian kitchen: heat makes steam, and steam pushes up the whistle.
- Dams like Idukki in Kerala, where water falling from high up spins turbines to make electricity.
- Your own warm hands on a cold morning, warmed by the energy from your breakfast.

## Open questions
- **What is energy, really?** Scientists can count it very exactly and it always adds up, but nobody can say what it is underneath.
- **What is dark energy?** The universe is spreading out faster and faster. Scientists call whatever is causing this 'dark energy', but nobody knows what it is.
- **Does the whole universe add up to zero energy?** Some physicists think the energy of all the stuff is balanced by gravity. Others say the question has no clear answer.
- **How can we store sunlight cheaply for the night?** Batteries work but cost a lot. Scientists are testing new batteries, pumped water and making fuels from sunlight.

## Connected maths
- In the Atta Crater Test, Kavya's three craters from 20 cm were 4 cm, 5 cm and 6 cm wide. What was the average width? **5 cm.**. Add the three widths: 4 + 5 + 6 = 15 cm. Share 15 among 3 tries: 15 divided by 3 is 5. So the average width is 5 cm. The average is one number that stands for all three tries. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Kavya dropped the marble from 20 cm, 40 cm and 80 cm. What is the pattern in the heights? What would the next height be? **Each height is double the one before. The next would be 160 cm.**. 20 doubled is 40. 40 doubled is 80. 80 doubled is 160. 160 cm is taller than most grown-ups, so a grown-up would need to stand on a stool. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- Here are made-up results: from 20 cm the crater was 4.5 cm wide, and from 80 cm it was 6.5 cm wide. The height went up 4 times. Did the width go up 4 times? How much wider was it? **No. It was only 2 cm wider, much less than 4 times.**. 4 times 4.5 cm would be 18 cm. The real crater was 6.5 cm. 6.5 minus 4.5 is 2, so it was 2 cm wider. The marble had 4 times the energy, but a wider crater must move much more atta. So the width grows slowly. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- Lifting a 100 gram apple 1 metre takes about 1 joule. How many joules does it take to lift 5 such apples 1 metre? What about lifting 1 apple 3 metres? **About 5 joules, and about 3 joules.**. Each apple lifted 1 metre takes about 1 joule, so 5 apples take 5 times as much: 5 joules. One apple lifted 3 metres is like 3 lifts of 1 metre each: 3 joules. More weight or more height means more energy. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In Pass the Sunshine, the yellow ball started with 6 sheets. One sheet was torn off into the HEAT bowl at each of the 5 passes. How many sheets were left in the ball? How many in the bowl? How many altogether? **1 sheet in the ball, 5 in the bowl, 6 altogether.**. 6 sheets minus 5 torn off leaves 1. The bowl has 5. 1 + 5 = 6, the same as at the start. Nothing was lost: it was only moved to the bowl. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
