# Measuring things: units, instruments, and why a coast has more than one length
`sci.how-we-know.measuring-things.L4`

**Summary.** Old school books say India's coast is 7,516 kilometres long. A newer count says 11,098 kilometres. The coast did not grow, so how can both be right? In this lesson you meet the units for length, mass, volume, time and temperature, and learn to choose and read the right instrument. You build a water clock from a bottle, test whether it keeps steady time, and ask what a measurement can tell us and what it cannot.

**Intro.** *Measure the width of a table in hand spans. Now ask a grown-up to measure the same table in their hand spans. You get two different numbers for one table. Which number is the table's real width?* Neither number is wrong. Each of you compared the table with your own hand and counted. The table stayed the same, and the hands were different. This small puzzle is the reason people invented the metre, the kilogram and the second: units that are the same for everyone. Today you will learn how those units were chosen, how to use the instruments that carry them, and where measuring stops being simple.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can give everyday examples where measuring is needed, such as buying goods, cooking, giving medicine and telling the time.
1. I can explain that measuring means comparing with a unit and counting, and that a measurement needs both a number and a unit.
2. I can name the standard units for length, mass, volume, time and temperature, and say why the whole world shares them.
3. I can choose a suitable instrument for a job and read it correctly.
4. I can explain why no measurement is perfectly exact, and why some things have no single true length.
5. I can estimate a length, a mass or a volume before I measure it.
6. I can say what measuring cannot tell us, and give one example.

## Terms
- **Measure**: To find out how much there is of something, as a number, by comparing it with a unit. *e.g.* You measure a table by laying your hand along it and counting how many hands fit.
- **Unit**: A fixed amount that we choose to compare other amounts with. *e.g.* One hand span is a unit. A table nine hand spans wide has been measured in that unit.
- **Measurement**: A number together with its unit. The number alone says nothing. *e.g.* '5' means nothing. '5 centimetres' is the width of a matchbox.
- **Hand span**: The distance from the tip of the thumb to the tip of the little finger when your hand is spread wide. *e.g.* A child's hand span is shorter than a grown-up's, so the same table needs more of the child's spans.
- **Body unit**: A unit taken from part of the body, such as a finger width, a hand span, a forearm or a foot. *e.g.* A flower seller in Madurai measures jasmine strings by the length of her forearm.
- **Forearm**: The part of the arm from the elbow to the wrist. As a unit, it is measured from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger. *e.g.* In Tamil the forearm length is called a muzham, and some flower sellers still use it.
- **Standard unit**: A unit that many people agree to keep exactly the same, by copying it from one shared example. *e.g.* A metre is the same in Kochi and in Kolkata, so a tailor in one can sew for a customer in the other.
- **Balance**: An instrument that finds how heavy something is by setting it against known weights until the two sides match. *e.g.* Vegetables on one pan and metal weights on the other. When the beam is level, the two sides match.
- **Weight**: A piece of metal made to a known mass, used on a balance. In daily talk, weight also means how heavy something is. *e.g.* A shopkeeper puts a one-kilogram weight on one pan and rice on the other.
- **Metric system**: A set of units built on the metre, the kilogram and the second. Bigger and smaller units are made by multiplying or dividing by ten. *e.g.* A doctor in Kochi and a doctor in Brazil both write a dose in milligrams and mean the same amount.
- **Metre**: The standard unit of length. It is about the distance from the floor to a door handle. *e.g.* A cricket pitch is a little over 20 metres long.
- **Prefix**: A short word put in front of a unit to make it bigger or smaller. *e.g.* Kilo in front of metre makes kilometre, which is 1,000 metres.
- **Kilo**: A prefix that means one thousand. *e.g.* A kilogram is 1,000 grams, and a kilometre is 1,000 metres.
- **Centi**: A prefix that means one hundredth. *e.g.* There are 100 centimetres in one metre.
- **Milli**: A prefix that means one thousandth. *e.g.* There are 1,000 millilitres in one litre, and 1,000 millimetres in one metre.
- **Length**: How long something is from one end to the other. *e.g.* The length of a pencil is about 15 centimetres.
- **Centimetre**: A unit of length. One hundredth of a metre. A finger is about one centimetre wide. *e.g.* A matchbox is about 5 centimetres wide.
- **Millimetre**: A unit of length. One thousandth of a metre. A fingernail is about one millimetre thick. *e.g.* The rain in India is reported in millimetres.
- **Kilometre**: A unit of length for journeys. One thousand metres. *e.g.* A road sign in India that says 'Madurai 40' means 40 kilometres.
- **Tape**: A long, bendy strip with units marked along it. A tailor's soft tape goes round a body, and a steel tape rolls into a case. *e.g.* A tailor wraps a soft tape round a waist, which a stiff ruler cannot do.
- **Mass**: The amount of matter in something. Its standard unit is the kilogram. *e.g.* A one-litre bottle of water has a mass of about one kilogram.
- **Kilogram**: The standard unit of mass. One thousand grams. *e.g.* A packet of sugar in a shop is usually one kilogram.
- **Gram**: A unit of mass. One thousandth of a kilogram. A paper clip is about one gram. *e.g.* A letter at the post office is weighed in grams.
- **Tonne**: A unit of mass equal to 1,000 kilograms. *e.g.* A lorry is weighed in tonnes at a weighbridge.
- **Volume**: The amount of space something takes up. *e.g.* A tall thin tumbler and a short wide bowl can have the same volume.
- **Litre**: The usual unit of volume for liquids. It is the space inside a cube 10 centimetres along each edge. *e.g.* A bucket holds about 15 litres, and a packet of milk is often half a litre.
- **Millilitre**: A unit of volume. One thousandth of a litre. A teaspoon holds about 5 millilitres. *e.g.* A cough syrup cap is marked at 5 and 10 millilitres.
- **Measuring cylinder**: A tall tube or jug with marks on the side, used to measure the volume of a liquid. *e.g.* A kitchen measuring jug works like a measuring cylinder.
- **Time**: What clocks measure. Nobody has a final account of what time is, only ways to count it. *e.g.* You measure how long an egg boils by counting minutes on a clock.
- **Second**: The standard unit of time. A slow clap takes about one second. *e.g.* Sixty seconds make one minute.
- **Clock**: Anything that repeats in a steady way and can be counted. *e.g.* A swinging pendulum, a dripping tap and the Sun's shadow can all be used as clocks.
- **Water clock**: A clock that uses water running out of a hole or into a bowl, so that the water's movement counts the time. *e.g.* In old India a copper bowl with a small hole floated in water and sank after one ghati.
- **Ghati**: An old Indian unit of time, equal to 24 minutes. Sixty ghatis make one day and one night. *e.g.* A copper bowl with a small hole floated on water and sank after one ghati.
- **Sundial**: A clock that tells the time by the shadow of a pointer as the Sun moves across the sky. *e.g.* At the Jantar Mantar in Jaipur, built about 1730 CE, a sundial 27 metres tall can be read to about two seconds.
- **Atomic clock**: A very exact clock that counts the waves given off by atoms. *e.g.* India's official time is kept by atomic clocks in New Delhi.
- **Temperature**: How hot or cold something is, shown as a number on a thermometer. *e.g.* A hot summer afternoon in Nagpur can reach 45 degrees Celsius.
- **Degree Celsius**: The everyday unit of temperature. Water freezes at 0 and boils at 100 at sea level. *e.g.* A healthy body is about 37 degrees Celsius.
- **Thermometer**: An instrument that measures temperature. *e.g.* A fever thermometer is put under the arm or in the mouth to read a child's temperature.
- **Fahrenheit**: Another scale of temperature. A healthy body is about 98.6 degrees Fahrenheit, which is about 37 degrees Celsius. *e.g.* Many fever thermometers in India show both scales.
- **Instrument**: A tool made to measure something, such as a ruler, a balance, a jug or a thermometer. *e.g.* A tailor's tape is an instrument for length.
- **Range**: The smallest and largest amount an instrument can measure. *e.g.* A fever thermometer reads only from about 35 to 42 degrees Celsius.
- **Smallest division**: The smallest step marked on an instrument. *e.g.* A bathroom scale moves in steps of 100 grams, so it cannot show a 20-gram letter.
- **Reading**: The number you take from an instrument. *e.g.* You read 12.4 centimetres from a ruler when the pencil's tip is a little past the 12.4 mark.
- **Zero**: The mark where counting starts on an instrument. Check it before you measure. *e.g.* A kitchen scale must show 0 before you put anything on it.
- **Tare**: The button on a scale that sets it back to zero with an empty bowl on it, so that only what you add is weighed. *e.g.* A vegetable seller presses tare with the empty bowl on the scale, and the display shows 0.
- **Estimate**: A careful guess at an amount, made by comparing it with something whose size you already know. *e.g.* A door is about 2 metres high, so a wall twice as tall is about 4 metres.
- **Coast**: The long, bendy line where the land meets the sea. *e.g.* India's coast runs round the south and both sides of the country.
- **Survey**: A careful measuring of land, using instruments, so that maps can be drawn. *e.g.* The Survey of India measures the country and makes its official maps.
- **Fractal**: A shape that stays jagged however closely you look at it, such as a coast. *e.g.* Look closely at the edge of a fern. Each small leaflet looks like a smaller copy of the whole fern.
- **Constant of nature**: A number that is found to be the same everywhere and at all times. *e.g.* Light in empty space always travels at 299,792 kilometres per second.
- **Net quantity**: How much is inside a packet, without the wrapping. *e.g.* A biscuit packet may show 60 g as its net quantity, however big the packet looks.
- **Indirect measurement**: Finding a size by measuring other things you can reach, such as angles and shadows, and then working it out. *e.g.* You can work out how tall a banyan stands from the length of a shadow on the ground, without climbing.
- **Angle**: How wide a corner is, or how far something has turned. A full circle is 360 degrees. *e.g.* The corner of a book is a right angle, which is 90 degrees.
- **Shadow**: The dark shape made when something blocks light. *e.g.* A stick standing in the sun makes a long shadow in the morning and a short one at noon.
- **Energy**: A number that physicists work out for a thing, which stays the same when things change and lets them predict what will happen. Science has no final account of what energy is. *e.g.* A cricket ball flying through the air has more energy than the same ball lying on the grass.
- **Gravity**: The pull that draws things with mass towards each other. Einstein described it as the bending of space and time. Nobody yet has a theory that joins gravity with the physics of very small things. *e.g.* Gravity pulls a dropped ball down to the floor.
- **Quantum physics**: The physics of the very small, such as atoms and the particles inside them, where things behave in ways that everyday life does not show. *e.g.* Quantum physics explains why a caesium atom gives off waves at one exact rate, which atomic clocks count.

## Explanations
### We measure when a guess is not good enough · *defined*
**Definition:** Measuring is finding out how much there is of something, as a number, so that different people can agree on it.

For many things a guess will do. You do not measure how far to step over a puddle. But think of the times a guess is not enough. A shopkeeper sells you rice, and both of you want to know it is one full kilogram. A nurse gives a baby medicine, and a little too much could harm. A train leaves at 6.15, not 'in the early evening'. A tailor cuts cloth that cannot be un-cut. In each case two people must agree, or a mistake would cost money, health or time. That is when people measure. Measuring turns 'a lot' and 'a little' into numbers that can be checked.
- e.g. Buying goods: 2 kilograms of onions, half a litre of milk, 3 metres of cloth, 5 litres of petrol.
- e.g. Telling time: the school bell, a bus timetable, how long to boil an egg.
- e.g. Cooking: one tumbler of rice to two tumblers of water.
- e.g. Health: your height and weight on a clinic card, a fever of 101 degrees, 5 millilitres of cough syrup.
- e.g. Building: a mason checks that a wall is straight and a door frame is 2 metres high.
### Measuring is comparing with a unit and counting · *defined*
**Definition:** A unit is a fixed amount that we choose to compare other amounts with. A measurement is a number together with its unit.

To measure a table you pick something to compare it with, such as one hand span. That chosen amount is called a unit. Then you count how many units fit. If nine fit, the table is nine hand spans wide. Every measurement works this way, however clever the instrument. A ruler is a strip with the unit already marked many times, so that the counting is done for you. This means a measurement always has two parts: a number and a unit. '5' alone says nothing. Five what? Five centimetres is the width of a matchbox. Five kilometres is an hour's walk.
- e.g. 'The medicine dose is 5' could mean 5 drops or 5 spoonfuls. The unit decides.
- e.g. A recipe that says 'add 2 of salt' cannot be followed.
- e.g. A road sign says 'Madurai 40'. In India we know it means kilometres. A visitor from the United States, who is used to miles, might think the town much further away.
### Body units came first, and they differ from person to person · *defined*
**Definition:** A body unit is a unit taken from part of the body, such as a finger width, a hand span, a forearm or a foot.

The first units were always at hand, because they were the hand. People measured cloth by the forearm, from the elbow to the tip of the middle finger. They measured a field by paces and a horse by hand widths. These units are quick, free and always with you. Their fault is plain. A tall weaver's forearm is longer than a short weaver's. Sell cloth by your own arm, and every sale is an argument. Body units work inside one family. They fail between strangers.
- e.g. A flower seller in Madurai still sells jasmine strings by the muzham, the length of the forearm.
- e.g. The words foot and hand span are still in daily use, though few people measure with their feet.
- e.g. A kolam is often set out in finger widths and hand spans, and it fits the doorstep of the person who draws it.
- **Kautilya's Arthashastra, book 2, chapter 20, composed in India about 2,000 years ago:** The text sets out a ladder of lengths. Eight middle-sized barley grains laid side by side make one angula, a finger width. Twenty-four angulas make one hasta, a forearm. By tying the finger to a count of grains, it tried to make a body unit the same for everyone.
- **Builders who follow the Vastu Shastra, India's old texts on building:** Some of these traditions size a house from the owner's own finger or forearm, so that the home fits the person who lives in it. For them a unit that differs from person to person is the purpose and not a fault.
### Standard units are much older than the metre: the Harappan weights · *defined*
**Definition:** A standard unit is a unit that many people agree to keep exactly the same, by copying it from one shared example.

The cure for quarrelling over units is to make one unit and copy it for everyone. The cities of the Harappan people, who lived along the Indus and in Gujarat about 4,500 years ago, already did this. Archaeologists have found hundreds of small stone cubes there. They are weights for a balance. The smallest common one is a little under one gram. The next ones are 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 and 64 times as heavy, each double the one before. The same weights turn up in cities about a thousand kilometres or more apart. Somebody was making sure that a trader in one city and a trader in another meant the same thing by one weight.
- e.g. At Lothal in Gujarat a piece of an ivory scale was found. Its marks are about 1.7 millimetres apart, very fine divisions for a scale of that age.
- e.g. Harappan bricks have the same proportions in city after city: the thickness, width and length are as 1 to 2 to 4.
- e.g. The Arthashastra, book 2, chapter 19, tells the king's officer to have weights and measures stamped at regular times. It also sets fines for cheating with them.
- **Archaeologists such as Jonathan Mark Kenoyer:** The sameness of the weights across so wide a land shows shared rules of trade, and perhaps officers who checked them.
- **Scholars who note that the Indus script is still unread:** Nobody has read the Harappan writing, so we do not know who set the standard or how it was enforced. It may have been merchants' agreement and not a ruler's order.
### The metric system: one set of units for the whole world · *defined*
**Definition:** The metric system is a set of units built on the metre, the kilogram and the second. Larger and smaller units are made by multiplying or dividing by ten. Its modern form is called the International System of Units, or SI.

Until about 200 years ago every region had its own units. In the 1790s scientists in France designed a new system meant 'for all people, for all time'. They did not base it on a king's foot. They based the metre on the size of the Earth: one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. They made every larger and smaller unit by tens, so that sums would be easy. Country after country joined. India passed a law in 1956, and in the years after it the old seer, tola and gaz slowly left the shops. Today scientists in every country, and nearly all shops, use the same units.
- e.g. Before the change, a seer of rice was a little less than one kilogram, and its size was not exactly the same in every province.
- e.g. A doctor in Kochi and a doctor in Brazil both write a dose in milligrams and mean the same amount.
- e.g. The United States still uses miles, pounds and gallons in daily life. Its scientists use metric units.
- **The French scientists of the 1790s, and scientists since:** One system, built on tens and open to all, removes cheating and mistakes and lets the whole world share its findings.
- **People who keep older units, such as carpenters who work in inches and feet:** A foot divides neatly into halves, thirds and quarters, which ten does not. Old units grew out of real work and often suit the hand and the job.
### Kilo, centi and milli: one unit made big or small by tens · *defined*
**Definition:** A prefix is a short word put in front of a unit to make it larger or smaller. Kilo means one thousand, centi means one hundredth and milli means one thousandth.

In the metric system you do not need a new name for every size. You take one unit and put a prefix in front of it. Kilo means a thousand. So a kilometre is 1,000 metres and a kilogram is 1,000 grams. Centi means a hundredth. So 100 centimetres make one metre. Milli means a thousandth. So 1,000 millimetres make one metre, and 1,000 millilitres make one litre. Learn the three prefixes once, and they work for the metre, the gram and the litre alike. Changing from one size to another is only a matter of moving by tens.
- e.g. Your height may be 130 centimetres. That is 1.3 metres, or 1,300 millimetres.
- e.g. A 500 millilitre packet of milk is half a litre.
- e.g. A paracetamol tablet for a child may hold 250 milligrams of medicine, which is a quarter of a gram.
- e.g. Mega means a million. A megawatt is a million watts.
### Length: the metre and its instruments · *defined*
**Definition:** Length is how long something is from end to end. Its standard unit is the metre.

A metre is about the distance from the floor to the handle of a door. For small things we use centimetres and millimetres. For journeys we use kilometres. Each size of job has its own instrument. A 15 centimetre ruler suits a line in a notebook. A tailor's soft tape goes round a waist, which a stiff ruler cannot do. A steel tape that rolls into a case measures a room. A car counts kilometres by counting the turns of its wheels. The modern metre is no longer tied to the Earth. Since 1983 it has been defined as the distance light travels in a tiny, exactly stated fraction of a second.
- e.g. The thickness of a fingernail is about 1 millimetre. The width of a finger is about 1 centimetre.
- e.g. A cricket pitch is 20.12 metres long.
- e.g. By rail, Chennai is about 2,180 kilometres from New Delhi.
- e.g. In 1865 and 1866 the surveyor Nain Singh Rawat measured the road to Lhasa by counting his own equal steps on a string of beads.
### Mass: the kilogram, and what a balance really does · *defined*
**Definition:** Mass is the amount of matter in something. Its standard unit is the kilogram. A balance finds a mass by comparing it with known masses.

A one-litre bottle of water has a mass of about one kilogram. Small masses are given in grams: a paper clip is about one gram. The oldest instrument is the two-pan balance. You put the vegetables on one pan and metal weights on the other. When the beam is level, the two sides match. A balance never tells you a mass directly. It only says 'these two are equal'. A digital kitchen scale works differently. It measures how hard the thing presses down, and shows the answer in grams. What matter finally is, scientists cannot yet say. The mass of everyday things turns out to come mostly from energy held inside the centres of atoms.
- e.g. A newborn baby is usually about 3 kilograms.
- e.g. A cooking gas cylinder holds 14.2 kilograms of gas.
- e.g. Gold is still often spoken of in tolas. One tola is about 11.7 grams. Jewellers' bills today are in grams.
- e.g. A letter is weighed in grams at the post office, and a lorry in tonnes at a weighbridge. A tonne is 1,000 kilograms.
### Volume: litres and millilitres, and how to measure a stone · *defined*
**Definition:** Volume is the amount of space something takes up. For liquids the usual units are the litre and the millilitre.

A litre is the space inside a cube that is 10 centimetres along each edge. A millilitre is the space inside a cube one centimetre along each edge. One teaspoon holds about 5 millilitres. Liquids are measured in a jug or a narrow tube with marks on the side, called a measuring cylinder. The makers of the metric system tied its units together neatly: one litre of water has a mass of one kilogram. What about the volume of a stone, which has no neat edges? Lower the stone into a measuring jug of water. The water level rises. The rise is exactly the volume of the stone.
- e.g. A milk packet is 500 millilitres. A bucket holds about 15 litres.
- e.g. The cap of a cough syrup bottle is marked at 5 and 10 millilitres, because a kitchen spoon is not a reliable measure for medicine.
- e.g. A tall thin tumbler and a short wide bowl can hold exactly the same volume, though the tumbler looks as if it holds more.
- e.g. Grain was long sold by volume in India, in measures such as the padi in Tamil Nadu and the nazhi and para in Kerala.
### Time: the second, and clocks from a sinking bowl to an atom · *defined*
**Definition:** The standard unit of time is the second. A clock is anything that repeats in a steady way and can be counted.

To measure time you need something that repeats steadily: a swing, a drip, a shadow going round. Old India used a copper bowl with a small hole, floated on water. The bowl filled slowly and sank in exactly one ghati, which is 24 minutes. Sixty ghatis made a day and a night. We divide an hour into 60 minutes because the people of ancient Babylon, more than 3,500 years ago, counted in sixties. Today the second is defined by an atom. A caesium atom gives off waves that repeat 9,192,631,770 times in one second, and atomic clocks count them. Clocks measure time very well. What time itself is has no agreed answer in science.
- e.g. At the Jantar Mantar in Jaipur, built about 1730 CE, a sundial 27 metres tall throws a shadow that moves about one millimetre every second. It can be read to about two seconds.
- e.g. India's official time is kept by atomic clocks at the National Physical Laboratory in New Delhi.
- e.g. Your pulse is a rough clock, beating about 70 to 110 times a minute when a child sits quietly.
- e.g. The Chinese pilgrim Yijing wrote in the 7th century CE about the monks at Nalanda. They kept the hours with a sinking bowl and struck a drum each time the bowl went down.
- **The Surya Siddhanta, an Indian text on astronomy, chapter 13:** It describes the copper bowl with a hole that sinks about sixty times in a day and a night. It treats time as something counted by the turning of the sky.
- **Isaac Newton, in 1687:** He held that time flows evenly by itself, the same everywhere, whether or not any clock is counting.
- **Albert Einstein in 1905 and 1915, and physicists since:** They showed that clocks moving at different speeds, or at different heights, run at different rates. So there is no single time for everyone. Some physicists, such as Carlo Rovelli, think time may not be a basic part of nature at all.
### Temperature: degrees Celsius, and a scale that people chose · *defined*
**Definition:** Temperature tells how hot or cold something is. The everyday unit is the degree Celsius, on a scale where water freezes at 0 and boils at 100.

A thermometer measures temperature. On the Celsius scale, ice melts at 0 degrees and water boils at 100 degrees at sea level. These two marks were chosen by people. When the Swedish scientist Anders Celsius made his scale in 1742, he put 100 at freezing and 0 at boiling. It was turned the other way round after his death. Fever thermometers in India often show another scale, called Fahrenheit, on which a healthy body is about 98.6 degrees. That is the same warmth as about 37 degrees Celsius. One temperature can have two numbers, and nothing about the body has changed.
- e.g. A fever thermometer covers only about 35 to 42 degrees Celsius, in small steps, because that is all a body needs.
- e.g. A laboratory thermometer covers about minus 10 to 110 degrees Celsius, in larger steps.
- e.g. A hot summer afternoon in Nagpur can reach 45 degrees Celsius. A winter night in Leh can fall to minus 15.
- e.g. Scientists also use the kelvin scale, which starts at the coldest temperature there can be, about minus 273 degrees Celsius.
### Choosing an instrument: how much can it measure, and how finely? · *defined*
**Definition:** The range of an instrument is the smallest and largest amount it can measure. Its smallest division is the smallest step marked on it.

Before you measure, ask two questions about the instrument. First, what is its range? A bathroom scale can weigh a person but not a lorry. Second, what is its smallest division? A bathroom scale shows steps of 100 grams, so it cannot weigh a letter of 20 grams. A kitchen scale shows steps of 1 gram, and it can. Pick an instrument whose range covers your thing and whose smallest step is much smaller than your thing. There is also a trick for things too small for your instrument. Measure many of them together and divide.
- e.g. One sheet of paper is too thin for a ruler. A pile of 100 sheets is about 1 centimetre thick. So one sheet is about one-hundredth of a centimetre, which is one-tenth of a millimetre.
- e.g. To find the mass of one grain of rice on a kitchen scale, weigh 100 grains and divide by one hundred.
- e.g. A jeweller's balance reads to one-thousandth of a gram and is kept inside a glass case, because a breath of air would disturb it.
### Reading an instrument: start at zero and look straight on · *defined*
**Definition:** A reading is the number you take from an instrument. To read correctly, the instrument must start from zero and your eye must be directly in line with the mark.

Three habits prevent most wrong readings. First, check the zero. On many rulers the zero mark is a little way in from the end, so line up the mark and not the end. A kitchen scale must show 0 before you put anything on it. Second, look straight on. If you read a ruler or a jug from one side, the mark seems to shift. Bring your eye level with the mark. Third, in a jug or tube, water curves up a little at the edges. Read the flat bottom of the curve, with the jug standing on a level table.
- e.g. Hold a pencil upright a little in front of a ruler and look with one eye, then the other. The pencil seems to jump along the ruler.
- e.g. Two children read the same jug as 240 and 260 millilitres because one looked from above and one from below.
- e.g. A shopkeeper presses the 'tare' button with the empty bowl on the scale. The scale then shows 0 and weighs only what goes into the bowl.
### Estimating: a sensible guess before you measure · *defined*
**Definition:** An estimate is a careful guess at an amount, made by comparing it with something whose size you already know.

Good measurers guess first. An estimate before you measure does two jobs. It helps you pick the right instrument. It also catches mistakes: if you guess 'about one metre' and your tape says 10, you look again. To estimate well you need a few sizes that you carry with you. Your finger is about 1 centimetre wide. A door is about 2 metres high. A teaspoon holds 5 millilitres. A full one-litre bottle has a mass of 1 kilogram. With these, most everyday things can be guessed to within about a quarter of their true size.
- e.g. A vegetable seller can put very nearly one kilogram of tomatoes on the pan by feel before the balance checks it.
- e.g. A cook adds salt 'by eye' and gets close to the same amount each day.
- e.g. The physicist Enrico Fermi was known for rough answers to big questions, worked out from a few known sizes. One of his questions was how many piano tuners work in a city.
### Some things have no single true length: India's coast · *defined*
**Definition:** The measured length of a very wiggly line, such as a coast, depends on the size of the step used to measure it. A smaller step follows more bends and gives a longer answer.

For many years India's coast was listed as about 7,516 kilometres. A few years ago the government measured again and gave about 11,098 kilometres. The sea had not moved. The new survey used maps showing far more detail, so the measuring line went in and out of many more bays and creeks. A coast is jagged at every size. Measure it with steps of 100 kilometres and you skip most of the bends. Measure with steps of one kilometre and you follow more. Measure round every rock and the number rises again. So asking for 'the length of the coast' is not enough. You must also say how fine the measuring was.
- e.g. Lay a thread loosely along the edge of a torn chapati, then measure the same edge with a stiff ruler in straight steps. The thread gives the longer answer.
- e.g. The length of a smooth thing, such as the edge of a steel plate, hardly changes when you use a finer ruler. The puzzle belongs to jagged things.
- e.g. A river's length and the edge of a banyan leaf behave like the coast.
- **Lewis Fry Richardson, an English scientist, in work printed in 1961:** He noticed that neighbouring countries gave different lengths for the border they shared. He found that the smaller the measuring step, the longer the border came out, in a regular way.
- **Benoit Mandelbrot, in 1967, in a paper titled 'How long is the coast of Britain?':** He showed that shapes like coasts keep their roughness however closely you look. He later named such shapes fractals and gave a number for how rough each one is.
- **Surveyors and governments:** For law and planning one number is needed. So they agree on a map scale and a method, and state it. The number is then a fair answer to a clearly stated question.
### Units now rest on nature's constants: the kilogram left its vault in 2019 · *defined*
**Definition:** Since 2019 every standard unit has been defined from a constant of nature. A constant of nature is a number that is found to be the same everywhere and at all times.

From 1889 the kilogram was a real object: a small cylinder of metal locked in a vault near Paris. Every country had a copy. India's is copy number 57, kept in New Delhi. There was a weakness. Over a hundred years the cylinder and its copies drifted apart by about 50 millionths of a gram, and nobody could say which had changed. An object can be scratched, lose atoms or be lost. So in 2019 the countries of the world agreed to define the kilogram from a fixed number of nature called the Planck constant. The metre was already tied to the speed of light, and the second to the caesium atom. Now any laboratory with the right equipment can make the units afresh.
- e.g. A shopkeeper's one-kilogram weight is checked against an inspector's weight. That is checked against a state laboratory's, and that against the national one. This chain of checks ties your kilogram of rice to the world's kilogram.
- e.g. In India, weights and scales used for selling must carry an inspector's stamp, under the Legal Metrology Act of 2009.
### Mixing up units can be costly · *defined*
**Definition:** A unit error is a mistake where a number is used with the wrong unit, or with no unit at all. The numbers can be right while the answer is wrong.

In September 1999 a spacecraft called Mars Climate Orbiter reached Mars after a journey of about nine months. It flew too low and was lost in the planet's air. The cause was found within weeks. One team had sent numbers for the pushes of the craft's small engines in an old American unit. The other team's computer read the same numbers as metric units. Nobody had checked the unit. The spacecraft had cost about 125 million dollars. The numbers were all correct. Only the units were missing.
- e.g. In 1983 a passenger aeroplane in Canada ran out of fuel in mid-air. The fuel had been worked out in pounds when the new aircraft needed kilograms. The pilots glided it to a safe landing on an old airfield at Gimli.
- e.g. A parent told 'give 5' of a medicine may give 5 millilitres or 5 spoonfuls. Doctors now ask that doses for children be written in millilitres only.
- e.g. Land in India is still sold by the bigha in many states. A bigha in one state can be twice as large as a bigha in another, or more.
### Measuring what you cannot reach: the Earth and Mount Everest · *defined*
**Definition:** An indirect measurement finds a size by measuring other things that can be reached, such as angles and shadows, and calculating.

Nobody has stretched a tape round the Earth or stood a ruler beside Everest. Such sizes are worked out. About 240 BCE, Eratosthenes looked after the great library at Alexandria. He heard that at noon on midsummer day the Sun shone straight down a well in Syene. In Alexandria, on the same day, a pillar threw a short shadow. From the angle of that shadow and the distance between the two towns, he worked out the distance round the Earth. His answer was close to the true value of about 40,000 kilometres, though nobody is sure how long his unit, the stadion, was. In 1852 Radhanath Sikdar, a mathematician from Bengal working for the Survey of India, calculated from far-off angles that a Himalayan peak was the world's highest. It was later named Mount Everest, after a British surveyor.
- e.g. The height first announced for the peak was 29,002 feet, about 8,840 metres. The figure agreed by Nepal and China in 2020 is 8,848.86 metres.
- e.g. You can find how tall a tree stands by measuring the shadow of the tree and the shadow of a metre stick at the same moment.
- e.g. Aryabhata, in 499 CE, gave the Earth's width as 1,050 yojanas. Scholars do not agree on the length of his yojana, so they do not agree on how close he came.
- **Indian writers and teachers who honour Radhanath Sikdar:** They hold that Sikdar found the highest peak and that naming it after George Everest, who never saw it, wiped out his credit.
- **Historians of the Great Trigonometrical Survey:** They point out that the calculation was the work of a team over several years. The records do not show clearly how much of the final result was Sikdar's alone.
### Other living beings measure too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings sense amounts such as distance, length of night and number of touches, and act on them.

Measuring did not begin with people. A desert ant in the Sahara walks a winding path to find food and then runs home in a straight line. How do they know the distance? In 2006 scientists glued tiny stilts to some ants' legs after the ants reached the food. With longer legs and longer steps, those ants ran too far and passed their nest. Ants whose legs were made shorter stopped too soon. The ants had been keeping count of their steps. Plants measure as well. Many flower only when the nights reach a certain length. A Venus flytrap shuts only after two touches on the hairs inside within about twenty seconds.
- e.g. Chrysanthemum plants flower when the nights grow long. Growers keep lamps on at night to hold them back until the flowers are wanted.
- e.g. A rice plant of a traditional variety senses the shortening days and flowers at the right week of the season.
- e.g. Your own body keeps a clock of about 24 hours, which is why you feel sleepy at nearly the same time each night.
- **Matthias Wittlinger, Rüdiger Wehner and Harald Wolf, who did the ant test in 2006:** They describe the ant as having a step counter, a body mechanism that adds up strides.
- **Scientists who study animal and plant minds:** The ant senses, remembers and decides when to stop and search. A plant counting touches is also keeping a memory. Some scientists call these automatic mechanisms. What it is like for the ant, nobody knows.
- **W. W. Garner and H. A. Allard, American plant scientists, in 1920:** They found that tobacco and soya plants flower according to the length of day and night, and showed that a plant keeps track of time.
### Everyday measuring in India: what to read on a packet and at a pump · *defined*
**Definition:** The law in India requires that goods sold by measure are measured with checked instruments, and that packets state how much they hold.

You can use this lesson every time you shop. Every packet must print its net quantity, which is how much is inside without the wrapping: 500 g, 1 L, 200 mL. A petrol pump must show zero before it starts and shows the litres as they flow. A shop's scale must carry an inspector's seal. Alongside the metric units, older ones live on. Height is spoken in feet and inches. Cloth is sometimes sold by the yard. Land is sold by the cent in Kerala and Tamil Nadu, the guntha in Maharashtra and the bigha in the north. Knowing which unit is being used, and asking when you are not sure, is part of measuring well.
- e.g. A biscuit packet may look large and hold only 60 grams. Compare the price for each 100 grams, not the size of the packet.
- e.g. A clinic card records a child's height in centimetres and weight in kilograms at every visit, to see how the child is growing.
- e.g. The India Meteorological Department measures rain in millimetres: the depth the water would have if it stayed on flat ground.
- e.g. An auto-rickshaw meter measures both the distance travelled and the time spent waiting.
### Can everything be measured? · *debated*
**Definition:** People disagree about whether everything real can be given a number, and about what is lost when something is turned into a number.

Measuring is so useful that it is easy to think that only what is measured is real. A doctor asks, 'How bad is the pain, from 0 to 10?' You say 6. Your friend with the same injury says 3. Has the doctor measured pain? There is no unit of pain to compare with and no instrument. The same trouble comes up with happiness, cleverness, beauty and kindness. Some scientists say that these will be measured one day, as temperature finally was. Others say that some things are known in other ways, by watching, by listening and by living with them. Science itself began as careful thinking about nature. Measuring is one of its tools.
- e.g. Before thermometers, hot and cold were only feelings. It took about 200 years of work to make temperature a number that everyone agreed on.
- e.g. A school mark turns a term of learning into one number. Ask what the number shows and what it leaves out.
- e.g. Putting a cold thermometer into a small cup of warm water cools the water a little. The act of measuring can change the thing measured.
- **William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, in a lecture of 1883:** He said that when you can measure what you are speaking about and express it in numbers, you have real knowledge of it. When you cannot, your knowledge is of a thin and unsatisfying kind.
- **William Bruce Cameron, an American sociologist, in 1963:** He wrote that not everything that can be counted counts, and not everything that counts can be counted.
- **Jain philosophers, in the Tattvartha Sutra 1.6, by Umasvati, about the 2nd to 5th century CE:** The text says that knowledge comes through full means of knowing and also through partial standpoints. Any one way of knowing a thing, a measurement included, gives one side of it.

## Mostly agreed
- The first metre was very slightly too short, and it was kept anyway. *How we know:* The French survey of the 1790s assumed a slightly wrong shape for the Earth. Later, better measurements showed that the metre bar was about one-fifth of a millimetre shorter than one ten-millionth of the distance from pole to equator. By then the bar was in use, so the bar stayed the metre. This shows that a unit is an agreement.
- Two very exact clocks at different heights tick at different rates. *How we know:* In 2010 a team led by James Chin-Wen Chou in the United States raised one of two atomic clocks by about 33 centimetres. The higher clock ran a tiny bit faster, as Einstein's theory of gravity predicts.
- People's eyes are easily fooled about length, which is one reason instruments are needed. *How we know:* In the drawing published by Franz Müller-Lyer in 1889, two equal lines look unequal because of the arrow-heads at their ends. Nearly everyone sees the illusion, and a ruler settles it at once.
- Human bodies have not always measured about 37 degrees Celsius. *How we know:* In 2020 a team led by Myroslava Protsiv studied body temperature records from the United States, from the 1860s to 2017. The average had fallen by about half a degree Celsius. So a 'normal' temperature is a measurement of certain people at a certain time.

## How we found out
**Jean-Baptiste Delambre, Pierre Méchain, the French Academy of Sciences, and the countries of the metre treaty, 1791 to 2019.** Before the 1790s, people held that each town's own measures were good enough. It was believable, because most buying and selling happened between neighbours. In France alone, historians count about a quarter of a million different local units, and the same name often meant different amounts in the next valley. Traders and tax collectors could cheat, and scientists could not compare results. In 1791 the French Academy of Sciences proposed a unit taken from the Earth itself, so that it would belong to no king. From 1792 to 1798 two astronomers, Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain, measured the land from Dunkirk in the north to Barcelona in the south. They worked through a revolution and a war, and were held more than once as suspected spies. Méchain found that some of his readings near Barcelona did not agree with each other. He hid this and it troubled him for the rest of his life. From their survey a platinum metre bar was made in 1799. In 1875 seventeen countries signed a treaty to share the metre and the kilogram. India joined in 1957. In 1983 the metre was tied to the speed of light, and in 2019 the kilogram was tied to a constant of nature.  
*What people believed before:* People held that every place could keep its own units without harm. It fitted a world where goods and ideas did not travel far. As trade and science crossed borders, the confusion and cheating showed that shared units were needed.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If you cannot measure something, your knowledge of it is poor.** (Lord Kelvin in 1883, and many scientists since; *debated*)  
  For: Sciences leapt forward each time something vague became a number: temperature, electric current, the age of rocks. Numbers can be checked by strangers, and opinions cannot.  
  Against: A field naturalist such as Jane Goodall learnt what chimpanzees are like by years of patient watching, before any numbers. Much of what we know about one another, and about other beings, is of this kind. A number can also give false confidence.
- **Intelligence can be measured with a single number.** (Charles Spearman in 1904, and makers of IQ tests since; *debated*)  
  For: People who do well on one kind of test tend to do well on others. For example, a child near the top of a class of 40 in sums is often near the top in reading too. Test scores also predict school results fairly well.  
  Against: In 1981 the biologist Stephen Jay Gould argued that a pattern in test scores had been mistaken for a real thing inside the head. Howard Gardner in 1983 proposed many kinds of intelligence. Tests have also been unfair to people of other languages and cultures, and say nothing about the minds of other beings.
- **The whole world should give up every old unit and use only metric ones.** (The international metre treaty of 1875 and most scientists; *debated*)  
  For: One system prevents costly mistakes, cheating and confusion, and lets all people share knowledge.  
  Against: Old units carry the knowledge of crafts and places. A carpenter's inches divide easily into halves and quarters. A unit such as the muzham needs no instrument. Some people ask why working customs should vanish to suit laboratories.
- **Measuring always disturbs what is measured, so we can never know a thing as it is.** (A popular reading of Werner Heisenberg's work of 1927; *minority*)  
  For: For the very smallest particles, measuring one feature exactly does leave another feature unsettled. A thermometer does change the temperature of a small drop.  
  Against: For everyday things the disturbance can be made far too small to matter: a ruler does not change a table. Physicists also point out that Heisenberg's finding is about what particles are like, not about clumsy instruments. In physics an 'observer' is any measuring device and not a mind.

## Roots
You have learnt how to measure and how units were agreed. Now we ask what measuring tells us about ourselves, about life and about this world.

**Deep truth.** You measured a torn edge with big steps and then with small steps, and the small steps gave a longer answer. India's coast did the same: 7,516 kilometres by one survey and 11,098 by a finer one. Each smaller step found more bends, so the answer kept growing instead of settling. Some things stay rough at every size we can measure, so their length stops growing only where we decide to stop looking. An honest answer to 'how long is it?' always says how fine the steps were.  
*Habit it breaks:* We expect careful measuring to settle on one final number, the way weighing a stone again and again settles on its mass. For a jagged edge it does not settle. Looking closer keeps finding more.  
*Evidence:* In the wiggly line test the same torn edge came out longer each time the step got smaller. For example, a child might get about 31 centimetres with 3 centimetre steps, about 37 with 1 centimetre steps, and longer still with the thread. Your numbers will differ. Put your own numbers here.

- *who am i:* Your height is a good example. A tape gives it in centimetres, and a finer ruler gives it in millimetres. Measure again at night and you are often about a centimetre shorter than you were in the morning. The closer anyone looks, the more there is to find, and no list of numbers is ever the whole of you. Understanding this, you need not mistake a height, a weight or a mark for yourself.
- *what is life:* Living beings are not only measured by us. They measure too: a desert ant keeps count of her strides, a rice plant senses the long nights, and you read a ruler. Each does so with its own body, for its own needs. Measuring is not something people added to the world. Life does it.
- *what is this world:* A coast has no single length because it stays rough as you look closer, and a kilogram is an agreement tied to a constant of nature. The world gives the same answer to everyone who asks the same question in the same way. It does not answer questions nobody has asked.

This lesson treated measuring as comparing. Then it found a case where the answer depends on the fineness of the comparison, and never settles. That is a measured fact about coasts. It suggests that a number marks the place where one way of looking stops, and not the place where the thing itself ends. Consciousness is the ground in which the measurer and the measured both arise, and measuring is one way that ground meets itself. This last step is not science. Quantum physics, where measuring also matters deeply, does not prove it either: in physics an 'observer' is any measuring device and not a mind. Many scientists stop at the measured fact, and their view is given below.

- **Isha Upanishad, the opening invocation:** That is whole, and this is whole. From the whole, the whole comes out. When the whole is taken from the whole, the whole still remains. (Every measurement in this lesson works by taking away and counting: so many units, with some left over. The verse speaks of something for which taking away changes nothing. It marks a place where measuring, by its own method, cannot go.)
- **Kena Upanishad, part 1, verse 6:** That which the mind does not grasp, but by which, they say, the mind itself is known. Know that alone to be Brahman, the one ground, and not what people take it to be. (The lesson found that every measurement needs a measurer. The sage points at what does the knowing and says it cannot be made into a thing known. Science has not found a way to measure the one who measures, which is the open question of consciousness.)
- **Protagoras of Abdera, about 450 BCE, as reported in Plato's 'Theaetetus', section 152a:** He said that the human being is the measure of all things. (The coast finding gives him a point: the answer does depend on the measurer. This lesson also shows where he stops short. The ant and the plant are measures too, each of their own world.)
- Science (settled): The measured length of a coast grows as the measuring step shrinks. This has been confirmed on many coasts and borders since Richardson's work was printed in 1961.
- Science (idea): Many physicists think there is a smallest length that has any meaning, called the Planck length, far smaller than an atom. No experiment has reached it.
- Other view, Scientific realists, and the Nyaya school of Indian philosophy: Things have their sizes whether or not anyone measures them. A stone has a mass and a steel plate has a width, and careful measurers close in on the same value. The coast is a special case of a jagged shape, and even there, once the step size is stated, everyone gets the same answer. Measuring finds what is there. It does not help to make it.
- Practice, Your own breath as a unit (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Breathe in and out slowly, and count ten breaths. That was a length of time, measured in your own unit. Now open your eyes and look at something far away, then something near. Before you reach for any number, notice that you already know which is further. Sit for three more breaths with that knowing.
- Ask: Your wiggly edge had several lengths. Which one was its real length, and why?
- Ask: Your height changes between morning and night. Which of the two is your real height?
- Ask: What do you know for certain about someone you love that you have never measured?

- *deep:* Your hand gave the table one width and a grown-up's hand gave another. Does the table have a width when nobody is measuring it? (hint: Ask what would be left of the word 'width' if there were nothing at all to compare the table with.)
- *deep:* India's coast is 7,516 kilometres by one count and 11,098 by another. Is there a true length that both are trying to reach? (hint: Imagine measuring round every pebble, then every grain of sand. What happens to the number?)

## Story: The Kilogram Nobody Could Weigh
*A made-up story set in Delhi. The children, the vegetable seller and the scientist are imagined. What is real: from 1889 to 2019 the kilogram was a metal cylinder kept in a vault near Paris. India holds copy number 57 at the National Physical Laboratory in New Delhi. Over about a century the cylinder and its copies drifted apart by about 50 millionths of a gram. Since 20 May 2019 the kilogram has been defined from the Planck constant. The kilogram was first chosen in the 1790s as the mass of one litre of water.*

At the Saturday market, Kamla didi put a kilo of tomatoes on one pan of her balance and an iron weight on the other. The beam swung and settled level.

Kabir, who was ten and liked an argument, pointed at the iron weight. 'How do we know that is really one kilo?'

Kamla didi was not offended. She turned the weight over. A small seal was pressed into the bottom. 'The inspector checks it against his own weights and stamps it. No stamp, and I pay a fine.'

'And who checks the inspector's weights?'

'Ask her mother,' said Kamla didi, nodding at Zoya. Zoya, who was also ten, had come with Kabir to carry the bags. Her mother Nasreen worked at the National Physical Laboratory, where India's measures are kept.

That evening Nasreen drew a chain of boxes on the back of an envelope. Kamla's weight. The inspector's weight. The state laboratory's. The national laboratory's. 'And until 2019,' she said, 'the chain ended here.' She drew a small cylinder under three glass covers. 'One piece of metal, locked in a vault near Paris since 1889. That cylinder was the kilogram. India has copy number fifty-seven. I have seen it.'

'Who checked the one in Paris?' said Kabir.

'Nobody could. That was the trouble. About every forty years, the copies were carried to Paris and compared with it. Over a hundred years they drifted apart, by about fifty millionths of a gram. That is about as heavy as one small grain of table salt.'

'So the Paris one got lighter,' said Zoya.

'Or the copies got heavier. Or both. There was no way to tell, because the cylinder was the kilogram, by definition. If it lost a speck of metal, it was still exactly one kilogram. The rule said so. Which meant that everything else in the universe had become a tiny bit heavier.'

Kabir laughed. Then he stopped. 'That is silly. But the rule does say it.'

'You two became heavier as well,' said Nasreen, 'without eating anything.'

Zoya was thinking about the two pans. 'A balance never tells you how heavy a thing is,' she said slowly. 'It only tells you that two things match. Kamla didi's weight matches the inspector's. His matches the next one. It is matching, all the way up. And the top one had nothing to match with.'

'So in 2019 the countries changed the top of the chain,' said Nasreen. 'Now the kilogram is tied to a number of nature called the Planck constant. It is part of how light and atoms behave, and as far as anyone can tell it is the same everywhere. A laboratory can build a special balance that matches the Earth's pull on a mass against an electric push worked out from that number. Any country can build one. Nothing is locked in a vault.'

'So now it is the true kilogram at last,' said Kabir.

'It is a kilogram that everybody can check,' said Nasreen. 'Is that the same as true? People chose its size, in the 1790s. They took the mass of one litre of water. It could have been any size. Nature did not hand it to us.'

The next Saturday, Kabir told Kamla didi the whole story. She listened while she weighed brinjals.

'In my grandmother's village she sold by the handful,' she said. 'No weights and no stamp. People bought from her because her handful was honest.'

'A handful cannot be checked,' said Kabir.

'It was checked every day,' said Kamla didi, 'by everyone who came back.'

Zoya watched the pans swing and settle. Tomatoes on one side and iron on the other, two things as different as could be, and the beam said only: these match. She tried to think of one thing, anywhere, that could be measured with nothing set beside it. She could not.

**For the parent:** Every weighing was a matching, from the market stall up to the vault in Paris, and the top of the chain had nothing to be matched with. A measurement turned out to rest on comparison and on trust: the stamp on a weight, a number of nature, or the buyers who came back.

- Ask: Zoya said a balance only tells you that two things match. Is there any instrument that does more than that?
- Ask: Nasreen asked if a kilogram that everybody can check is the same as a true kilogram. What do you think?
- Ask: Kamla didi's grandmother was checked by the people who came back. Is that a kind of measuring?
- Ask: Can you think of one thing that could be measured with nothing beside it?

  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Shuffle them. Put them face down. Each player takes paper and a pencil.
  - Note: A grown-up can do the cutting for a child who is not yet handy with scissors. Keep the cards in an envelope after the game.
- **g1s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play one practice card together. Turn over the top card. Each player writes a guess with its unit. Show your guesses. Read the answer and score as in the rules.  
  *Parent says:* Say your guess with its unit. Which anchor did you use? Then we will see how close you got.  
  *Child mode:* Practise with one card. Write a guess and its unit. See how close you are.
  - Note: Practise scoring once, because the fractions are the hardest part. Within one tenth means for example 90 to 110 when the true size is 100.
- **g1s3 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play 10 cards. Each player guesses with a unit, shows the guess, and then the card is turned. Work out the points for each player after each card and write them on the score sheet.  
  *Parent says:* What is your guess? Which anchor did you use? Is it close?  
  *Child mode:* Guess each card with its unit. Score the points.
  - Ask: When a guess was far off, which anchor did you use? (good: An anchor that did not fit the thing.; I did not have an anchor for that size.)
  - Note: Let children argue for their guess. Two different anchors can both be defensible, such as a hand for a table or a door for a wall. The point is to say why.
- **g1s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the points. Talk about which card was the hardest, and which anchor helped most.  
  *Parent says:* Which size was hardest to guess, and why? Which anchor was the best?  
  *Child mode:* Add up the points. Say which card was the hardest.
  - Note: Very big sizes, such as the Earth, are hard to guess. A good habit is to say the unit first and then think about what size of number fits it.
  - Think: Which size would you like to be able to guess well, and how could you practise?

## Experiments
### e1 · The dripping bottle clock test: does a water clock keep steady time? (46 min)
- **question:** A bottle with a small hole near the bottom drips as the water runs out. Does the water level fall by the same amount in every 2 minutes?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will the level fall by the same amount in each 2 minutes, or by different amounts? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same bottle, the same hole, the same starting line and the same water are used from start to finish. Only the time changes. Every mark is made at a call from one phone stopwatch. If you have time, run the test a second time, because one run can be spoiled by a bump or a bubble. If you cannot, look for the same pattern in the first half and the second half of your one run.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the hole, with the bottle on a folded towel. Push the head of the pin into a cork or a rubber, so that the pin does not hurt the fingers. Point it away from the hands. Do the test over a sink or a tray, and wipe up spills at once so nobody slips. Use a plastic bottle, never glass. Pour the water on a plant afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Work out each fall by subtracting. Then write three sentences: what I thought would happen, what I saw, and why I think it happened. Last, write how many minutes one whole emptying took.
- **results table:** Time (minutes) | Level above the hole (centimetres) | Fall since the last mark (centimetres)
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up lays the empty bottle on a folded towel. Find a spot on the straight side wall about 2 centimetres above where the bottom curves up. Push the sewing pin through the wall and twist it once, pushing away from your hands. Then stand the bottle on the upturned tumbler in the sink with the cap off. Tape the paper strip up the opposite side, with the bottom of the strip level with the hole. Draw a pencil line across the strip 12 centimetres above the hole.  
  *Parent says:* A bottle with a hole is a clock if the water leaves at a steady pace. We will find out if it does. First, why do you think we put the strip on the side away from the hole?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes the hole with a pin. Stand the bottle in the sink. Tape a strip up the other side. Draw a line 12 centimetres above the hole.
  - Note: Start with the sewing pin. A hole can be made wider but never smaller, so a small one is the safe start. The 12 centimetre line keeps the water level in the straight part of the bottle, below the sloping shoulder. The strip is on the far side so the water jet does not wet it.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Tune the hole so that the bottle takes about 15 to 25 minutes to empty. Hold a finger over the hole and pour water to the 12 centimetre line. Start the stopwatch and lift your finger. After exactly 1 minute, mark the level on the strip and measure how far it fell. A fall of 1 to 1.5 centimetres in the first minute is right. If the fall is smaller, the hole is too small. A grown-up pushes the pin or the thin nail in again. The grown-up twists it once to widen the hole. Then you try again. If the fall is bigger, the hole is too big. Dry the bottle and press two layers of sticky tape over the hole. Then make a new hole with the pin about 3 centimetres to the side, and try again. Each try takes about 3 minutes, and most bottles need two or three.  
  *Parent says:* A clock that empties in 3 minutes is too quick for us to mark. One that takes an hour is too slow. We want the water to fall about a finger-width in the first minute. Is it falling too fast or too slowly?  
  *Child mode:* Fill to the line and start the stopwatch. After 1 minute, measure the fall. Under 1 centimetre means widen the hole. Over 1.5 means tape it and make a new hole.
  - Note: A fall of 1 to 1.5 centimetres in the first minute means about 16 to 24 minutes for the whole emptying. That gives 8 to 12 marks. Water leaves a bottle faster when the water above the hole is deeper. So the first minute is the fastest, and the fall slows after that. Bottles differ in width and holes differ in size, which is why we tune instead of guessing. A hole that does this is usually about 1 millimetre wide, a little wider than a sewing pin, but it depends on your bottle.
  - Check: The water fell 3 centimetres in the first minute. What should you do? → **Cover the hole and make a smaller one**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Refill the bottle to the 12 centimetre line while a finger covers the hole. Ask your child to say their guess and reason from the predict question. Write the guess down. Do not correct it.  
  *Parent says:* Will the water fall by the same amount in each 2 minutes? Say your guess and why you think so.  
  *Child mode:* Say your guess and why you think so. Then wait for the stopwatch.
  - Note: Children often say the water will fall by equal amounts, because a clock should tick evenly. That is a good guess. Write it down and let the bottle answer it.
- **s4 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lift your finger and start the stopwatch at the same moment. When the stopwatch shows 2 minutes, call 'mark'. Your child draws a pencil line across the strip at the water level and writes the minute beside it. Do the same at 4, 6 and 8 minutes, then every 2 minutes after that. Carry on until the water stops running.  
  *Parent says:* When I call mark, draw a line where the top of the water is. Halfway through, tell me this. The water started 12 centimetres up. Half of that is 6 centimetres. Will the water be at 6 centimetres after half of the time? Say your guess.  
  *Child mode:* Mark the water level every 2 minutes when your grown-up calls. Write the minute next to each line. Keep going until the water stops falling.
  - Ask: At the halfway time of the whole run, is the water also half way down? (good: No. It has already fallen more than half.; It fell fast at the start, so the level is lower than the middle.)
  - Note: Marking is easier if one person watches the stopwatch and the other watches the water. If the whole run takes under 14 minutes or over 28, still finish the test and write the time. In your results, one whole emptying is the time from lifting your finger until the water stops running. Water clings to a small hole, so it stops a little above the hole and not at the hole. The level reaches 6 centimetres after only about 6 minutes of a 20-minute run.
  - Wait: Keep watching until the water stops falling
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take the strip off and lay it flat. Measure from the zero line to each pencil line and write the levels in the table. Subtract to find each fall. Look at the falls from first to last. Then ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Last, write how many minutes one whole emptying took.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each line from zero. Write the levels. Find each fall. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Ask: Why did the falls get smaller? (good: Less water above the hole pushes less.; The water ran out slower as the bottle emptied.)
  - Ask: Could you use this bottle as a clock if you only ever used a whole emptying? (good: Yes, if it takes the same time on each try.; Each whole emptying could be one unit of time.)
  - Note: You should see the falls shrink. For example, the first falls may be about 2 centimetres and the last well under 1 centimetre. The exact numbers depend on your bottle. Water pushes out harder when more water sits above the hole. So the bottle runs fast when full and slowly when nearly empty. So it is an uneven clock, even though the water is running all the time. An old Indian water clock was not read by marks along the side. It was judged by one whole sinking of the bowl, which took the same time on each try. Your whole emptying time can be used the same way. What the test cannot tell us is whether the stopwatch is perfectly steady. We trust it because it agrees with other clocks, and those agree with the Sun and with atomic clocks. Ask your child how they could check a clock that they trust.
  - Think: If you filled the bottle only to half, would it take half as long to empty?

### a1 · The wiggly line test: does a smaller step give a longer edge? (25 min)
- **record:** Write three lengths for your edge: with the 3 centimetre step, the 1 centimetre step and the thread. Under them, write the straight distance between the two ends. Then write one sentence on what happens as the step gets smaller.
- **a1s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tear a sheet of A4 paper into two pieces, from the top edge to the bottom edge. Tear slowly, in bites about 2 centimetres long. Turn the tear a lot with each bite, so that the edge zigzags like a row of small hills. Lay the torn piece on the clean sheet. Run a pencil along the torn edge to trace it. Make a dot at each end of the pencil line.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to ask a plain question. How long is this edge? Before we measure, guess a number of centimetres.  
  *Child mode:* Tear the paper slowly, in many small zigzag bites. Trace the edge on the clean sheet. Put a dot at each end.
  - Note: A neat, straight tear gives a small result, so tear deliberately wobbly. If your first edge looks nearly straight, tear another with bigger zigzags. Backup: draw a very wiggly coast with a pencil, with deep bays and sharp points, about 30 centimetres from end to end, and use that.
- **a1s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts two strips of card. One is exactly 3 centimetres long and one is exactly 1 centimetre long, each about 1 centimetre wide. Check each with the ruler.  
  *Parent says:* These strips will be our steps. One is long and one is short. Which do you think will give the longer answer?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts two card strips, 3 centimetres and 1 centimetre. Guess which will give the longer answer.
  - Note: Accurate strips matter more than neat ones. Measure the cut strip with the ruler and trim if needed.
- **a1s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start with the 3 centimetre strip. Put one end on the first dot. Swing the other end until it touches the pencil line, and make a dot there. Slide the strip so that its first end is on the new dot, and repeat. Count each step until you reach the far end of the line. Measure the small gap left at the end with the ruler. Work out the length: steps times 3, plus the gap. Do the same with the 1 centimetre strip.  
  *Parent says:* Count your steps out loud. The strip is always straight, so each step is exactly 3 centimetres in a straight line. Why might that skip some of the bends in the edge?  
  *Child mode:* Step along the line with the 3 centimetre strip. Count the steps and measure the little gap at the end. Do it again with the 1 centimetre strip.
  - Note: Expect the 1 centimetre steps to give a longer total than the 3 centimetre steps. For example, a child might get about 31 centimetres with 3 centimetre steps and about 37 with 1 centimetre steps. Your numbers will differ. If the two totals are within 3 centimetres, tear a more jagged edge and try again, because the effect grows with the number of bends.
- **a1s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the thread along the pencil line, following every bend. Hold the thread at the far dot, pull it straight and measure it with the ruler. Then measure the straight distance between the two dots. Write all four lengths down in order of step size, from the biggest step to the smallest.  
  *Parent says:* You now have four answers for one edge. Which one is the real length? What do you notice as the steps get smaller?  
  *Child mode:* Lay the thread along the line and measure it straight. Measure the straight distance between the dots too. Say what you notice as the steps get smaller.
  - Ask: What happens to the length as the step gets smaller? (good: It gets longer.; A smaller step follows more bends.)
  - Ask: Which of your four answers is the real length of the edge? (good: Each is the length for that step size.; There is no single answer until we say how fine the measuring was.)
  - Note: The straight distance between the dots is the biggest possible step and gives the shortest answer. The thread follows the most bends and gives the longest. Each time the steps shrink, the total grows. The edge did not change. A real coast does the same, which is why India's coast has more than one length. What this test cannot tell us is whether the thread's answer is the true length. A thread also skips the tiny bends between the paper fibres. Ask your child what they would use to follow even smaller bends.
  - Check: You make the step half as long. What do you expect the total to do? → **Get a little longer**
### a2 · Two hands, one table: why we need units that are the same for everyone (15 min)

### a3 · Guess first, then measure: how close can your guess get? (20 min)
- **record:** Make a table with four columns: the thing, my guess, the measured value and how far off I was. Circle every guess that came within a quarter of the true value.
- **a3s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find your anchors. Measure the width of your finger with a ruler. Measure the height of a door with a tape, or know it is about 2 metres. Hold the full one-litre bottle of water so you know how 1 kilogram feels. Fill a teaspoon with water and remember that it holds about 5 millilitres.  
  *Parent says:* These are your anchors. A good guess starts by comparing a new thing with something whose size you already know. How wide is your finger?  
  *Child mode:* Measure your finger. Hold the litre bottle. Remember a door is about 2 metres high and a teaspoon holds 5 millilitres.
  - Note: Anchors are the sizes you carry with you. A finger is about 1 centimetre wide and a door is about 2 metres high. A full litre bottle is your kilogram. Do not tell your child the answers to the later guesses.
- **a3s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pick six things to guess. Use the width of a window, the length of the room and the height of a chair. Then add three more: a packet of rice (its mass), a tumbler of water (its volume) and a cooking pot (its volume). Ask your child to guess each one, with its unit, and write the guess. Ask which anchor they used for each.  
  *Parent says:* Guess each one and tell me which anchor you used. A guess without a unit does not count.  
  *Child mode:* Guess six things, with their units. Write each guess before you measure. Say which anchor you used.
  - Note: Do not allow measuring until all six guesses are written. The unit is part of the guess. For the rice packet, hide the label until after the guess.
- **a3s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure each thing. Use the ruler or tape for lengths and the label for the rice packet. Use the jug or spoon for the volumes. Write each measured value and work out how far off the guess was. A guess within a quarter of the true value is a good one. For example, if the true value is 100, any guess from 75 to 125 is good.  
  *Parent says:* Now we measure. How far off was each guess? Which was closest, and which anchor did you use for it?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each thing. Work out how far off you were. Circle the guesses within a quarter.
  - Ask: Which of your six guesses was closest, and why? (good: The one near an anchor I knew well.; Comparing with a known size helped most.)
  - Note: Most children guess lengths well and volumes badly. That is useful to know. A tumbler usually holds 200 to 250 millilitres, which is 40 to 50 teaspoons.
- **a3s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the six rows. Ask your child whether a guess ever caught a mistake, such as reading a tape in the wrong unit. Ask which instrument they would pick to check a guess that is far off.  
  *Parent says:* If your guess is 2 metres and the tape says 20, what do you do?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you would do if your guess and your tape were very different.
  - Note: A guess works like a safety net. If it disagrees badly with the instrument, we check the unit, the zero and the reading before we trust either.
  - Think: Which of your guesses would you trust most if you could not measure it?
### a4 · Reading a ruler and a jug: the zero, your eye and the curved water (20 min)
- **record:** Write what you found for each trap in one sentence: the zero, the angle of your eye and the curve of the water.
- **a4s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at your ruler. Find the zero mark and see if it is at the very end or a little way in. Lay a pencil along the ruler with its end on the 1 centimetre mark, not the zero. Read where the other end reaches. Subtract 1 to find the pencil's length. Then slide the pencil back so its end sits on the zero mark and read again. Compare the two lengths.  
  *Parent says:* A measurement starts counting from a mark. Where does your ruler start counting? What happens if you start the pencil somewhere else?  
  *Child mode:* Put the pencil's end on the 1 centimetre mark. Read the other end. Take away 1. Then put the end on zero and read again.
  - Note: Both ways give the same length when you subtract the starting mark. This is the habit of starting at zero or, when you cannot, writing down where you started. Open that eye and close the other. Note the mark again. Then look straight down from above and say which mark the pencil really touches.

  *Child mode:* Hold the pencil in front of the ruler. Look with one eye, then the other. The pencil seems to jump along the ruler.
  - Ask: Why does the pencil seem to jump? (good: Each eye looks from a different place.; We see the pencil against a different part of the ruler.)
  - Note: The pencil seems to jump a few centimetres, because your two eyes are about 6 centimetres apart. Reading a ruler or a jug from one side makes the mark seem to shift. The fix is to bring your eye level with the mark and look straight on.

  *Child mode:* Bend down until your eye is level with the water. Look at the water's edge. Read the flat middle.
  - Note: Water climbs the glass slightly at the edge, so the surface is a shallow curve. Read the flat bottom of the curve. Reading from above or below can shift a reading by 10 or 20 millilitres in a small jug.
  - Think: Why might two people get different readings from the same jug?

### ch2 · A sinking-bowl clock like the old Indian ghati bowl (30 min)
- **problem:** Make a clock from a plastic cup with a pinhole, floating on water in a bucket, that sinks in about 5 minutes. Three runs should agree within 30 seconds. There is more than one good answer.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes and widens every hole, with the cup resting on a folded towel, just as in the bottle clock. Use a plastic cup, never glass. Keep the bucket on the floor or in a sink, so that a spill does no harm, and tip the water on a plant afterwards.

- **general:** Tune the hole size by widening or taping and re-piercing until the sink time is about 5 minutes.; Keep the cup upright with three small lumps of plasticine inside, round the bottom edge. Keep them away from the hole, so that the cup fills only through the hole.; Run three times and use the middle value.
- **creative:** Try a different plastic container, such as a curd cup or a small bowl, and compare how long it takes to sink with the first cup.; Make the same clock with two different holes, and mark on a chart which one makes a 5-minute clock and which makes a 10-minute one.; Count sinkings. - **ch2s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up makes one hole in the middle of the cup's bottom with the sewing pin. The pin points away from the hands. Float the cup upright in the bucket and see that it floats level.   *Parent says:* Long ago, an Indian timekeeper floated a copper bowl with a small hole in a vessel of water. The bowl filled slowly and sank after exactly one ghati, which is 24 minutes. We will make a smaller one. Why does the cup need a hole?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes one hole in the cup's bottom. Float the cup upright in the bucket.
  - Note: Make the first hole with the sewing pin. A hole can be widened and never made smaller. A cup that tips can fill from the top instead of the hole, so keep it upright.
- **ch2s2 (10 min)**

  - Note: Expect the first try to be very slow, because a pin hole is tiny. The cup may take half an hour or more. Do not wait more than 10 minutes. Go straight to the tuning step if the cup has not sunk by then. A run in which the cup tips over should be thrown away and done again.

  *Child mode:* Too slow: ask a grown-up to widen the hole. Too fast: ask a grown-up to tape it and make a smaller hole.

  *Parent says:* Are your three runs close together? What would you change to make a better clock?  
  *Child mode:* Time three runs. Work out the gap between the longest and the shortest.
  - Note: The old bowls were judged by whether they sank in the same time on every run, not by being exactly 24 minutes. A clock is good when it is steady and then checked against a better one.
  - Think: If your three times were 4.9, 5.1 and 5.0 minutes, would you trust the cup? What else would you check?

14. (mcq) A cold thermometer is put into a small cup of warm water. What may happen to the water?  
   Options: It heats up / Nothing. Measuring never changes anything. / It cools a little, so measuring changed it  
   **Answer:** It cools a little, so measuring changed it. *Why:* The thermometer takes a little warmth from the water. For small amounts, measuring changes the thing measured a little.
15. (mcq) A 400 gram packet costs 60 rupees and a 1 kilogram packet costs 140. Which is cheaper for each 100 grams?  
   Options: The 400 gram packet at 15 rupees / The 1 kilogram packet at 14 rupees / They are the same  
   **Answer:** The 1 kilogram packet at 14 rupees. *Why:* 60 ÷ 4 = 15 rupees for each 100 grams, and 140 ÷ 10 = 14. The bigger packet is cheaper here. Comparing the totals alone would not show this.
16. (sort) Sort each by whether it has a standard unit and instrument.  
   **Answer:** the height of a door → Has a standard unit; the heat of a cup of tea → Has a standard unit; how beautiful a kolam is → Has no standard unit; how kind a neighbour is → Has no standard unit; the time a bus takes → Has a standard unit; how much a tooth hurts → Has no standard unit. *Why:* Length, temperature and time have units and instruments. For beauty, kindness and pain, scientists still argue about whether any unit could be agreed.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which job needs a measurement rather than a guess?  
   Options: Stepping over a puddle / Choosing a seat on a bus / Giving a baby medicine  
   **Answer:** Giving a baby medicine. *Why:* Too much medicine can harm a baby, and two people must agree on the dose. A puddle or a seat is safe to guess.
2. (mcq) A road sign says 'Madurai 40'. Why might a visitor from abroad be puzzled?  
   Options: 40 is too big a number / The sign needs a longer name / They may expect miles, not kilometres  
   **Answer:** They may expect miles, not kilometres. *Why:* A number needs its unit. Locals know the unit, and a visitor from a country that uses miles may not.
3. (mcq) What do you do when you measure a table?  
   Options: Choose a unit, then count how many fit / Guess, then check with a friend / Find the real width hiding in the table  
   **Answer:** Choose a unit, then count how many fit. *Why:* A measurement is a unit and a count. There is no hidden 'real width' without a unit to compare with.
4. (mcq) Which is true about the prefix milli?  
   Options: 100 millilitres make one litre / One millilitre makes one litre / 1,000 millilitres make one litre  
   **Answer:** 1,000 millilitres make one litre. *Why:* Milli means a thousandth, so it takes 1,000 of them to make the whole. A hundred is the prefix centi.
5. (mcq) A one-litre bottle of water has a mass of about 1 kilogram. What is the mass of 500 millilitres?  
   Options: Half a kilogram / About 50 grams / About 5 kilograms  
   **Answer:** Half a kilogram. *Why:* 500 millilitres is half a litre, so its mass is half a kilogram, which is 500 grams.
6. (mcq) A thermometer is marked from 35 to 42 degrees. Which job does it suit?  
   Options: Taking a child's temperature / Checking boiling water / Checking a freezer  
   **Answer:** Taking a child's temperature. *Why:* A body is between about 35 and 42 degrees, which is the instrument's range. Boiling water and a freezer are far outside it.
7. (mcq) A ruler's zero mark is 3 millimetres in from its end. A pencil starts at the ruler's end and reaches 14.0 centimetres. How long is it?  
   Options: 14.0 centimetres / 13.7 centimetres / 14.3 centimetres  
   **Answer:** 14.3 centimetres. *Why:* The pencil starts 3 millimetres before the zero mark, so it is 14.0 plus 0.3, which is 14.3 centimetres. Reading from the end hides the gap.
8. (mcq) A torn edge is stepped with 3 centimetres and then with 1 centimetre. What happens to the total?  
   Options: It gets longer, as the small step follows more bends / It stays the same, as the edge did not change / It gets shorter, as small steps are more exact  
   **Answer:** It gets longer, as the small step follows more bends. *Why:* A smaller step follows more of the bends, so the total grows. The edge did not change, but the way of measuring did.
9. (mcq) Which is true of every measurement?  
   Options: It is exact if the tool is good / It is always the same for everyone / It cannot see finer than the instrument's smallest step  
   **Answer:** It cannot see finer than the instrument's smallest step. *Why:* Every instrument has a smallest step, and no reading can be finer than that. A good tool makes the step small but not zero.
10. (mcq) Why guess before you measure?  
   Options: A guess makes measuring unnecessary / A guess gives the exact answer / A guess catches a mistake such as a wrong unit  
   **Answer:** A guess catches a mistake such as a wrong unit. *Why:* If the guess and the tape disagree a lot, you check the unit, the zero and the reading. A guess is a safety net.
11. (mcq) A teaspoon holds 5 millilitres. About how much does a tumbler of water hold?  
   Options: 25 millilitres / 250 millilitres / 2.5 litres  
   **Answer:** 250 millilitres. *Why:* A tumbler holds about 40 to 50 teaspoons, which is 200 to 250 millilitres. 25 millilitres is only five teaspoons.
12. (mcq) Which of these can a thermometer not tell you?  
   Options: How hot a cup of tea is / How uncomfortable the heat feels to someone / The temperature of a child's forehead  
   **Answer:** How uncomfortable the heat feels to someone. *Why:* A thermometer gives a number for temperature. It cannot tell how that heat feels to a person, which has no unit.
13. (mcq) Someone says that old definitions of the kilogram, such as a metal cylinder, were fine for ever. What is a good reply?  
   Options: An object can change or be lost, so a constant of nature is steadier / Metal never changes / Nobody needed a better one  
   **Answer:** An object can change or be lost, so a constant of nature is steadier. *Why:* A cylinder can lose atoms, be scratched or be lost. A constant of nature is the same everywhere, so any laboratory can make the unit again.
14. (mcq) Which is a fair thing to say about a school mark?  
   Options: It shows everything a child has learnt / It is false because numbers are false / It is a number that leaves out much of what was learnt  
   **Answer:** It is a number that leaves out much of what was learnt. *Why:* A mark is useful, but it turns a whole term into one number. What it leaves out is part of what we know.

## Applications
- Cooking in many South Indian homes follows ratios in one cup. Idli batter is often made with about two or three tumblers of rice for one of urad dal. The size of the tumbler does not matter, because the same unit is used for both.
- Medicine for children is measured in millilitres with the marked cap or a syringe. A kitchen spoon can hold twice as much as another, so doctors ask for the cap. Check the cap's lines before you give any dose.
- At a petrol pump the display must show zero before the petrol starts, and the pump's meter is checked by the Legal Metrology department. This is the same habit as checking the zero of a ruler or a scale.
- The India Meteorological Department reports rain in millimetres. One millimetre of rain means a litre of water on each square metre of flat ground. A straight-sided jar left outside can show the depth.
- A plot of land may be advertised in cents, gunthas or bighas. Ask the seller to write the area in square metres too, so that two plots can be compared in one measure.
- Surveyors measure what they cannot reach by angles and distances. The maps of the Survey of India, from roads to mountain heights, rest on many measured angles and on a base line measured with great care.

## Spotted around us
- On a cough syrup bottle, look at the cap. Find the marks at 5 and 10 millilitres. Notice that the doctor's instruction is written in millilitres and not in spoons.
- At a petrol pump or on a milk packet, find the number and the unit. Notice that the pump shows zero before it starts, and the packet says its net quantity, such as 500 mL.
- At the vegetable seller's stall, look at the underside of a metal weight. Notice the small stamp that shows it has been checked by an inspector.
- At a tailor's shop, watch the tailor measure. Notice that she uses a soft tape, writes the numbers in a book, and often measures a second time before cutting.
- Beside a road, look at a milestone or a sign with a town and a number. Notice that it says kilometres, and that the number goes down as you get closer.
- Look at the clock in your home and at the clock on your phone. Notice that they may differ by a minute. Ask how each was set, and what they were checked against.
- Look at two rulers or tapes. Notice where each one starts, and whether the zero is at the very end or a little way in.

## Open questions
- **Is there a smallest possible length and a shortest possible time?** Some theories that try to join gravity with the physics of tiny particles say that space and time come in smallest pieces. Those pieces would be far too small for any instrument that exists, so the idea cannot yet be tested.
- **What is time?** Clocks measure time to astonishing exactness, and physics uses it in every sum. There is still no agreed account of what time is, or of why it seems to flow one way. Some physicists think it is not a basic part of nature.
- **Are the constants of nature truly constant?** The units now rest on numbers such as the speed of light and the Planck constant. Astronomers compare light from very distant, very old galaxies with light made in laboratories to see if these numbers have changed. So far no change has been confirmed, but the search goes on.
- **Can experiences such as pain be measured?** Brain scans show activity that goes with pain, and people can rate their pain from 0 to 10. Nobody has shown how to compare one being's pain with another's, because there is no unit and no way to set two experiences side by side.
- **What is matter, and where does mass come from?** Most of the mass of everyday things comes from energy that holds together the particles inside the centres of atoms. What matter finally is, and why the basic particles have the masses they have, is not known.

## Connected maths
- A paperback's pages are 2.4 centimetres thick and have 120 leaves. Find the thickness of one leaf in millimetres. A ruler reading can be wrong by up to 0.5 millimetre. How big is that error for one leaf measured alone, and for one leaf found from the stack? **0.2 millimetre for one leaf. An error of 0.5 millimetre on one leaf is bigger than the leaf. From the stack it spreads over 120 leaves, which is about 0.004 millimetre.**. 2.4 cm = 24 mm, and 24 ÷ 120 = 0.2 mm. Measured alone, a 0.5 mm error is more than twice the 0.2 mm leaf. In the stack, 0.5 ÷ 120 ≈ 0.004 mm. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L4`)
- Imagine a bottle clock with the water level at 12.0, 9.7, 7.7, 5.9 and 4.3 centimetres at 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 minutes. Find the fall in each 2 minutes. What pattern do you see, and what would the falls be for an even clock? **2.3, 2.0, 1.8 and 1.6 centimetres. The falls shrink each time. An even clock would fall by the same amount each time, for example 1.2 centimetres if it emptied 12 centimetres in 20 minutes.**. 12.0 − 9.7 = 2.3, 9.7 − 7.7 = 2.0, 7.7 − 5.9 = 1.8 and 5.9 − 4.3 = 1.6. Each fall is a little smaller than the one before. An even clock covering 12 cm in 20 minutes would fall 12 ÷ 10 = 1.2 cm every 2 minutes. (queues `math.time.speed.L4`)
- A packet of 60 grams of biscuits costs 10 rupees. Another packet of 250 grams costs 45 rupees. Find the price for each 100 grams of each. Which is cheaper for its size? **About 16.7 rupees for each 100 grams for the small packet, and 18 for the big one. The small packet is cheaper for its size.**. 10 ÷ 60 × 100 ≈ 16.7 rupees. 45 ÷ 250 × 100 = 18 rupees. The two packets have different sizes, so compare a price for the same amount. (queues `math.money.unit-price.L4`)
- Two towns are about 800 kilometres apart. The Sun's rays reach the ground at angles that differ by 7.2 degrees between them. A full circle is 360 degrees. Work out the distance round the Earth. What would it be if the angle were 7.5 degrees? **About 40,000 kilometres. With 7.5 degrees it would be about 38,400 kilometres.**. 360 ÷ 7.2 = 50, so 800 km is one fiftieth of the way round, and 800 × 50 = 40,000 km. With 7.5 degrees, 360 ÷ 7.5 = 48, and 800 × 48 = 38,400 km. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Imagine a coast measured with steps of 100 kilometres takes 30 steps, and with steps of 10 kilometres it takes 420 steps. Find the two lengths. How many times more steps? How many times longer is the coast? **3,000 kilometres and 4,200 kilometres. There are 14 times more steps. The coast is 1.4 times longer.**. 30 × 100 = 3,000 km. 420 × 10 = 4,200 km. 420 ÷ 30 = 14, and 4,200 ÷ 3,000 = 1.4. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L4`)
