# Moon phases and eclipses: how much of the Moon is lit, and where a shadow ends
`sci.sky.moon-phases.L5`

**Summary.** The Moon's dark shadow only just reaches the Earth, and in some months it falls short. So why are total solar eclipses rare? In this unit you will measure how much of a sock Moon is lit at seven places, and find where the dark shadow of a seed ends.

**Intro.** *The Sun is about 400 times as wide as the Moon. So how can the small Moon cover the whole Sun in a solar eclipse? And why does it sometimes leave a bright ring of Sun showing?* In this unit you answer both questions with a coin, a plate and a lamp. First you hide a steel plate behind a coin. Then you measure how much of a model Moon is lit at seven places on its path. You lift seeds under a lamp and find where each dark shadow ends. At the end you can say why eclipses are rare and why the eclipsed Moon turns red. You can also say how people measured the Earth with a shadow.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can explain the Moon's phases and the two pakshas, and say why phases are not the Earth's shadow.
1. By the end I can use the Moon's angle from the Sun to say how much of its face is lit.
2. By the end I can explain that moonlight is reflected sunlight, and why the dark Moon looks white.
3. By the end I can tell the umbra from the penumbra, and use the rule for where a dark shadow ends.
4. By the end I can explain total, partial and ring solar eclipses, using the Moon's shadow and its distance.
5. By the end I can explain a lunar eclipse, why the Moon turns red, and who can see it.
6. By the end I can explain why eclipses do not come every month, and find the pattern of eclipse seasons.
7. By the end I can tell how eclipses were used to measure the Earth and the Moon, and how to watch one safely.

## Terms
- **Phase**: The shape of the lit part of the Moon that we see on one night. The Moon itself stays a whole round ball. *e.g.* A thin crescent, a half Moon and a full Moon are three phases.
- **Amavasya (new Moon)**: The night when the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun. Its lit half faces away from us, so we see no Moon. *e.g.* Diwali falls on Amavasya, the darkest night of its month.
- **Purnima (full Moon)**: The night when the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon. We see the whole lit half of the Moon. *e.g.* On Purnima the Moon rises in the east as the Sun sets in the west.
- **Shukla Paksha**: The bright fortnight, from Amavasya to Purnima. The lit part of the Moon that we see grows each night. *e.g.* Three days into Shukla Paksha, a thin crescent shows in the west after sunset.
- **Krishna Paksha**: The dark fortnight, from Purnima to Amavasya. The lit part of the Moon that we see shrinks each night. *e.g.* A week into Krishna Paksha, a half Moon rises near midnight.
- **Reflected light**: Light that falls on a thing and bounces off it. The Moon makes no light. Moonlight is sunlight that has bounced off the Moon. *e.g.* A white wall in sunshine lights up a shady room with reflected light.
- **Reflectivity**: The share of the light falling on a surface that the surface sends back. It is given as parts in every 100. *e.g.* Fresh snow sends back about 85 parts in every 100. The Moon sends back about 12.
- **Angle from the Sun**: How far apart the Moon and the Sun are in our sky, measured in degrees. It grows by about 12 degrees each day after Amavasya. *e.g.* At a half Moon the angle is 90 degrees. At Purnima it is 180 degrees.
- **Eclipse**: What happens when one body in space moves into the shadow of another. The Sun, the Earth and the Moon must be in one straight line. *e.g.* On 7 September 2025 the Moon passed through the Earth's shadow, and people across India saw a lunar eclipse.
- **Solar eclipse**: An eclipse in which the Moon comes between the Sun and the Earth. The Moon's shadow falls on part of the Earth. It can only happen at Amavasya. *e.g.* On 22 July 2009 a total solar eclipse crossed India from Surat to Patna.
- **Lunar eclipse**: An eclipse in which the Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon. The Earth's shadow falls on the Moon. It can only happen at Purnima. *e.g.* In a total lunar eclipse the whole Moon is inside the Earth's shadow, and it turns a dark red.
- **Umbra**: The dark middle of a shadow. From inside the umbra, the whole lamp or the whole Sun is hidden. *e.g.* People standing in the Moon's umbra see a total solar eclipse.
- **Penumbra**: The paler ring round the dark middle of a shadow. From inside the penumbra, part of the lamp or part of the Sun still shows. *e.g.* People standing in the Moon's penumbra see a partial solar eclipse.
- **Totality**: The part of an eclipse when the Sun or the Moon is completely covered. In a solar eclipse it never lasts longer than about seven and a half minutes. *e.g.* At Varanasi on 22 July 2009, totality lasted about three minutes.
- **Annular eclipse**: A solar eclipse in which the Moon is too far from the Earth to cover the whole Sun. A bright ring of Sun shows round the Moon. Annular means ring-shaped. *e.g.* On 26 December 2019 people in Coimbatore, Ooty and Kannur saw a ring of Sun.
- **Corona**: The Sun's faint outer layer of very hot, thin gas. It is always there, but the Sun's glare hides it. It shows only when the Sun's bright face is covered. *e.g.* In totality the corona appears as a pale white glow round the black Moon.
- **Node**: One of the two points where the Moon's tilted path crosses the Earth's path round the Sun. Eclipses happen only near a node. Indian astronomers named the two nodes Rahu and Ketu. *e.g.* If Purnima comes when the Moon is near a node, there is a lunar eclipse.
- **Eclipse season**: A stretch of about five weeks when the Sun is near a node, so eclipses can happen. Eclipse seasons come about every six Moon months. *e.g.* In 2026 the eclipse seasons were in February and March, and again in August.

## Explanations
### Moonlight is reflected sunlight · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon makes no light of its own. The Sun lights it, and some of that sunlight bounces off the Moon to our eyes.

Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach the Moon. The Moon's ground sends back about 12 parts in every 100 of that light. The reflected light reaches the Earth about 1.3 seconds later. Very little of the Sun's light takes this path. So the full Moon is about 400,000 times dimmer than the Sun. That is why you can look at the Moon for as long as you like, and must never look at the Sun. The Earth reflects sunlight too. Seen from the Moon, the full Earth shines more than 40 times as brightly as the full Moon shines for us.
- e.g. The Sun, a lamp and a firefly make their own light.
- e.g. The Moon, the planets and a white wall only reflect light.
- e.g. Moonlight is sunlight that left the Sun about eight and a half minutes earlier.
### Why the Moon has phases · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun always lights the half of the Moon that faces it. The Moon goes round the Earth, so we see a different share of that lit half each night.

At Amavasya the Moon is between us and the Sun. Its lit half faces away from us, and we see nothing. Over the next two weeks the Moon moves round the Earth, and we see more of the lit half each night. This fortnight is Shukla Paksha. At Purnima the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, and we see the whole lit half. Then the lit part shrinks through Krishna Paksha. One full round, from Amavasya to Amavasya, takes about 29 and a half days. The phases are not made by the Earth's shadow. The Earth's shadow touches the Moon only during a lunar eclipse. A crescent Moon is often in the sky beside the Sun, where the Earth's shadow cannot reach.
- e.g. At Amavasya, the lit half of the Moon faces away from the Earth.
- e.g. At a half Moon, we see half of the lit half. That is a quarter of the whole ball.
- e.g. At Purnima, the whole lit half of the Moon faces the Earth.
### How much of the Moon is lit: the angle rule · *defined*
**Definition:** The share of the Moon's face that looks lit depends on the angle between the Moon and the Sun in our sky. It is 0 at 0 degrees, half at 90 degrees and all at 180 degrees.

In How Much of the Moon Is Lit, a ball half covered by a dark sock stood at seven places round a viewer. At 30 degrees from the Sun, only about 7 parts in 100 of its face looked lit. At 60 degrees it was 25, at 90 degrees 50, at 120 degrees 75, and at 150 degrees about 93. The lit share does not grow evenly. It grows slowly near Amavasya and near Purnima, and fastest near the half Moon. The reason is the direction we look from. Near Amavasya the lit half is turned almost fully away, and we see only its edge. The Moon moves about 12 degrees further from the Sun each day. So the angle tells you the phase, and the phase tells you the angle.
- e.g. About 2 and a half days after Amavasya, the Moon is 30 degrees from the Sun. About 7 parts in 100 of its face are lit.
- e.g. About 7 and a half days after Amavasya, the Moon is 90 degrees from the Sun. It looks like a half Moon.
- e.g. The shares at 60 and 120 degrees add up to 100. So do the shares at 30 and 150 degrees.
### The Moon is dark, but it looks white · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon's ground is dark grey. It reflects about 12 parts in every 100 of the light that falls on it, about the same as an old tar road.

In A Black Card in the Dark, a black card lit alone in a dark room looked pale grey or even white. When a white strip came into the same light, the card looked black again. Our eyes judge how light a thing is by comparing it with what is near it. The Moon hangs in a black sky, with nothing brighter beside it. So the brightest thing we can see looks white. In the daytime sky the same Moon looks pale and dull beside a sunlit cloud. Neil Armstrong, the first person to walk on the Moon, said its dust stuck to his boots like powdered charcoal.
- e.g. Fresh snow reflects about 85 parts in 100, and white paper about 80.
- e.g. The Earth, with its clouds and seas, reflects about 30 parts in 100.
- e.g. The Moon reflects about 12 parts in 100, and charcoal about 4.
### A shadow has a dark middle, a pale ring and an end · *defined*
**Definition:** A shadow made by a wide light has two parts. The umbra is the dark middle, where the whole light is hidden. The penumbra is the pale ring, where part of the light still shows. The umbra gets narrower with distance and ends at a point.

In The Shadow That Runs Out, you lifted a seed above paper under a lamp. The dark middle of its shadow shrank and then vanished, and only a pale smudge was left. A seed twice as wide had a dark shadow twice as long. The rule was the same for every seed. Divide the lamp's distance by the lamp's width, and you get the length of the dark shadow in seed-widths. The Sun is about 108 Sun-widths away from us. So in sunlight every dark shadow ends about 108 widths behind the thing that makes it. The shadow is a cone of darkness in space, with its tip pointing away from the Sun.
- e.g. A peppercorn 5 millimetres wide has a dark shadow about 54 centimetres long in sunlight.
- e.g. The Moon is 3,474 kilometres wide. Its dark shadow is about 375,000 kilometres long.
- e.g. The Earth is 12,742 kilometres wide. Its dark shadow is about 1.4 million kilometres long.
### A solar eclipse is the tip of the Moon's shadow · *defined*
**Definition:** In a total solar eclipse, the tip of the Moon's umbra touches the Earth. Only people inside that small dark spot see the Sun fully covered.

The Moon's dark shadow is about 375,000 kilometres long. The ground below the Moon is about that far away. So only the narrow tip of the cone reaches the Earth. The dark spot on the ground is never more than about 270 kilometres wide. It moves across the Earth at more than 1,000 kilometres an hour, along a strip called the path of totality. At any one place, totality lasts a few minutes, and never longer than about seven and a half. Round the dark spot lies the penumbra, thousands of kilometres wide. People there see a partial eclipse, with a bite taken out of the Sun. Everyone else sees no eclipse.
- e.g. Someone standing inside the umbra sees a total eclipse. The sky is dark and the corona shows.
- e.g. Someone standing inside the penumbra sees a partial eclipse. Part of the Sun is still bright.
- e.g. Any one town sees a total solar eclipse about once in 375 years, on average.
### Total or ring: the Moon's distance changes · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon's path round the Earth is a slightly stretched circle. When the Moon is near, its dark shadow reaches the ground and the eclipse is total. When it is far, the shadow ends above the ground and a ring of Sun is left.

In The Coin Eclipse, a coin near your eye hid the whole plate. When you moved the coin a little further away, a ring of plate showed round it. The Moon does the same. Its centre is on average about 363,000 kilometres from the Earth's centre at its nearest, and about 405,000 at its furthest. The Sun is about 400 times as wide as the Moon, and about 390 times as far away. So the two look almost exactly the same size in our sky. A near Moon looks a little bigger than the Sun, and covers it. A far Moon looks a little smaller. Then people on the centre line see an annular eclipse, a ring of Sun round a black Moon. The sky stays bright and the corona stays hidden.
- e.g. A total solar eclipse was seen on 22 July 2009 from Surat, Varanasi and Patna.
- e.g. A ring eclipse was seen on 26 December 2019 from Kannur, Ooty and Coimbatore.
- e.g. Another ring eclipse was seen on 15 January 2010 from Kanyakumari and Rameswaram.
### A lunar eclipse: the Moon inside the Earth's shadow · *defined*
**Definition:** In a lunar eclipse the Moon passes through the Earth's shadow. It can happen only at Purnima, when the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon.

The Earth's dark shadow is about 1.4 million kilometres long, far longer than the distance to the Moon. Where the Moon crosses it, the dark shadow is still about 9,200 kilometres wide. That is about 2.6 times the width of the Moon. So the whole Moon can go inside, and it can stay there for up to about an hour and three-quarters. Everyone on the night side of the Earth can watch, because the shadow is on the Moon itself. A lunar eclipse is safe to watch with bare eyes. If only part of the Moon enters the dark shadow, the eclipse is partial. If the Moon passes only through the pale penumbra, it dims so little that most people notice nothing.
- e.g. In a total lunar eclipse, the whole Moon is inside the umbra.
- e.g. In a partial lunar eclipse, a curved dark edge covers part of the Moon.
- e.g. In a penumbral lunar eclipse, the Moon is only in the pale outer shadow.
### Why the eclipsed Moon turns red · *defined*
**Definition:** During a total lunar eclipse a little sunlight still reaches the Moon. The Earth's air bends this light into the shadow, and the air lets mostly red light through.

The Earth's air works like a thin lens all round the planet. Sunlight that skims through the air is bent inwards, into the Earth's shadow. On the way, the air scatters most of the blue light out sideways. The same thing reddens the Sun at sunrise and sunset. So the light that reaches the Moon is the red light of every sunrise and sunset on the Earth at that moment. An astronaut on the Moon would see the black Earth cover the Sun, with a thin red ring of glowing air round it. The colour changes from one eclipse to the next. When a big volcano has filled the high air with dust, less light gets through. After Mount Pinatubo erupted in 1991, the eclipsed Moon of December 1992 was so dark that it almost vanished.
- e.g. When the high air is clear, the eclipsed Moon is a bright copper-red or orange.
- e.g. When the high air is dusty after a volcano, the eclipsed Moon is dark brown or grey.
- e.g. Astronomers rate the brightness from 0 to 4 on the Danjon scale, made by Andre Danjon in 1921.
### Why there is not an eclipse every month · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon's path is tilted about 5 degrees against the Earth's path round the Sun. So at most Amavasyas and Purnimas, the Moon passes above or below the straight line, and the shadows miss.

The two paths cross at two points, called nodes. Indian astronomers named them Rahu and Ketu. An eclipse happens only when Amavasya or Purnima comes while the Moon is near a node. The Sun lines up with a node about every 173 days. Around that time there is an eclipse season of about five weeks. Every season has at least one solar eclipse and one lunar eclipse, about two weeks apart. In Eclipse Detective, the 12 eclipses of 2024, 2025 and 2026 fell into six seasons. Each season came about six Moon months after the one before. The seasons come about 19 days earlier each year, because the nodes slowly slide round the Moon's path.
- e.g. A year has 12 or 13 Purnimas, but only 2 or 3 lunar eclipses.
- e.g. The eclipses of 2025 came in March and in September.
- e.g. The eclipses of 2026 came in February and March, and in August.
### Eclipses repeat: the saros · *defined*
**Definition:** After 18 years and about 11 days, the Sun, the Moon and the node come back to almost the same places. So each eclipse is followed by a very similar one after that time. This cycle is called the saros.

Sky-watchers in Babylon, in today's Iraq, found this pattern more than 2,000 years ago from long lists of eclipses written on clay tablets. They could say when to expect an eclipse without knowing what caused it. The total solar eclipse of 29 March 2006 was followed by a similar one on 8 April 2024. The next in that family comes on 20 April 2042. The extra third of a day in the cycle lets the Earth turn a third of the way round. So each eclipse in the family falls about a third of the way round the world from the last. Today astronomers work out the Moon's path so exactly that eclipses are known to the second, hundreds of years ahead.
- e.g. One saros is 223 Moon months, which is 18 years and about 11 days.
- e.g. 29 March 2006, 8 April 2024 and 20 April 2042 are three total solar eclipses in one family.
- e.g. India's Aryabhata gave rules in 499 CE for working out the time and size of an eclipse.
### Measuring the Earth, the Moon and the Sun with eclipses · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth's shadow on the Moon is always curved like part of a circle. Its size, compared with the Moon, shows how big the Earth is compared with the Moon.

About 2,400 years ago the Greek thinker Aristotle pointed out that the Earth's shadow on the Moon is round in every lunar eclipse. Only a ball throws a round shadow from every side. So the Earth is a ball. Some decades later Aristarchus of Samos compared the curve of the shadow with the Moon. In Fit the Earth's Shadow you did the same with a photograph. The shadow is about 2.6 Moons wide. It has narrowed by about one Moon-width on its way to the Moon. So the Earth is about 3.6 Moons wide. The real figure is 3.7. Aristarchus also tried to measure the Sun's distance from the angle of the half Moon. In Aristarchus's Angle you saw why that failed. The angle is too close to 90 degrees to measure by eye.
- e.g. The Earth is 12,742 kilometres wide and the Moon is 3,474. The Earth is 3.7 times as wide.
- e.g. The shadow's curved edge fits a circle about 2.6 Moons wide.
- e.g. Aristarchus's angle was 87 degrees. The true angle is 89.85 degrees.
### What a total solar eclipse shows: the corona · *debated*
**Definition:** The corona is the Sun's outer layer of thin gas. It is hotter than a million degrees Celsius, far hotter than the Sun's bright surface below it, which is about 5,500 degrees.

The corona is about a million times fainter than the Sun's face. So it shows only when the Moon covers that face exactly. In the last seconds before totality, sunlight shines through valleys on the Moon's edge as a row of bright dots. They are called Baily's beads. Then the sky darkens, bright stars and planets appear, and the corona spreads out round the black Moon. Until the 1930s, total eclipses were the only way to study the corona. Now spacecraft block the Sun with a disc of their own. India's Aditya-L1 carries such an instrument. Nobody yet knows for certain why the corona is so hot.
- e.g. On 29 July 1878 the American astronomer Maria Mitchell took five of her students from Vassar College to Denver to watch the eclipse.
- e.g. On 22 January 1898 Annie Maunder photographed the corona from Talni in Maharashtra. Her picture showed one of the longest streamers of light seen up to that time.
- e.g. Helium was first found in light from the Sun's edge, in the eclipse of 18 August 1868, seen from Guntur.
- **Scientists who study waves in the Sun's gas:** The Sun's magnetic field shakes like a plucked string. These waves carry energy up from the surface and give it to the corona as heat.
- **Scientists who study small flares:** Millions of tiny explosions, called nanoflares, go off all the time where magnetic lines snap and join again. Together they keep the corona hot. Eugene Parker suggested this in 1988.
### Watching an eclipse safely · *defined*
**Definition:** Never look at the Sun, eclipse or no eclipse. Looking at a lunar eclipse is completely safe.

Even when most of the Sun is covered, the part that still shows can burn the back of the eye. It does not hurt while it happens, because that part of the eye has no pain nerves. Sunglasses, smoked glass, X-ray film and water in a bowl are not safe. Use eclipse glasses marked ISO 12312-2, and check them for scratches first. You can also watch without looking up at all. Small gaps between the leaves of a tree throw tiny pictures of the Sun on the ground. During a partial eclipse each round patch turns into a crescent. During an annular eclipse each patch turns into a ring. Eating, drinking and going outdoors during an eclipse are safe. An eclipse is a shadow, and a shadow adds nothing to the air, the water or the food.
- e.g. These are safe: eclipse glasses marked ISO 12312-2, and the patches of light under a tree.
- e.g. These are not safe: sunglasses, X-ray film, smoked glass, or a camera or binoculars without a proper filter.
- e.g. A lunar eclipse needs no protection at all.

## Mostly agreed
- The Moon shines only by reflected sunlight, and the Sun always lights half of it. *How we know:* The lit side of the Moon always points towards the Sun. Spacecraft that go round the Moon photograph its day side and its night side, as we do for the Earth.
- The Moon's phases come from the direction we see it from, and not from the Earth's shadow. *How we know:* A crescent Moon is seen close to the Sun in the sky, where the Earth's shadow cannot fall. A half-covered ball shows every phase when you only change where you stand.
- A solar eclipse is the Moon's shadow on the Earth, and a lunar eclipse is the Earth's shadow on the Moon. *How we know:* Eclipses are worked out from the paths of the Earth and the Moon, and they arrive on time to the second. Satellites have photographed the Moon's dark spot crossing the Earth.
- The Moon's dark shadow is about 375,000 kilometres long, so its tip only just reaches the Earth. *How we know:* The dark spot on the ground is never more than about 270 kilometres wide. When the Moon is far away, the eclipse is a ring and no dark spot reaches the ground.
- The eclipsed Moon is red because sunlight is bent and reddened by the Earth's air. *How we know:* The eclipsed Moon is darker after big volcanic eruptions, when the high air is dusty. Japan's Kaguya spacecraft, going round the Moon, filmed the Earth covering the Sun in February 2009, with a bright ring of air round it.

## How we found out
**Aristarchus of Samos, and later Giovanni Cassini and Jean Richer, Aristarchus worked about 270 BCE. Cassini and Richer measured Mars in 1672..** About 2,300 years ago, on the Greek island of Samos, Aristarchus tried to measure the sky with eclipses. During a lunar eclipse he watched the Earth's curved shadow cross the Moon. He judged that the shadow was about two Moons wide. From that he worked out that the Moon is roughly a third as wide as the Earth, which was close. Then he measured the angle between the half Moon and the Sun as 87 degrees. From this he worked out that the Sun is about 19 times as far away as the Moon. That number for the Sun's distance was wrong, but astronomers repeated it for nearly 2,000 years. His method was sound. The true angle is 89.85 degrees, and no eye can tell that from 90. In 1672 Giovanni Cassini in Paris and Jean Richer in South America measured the position of Mars from two places at the same time. Their result put the Sun about 140 million kilometres away. So the Sun is about 390 times as far away as the Moon.  
*What people believed before:* That the Sun is only about 19 times as far away as the Moon. Measurements of Mars from two places in 1672 showed that the Sun is about 390 times as far.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A half Moon gives half as much light as a full Moon.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: At a half Moon, half of the Moon's face is lit. So it seems that half the light should reach us.  
  Against: Measurements show that a half Moon gives only about one-tenth of the light of a full Moon. At a half Moon, sunlight hits the rough ground from the side, and long shadows cover much of it. At a full Moon the shadows are hidden behind the things that cast them.
- **Solar eclipses are much rarer than lunar eclipses.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Most people have seen several lunar eclipses and no total solar eclipse. So solar eclipses seem rare.  
  Against: Counted over the whole Earth, there are about 240 solar eclipses in a century. There are only about 150 lunar eclipses in which the dark shadow touches the Moon. A lunar eclipse is seen from half the Earth. A total solar eclipse is seen only from a strip up to about 270 kilometres wide.
- **The Sun is far bigger than the Earth, so the Earth must go round the Sun.** (Aristarchus of Samos, about 2,300 years ago; *accepted*)  
  For: Aristarchus used eclipses and the half Moon to show that the Sun is much bigger than the Earth. He reasoned that the smaller body should circle the larger one.  
  Against: Almost nobody agreed with him at the time. People could not feel the Earth move. They also saw no shift in the stars through the year, which a moving Earth should cause. That shift is real, but far too small to see without a telescope.
- **Thales of Miletus predicted the solar eclipse of 28 May 585 BCE.** (The Greek historian Herodotus, writing about 150 years later; *debated*)  
  For: Herodotus wrote that Thales had told people the year of the eclipse in advance. The eclipse itself is real, and astronomers can work out its date.  
  Against: Nobody in 585 BCE knew a method that could say where a solar eclipse would be seen. Many historians think the story grew in the telling, or that Thales made a lucky guess.
- **Animals know that a solar eclipse is coming before it starts.** (Many eclipse watchers; *debated*)  
  For: People report birds going to roost and cattle walking home as the light fades. In the total eclipse of 21 August 2017, microphones in flower fields showed that bees stopped flying during totality.  
  Against: The bees kept flying through the partial part and stopped only when the sky went dark. So far the careful studies show animals answering the change in light. They have not shown any animal sensing an eclipse in advance.

## Roots
We have learned how the Moon's phases and eclipses work, and that a dark shadow has a length and a tip. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In The Shadow That Runs Out, the dark middle of every shadow shrank to a point and ended. A shadow is a cone of space behind a thing, and it has a tip. The Moon's dark cone is about 375,000 kilometres long, and the Earth is about that far away. So a total solar eclipse is the tip of a shadow just touching the ground.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a shadow as a flat dark patch on a floor or a wall. The patch is only the slice where a surface cuts through the shadow. The shadow fills the space behind the thing, even where nothing is there to show it.  
*Evidence:* Take a bulb 6 centimetres wide, 180 centimetres above the paper. A peppercorn's dark shadow ends about 15 centimetres below the peppercorn, and a chana's about 24 centimetres below. Above and below the paper the shadow was still there, in the air. In sunlight the rule gives 108 widths. For the Moon that is 3,474 times 108, which is about 375,000 kilometres.

- *who am i:* Every evening the turning Earth carries you into the Earth's own shadow, and every morning it carries you out. Night is simply the time when your side of the Earth is inside that shadow. The shadow is a dark cone in space, about 1.4 million kilometres long.
- *what is life:* When the Moon's shadow passes, bees stop flying and birds go to roost. Living beings read the light all day long, and answer when it changes.
- *what is this world:* Every planet and every moon has a dark cone of shadow behind it, pointing away from the Sun. We notice a cone only when something moves into it.

The Sun, the Moon and the Earth are three separate bodies. An eclipse happens only when all three stand in one line, and then one shadow joins them. The red light on the eclipsed Moon has passed through the Earth's air, at every place where the Sun is rising or setting. So the Moon, the Earth's air and the Sun all take part in one event. When we see the Moon turn red, we are seeing the light of sunrises and sunsets that are happening on the Earth at that very moment.

- **Aryabhata, Aryabhatiya (499 CE), Golapada, the verses on eclipses:** The Moon covers the Sun, and the great shadow of the Earth covers the Moon. (Aryabhata treated the Earth's shadow as a real thing with a size, reaching out into space. From its size he worked out how long an eclipse would last.)
- **Zhuangzi, chapter 2, 'On Making Things Equal':** “罔兩問景曰：「曩子行，今子止，曩子坐，今子起，何其无特操與？」 / 景曰：「吾有待而然者邪？吾所待，又有待而然者邪？」” Penumbra asks Shadow why it keeps moving and stopping, and has no will of its own. Shadow answers that it depends on something else, and that thing may depend on something else again. (In The Shadow That Runs Out, the dark shadow depended on the seed, the lamp and the distance between them. Change any one, and the shadow changed.)
- **Plato, Republic, Book 7, 514a to 517a:** “ἰδὲ γὰρ ἀνθρώπους οἷον ἐν καταγείῳ οἰκήσει σπηλαιώδει / εἰ οὖν διαλέγεσθαι οἷοί τ’ εἶεν πρὸς ἀλλήλους, οὐ ταῦτα ἡγῇ ἂν τὰ ὄντα αὐτοὺς νομίζειν ἅπερ ὁρῷεν; / οἱ τοιοῦτοι οὐκ ἂν ἄλλο τι νομίζοιεν τὸ ἀληθὲς ἢ τὰς τῶν σκευαστῶν σκιάς.” Prisoners in a cave see only shadows on a wall, and take the flat shadows for the real things. - Science (settled): Eclipses are worked out from the sizes, distances and paths of the Sun, the Earth and the Moon. The predictions are right to within a second, hundreds of years ahead.
- Science (open): The Sun's corona, which a total eclipse shows, is hotter than a million degrees. Why it is so much hotter than the surface below is still being worked out.
- Other view, A careful physicist: A shadow is not a thing at all. Only the light, the Moon and the Earth are real. The 'cone' is a way of saying which places the light does not reach. It has no weight, and it cannot push or pull anything.
- Practice, Lift the shadow (3 min): Sit under one lamp with a sheet of white paper in front of you. Hold your hand flat, just above the paper. Look at its shadow, dark and sharp. Now lift your hand slowly while you breathe in. Watch the edges soften and the dark part shrink. Lower your hand slowly while you breathe out. Do this three times. Then hold your hand still, halfway up. Feel that the shadow fills the air between your hand and the paper.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A shadow is a place where light is missing. Is a shadow a thing, or the lack of a thing? (hint: Think of The Shadow That Runs Out. The dark shadow had a width, a length and a tip that you could measure.)
- *deep:* The Moon is as dark as an old tar road, but everyone calls it white. Who is right? (hint: Think of A Black Card in the Dark. The card looked pale alone, and black beside a white strip.)
- *deep:* Babylonian sky-watchers could predict eclipses without knowing what caused them. Is predicting the same as understanding? (hint: Think of what you could do after Eclipse Detective, and what you could explain after The Shadow That Runs Out.)
- *big:* The Sun and the Moon look the same size in our sky. Would people have learned less about the sky if they did not? (hint: Think of what a total eclipse shows that nothing else did: the corona, and helium in 1868.)
- *big:* During a lunar eclipse, the Moon is lit by every sunrise and sunset on the Earth at the same moment. Is a sunset something that happens in one place, or all around the Earth at once? (hint: A sunset happens at one place for you. Somewhere on the Earth, it is sunset at every moment.)
- *big:* Aristarchus used the right method and got the wrong answer. Was his work a success or a failure? (hint: Think of Aristarchus's Angle. His method still works. Only the measuring was beyond the human eye.)

## Story: The Morning That Did Not Go Dark
*A made-up story. Yazhini, Kathir, Aaya and Shanthi Miss are imagined, and so is everything they say and do. The annular eclipse of 26 December 2019 is real. In Coimbatore the ring lasted about three minutes, a little before half past nine in the morning. That week the Earth was close to its nearest point to the Sun, and the Moon was not at its nearest to the Earth. So the Moon looked a little smaller than the Sun. Gaps between leaves really do throw ring-shaped pictures of the Sun on the ground during such an eclipse. Aaya is a word for grandmother used in parts of Tamil Nadu. Many families used to stay indoors during an eclipse. An eclipse harms nobody who does not look at the Sun.*

On Christmas night in 2019, all of Coimbatore seemed to be talking about the Sun. The next morning, a little before half past nine, the Moon would pass in front of it.

Yazhini was ten. Her cousin Kathir, who was seven, had come for the holidays, and he knew exactly what was going to happen.

"It goes dark," he said. "Completely dark. The stars come out in the morning and the birds go to sleep. I saw it in a book."

"Not tomorrow," said Yazhini. "Shanthi Miss told us. Tomorrow is a ring eclipse. A ring of Sun stays all round the Moon. It won't go dark."

"Why not?" said Kathir. "The Moon covered the whole Sun in my book. Has the Moon become smaller since then?"

Yazhini opened her mouth and shut it again. She did not know. The Moon could not shrink. But her teacher had said a ring. She had given every child a card viewer with a square of dark film in it, and had told them never to look at the Sun without it.

Aaya, their grandmother, was shelling groundnuts on the floor. "There was an eclipse when I was a girl," she said. "My mother shut every window, and we sat inside until it was over. I have never seen one in my life." She did not sound pleased about it.

After dinner Yazhini sat on the swing in the long front room and thought about Kathir's question. On the far wall hung the round clock, as wide as a steel plate. On the shelf beside her lay a five-rupee coin.

She shut one eye and held the coin out at the full length of her arm. The coin sat in the middle of the clock like a dark button. The white face of the clock showed all round it, in a ring.

"Let me," said Kathir. He climbed onto the swing beside her, shut one eye and held the coin out. "It covers the whole clock," he said. "There's no ring. You're doing it wrong."

"I am not." She took the coin back and saw a ring. She gave it to him, and he saw no ring. It was the same coin and the same clock, and they were sitting on the same swing.

She looked at his arm, stretched out next to hers. His fist reached only as far as her wrist.

"Don't move," she said. She took hold of his hand and drew it slowly forward, until the coin was level with her own fingers. "Now look."

"Oh," said Kathir. "There's a ring."

So that was it. The coin had not changed. It was only further from his eye, and a thing that is further away looks smaller. When the coin was further away, it left a ring. When it was nearer, it covered the clock.

Yazhini sat very still. Then the Moon did not have to shrink. It only had to be further away. But was the Moon ever further away than usual?

She ran to her school bag and pulled out the sheet that Shanthi Miss had given them. She had not read past the first line. Near the bottom it said that the Moon's path round the Earth is not a perfect circle. In some weeks the Moon is nearer to us, and in some weeks it is further. This week it was on the far part of its path.

"Kathir," she said, "your book and my teacher are both right." She explained it to him with the coin, twice, until he could explain it back to her.

In the morning the terrace was full of neighbours, passing viewers from hand to hand. Through the dark film the Sun was a small pale disc with a black bite out of one side. The bite grew. Aaya stayed downstairs in the doorway with her groundnuts.

By nine o'clock the light had gone strange. It was not dark. It was thin, like the light before a storm, and the shadows of the railings were very sharp. The air felt cooler on Yazhini's arms. The crows on the water tank had gone quiet.

Then, a little before half past nine, the black Moon slid right inside the Sun and did not cover it. All round the Moon there was a thin, even, burning ring.

"It's too far away," whispered Kathir, with the viewer pressed to his face. "It can't reach the edges."

Yazhini handed her viewer to the girl next door and ran down the stairs. "Aaya, come out. You don't have to look up. Look down."

Under the neem tree by the gate, the ground was covered with rings. There were hundreds of them, small and bright. They lay on the swept earth and on the white veshti drying on the line, wherever sunlight came through a gap in the leaves. Each one was a little picture of the Sun, with the Moon in the middle of it.

Aaya came out slowly, with the end of her sari over her head. She looked at the ground for a long time. Then she put out her hand, and a ring lay in her palm.

"Sixty years I sat inside," she said. "And it was a thing like this."

The rings lasted nearly three minutes. Then each one broke on one side and became a thin crescent, and the crescents began, very slowly, to grow fat again. Upstairs somebody cheered. Kathir came running down to tell Aaya that it had not gone dark, as if he had known all along.

Yazhini kept the coin. That night she held it up against the clock once more, at the end of her arm, and looked at the white ring round it.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn comes on the swing. The same coin covers the clock for Kathir and leaves a ring for Yazhini, because his arm is shorter and the coin is nearer his eye. Yazhini sees that the Moon need not shrink to leave a ring. It only has to be further away. The story shows why some solar eclipses are total and some are rings. It also shows the eclipse watched safely, with a viewer and with the patches of light under a tree.

- Ask: Why did the same coin leave a ring for Yazhini and cover the whole clock for Kathir?
- Ask: What has to be true of the Moon for a solar eclipse to leave a ring of Sun?
- Ask: Why did the morning not go dark, though most of the Sun was covered?
- Ask: Why were the patches of light under the neem tree shaped like rings?
- Ask: Aaya had stayed indoors for every eclipse of her life. What would you tell someone who is afraid of an eclipse?

### Phases, moonlight and the two kinds of eclipse (core, 15 questions)
1. (order) Put these phases in order, starting from Amavasya.  
   **Answer:** ['Amavasya', 'Thin growing crescent', 'Growing half Moon', 'Purnima', 'Shrinking half Moon']. *Why:* After Amavasya the lit part grows through Shukla Paksha to Purnima. Then it shrinks through Krishna Paksha.
2. (fill) The fortnight from Amavasya to Purnima, when the lit part grows, is called ____ Paksha.  
   **Answer:** ['Shukla']. *Why:* Shukla Paksha is the bright fortnight. Krishna Paksha is the dark fortnight, from Purnima back to Amavasya.
3. (truefalse) The Moon makes its own light.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Moon only reflects sunlight. Its lit side always faces the Sun.
4. (mcq) What causes the Moon's phases?  
   Options: The Earth's shadow covers part of the Moon / We see different shares of the lit half / Clouds hide part of the Moon / The Moon changes its shape  
   **Answer:** We see different shares of the lit half. *Why:* The Sun always lights half of the Moon. As the Moon goes round the Earth, we see more or less of that half. The Earth's shadow touches the Moon only in a lunar eclipse.
5. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Amavasya → The night when no Moon is seen; Purnima → The night when the whole lit half is seen; Krishna Paksha → The fortnight when the lit part shrinks; Umbra → The dark middle of a shadow; Penumbra → The pale ring round the dark middle; Corona → The Sun's faint outer layer. *Why:* These are the main words of the unit. The first three are about phases. The umbra and the penumbra are the two parts of a shadow. The corona shows only in a total solar eclipse.
6. (sort) Solar eclipse or lunar eclipse? Sort each line.  
   **Answer:** It happens at Amavasya → Solar eclipse; It happens at Purnima → Lunar eclipse; The Moon's shadow falls on the Earth → Solar eclipse; The Earth's shadow falls on the Moon → Lunar eclipse; It is seen from half the Earth → Lunar eclipse; It is total only in a narrow strip → Solar eclipse; It is safe to watch with bare eyes → Lunar eclipse; It needs eclipse glasses → Solar eclipse. *Why:* In a solar eclipse the Moon is between the Sun and the Earth. In a lunar eclipse the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon. The Earth's shadow is on the Moon itself, so half the Earth can see it.
7. (write) Some people think the Moon's phases are the Earth's shadow. Give one piece of evidence against this idea.  
   **Answer:** A crescent Moon is often seen close to the Sun in the sky. The Earth's shadow points away from the Sun, so it cannot fall there. Also, a half-covered ball shows every phase when only its place changes, with no shadow from anything.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a fact that the shadow idea cannot explain. The best one is a crescent or a half Moon seen in the same part of the sky as the Sun.
8. (draw) Draw the Sun, the Earth and the Moon at Purnima. Shade the half of the Moon that is dark.  
   **Answer:** The three are in a line with the Earth in the middle. The half of the Moon facing the Sun and the Earth is left bright. The half facing away is shaded.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the Earth between the Sun and the Moon. It shades the far half of the Moon, so that the lit half faces both the Sun and the Earth.
9. (count) The Moon moves about 12 degrees further from the Sun each day. How many degrees is that after 5 days?  
   **Answer:** 60. *Why:* 12 times 5 is 60 degrees. At 60 degrees about a quarter of the Moon's face is lit.
10. (mcq) The Moon is 90 degrees from the Sun. How much of its face is lit?  
   Options: A quarter / Half / Three-quarters / All of it  
   **Answer:** Half. *Why:* At 90 degrees we look at the Moon from the side. So we see half of the lit half and half of the dark half. That is a half Moon.
11. (truefalse) A lunar eclipse can be seen only from a narrow strip of the Earth.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Earth's shadow falls on the Moon itself. Everyone on the night side of the Earth sees the same darkened Moon.
12. (label) Label the parts of this picture of a solar eclipse.  
   **Answer:** ['The Moon: the small ball between the Sun and the Earth', 'The umbra: the narrow dark cone behind the Moon', 'The penumbra: the wide pale cone round the umbra', 'The place that sees a total eclipse: the small spot where the umbra touches the Earth']. *Why:* The umbra is the dark middle of the Moon's shadow, and only its tip reaches the Earth. The penumbra is much wider, and people inside it see a partial eclipse.
13. (mcq) The Moon's ground is dark grey. Why does the Moon look bright in the night sky?  
   Options: It is lit by city lights / Nothing brighter is near it in the sky / It glows from heat / It is covered in ice  
   **Answer:** Nothing brighter is near it in the sky. *Why:* The eye judges how light a thing is by comparing it with its neighbours. The Moon reflects only about 12 parts in 100 of the sunlight on it, but the sky round it is black.
14. (perspective) Which is the better eclipse to see: a total solar eclipse or a total lunar eclipse?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For the first: the corona shows only in totality. For the second: everyone on the night side of the Earth can see a lunar eclipse. For the third: the red colour of the eclipsed Moon comes from the Earth's air.. *Why:* A strong answer uses a fact about how each eclipse works, and treats the other views fairly. There is no single right choice.
15. (odd) Three of these make their own light. Which one does not?  
   Options: The Sun / A firefly / A candle flame / The Moon  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The Moon only reflects sunlight. The Sun, a firefly and a flame each make light.

10. (truefalse) A far Moon looks smaller in the sky than a near Moon.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Moon's distance changes from about 363,000 to about 405,000 km. A thing that is further away looks smaller.

### Testing ideas about eclipses (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why is there not a solar eclipse at every Amavasya?  
   Options: The Moon's path is tilted about 5 degrees / The Moon is too small / The Sun moves away / Clouds hide most of them  
   **Answer:** The Moon's path is tilted about 5 degrees. *Why:* The tilt carries the Moon above or below the straight line at most Amavasyas, so its shadow misses the Earth. An eclipse needs Amavasya near a node.
2. (truefalse) Every eclipse season has both a solar eclipse and a lunar eclipse.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The Sun stays near a node for about five weeks. That is long enough for one Amavasya and one Purnima to fall near the node.
3. (count) Eclipse pairs come about 177 days apart. A Moon month is 29.5 days. How many Moon months is that?  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* 177 divided by 29.5 is 6. Eclipses need Amavasya or Purnima, so the gap between seasons is a whole number of Moon months.
4. (write) Babylonian sky-watchers predicted eclipses from lists of dates. What could they not explain that you now can?  
   **Answer:** They knew when an eclipse was likely, from the pattern of dates. They could not say why eclipses come in pairs, or why a solar eclipse is total in one place and partial in another. Shadows, the tilt of the Moon's path and the Moon's distance explain these.. *Why:* A strong answer separates knowing the pattern from knowing the cause. It names at least one thing that the shadow explanation adds.
5. (mcq) After a big volcanic eruption, the eclipsed Moon looks much darker. What does this show?  
   Options: The Moon's light passed through the Earth's air / Volcanic smoke reaches the Moon / The Moon has moved away / The Sun has grown dimmer  
   **Answer:** The Moon's light passed through the Earth's air. *Why:* Dust high in the Earth's air blocks some of the sunlight that is bent into the shadow. So the eclipsed Moon is a report on the Earth's air.
6. (order) Put the events of a total lunar eclipse in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The Moon enters the pale penumbra', 'A curved dark edge crosses the Moon', 'The whole Moon turns dark red', 'The dark edge slides off the Moon']. *Why:* The Moon meets the pale outer shadow first, then the edge of the umbra. Once it is wholly inside the umbra it is lit only by red light bent through the Earth's air.
7. (perspective) Was Aristarchus's measurement of the Sun's distance good science?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For the first: the same triangle gives the right answer with the true angle of 89.85 degrees. For the second: near 90 degrees a tiny error in the angle gives a huge error in the distance. For the third: his error was traced to one number, the angle of 87 degrees.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the triangle and its numbers. It treats the other views fairly. Historians still disagree about how to judge early measurements.
8. (mcq) In Aristarchus's triangle the angle at the Earth changes from 87 to 89 degrees. What happens to the Sun's distance?  
   Options: It grows a little / It becomes about three times as great / It halves / It does not change  
   **Answer:** It becomes about three times as great. *Why:* At 87 degrees the Sun is 19 times as far as the Moon. At 89 degrees it is 57 times as far. Close to 90 degrees, the two lines are almost side by side, so they meet very far away.
9. (truefalse) A half Moon gives half as much light as a full Moon.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A half Moon gives only about one-tenth as much. Sunlight strikes its rough ground from the side, and long shadows cover much of the lit part.
10. (write) One town sees a total solar eclipse about once in 375 years. Lunar eclipses are seen often. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The Moon's dark shadow has nearly ended when it reaches the Earth, so the dark spot is at most about 270 km wide. Only people in its narrow path see totality. The Earth's dark shadow is about 9,200 km wide at the Moon and covers it completely. Everyone on the night side of the Earth can see that.. *Why:* A strong answer compares the sizes of the two shadows. It says where each shadow falls: on a small part of the Earth, or on the Moon itself.
11. (match) Match each observation to what it showed.  
   **Answer:** The Earth's shadow on the Moon is always round → The Earth is a ball; The shadow's curve is about 2.6 Moons wide → The Earth is nearly 4 Moons wide; The eclipsed Moon is red → Sunlight is bent through the Earth's air; A ring of Sun is left round the Moon → The Moon is far from the Earth; A pale glow shows round the covered Sun → The Sun has a faint outer layer. *Why:* Each observation is evidence for one conclusion. The shadow is 2.6 Moons wide after losing about one Moon-width on the way. That gives about 3.6 Moons for the Earth, close to the real 3.7.
12. (draw) Draw what an astronaut on the Moon sees during our total lunar eclipse.  
   **Answer:** A black sky. A large black disc, the Earth, covers the Sun. A thin ring of red-orange light glows all the way round the Earth's edge. The ground of the Moon in front is dull red.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the Earth much wider than the hidden Sun, and a thin red ring of air round the Earth. That ring is the light of every sunrise and sunset on the Earth.
13. (count) The Earth's shadow, 2.6 Moons wide at the Moon, has narrowed by 1 Moon-width. How many Moons wide is the Earth?  
   **Answer:** 3.6. *Why:* 2.6 plus 1 is 3.6. The real figure is 12,742 divided by 3,474, which is about 3.7.
14. (fill) The two points where the Moon's path crosses the Earth's path are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['nodes', 'the nodes', 'Rahu and Ketu']. *Why:* Eclipses happen only when Amavasya or Purnima falls near a node. Indian astronomers named the two nodes Rahu and Ketu.
15. (sort) Safe or not safe for watching a partial solar eclipse?  
   **Answer:** Eclipse glasses marked ISO 12312-2 → Safe; Sunglasses → Not safe; Patches of light under a leafy tree → Safe; X-ray film → Not safe; Binoculars with no filter → Not safe; A pinhole picture on paper → Safe. *Why:* The safe ways either use a tested filter or do not look at the Sun at all. Sunglasses and X-ray film let through light that can burn the eye without any pain.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) On which night is no Moon seen at all?  
   Options: Purnima / Amavasya / The night of the half Moon / Every night of Krishna Paksha  
   **Answer:** Amavasya. *Why:* At Amavasya the Moon is between the Earth and the Sun, and its lit half faces away from us.
2. (mcq) At Purnima, where is the Earth?  
   Options: Between the Sun and the Moon / Behind the Moon / On the far side of the Sun / Between the Moon and the stars  
   **Answer:** Between the Sun and the Moon. *Why:* At Purnima the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, so we look at the Moon's whole lit half. The three are usually not in an exact line, so there is no eclipse.
3. (mcq) Which fact shows that the phases are not the Earth's shadow?  
   Options: The Moon is far away / Eclipses are rare / A crescent Moon is seen near the Sun / The Moon is round  
   **Answer:** A crescent Moon is seen near the Sun. *Why:* The Earth's shadow points away from the Sun. A crescent Moon is close to the Sun in the sky, where that shadow cannot fall.
4. (mcq) The Moon is 120 degrees from the Sun. About how much of its face is lit?  
   Options: About a quarter / About half / About two-thirds / About three-quarters  
   **Answer:** About three-quarters. *Why:* At 120 degrees about 75 parts in 100 are lit. Choosing two-thirds means expecting the lit part to grow evenly with the angle.
5. (mcq) How much of the sunlight that falls on it does the Moon reflect?  
   Options: About 12 parts in 100 / About 50 parts in 100 / About 80 parts in 100 / All of it  
   **Answer:** About 12 parts in 100. *Why:* The Moon's ground is dark grey, like an old tar road. It looks white only because the sky round it is black.
6. (mcq) From inside the umbra of the Moon's shadow, what does the Sun look like?  
   Options: Half hidden / Completely hidden / A bright ring / Unchanged  
   **Answer:** Completely hidden. *Why:* The umbra is the part of the shadow where the whole Sun is covered. In the penumbra only part of the Sun is covered.
7. (mcq) In sunlight, about how many widths behind a thing does its dark shadow end?  
   Options: About 10 / About 30 / About 108 / It never ends  
   **Answer:** About 108. *Why:* The Sun is about 108 Sun-widths away, so every dark shadow in sunlight is about 108 widths long. Choosing 'never' treats a shadow as a flat patch with no length.
8. (mcq) A solar eclipse is exactly on the centre line. What decides whether it is total or a ring?  
   Options: The time of day / The season / The Moon's distance from the Earth / The Moon's phase  
   **Answer:** The Moon's distance from the Earth. *Why:* A near Moon looks big enough to cover the Sun, and its dark shadow reaches the ground. A far Moon leaves a ring.
9. (mcq) Why does the Moon turn red in a total lunar eclipse?  
   Options: The Moon gets hot / Sunlight bent through the Earth's air reaches it / The Sun turns red / Dust on the Moon glows  
   **Answer:** Sunlight bent through the Earth's air reaches it. *Why:* The Earth's air bends a little sunlight into the shadow and scatters the blue out of it. The same thing reddens the Sun at sunrise and sunset.
10. (mcq) Who can see a total lunar eclipse?  
   Options: Only people in a narrow strip / Only people near the equator / Everyone on the night side of the Earth / Only people with telescopes  
   **Answer:** Everyone on the night side of the Earth. *Why:* The Earth's shadow is on the Moon itself. Everyone who can see the Moon sees it darkened.
11. (mcq) How often does an eclipse season come?  
   Options: Every Moon month / About every six Moon months / Exactly once a year / Every 18 years  
   **Answer:** About every six Moon months. *Why:* The Sun lines up with a node of the Moon's path about every 173 days. Choosing 18 years mixes this up with the saros, after which eclipses repeat.
12. (mcq) Which is a safe way to watch a partial solar eclipse?  
   Options: Through sunglasses / Through X-ray film / With bare eyes for a few seconds / Through eclipse glasses marked ISO 12312-2  
   **Answer:** Through eclipse glasses marked ISO 12312-2. *Why:* Only a tested solar filter is safe for looking at the Sun. The eye feels no pain while sunlight burns it, so a short look is not safe.

## Applications
- Astronomers work out every eclipse years ahead, to the second. Their tables tell people where to stand to see one. Indian panchangams list the eclipses of the year in the same way.
- A tithi is a day of the Indian Moon calendar. One tithi is the time the Moon takes to move 12 degrees further from the Sun. So festival dates come from the Moon's angle from the Sun.
- Spacecraft that study the Sun carry a disc that hides the Sun's bright face, like a small Moon. India's Aditya-L1 watches the corona this way all the time.
- Engineers who send landers to the Moon plan for lunar eclipses. For a lander on the Moon, our lunar eclipse is a solar eclipse. Its solar panels get no sunlight for hours, and the ground cools quickly.
- Historians use old records of eclipses to fix the dates of kings and battles. The same records show that the Earth's spin is slowing. A day is now about 2 thousandths of a second longer than it was a hundred years ago.
- Astronomers find planets round other stars by a kind of eclipse. When a planet passes in front of its star, the star's light dips a little, again and again.
- A white roof reflects most of the sunlight that falls on it, and a dark roof takes most of it in. Some Indian cities paint roofs white so that the rooms below stay cooler.

## Spotted around us
- Round patches of light on the ground under a neem, mango or banyan tree at midday. Each patch is a small picture of the Sun, made by a gap between leaves.
- A thin crescent Moon low in the west after sunset. Its lit side points down towards the place where the Sun went.
- A half Moon in the afternoon sky. It looks pale and dull beside a sunlit white cloud.
- The Moon rising large and orange on Purnima evening, just as the Sun sets on the other side of the sky.
- The words grahanam or grahan on a wall calendar or in a panchangam. They mark the dates of eclipses, worked out long in advance.
- The shadow of your hand on the floor. It is sharp when your hand is near the floor, and soft and pale when you lift your hand.
- A kite or an eagle flying high on a sunny day. The dark middle of the bird's shadow ends high in the air, so no dark shadow reaches the ground.
- The Kodaikanal Solar Observatory in Tamil Nadu, which has photographed the Sun on clear days for more than a hundred years.

## Open questions
- **Why is the Sun's corona so much hotter than its surface?** The surface is about 5,500 degrees Celsius and the corona is over a million. Waves and tiny flares are the two main ideas. Aditya-L1 and other spacecraft are measuring which matters more.
- **How dark will the next total lunar eclipse be?** The colour depends on dust and cloud in the Earth's air on that day. A volcano can erupt months before and change it. Nobody can say exactly in advance.
- **What do other animals and plants sense during a solar eclipse?** Bees stop flying, and some birds go to roost. Scientists do not yet know whether they answer only the fading light, or also the cooling air and the change in the wind.
- **Exactly how fast is the Earth's spin slowing, and why does the rate change?** Old eclipse records show a slowing that is not steady. Tides, melting ice and movements deep inside the Earth all play a part. Their shares are still being worked out.
- **Did Thales really predict the eclipse of 585 BCE?** The only account was written about 150 years later. No method known at the time could say where a solar eclipse would be seen. Historians still argue about it.

- In a photo from How Much of the Moon Is Lit, the ball is 4.8 cm wide. Its bare part is 1.2 cm wide. What percent is lit? Which floor mark was it? **25 percent. That is the 60 degree mark.**. 1.2 divided by 4.8 is 0.25, which is 25 percent. A quarter of the face is lit when the Moon is 60 degrees from the Sun. - The percent lit was 7 at 30 degrees and 25 at 60 degrees. Without measuring, what should it be at 150 degrees and at 120 degrees? Say why. **About 93 percent at 150 degrees and 75 percent at 120 degrees.**. The part that is lit at 30 degrees is the same size as the part that is dark at 150 degrees. - The Moon moves about 12 degrees further from the Sun each day. How many days after Amavasya is it 90 degrees from the Sun? Which phase is that? **About 7 and a half days. It is the growing half Moon.**. 90 divided by 12 is 7.5. Halfway through Shukla Paksha the Moon is a half Moon. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- A bulb 5 cm wide hangs 200 cm above the paper. Where should the dark shadow of a chana 6 mm wide end? **About 240 mm, or 24 cm, above the paper.**. 200 divided by 5 is 40. The dark shadow is 40 widths long. 6 mm times 40 is 240 mm. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)

- The Moon is 3,474 km wide. In sunlight a dark shadow is about 108 widths long. How long is the Moon's dark shadow? Does it reach ground that is 399,000 km away? **About 375,000 km. No, it ends about 24,000 km short.**. 3,474 times 108 is 375,192, which is about 375,000 km. 399,000 minus 375,000 is 24,000. The dark shadow ends above the ground, so people see a ring eclipse. - Eclipse pairs come about 177 days apart. A lunar eclipse was on 3 March 2026. On about which date was the next lunar eclipse? **About 27 August 2026. The real date was 28 August 2026.**. From 3 March to 3 September is 184 days. Going back 7 days gives 27 August. The real eclipse came one day later, because six Moon months are a little over 177 days. (queues `math.time.calendar.L5`)
