# What makes vehicles go: muscles, fuels and electricity
`sci.energy.vehicles-and-fuels.L2`

**Summary.** A cycle, a bullock cart, a bus and an electric scooter all travel down the same road. None of them moves by itself, so what is pushing each one? In this lesson you send a balloon rocket along a string to see how stored push becomes movement. You follow petrol back to tiny sea beings of long ago, and electricity back to the power station. You also compare city and village traffic, and work out real ways to save petrol and diesel.

**Intro.** *Put a toy car on the floor and look at it. Wait. Does it go anywhere? Now do whatever you like to make it move, and say what you did.* The car sat still until you pushed it, pulled it, tilted the floor or wound it up. Nothing with wheels starts by itself. Something has to give it a push, and the push has to come from somewhere. A real bus cannot be shoved along by hand. So what is doing the pushing inside it, and where did that push come from? Today you follow it all the way back.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that every vehicle needs something to make it go, and name what that is for a cycle, a bullock cart, a bus and an electric scooter.
1. I can say what a fuel is and name petrol, diesel and CNG.
2. I can tell where petrol and diesel come from, and why they will not last for ever.
3. I can say how an electric vehicle is different from a petrol one, and one reason it is not perfectly clean.
4. I can give five ways to save petrol and diesel.
5. I can compare the traffic in a city with the traffic in a village.
6. I can work out why a full bus uses less fuel for each person than a car.
7. I can test if more air makes a balloon rocket travel farther, and say what I found.

## Terms
- **vehicle**: Anything made to carry people or goods from one place to another. *e.g.* A cycle, a bus, a bullock cart and a boat are all vehicles.
- **push**: Something that makes a thing start to move, or move faster. *e.g.* You give a toy car a push, and it rolls across the floor.
- **pull**: Something that draws a thing towards you. *e.g.* A bullock pulls a cart along the road by leaning into the yoke.
- **gravity**: The pull that makes things fall towards the Earth. Scientists describe how it works very well, but what causes it is still debated. *e.g.* When you drop a spoon, gravity pulls it to the floor.
- **particle**: A very tiny bit of something, too small to see on its own. *e.g.* Soot is made of tiny particles of burnt fuel that float in the air.
- **energy**: What lets anything move, heat up or change. Scientists can count it, but nobody can say for sure what it is. *e.g.* Your breakfast gives your body energy to walk to school.
- **muscle power**: Movement made by the muscles of a person or another animal. *e.g.* A cycle rickshaw driver uses muscle power to pedal three passengers.
- **bullock cart**: A cart with two wheels or four that bullocks pull. *e.g.* In many villages a bullock cart still carries sugarcane or hay.
- **cycle rickshaw**: A three-wheeled cycle that carries passengers, pedalled by the driver. *e.g.* In Kolkata and Mathura, cycle rickshaws carry children to school.
- **sail**: A big piece of cloth that catches the wind and pushes a boat. *e.g.* The wooden boats of Beypore, Kerala, have sails that catch the wind.
- **monsoon wind**: The wind that blows from the sea in summer and brings rain to India. It blows back the other way in winter. *e.g.* Old sailing ships left Kerala with one wind and came home with the other.
- **current**: Water moving along in one direction, as in a river or the sea. *e.g.* A leaf dropped in a stream floats away with the current.
- **fuel**: A material that gives out heat when it burns. *e.g.* Wood, coal, petrol and cooking gas are all fuels.
- **burn**: To join with oxygen in the air and give out heat and light. *e.g.* A diya burns oil and gives out a small flame.
- **petrol**: A thin liquid fuel that catches fire very easily. Scooters and many cars burn it. *e.g.* A scooter stops at a petrol pump to fill its tank.
- **diesel**: A thicker, oily fuel. Buses, lorries, tractors and many cars burn it. *e.g.* A big diesel bus carries fifty people from town to town.
- **CNG**: A fuel gas squeezed into a strong cylinder. The letters stand for compressed natural gas. *e.g.* Many auto rickshaws in Delhi and Mumbai have CNG painted on them.
- **cylinder**: A round tube. A vehicle's engine has cylinders inside, and a CNG vehicle carries a tall cylinder of gas. *e.g.* The kitchen cooking-gas cylinder is a strong round container.
- **engine**: A machine that burns fuel and turns the heat into movement. *e.g.* A scooter's engine goes brrm when the fuel burns inside it.
- **piston**: A metal plug inside an engine's cylinder. Hot gas shoves it down, over and over. *e.g.* Your arm pumping a bicycle pump moves like a piston.
- **spark**: A tiny flash of fire. A small part of a petrol engine makes one to light the fuel. *e.g.* A spark makes the petrol and air burn inside the cylinder.
- **exhaust**: The used gases that leave an engine through a pipe at the back. *e.g.* Smoke from a bus's exhaust pipe is the air and fuel after burning.
- **petroleum (crude oil)**: A thick dark oil found deep under the ground. Petrol and diesel are made from it. *e.g.* Oil workers drill a well to pull petroleum up out of the ground.
- **oil well**: A deep hole drilled to bring oil up from under the ground. *e.g.* India's first oil well was drilled at Digboi in Assam.
- **refinery**: A factory that cleans crude oil and splits it into petrol, diesel and other things. *e.g.* Crude oil goes into a refinery and petrol comes out.
- **fossil fuel**: A fuel made from the buried remains of living beings from millions of years ago. Coal, oil and natural gas are fossil fuels. *e.g.* Petrol is a fossil fuel made from tiny sea beings of long ago.
- **coal**: A hard black rock that burns. It is made from forests that were buried long ago. *e.g.* Many power stations in India burn coal to make electricity.
- **carbon dioxide**: An invisible gas made when fuel burns. Too much of it in the air traps heat around the Earth. *e.g.* A burning diya makes a little carbon dioxide, and a bus makes a lot.
- **oxygen**: The part of air that fuel needs to burn and that living beings breathe in. *e.g.* You breathe in oxygen all day, and a fire takes it from the air too.
- **soot**: Tiny black bits of burnt fuel in smoke. *e.g.* Black soot from a diesel lorry settles on a white wall by the road.
- **pollution**: Dirt or harm that is put into the air, water or soil. *e.g.* Smoke from vehicles is air pollution that people and other animals breathe.
- **electricity**: A kind of energy that moves along wires and makes lights, fans and motors work. *e.g.* A switch lets electricity flow to a fan and the fan spins.
- **battery**: A store of electricity in a case. It can run a torch, a phone or a vehicle. *e.g.* An electric scooter carries a big battery under its seat.
- **motor**: A machine that uses electricity and magnets to make something spin. *e.g.* The motor inside a table fan makes the blades turn.
- **charge**: To fill a battery with electricity again. *e.g.* You charge a phone at night so that it works in the morning.
- **electric vehicle**: A vehicle that runs on a battery and a motor and has no fuel tank. *e.g.* An electric scooter hums quietly down the road.
- **power station**: A big place where electricity is made and sent out through wires. *e.g.* A power station may burn coal, or use water, wind or sunshine.
- **solar panel**: A flat panel that turns sunlight into electricity. *e.g.* A solar panel on a roof can charge a battery.
- **mine**: A place where people dig metals or coal out of the ground. *e.g.* Batteries need metals such as lithium, which are dug from mines.
- **ethanol**: A liquid fuel made from plants such as sugarcane. In India it is mixed into petrol. *e.g.* Sugarcane juice becomes ethanol when yeast feeds on it.
- **share a ride**: To travel together in one vehicle in place of one vehicle each. *e.g.* Four neighbours going to the market in one car share a ride.
- **public transport**: Buses, trains and metros that carry many people on set routes. *e.g.* A city bus is public transport. Fifty people can ride it.
- **metro**: A train that runs in a city, often under or above the ground, on electricity. *e.g.* The Chennai Metro carries thousands of people over the traffic.
- **idling**: Running an engine while the vehicle is standing still. *e.g.* A car idling at a railway gate burns fuel and goes nowhere.
- **tyre pressure**: How hard a tyre is pumped up with air. *e.g.* A soft tyre rubs more on the road, so a pumped-up tyre saves fuel.
- **save fuel**: To get the same travel done while burning less fuel. *e.g.* Walking to the corner shop saves fuel.
- **traffic**: All the vehicles and people moving along a road. *e.g.* At six in the evening, traffic fills the city crossing.
- **traffic jam**: A road so full of vehicles that they hardly move. *e.g.* A traffic jam makes a short trip take a long time.
- **signal**: A light at a crossing that tells vehicles when to stop and go. *e.g.* Cars stop when the signal is red.
- **flyover**: A road that goes over another road on a bridge. *e.g.* A flyover lets vehicles skip a crossing below.
- **city**: A big place where many people live close together, with many vehicles and tall buildings. *e.g.* Chennai, Delhi and Kochi are cities.
- **village**: A small place where fewer people live, often with fields around it. *e.g.* In a village, you may see more cycles, tractors and bullock carts than buses.
- **tractor**: A strong vehicle with big back wheels that pulls ploughs and trailers on a farm. *e.g.* A tractor pulls a trailer of sugarcane to the mill.
- **draught animal**: An animal who pulls or carries loads for people, such as a bullock, a horse or a camel. *e.g.* A camel pulling a cart in Rajasthan is a draught animal.
- **migration**: A long yearly journey that animals make to find food or a safe place to live. *e.g.* Amur falcons fly from Siberia to Nagaland every year.
- **wheel**: A round frame that turns on a rod. It lets a load roll in place of rubbing along the ground. *e.g.* A cart rolls because its wheels turn.
- **axle**: The rod in the middle of a wheel that lets the wheel turn. *e.g.* A toy car's wheels turn on thin metal axles.
- **footpath**: A path beside a road for people on foot. *e.g.* A wide footpath is safe for a child walking to school.

## Explanations
### No vehicle starts moving by itself: something must push or pull it · *defined*
**Definition:** A vehicle is anything made to carry people or goods from place to place. To start moving, every vehicle needs a push or pull from a source of energy.

A parked cycle stays parked. A boat with no wind and no oars only drifts. A bus with an empty tank will not start, however hard the driver turns the key. Every vehicle needs something to set it moving and keep it moving. Scientists call that something energy. For a cycle, the push comes from your legs. For a cart, it comes from a bullock. For a bus, it comes from burning fuel. For an electric scooter, it comes from a battery. The wheels and the body only carry and steer.
- e.g. A swing stops unless someone pushes it or you pump your legs.
- e.g. A paper boat in a bucket moves only when you blow on it or stir the water.
- e.g. A cycle going downhill is being pulled by the Earth, and you paid for that earlier by pedalling up.
- **Aristotle, a Greek thinker of the 4th century BCE:** A moving thing needs a push all the time, and it stops the moment the push ends. This fits a cart on a rough road very well, and people believed it for about 2,000 years.
- **Galileo Galilei and Isaac Newton, in the 1600s:** A moving thing would go on for ever if nothing rubbed against it. On a real road, the rubbing of tyres and air slows it down. So a vehicle needs energy to start, and then more to make up for what the rubbing takes.
- **The Vaisheshika Sutra of Kanada, chapter 5, composed in India more than 2,000 years ago:** Kanada wrote that motion begins from a push, a pull or a blow. He added that a moving thing carries a tendency, called vega, that keeps it going until something stops it. That is close to the later view.
### Muscle power: legs and animals have moved people for most of history · *defined*
**Definition:** Muscle power is movement made by the muscles of a person or another animal. The energy for it comes from food.

For most of history, going somewhere meant using muscles. People walked. They rowed boats. They rode horses and camels. They loaded carts and bullocks pulled them. Muscles are still at work everywhere. A child walks to school, a vegetable seller pushes a handcart, a cycle rickshaw driver pedals three passengers along. A muscle needs fuel too, and its fuel is food. Your breakfast is what moves your legs. The bullock's breakfast of straw and oilcake moves the cart.
- e.g. A person walks at about 5 kilometres in an hour and cycles at about 15.
- e.g. A pair of bullocks pulls a cart with about a tonne of sugarcane at walking speed.
- e.g. Dabbawalas in Mumbai deliver about two lakh lunch boxes a day using legs, cycles, handcarts and local trains.
### A cycle carries you farther on a plate of food than any other way of travelling · *defined*
**Definition:** A person on a bicycle uses about one-fifth of the energy that the same person uses to walk the same distance.

Walk one kilometre to a shop, and your body burns a little of your breakfast. Cycle the same kilometre, and you burn only about a fifth as much. Why? When you walk, your legs lift your whole body a little at every step and catch it again. On a cycle, the saddle holds you up and the wheels roll. Your legs only turn the pedals. In 1973 the engineer S. S. Wilson compared many animals and machines. For moving one's own weight a certain distance, a person on a cycle beat every animal and every engine he measured.
- e.g. You can cycle 5 kilometres and still feel fresh. Walking 5 kilometres takes an hour and tired legs.
- e.g. A cycle weighs about 15 kilograms and carries a person of 60. A car weighs about 1,000 kilograms and often carries one person of 60.
- e.g. India makes more than one crore bicycles a year, many of them in Ludhiana in Punjab.
### Wind and flowing water carried boats for thousands of years before engines · *defined*
**Definition:** A sail catches moving air and a river current carries a floating boat, so wind and water can move vehicles without any fuel being burned.

Long before engines, sailors let the wind do the work. A sail is a big cloth that catches moving air. Indian sailors knew that the monsoon wind blows from the south-west for half the year and from the north-east for the other half. So ships left Kerala and Gujarat for Arabia and East Africa with one wind and came home with the other. Wooden ships called urus are still built by hand at Beypore in Kerala. On rivers, boats float down with the current. Wind and water are free. But they go only where and when they choose.
- e.g. The English word 'catamaran' comes from the Tamil kattumaram, a boat of logs tied together.
- e.g. A paper boat in a gutter after rain is carried by the water with no push from you.
- e.g. The seeds of a silk-cotton tree ride the wind on fluff, with no fuel at all.
- **A Greek sailors' guide of the 1st century CE, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea:** It says a Greek pilot named Hippalus discovered how to cross the open sea to India on the monsoon wind.
- **Historians of the Indian Ocean, such as Lotika Varadarajan:** Indian, Arab and East African sailors were crossing on the monsoon long before any Greek ship came. The Greek book records the moment when Greeks learned of it, and not the moment of discovery.
### A fuel is something that gives out heat when it burns · *defined*
**Definition:** A fuel is a material that releases stored energy as heat when it burns. Petrol, diesel, CNG, cooking gas, coal and wood are fuels.

Light a diya, and the oil slowly disappears while the flame gives light and warmth. The oil is a fuel. A fuel is anything that gives out heat when it burns. Wood, coal, kerosene and cooking gas are fuels. Most vehicles on the road burn one of three. Petrol is a thin liquid that catches fire very easily, and it is used in scooters and many cars. Diesel is a thicker, oily liquid, used in buses, lorries and tractors. CNG is a gas squeezed tightly into a strong cylinder, used in many autos and city buses. Burning also needs air.
- e.g. A candle under an upturned tumbler goes out in a few seconds, because the air inside is used up. An engine needs a steady supply of air for the same reason.
- e.g. A petrol pump has separate nozzles for petrol and diesel. Putting the wrong one in a vehicle spoils the engine.
- e.g. Aeroplanes burn a fuel much like kerosene. Big ships burn a thick, tarry oil.
### An engine burns fuel in a closed space, and the hot gas pushes the wheels round · *defined*
**Definition:** An engine is a machine that turns the heat from burning fuel into movement. In a vehicle engine, fuel burns inside closed tubes called cylinders and the expanding hot gas pushes a piston.

Inside an engine are metal tubes called cylinders. In each tube sits a metal plug that can slide, called a piston. A tiny spray of fuel and some air go into the tube. A spark sets them alight. The burning makes very hot gas, and hot gas needs far more room. It shoves the piston down hard. This happens many times a second. Rods and gears turn those shoves into the spinning of the wheels. But most of the heat never reaches the wheels. In a petrol car, only about a quarter of the fuel's energy moves the car. The rest warms the engine and the air.
- e.g. The bonnet of a car that has just been driven is hot to stand near. That warmth is fuel that did no pushing.
- e.g. The 'dug-dug-dug' of a motorcycle is the sound of fuel burning in the cylinder, one small bang after another.
- e.g. A pressure cooker's whistle is lifted by hot steam that needs more room. A steam engine uses the same push.
### Petrol and diesel come from oil under the ground, made from tiny sea beings of long ago · *defined*
**Definition:** Petrol and diesel are made from petroleum, a thick dark oil found deep underground. Petroleum formed over millions of years from the buried remains of tiny beings that lived in ancient seas. Coal formed from ancient forests. These are called fossil fuels.

Where does petrol come from? Many people think of dinosaurs. The real answer is smaller and stranger. Long before the dinosaurs, and during their time, warm seas were full of tiny floating beings, too small to see. When they died, they sank into the mud. More mud covered them. Over millions of years the layers were squeezed and heated deep underground. The remains slowly turned into a thick dark oil called petroleum, and into gas. Today people drill deep wells to bring up the oil. At a factory called a refinery it is separated into petrol, diesel, kerosene, cooking gas and tar.
- e.g. India's first oil well was drilled at Digboi in Assam in about 1889, and oil is still pumped there.
- e.g. Bombay High, an oil field under the sea west of Mumbai, has been worked since the 1970s.
- e.g. India finds only a small part of the oil it uses. About 85 out of every 100 litres come by ship from other countries.
- e.g. The black tar on a road is the thickest part of the same oil.
### Fossil fuel is very old sunlight, and we burn it far faster than it was made · *defined*
**Definition:** The energy in fossil fuels was first caught from sunlight by living beings millions of years ago. These fuels take millions of years to form, so the amount that can be taken from the Earth is limited.

Those tiny sea beings lived on sunlight, as green plants do today. They caught the Sun's energy and stored it in their bodies. When their remains became oil, the stored energy stayed. So when an engine burns petrol, the heat that comes out is sunlight that fell on a sea millions of years ago. The oil took millions of years to make. People began burning it in large amounts only about 150 years ago. Nobody can make more. That is the first reason to use petrol and diesel carefully. One honest note: science can measure energy exactly, but has no final answer to what energy is.
- e.g. The grass a bullock eats caught its sunlight this season. The diesel in a tractor caught its sunlight before there were any people.
- e.g. A piece of coal sometimes shows the print of a leaf from a forest of about 30 crore years ago.
- e.g. The world burns about 10 crore barrels of oil every single day. A barrel holds about 159 litres.
- **Geologists such as M. King Hubbert, who warned in 1956 that oil would run short:** The oil that is easy to reach will be used up, and what is left will cost more and more to get. He predicted that oil from American wells would begin to fall by about 1970, and it did for several decades.
- **Economists such as Morris Adelman:** New methods keep finding and reaching more oil. The world will stop burning oil because of the harm it does and because better things replace it, long before the last drop is gone.
### Burning one litre of petrol puts more than two kilograms of an unseen gas into the air · *defined*
**Definition:** When fuel burns, it joins with oxygen from the air and forms carbon dioxide, an invisible gas that traps heat around the Earth. Burning one litre of petrol produces about 2.3 kilograms of carbon dioxide.

Burning does not make the fuel vanish. It joins with a part of the air called oxygen and becomes new gases. The main one is carbon dioxide. You cannot see or smell it. A litre of petrol weighs less than a kilogram, yet burning it makes about 2.3 kilograms of carbon dioxide. The extra weight is oxygen taken from the air. Carbon dioxide spreads through the sky and holds in heat, like a blanket. More of it means a warmer Earth. Exhaust pipes also give out soot and gases that hurt lungs. That is the second reason to save fuel.
- e.g. Hold a white cloth near the exhaust of an old diesel vehicle for a moment, and it turns grey with soot. A grown-up should do this, and never breathe the smoke.
- e.g. On a busy road your eyes sting. In the open country they do not.
- e.g. A car that runs 15 kilometres on a litre puts out about 150 grams of carbon dioxide for every kilometre.
- **Eunice Foote, an American scientist, in 1856:** She filled glass tubes with different gases and stood them in the sun. The tube of carbon dioxide grew hottest and stayed hot longest. She wrote that an atmosphere of this gas would give the Earth a higher temperature. Her work was forgotten for about 150 years.
- **Svante Arrhenius, a Swedish scientist, in 1896:** He worked out how much the Earth would warm if the carbon dioxide in the air doubled. He thought it would take thousands of years and might be pleasant. At today's rate of burning it is taking about two hundred.
### CNG is a cleaner-burning gas, and Delhi's buses and autos switched to it by 2002 · *defined*
**Definition:** CNG, compressed natural gas, is mostly a gas called methane, squeezed into strong cylinders. It burns with much less soot than diesel.

Natural gas is found underground, often together with oil. It is squeezed until a great deal fits in a cylinder, and it is then called CNG. Look for a green or yellow auto or bus with 'CNG' painted on it. This gas burns with almost no black smoke. In the 1990s Delhi's air was thick with diesel soot. A lawyer, M. C. Mehta, took the matter to the Supreme Court. Scientists and writers at the Centre for Science and Environment, led by Anil Agarwal and Sunita Narain, gathered the evidence. The court ordered every bus, auto and taxi in Delhi to switch. By the end of 2002 they had.
- e.g. A CNG bus pulling away from a stop leaves no black cloud behind.
- e.g. CNG is still a fossil fuel. Burning it makes carbon dioxide, though somewhat less than petrol or diesel for the same journey.
- e.g. Mumbai, Ahmedabad, Pune and many other cities now run autos and buses on CNG.
- **The Centre for Science and Environment, and studies soon after the switch:** Soot and some harmful gases in Delhi's air fell in the first years after 2002. It showed that a city can change its fuel in a few years when it decides to.
- **Researchers who measured Delhi's air in later years, such as Sarath Guttikunda:** The gain was soon swallowed. The number of private cars and two-wheelers kept rising, and smoke from lorries, dust, factories and burning waste grew as well. A cleaner fuel helps, and it cannot make up for many more vehicles.
### An electric vehicle has a battery and a motor in place of a fuel tank and an engine · *defined*
**Definition:** An electric vehicle stores electrical energy in a battery. An electric motor uses magnets to turn that energy directly into the spinning of the wheels, without burning anything.

An electric scooter has no fuel tank and no exhaust pipe. It carries a battery, which is a store of electricity like the one in a phone, only far bigger. Wires take the electricity to a motor. Inside a motor, electricity makes magnets push and pull each other, and this turns a rod round and round. Nothing burns, so the motor is quiet and stays fairly cool. It wastes little. More than three-quarters of the battery's energy reaches the wheels. When the battery is empty, it is filled again from a plug. That takes far longer than filling a tank.
- e.g. Stand beside an electric auto as it starts. You hear a soft whine and no rattle.
- e.g. A ceiling fan, a mixer and a toy car all have electric motors inside.
- e.g. India's first electric car, the Reva, was built in Bengaluru in 2001 by Chetan Maini's company.
- e.g. E-rickshaws, which run on batteries, now carry crores of short rides in Indian towns every day.
### Electric cars are older than petrol cars, and they lost the first race · *defined*
**Definition:** Battery-powered carriages were built from the 1830s. In the year 1900 more electric cars than petrol cars were made in the USA.

Most people think the electric car is a new invention. It is older than the petrol car. Inventors built small carriages run by batteries in the 1830s. By 1900 electric taxis worked in New York and London. They were quiet and clean and needed no cranking to start. In that year American factories made more electric cars than petrol cars, and more steam cars than either. Then petrol won. Oil was found in huge amounts and became cheap. A petrol tank took a car much farther than the heavy batteries of that time could. For a hundred years the electric car was almost forgotten.
- e.g. Milk was delivered by quiet electric vans in British cities for most of the 1900s.
- e.g. In 2019 the Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to John Goodenough, Stanley Whittingham and Akira Yoshino for the light, strong lithium battery that brought electric vehicles back.
- e.g. Electric trains never went away. Mumbai's first electric train ran in 1925.
### Is an electric vehicle clean? It depends on where the electricity comes from and how the battery is made · *debated*
**Definition:** An electric vehicle gives out no exhaust on the road. Its total effect on air and climate depends on how its electricity is generated and on the mining and making of its battery.

An electric scooter has no smoke coming out of it. But the electricity in its battery was made somewhere. In India about seven units of electricity in every ten come from burning coal at power stations. So some smoke has only moved, from the street to a chimney far away. The battery needs metals such as lithium and cobalt, which are dug from mines. Much of the world's cobalt comes from the Congo in Africa, where some mines are dangerous and children have been found working in them. So is an electric vehicle better? Most careful studies say yes, by a modest amount today and by more in future. Not everyone agrees on how much.
- e.g. An electric scooter charged from solar panels on a roof in the daytime is running on that day's sunlight.
- e.g. City air gets cleaner where people breathe it, even when the power station still burns coal.
- **The International Council on Clean Transportation, in a study led by Georg Bieker in 2021:** Counting everything from mine to scrapyard, an electric car in India caused about a fifth to a third less warming gas over its life than a petrol car. The gap will widen as India builds more solar and wind power.
- **Critics such as the energy writer Vaclav Smil:** Mining, heavy batteries and coal power take away much of the gain. Replacing a billion engines with a billion batteries leaves the real problem, which is too many heavy vehicles.
- **People who live near the mines, and groups such as Amnesty International in a report of 2016:** The cleaner air of rich cities is paid for by miners and villages elsewhere. A fair change must count their health and their land as well.
### Fuel from plants: ethanol in petrol, and the argument over food or fuel · *debated*
**Definition:** Ethanol is a liquid fuel made by fermenting the sugar or starch of plants such as sugarcane, maize and rice. In India it is mixed into petrol.

Fuel can be grown. Sugarcane juice is left for tiny beings called yeast to feed on. They turn the sugar into a liquid that burns, called ethanol. Since 2025, about one-fifth of the petrol sold at most Indian pumps has been ethanol. The good side is that the plants caught their sunlight this year, and new plants grow next year. India also buys less oil from abroad. But there is another side. Sugarcane is a very thirsty crop. And land, water and grain used for fuel are not being used for food.
- e.g. A sticker on many petrol pumps reads 'E20', meaning 20 parts ethanol in every 100.
- e.g. Brazil has run most of its cars on sugarcane ethanol since the 1970s.
- e.g. Rudolf Diesel showed one of his first engines running on groundnut oil at a fair in Paris in 1900.
- **The Government of India and NITI Aayog, in the ethanol plan of 2021:** Blending ethanol saves foreign money, gives farmers a second buyer for their crop and reduces some harmful exhaust gases.
- **Water and food researchers, such as the economist Ashok Gulati:** Growing the sugarcane for one litre of ethanol takes thousands of litres of water, in states that are already short of it. Using rice and maize for fuel can push up the price of food for the poor.
- **Engineers working on ethanol from farm waste:** Ethanol can also be made from straw, husks and stalks left after harvest, which nobody eats. This is harder and costlier today. If it can be made cheaply, the quarrel between food and fuel goes away.
### A full bus uses far less fuel for each person than a car or scooter · *defined*
**Definition:** Fuel use per person is the fuel a vehicle burns divided by the number of people it carries. A vehicle with many passengers shares its fuel among them.

A city bus is a thirsty machine. It goes only about 4 kilometres on a litre of diesel, while a car goes about 15. So is the car better? Count the people. If the bus carries 50 people, that one litre has moved 50 people 4 kilometres. That is the same as moving one person 200 kilometres. The car with only its driver has moved one person 15 kilometres. The bus is more than ten times better. Fill the car with four people and it improves to 60. Trains and metros do better still. Sharing is the biggest fuel saver there is.
- e.g. A scooter that goes 45 kilometres on a litre and carries two people has moved one person 90 kilometres on that litre.
- e.g. A school bus with 40 children replaces 40 separate trips by car or scooter.
- e.g. An empty bus is worse than a car. Sharing saves fuel only when the seats are filled.
### Ways to save petrol and diesel: fewer trips, shared trips and gentle driving · *defined*
**Definition:** Saving fuel means getting the same travel done while burning less. It can be done by avoiding engine trips, by sharing vehicles and by using each vehicle carefully.

There are three kinds of saving. First, do not burn fuel at all: walk or cycle for short trips, and do three errands in one outing in place of three. Second, share: take the bus, train or metro, use the school bus, or travel with neighbours going the same way. Third, drive with care. Switch off the engine at a long red light or a railway gate. Keep the tyres pumped up, because soft tyres drag. Drive at a steady 45 to 55 kilometres an hour without sudden racing and braking. Have the engine serviced. Do not carry loads you do not need.
- e.g. A car standing with the engine running burns roughly half a litre to one litre of petrol in an hour and goes nowhere.
- e.g. At a closed railway gate, count how many drivers switch off and how many keep the engine running.
- e.g. A shop 500 metres away is six or seven minutes on foot. Starting a cold engine for that trip uses extra fuel.
- e.g. India's Petroleum Conservation Research Association has taught these tips to drivers since 1978.
### When engines waste less fuel, people often travel more: the saving that gets eaten · *debated*
**Definition:** The rebound effect is the tendency for people to use more of something when it becomes cheaper to use. Better engines lower the cost of each kilometre, which can lead to more driving and larger vehicles.

Here is a puzzle. Engines today get much more out of a litre than engines fifty years ago. Yet the world burns more petrol and diesel than ever. How can that be? In 1865 an English thinker, William Stanley Jevons, noticed the same thing with coal. Steam engines had been made far more saving. Coal use went up, not down. Cheaper running meant that people built more engines and used them more. The same has happened on the roads. When each kilometre costs less, people drive farther and buy bigger vehicles. So better machines alone may not save fuel.
- e.g. Cars sold in India have grown taller and heavier over twenty years, even as their engines improved.
- e.g. A family that buys a scooter after years of cycling may make trips it never made before.
- **William Stanley Jevons, in 'The Coal Question', 1865:** It is a confusion of ideas to suppose that using a fuel more efficiently leads to using less of it. The very opposite is the truth. Efficiency makes a fuel more useful, so more is wanted.
- **Energy researchers such as Steve Sorrell, in a review of 2007:** The rebound is real but usually partial. For cars, something like a tenth to a third of the saving is eaten by extra driving. The rest is a true saving, so better engines are still worth having.
- **Many economists:** A better engine alone is not enough. Saving happens when it is combined with a limit, such as a fuel tax, fewer parking spaces or good buses that make people choose not to drive.
### City traffic and village traffic are different in number, kind, speed and noise · *defined*
**Definition:** Traffic is all the vehicles and people moving on roads. A city has many more vehicles in a small space than a village, and a different mix of them.

Stand at a city crossing for five minutes. You may count hundreds of vehicles: cars, scooters, autos, buses, lorries, perhaps a metro train overhead. There are signals, flyovers, horns, and often a jam. Now stand on a village road. In the same five minutes you may count ten: a tractor, a shared auto, two motorcycles, some cycles, a bullock cart, a bus that comes four times a day. People walk in the road and animals cross it. The air is clearer and you can hear birds. But the school, the market or the hospital may be many kilometres away, with few ways to reach them.
- e.g. India has more than 30 crore registered motor vehicles, and about three in every four are two-wheelers.
- e.g. In Bengaluru in 2023, a trip of 10 kilometres took about 28 minutes on average, slower than a steady cyclist.
- e.g. In many villages one jeep or shared auto carries schoolchildren, milk cans and vegetable sacks in the same trip.
- e.g. At harvest time a village road fills with tractors and loaded carts, and for a few weeks it has a rush hour of its own.
- **City dwellers and city planners:** Cities have many choices: buses every few minutes, metros, taxis, hospitals close by. The price is jams, noise, dirty air and danger in crossing the road.
- **Village residents and rural researchers:** Village roads are quieter and the air is cleaner. But a sick person may wait hours for a vehicle, and a girl may leave school because it is too far to walk. India's rural roads programme, begun in 2000, was started for this reason.
### Why jams happen, and why building wider roads often brings them back · *defined*
**Definition:** A traffic jam forms when more vehicles try to use a road than it can carry. A jam can also start with no obstacle at all, when one driver brakes and the drivers behind brake harder.

A road is like a pipe. Only so much can flow through. When too many vehicles enter, they slow and stop. But jams also appear from nothing. In 2008 Yuki Sugiyama and his team in Japan put 22 cars on a circular track and asked the drivers to keep a steady speed. Within minutes one driver slowed a little. The one behind slowed more. Soon a clump of stopped cars was creeping backwards round the circle. What about building a wider road? Studies of many cities found that new road space fills up within a few years. Easier driving brings out more drivers.
- e.g. A jam on a highway often has no accident at its front. It is a wave of braking passing backwards down the line.
- e.g. After a new flyover opens, traffic moves well for a year or two and then thickens again.
- e.g. Ants on a busy trail hardly ever jam. In tests reported in 2019, Argentine ants kept moving smoothly even when their bridge was four-fifths full.
- **Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner, economists, in a study of American cities in 2011:** Driving rises in step with road building, almost one for one. Building roads to cure jams does not work. Charging for road use at busy hours and running good public transport do.
- **Road engineers and many city governments:** A growing city does need more roads and bridges, and a new road lets more people travel even if it is full. The jam returns, but more journeys are being made.
- **Audrey Dussutour and colleagues, who study ants, in 2019:** Ants avoid jams because each ant adjusts to the others. When the trail is crowded they slow slightly, and they stop joining the trail when it is too full. No ant tries to get ahead of the rest.
### Bullocks, horses and camels who pull carts are workers who tire, feel heat and need rest · *defined*
**Definition:** A draught animal is an animal who pulls or carries loads for people. Indian law limits how long and in what heat such animals may be worked.

A tractor can be left in the sun all day. A bullock cannot. Animals who pull carts are living beings who do a job. They get tired and thirsty. They feel the heat, the weight and the whip. India has a law for them. Rules made in 1965 say a working animal must not be worked for more than nine hours in a day, and must rest in the hottest part of a hot day. An animal who is sick, hurt or too young must not be worked at all. A good cart owner knows each animal's ways and looks after them like a partner.
- e.g. At Pongal in Tamil Nadu, on the day called Mattu Pongal, cattle are bathed, their horns are painted and they are fed sweet rice.
- e.g. A bullock cart with rubber tyres and good bearings is far easier to pull than one with wooden wheels.
- e.g. Hand-pulled rickshaws, in which a person does this work for other people, still run in parts of Kolkata.
- **Farmers and cart owners:** A bullock eats straw from the farm, gives dung for the field and goes where no lorry can. For a small farmer the animal is both family and livelihood.
- **Animal rights thinkers such as Peter Singer, in 'Animal Liberation', 1975:** An animal cannot agree to work and cannot leave. Using another being as an engine is wrong however kindly it is done, now that machines can do the job.
- **N. S. Ramaswamy, an Indian management teacher who studied animal power from the 1970s:** He counted crores of working animals in India and argued for better carts, harnesses and animal health care. In his view the animals did a large share of the country's haulage and were being ignored.
### Living travellers carry their own fuel: a small bird can fly for eleven days without landing · *defined*
**Definition:** Migrating animals store energy from food as body fat before a long journey and use it up as they travel.

People are not the only ones who make long journeys. A bird called the bar-tailed godwit flies from Alaska to New Zealand and Australia every year. In 2022 a young godwit wearing a tiny tracker flew about 13,500 kilometres in 11 days without landing once. Before leaving, godwits eat until nearly half their body is fat. That fat is their fuel tank. Amur falcons gather in Nagaland every autumn in lakhs. Then they cross the Arabian Sea to Africa without a rest, more than 3,000 kilometres over water. Their food got its energy from plants, and the plants got it from the Sun.
- e.g. A monarch butterfly crosses North America on the sugar of flower nectar.
- e.g. A coconut can float across an ocean on the currents and sprout on a far beach, using no fuel at all.
- e.g. Before the flight, a godwit's stomach and gut shrink to save weight, as a racing cyclist strips a cycle.
### Wheels and carts are about 5,500 years old, and Harappan children played with toy carts · *defined*
**Definition:** The wheel is a round frame that turns on a central rod called an axle. Wheeled carts appear in the records of several regions from about 3500 BCE.

Dragging a load is hard, because the whole underside rubs on the ground. A wheel rolls, so it rubs only at the axle. The oldest signs of wheeled carts are about 5,500 years old. They come from Mesopotamia, from eastern Europe and from the lands north of the Black Sea, at nearly the same time. Nobody knows who was first. In the cities of the Indus valley, at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, people made small clay carts with solid wheels more than 4,000 years ago. Many seem to have been toys. Bullock carts in Sindh and Punjab kept nearly the same shape into modern times.
- e.g. The stone chariot at Hampi in Karnataka and the great stone wheels of the Sun temple at Konark in Odisha show how much the wheel meant to their builders.
- e.g. The peoples of ancient Mexico made wheeled toys but used no carts. They had no large animals to pull them and their land was steep.
- **Katha Upanishad 1.3.3 and 1.3.4:** Know the self as the rider in a chariot and the body as the chariot. Know the understanding as the driver and the mind as the reins. The senses are the horses. The old teachers used the best vehicle of their day as a picture of a person.
- **The Ramayana, in its account of the Pushpaka vimana:** The epic describes a flying chariot that went wherever its owner wished. This is a work of imagination, showing that people dreamed of a vehicle with no animals long before one existed. It is not evidence of ancient aircraft.
- **Archaeologists such as David Anthony, in 2007:** The wheel spread very quickly once it appeared, so its birthplace is hard to pin down. It needed good woodworking tools of metal, and that may be why it appeared when it did.
### Who is a road for? Most Indians walk, cycle or take the bus, and most road space goes to cars · *debated*
**Definition:** Road space is shared among walkers, cyclists, buses, two-wheelers, cars and goods vehicles. How to divide it is a choice that cities argue about.

Look at a city road and ask who uses it. In the 2011 census, about one in three working Indians who travelled to work went on foot or by cycle. Many more took a bus. Only a few went by car. Now look at how the road is built. Most of the width is for motor vehicles. The footpath is narrow, broken or missing. About 1.7 lakh people died on India's roads in 2022, and about one in five were walking. So people ask a fair question. Should a city be built so that cars move fast, or so that people can get about safely?
- e.g. Chennai adopted a policy in 2014 that put walking and cycling first, and rebuilt many footpaths wide enough for a wheelchair.
- e.g. Count the footpath on your own street. Is it wide enough for two people to pass? Is there a slope at the kerb?
- e.g. On 'Raahgiri' or 'open street' days in Gurugram, Bhopal and other cities, a road is closed to motor vehicles for a morning and people walk, skate and play on it.
- **Enrique Peñalosa, mayor of Bogotá in Colombia from 1998:** A good city is one where a child on a tricycle can go anywhere safely. He took road space from cars for buses, cycle tracks and footpaths. He said a bus with 80 people has a right to 80 times the road space of a car with one.
- **Jane Jacobs, a writer in New York, in 1961:** Streets are where a city's life happens: shops, children, neighbours watching out for each other. She led residents in stopping a highway that planners wanted to drive through their neighbourhood.
- **Planners who favour fast roads, and many vehicle owners:** Goods, ambulances and workers must move quickly for a city to earn its living. People who have saved for years to buy a vehicle want roads they can use.

## Mostly agreed
- The amount of carbon dioxide in the air has risen by about half since people began burning fossil fuels in large amounts. *How we know:* Charles David Keeling began measuring the air on the mountain Mauna Loa in Hawaii in 1958 and found about 315 parts of carbon dioxide in every million parts of air. The same station now records more than 420. Air bubbles trapped in old ice show about 280 before the age of engines.
- Lead was once added to petrol, harmed children's brains, and has now been removed everywhere. *How we know:* The geochemist Clair Patterson showed in the 1960s that lead in the air and in people's bodies had risen enormously since leaded petrol began. India stopped selling leaded petrol in 2000. The last country to stop was Algeria, in 2021. Lead in children's blood fell wherever it was removed.
- Heavier vehicles need more energy to go the same distance, whatever they run on. *How we know:* Tests of petrol, diesel and electric vehicles all show that energy use climbs with weight, because more weight means more rubbing at the tyres and more push needed to speed up. This is why a scooter uses a fraction of what a car does.
- A helmet and a seat belt greatly reduce the chance of dying in a crash. *How we know:* Reviews of many crash studies find that a proper helmet cuts a two-wheeler rider's risk of death by about two-fifths, and a seat belt cuts a front-seat passenger's risk by about half.

## How we found out
**Eunice Foote, Joseph Henry, John Tyndall, Svante Arrhenius, Charles David Keeling, Raymond Sorenson, 1856 to 2011.** For most of the time that people have burned coal and oil, they believed that smoke simply blows away. The sky is so large that nothing people did could change it. That was reasonable. A chimney's smoke thins out and is gone within minutes. In 1856 Eunice Foote, an American scientist, tested gases in glass tubes set in sunlight, with a thermometer in each. The tube filled with carbon dioxide grew much hotter than the tube of ordinary air and took far longer to cool. She wrote that more of this gas in the air would make the Earth warmer. As a woman, she did not present her paper at the science meeting that year. A male scientist, Joseph Henry, read it out for her, and it was soon forgotten. In 1859 John Tyndall in London made finer measurements and is usually given the credit. In 1896 Svante Arrhenius calculated the warming by hand. Still nobody knew if the gas was really building up. In 1958 Charles David Keeling began measuring the air on a mountain in Hawaii, far from any city. His record climbed year after year, and has not stopped. Foote's paper was found again in 2011 by a retired geologist, Raymond Sorenson.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that smoke from burning fuel blows away and cannot change something as vast as the air. It was believable because smoke does thin out and vanish from sight within minutes.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Petroleum did not come from living beings. It formed from carbon deep inside the Earth and is still seeping upward.** (Dmitri Mendeleev in 1877, Nikolai Kudryavtsev and other Soviet geologists from 1951, and the astronomer Thomas Gold in the 1980s and 1990s; *rejected*)  
  For: Simple gases such as methane are found on planets and moons with no known life. Oil sometimes turns up in rocks that never lay under a sea.  
  Against: Oil contains molecules that are plainly the worn remains of molecules from living cells. A deep hole drilled in Sweden from 1986 to test Gold's idea found almost no oil. Oil companies find oil by looking where ancient life was buried, and it works.
- **Hydrogen, and not batteries, will power the vehicles of the future.** (Car makers such as Toyota and Hyundai, and India's National Green Hydrogen Mission of 2023; *debated*)  
  For: Hydrogen is a gas that gives only water when used in a device called a fuel cell. A tank fills in minutes and carries a lorry a long way without a heavy battery.  
  Against: Making hydrogen cleanly needs a great deal of electricity, and much is lost on the way. Charging a battery directly is about three times less wasteful. Few filling stations exist. Many engineers expect hydrogen to be used for ships and steel making and not for cars.
- **The answer to fuel and traffic problems is fewer vehicles, and not cleaner ones.** (Ivan Illich in 'Energy and Equity', 1974, and many city planners today; *minority*)  
  For: Illich worked out that when you count the hours people work to pay for a car, its fuel and its roads, the car moves them at about the pace of a cyclist. Cities built for walking and cycling use a fraction of the fuel.  
  Against: Vehicles brought villages within reach of hospitals, schools and markets. People in poorer countries are only now getting what richer ones have long had, and it is unfair to ask them to go without.
- **CNG made Delhi's air clean.** (A popular belief after the Supreme Court's orders took effect in 2002; *debated*)  
  For: Black smoke from buses and autos disappeared, and measurements in the first years showed less soot.  
  Against: By the 2010s Delhi's air was among the worst in the world again, because of many more vehicles, lorries, dust, industry and burning. Some studies also found that CNG engines give out very fine particles of their own.

## Roots
You have followed the push in a cycle, a bullock cart and a bus back to where it came from. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** You blew up a balloon, and the air you blew sent the balloon along the string. Your blowing came from your muscles, and your muscles ran on your breakfast. The rice in your breakfast caught its energy from the Sun. Diesel in a bus also holds sunlight, caught by tiny sea beings long ago. So 'food' and 'fuel' are two names for stored sunlight of different ages.  
*Habit it breaks:* We keep 'food' for living bodies and 'fuel' for machines, and 'muscle power' apart from 'engine power', as if they were different in kind. The labels hide that both are stores of the same sunlight.  
*Evidence:* In the balloon rocket test, the only energy that goes in is the child's own breath. More breaths send the balloon farther. A meal has been turned into the travel of a machine, with nothing else added.

- *who am i:* When you pedal, the energy in your legs was sunlight a few months ago. You are not a separate thing that uses energy. You are one of the places where sunlight is passing through and doing something.
- *what is life:* Living beings catch energy and hand it on. Grass catches sunlight, the bullock eats the grass, and the cart moves. Tiny sea beings caught sunlight so long ago that they have become oil. Life has been storing the Sun for a very long time.
- *what is this world:* The bus, the bird, the bullock and you run on one source. What looks like many different kinds of power is one flow that takes different shapes. No one has found energy being made from nothing or disappearing into nothing.

Ask of anything that moves, 'what is pushing it?', and keep asking. Almost every answer on a road leads back to the Sun, through a leaf or a tiny sea being. So the differences we care about most, such as petrol or muscle and machine or animal, are differences in the age and the container of one thing. How we spend that store is then a question about everyone, including beings long dead and children not yet born. The old Indian teachers used the chariot as their picture of a person, and asked who is really riding. A Buddhist teacher answered that there is no rider to be found, and his view is given below.

- **Katha Upanishad 1.3.3 and 1.3.4:** Know the self as the rider and the body as the chariot. Know the understanding as the driver and the mind as the reins. The senses, they say, are the horses, and the things they sense are the roads. (The lesson asked what moves a vehicle and followed the answer back through engine, fuel and Sun. The Upanishad asks the same question of a person: when the body moves, who or what is moving it? Science can trace the energy. It has not found the rider.)
- **Rig Veda 1.164.2:** Seven yoke the chariot that has one wheel. One horse with seven names draws it. The wheel has three hubs, does not wear out and cannot be stopped, and on it all these worlds rest. (The poets pictured the Sun as a single chariot on which all worlds depend. The lesson's science agrees with the plain part: the movement of nearly every living body and every engine on Earth is drawn from the Sun.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 3.19:** The Sun is to be honoured as Brahman, the ground of all. When the Sun was born, the text says, shouts of joy arose, and all beings and all that they desire rose up with it. (The text ties every being and every wish to the Sun's rising. In terms of energy, a scientist can say much the same: the food, the wind, the rain and the fuel all rise with the Sun.)
- Science (settled): The energy in food, wood, wind, flowing rivers and fossil fuels all came first from sunlight. The energy of the Earth's inner heat, of the tides and of nuclear power did not.
- Science (open): Energy is never made or destroyed, only changed from one form to another. What energy is in itself has no agreed answer.
- Other view, The Buddhist monk Nagasena, in the Milindapanha, book 2, chapter 1: Nagasena asks King Milinda how he came, and the king says by chariot. 'Is the pole the chariot? The axle? The wheels? The frame?' To each the king says no. 'Then where is the chariot?' Nagasena's point is that 'chariot' is only a name for parts that have come together, and a person is the same. On this view there is no rider behind the body and no one ground behind the many forms. There are only parts and causes, flowing on.
- Practice, Follow it back (3 min): Sit by a window where you can see the road or hear it. Pick one thing that is moving: a scooter, a bird, a person walking. Now follow it back, slowly, out loud. What is pushing it? Where did that come from? And before that? Keep going until you reach the Sun or get stuck. Now put a hand on your chest and feel your own breathing. Follow that back too. Sit for three slow breaths.
- Ask: If the bus and you both run on sunlight, what is the real difference between a machine moving and a living being moving?
- Ask: The tiny sea beings who became oil lived and died long before people. Are they part of your bus ride?
- Ask: When you follow a push all the way back, does it ever reach a place where it truly starts?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Petrol is sunlight that was caught millions of years ago. Your breakfast is sunlight that was caught a few months ago. So is there any vehicle, or any living traveller, that does not run on the Sun? (hint: Try a sailing boat, an electric train and a bird. Follow each one's energy backwards as far as you can.)
- *deep:* You ride a cycle to the shop. What moved the cycle: your legs, your breakfast, the rice plant or the Sun? (hint: Ask if you can leave any one of them out and still get to the shop.)
- *big:* The oil took millions of years to form, and people may burn most of it in a few hundred. Does the oil belong only to the people alive now? (hint: Think about children who will be born a hundred years from now, and the beings who became the oil.)
- *big:* An electric scooter makes no smoke in your street, but a power station far away burns coal to charge it. Is the scooter clean? (hint: Ask who breathes the smoke in each case. Does it matter whether you can see them?)
- *deep:* If engines that waste less lead people to drive more, what would actually make a country burn less fuel? (hint: Think about what makes your own family decide to make a trip or not.)
- *big:* A bullock who pulls a cart gets tired and thirsty. A tractor does not seem to. Is it fair to ask an animal to do the work of an engine? (hint: Think of what the bullock gets and gives, and of what the bullock would choose if asked.)
- *deep:* Ants on a crowded trail do not jam, and people in cars do. What do the ants do that we do not? (hint: Watch a line of ants for five minutes. Look for overtaking, pushing and waiting.)
- *big:* A village has clean air and few buses. A city has many buses and dirty air. Which would you choose to live in, and what would you change about each? (hint: Think about a day when someone in the family is ill, and a day when everyone is well.)
- *big:* Most people on a road are walking, cycling or in a bus, yet most of the road is given to cars. Who should decide how a road is shared? (hint: Imagine you are dividing a road with chalk. Whom would you ask first?)
- *deep:* Scientists can measure energy exactly, in a litre of petrol or a plate of idlis, but cannot say what energy is. Can you really know something that you can only measure? (hint: Think of something else you can count or measure without being able to say what it is, such as time.)

## Story: How Old Is Your Sunlight?
*A made-up story set in Chennai and in a village near Pollachi, Tamil Nadu. The family and the bullock are imagined. What is real: petroleum formed from tiny sea beings over millions of years, the energy in food and in fossil fuel was first caught from sunlight, and coconut farms around Pollachi use bullock carts, tractors and lorries side by side.*

Kuzhali lived in Chennai, and she knew about traffic. Her school was six kilometres from her flat. On a good day the car took thirty minutes. On a bad day it took an hour, and she ate her idlis in the back seat looking at the same bus advertisement the whole time.

In the Pongal holidays she went to stay with her cousin Sevanthi, in a village near Pollachi. Sevanthi was eight, a year older, and she knew about everything else.

On the first morning Sevanthi wheeled out two cycles. 'School is closed, but I will show you my road,' she said. They rode three kilometres between coconut trees. It took fifteen minutes. In all that way they met one bus, four motorcycles, a tractor, eleven goats and a peacock who would not move.

'Where is everybody?' asked Kuzhali.

'This is everybody,' said Sevanthi.

At the farm, their aunt Ponni was under the tractor with a spanner. Ponni athai mended every engine for ten villages around. Beside the shed stood a cart heaped with coconuts, and in front of the cart stood a bullock named Karuppan, chewing slowly, the colour of a monsoon cloud.

'Athai,' said Kuzhali, 'which is the best vehicle? I am making a list.' She had a notebook. 'The car is the fastest. So the car is first.'

'Is it?' said Sevanthi. 'You said six kilometres takes you an hour. I can cycle six kilometres in half an hour. My cycle beats your car.'

'Only in Chennai,' said Kuzhali, and then she heard what she had said.

'The tractor is the strongest,' said Ponni athai from underneath. 'It pulls what twenty people could not. So is the tractor first?'

'Karuppan is the quietest,' said Sevanthi. 'He needs no road. He goes between the trees where the tractor cannot turn. He finds his own way home if you fall asleep on the cart.'

'A car cannot do that,' Kuzhali agreed. She looked at her list. It had three firsts on it now. 'Then I will ask it another way. Which one eats the least?'

Ponni athai slid out and sat up, wiping her hands. 'Now that is a mechanic's question. What does Karuppan eat?'

'Grass. Straw. Oilcake.'

'And what did the grass eat?'

Kuzhali knew this from school. 'Sunlight. And water and air.'

'When?'

'When? This year, I suppose. After the rains.'

'Good. Karuppan runs on this season's sunlight. What do you run on, when you pedal?'

'Idli,' said Kuzhali. 'Rice. The rice grew... last year?'

'Last year's sunlight. Now.' Ponni athai tapped the tractor's tank, and it rang. 'Diesel. Where from?'

'From under the ground.'

'And before that, tiny beings in an old sea, smaller than a grain of dust. They lived on sunlight as grass does. They died and sank and were buried, and the Earth cooked them slowly into oil. How long ago?'

Kuzhali did not know.

'Before there were people. Before there were coconut trees. Crores of years.' Ponni athai looked at the tractor almost kindly. 'So this fellow runs on the oldest sunlight of all. Sunlight that fell before anyone had eyes to see it.'

Kuzhali looked at the bullock, the cycle and the tractor. A minute ago they had been three different kinds of thing. 'They are all eating the Sun,' she said. 'Only the age is different. This year's, last year's, and crores of years old.'

'Then here is the question for your notebook,' said Ponni athai. 'Grass grows back by next rains. Rice grows back by next harvest. How long before the old sea makes more diesel?'

Kuzhali did the sum and did not like it. 'Crores of years.'

'And when we burn it, where does it go?'

Sevanthi answered. 'Into the air. Teacher said it makes the air hold more heat.'

'So you two tell me,' said Ponni athai. 'Which sunlight should we spend first? The kind that comes back every year, or the kind that came once?'

Nobody spoke. Karuppan swished his tail.

'But Athai,' said Kuzhali at last, 'you mend diesel engines. That is your work.'

'It is,' said Ponni athai. 'Without this tractor, the coconuts do not reach the lorry and the lorry does not reach Chennai, and you do not get chutney with your idli. I did not say the answer was easy. I said it was your question.' She picked up her spanner. 'Last month I put a solar panel on the pump shed. That is today's sunlight, not even one day old. I am an old mechanic learning a new engine.'

That evening Kuzhali crossed out her list. On a clean page she wrote three words in a column: today, this year, long ago. Then she sat on the cart beside Karuppan, who smelt of hay, and tried to work out which column she belonged in herself.

**For the parent:** A bullock, a cyclist and a tractor all run on energy that was first caught from the Sun. What differs is how long ago it was caught, and so how soon it can be had again.

- Ask: Kuzhali's list had three 'firsts'. Is there one best vehicle, or does it depend on what you ask?
- Ask: Which sunlight would you spend first: this year's or the kind that came once? What makes that hard to do?
- Ask: Kuzhali tried to work out which column she belonged in. Which would you put yourself in?

## Games
### g1 · Back to the Sun: a family-of-cards game with 20 cards (25 min)
- **rules:** There are five families of four cards. Each family begins with a vehicle and ends with the sunlight that first gave the push.; Deal 5 cards to each player for two players, or 4 cards each for three or four players. Put the rest face down as the draw pile.; On your turn, ask one player for a card from a family you already hold. For example, 'Do you have the cycle's breakfast card?' ## Experiments
### e1 · Balloon rocket: does more air carry it farther? (30 min)
- **question:** If you blow twice as much air into a balloon, does it travel twice as far along the string?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will double the air carry the balloon double the distance, a little farther, or the same? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Change only the number of breaths: 2, 4 and 6. Keep the same balloon, the same straw, the same string, the same person blowing and the same start mark. Try each amount 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A burst balloon leaves small pieces that a child under 5 can choke on, so pick up every piece at once. Tie the string at about chair-back height, never at face height.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Write each distance in ruler lengths (one school ruler is 30 centimetres). Circle the middle value for each amount of air. If the balloon reaches the far end, write END.
- **results table:** Breaths of air | Try 1 (ruler lengths) | Try 2 (ruler lengths) | Try 3 (ruler lengths) | Middle value
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take about 4 metres of smooth thread. Push one end through a straw. Tie the two ends tight to two chair backs, so the string is straight and level. Stick a sticky note on the string near one chair for the start mark.  
  *Parent says:* We are making a track for a balloon rocket. The string must be tight and straight, or the balloon will wobble.  
  *Child mode:* Thread the straw on the string. Help to tie it tight between two chairs.
  - Note: Use smooth thread or nylon fishing line. Wool and rough twine slow the straw too much. The string should be at chair-back height. If the string sags, pull the chairs further apart.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess. Write the guess in the margin of the table before any test. Then ask for the reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will try 2, 4 and 6 breaths. Will double the air carry the balloon double the distance? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what will happen if you put in more air. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: What do you think more air will do to the distance, and why? (good: More air gives a bigger push, so it goes farther.; Any honest guess with a reason, such as 'the same, because it is the same balloon'.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A guess is a guess, and the test will answer it.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Blow up a balloon with 2 big breaths of your own. Pinch the neck so that no air escapes. Your child tapes the balloon under the straw, with the neck pointing back to the start mark. Pull the straw to the start. Count 3, 2, 1, then let go. Mark where the balloon stops with a sticky note.  
  *Parent says:* Count with me. Three, two, one, go! Watch where the balloon stops.  
  *Child mode:* Count 3, 2, 1. Let go. Mark where it stops.
  - Ask: Where do you think the air goes when you let go? (good: Out of the neck, backwards.; It pushes backwards, and the balloon goes forwards.)
  - Note: Each try needs a fresh breath of air. Blow out the old air and tape the balloon again. Do this 3 times for 2 breaths. A big breath means breathing all the way out. If the balloon pops, use a spare one and repeat that try. A balloon grows softer with use, so tell your child that the first tries may go a little shorter.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same with 4 big breaths, three times. Measure each try from the start mark to the sticky note, using the ruler end to end. Write the number of ruler lengths in the table.  
  *Parent says:* This time we use 4 breaths. Count the ruler lengths each time. Which try went farthest?  
  *Child mode:* Do three tries with 4 breaths. Count ruler lengths. Write them in the table.
  - Note: Lay the ruler end to end along the string and count. If you have a measuring tape, use it, and write centimetres in the table in place of ruler lengths. Keep the same unit all through.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same with 6 breaths, three times. If the balloon reaches the end of the string, write END in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now 6 breaths. Is it going farther than before? Is it going twice as far as 2 breaths?  
  *Child mode:* Do three tries with 6 breaths. Write each distance in the table.
  - Note: If your string is short and the balloon reaches the end each time, that already shows that more air carries it farther. For a longer run, move the chairs apart.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Circle the middle value of each amount of air. Compare with the guess you made. Ask what the test cannot tell us.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your circled numbers. Did double the air give double the distance? Was your guess right?  
  *Child mode:* Circle the middle number for each amount. Was your guess right?
  - Ask: Did double the air give double the distance? Where do you think the rest of the push went? (good: More air always went farther, but not exactly double.; Some push is lost to rubbing on the straw and to pushing the air aside.)
  - Ask: What could we test next? (good: A longer string, a different balloon, or a bigger balloon.; Weight on the balloon, which is the next experiment.)
  - Note: The result is usually that more air goes farther, but double the air does not give double the distance. A part of the push is lost to rubbing between the straw and the string and to pushing the air in front of the balloon aside. The test cannot say exactly how much air is in each balloon. Breaths differ in size, so the amounts are only roughly 2, 4 and 6. Scientists measure air with a bag of known size. The idea to leave your child with is that the push was stored in the balloon and came out as movement.
  - Think: If the balloon holds a push, where did you get that push from?
### e2 · A load on the rocket: does a heavy balloon go as far? (20 min)
- **question:** If the balloon rocket carries coins, does it go as far with the same amount of air?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will 2 coins and then 4 coins make the balloon go a little less far, a lot less far, or the same? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Change only the number of coins: 0, 2 and 4. Use 4 breaths of air every time, with the same balloon, straw, string and start mark. Try each load 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A burst balloon leaves small pieces that a child under 5 can choke on, so pick up every piece at once.
- **record:** Fill in the table with ruler lengths. Circle the middle value for each load.
- **results table:** Coins carried | Try 1 (ruler lengths) | Try 2 (ruler lengths) | Try 3 (ruler lengths) | Middle value
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set up the string and straw as before. Ask your child to guess how the coins will change the distance. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Last time more air went farther. Now we add coins to the balloon but keep the air the same. What do you think will happen? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess what coins will do. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: Will the balloon go as far when it carries coins? (good: No, it is heavier, so the same push moves it less far.; Any honest guess with a reason.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do 3 tries with no coins and 4 breaths. Mark and measure each one. Write the ruler lengths down.  
  *Parent says:* First the plain balloon, with no coins. This is the one to compare with.  
  *Child mode:* Do three tries with no coins. Write the distances.
  - Note: If you did the 4 breath test in the first experiment, you can use those numbers. Repeat one try to check that today's balloon behaves the same.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape 2 coins in a row along the balloon's belly, close to the straw. Do 3 tries with 4 breaths. Mark and measure each one.  
  *Parent says:* Now 2 coins. The air is the same. Watch the balloon. Is it as fast as before?  
  *Child mode:* Tape 2 coins on. Do three tries. Write the distances.
  - Note: Keep the balloon level. Coins hanging at one end tilt the balloon and slow it. If the tape slips, press it down firmly with your thumb.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same with 4 coins. If the balloon barely moves, write 0 for that try. Then try 2 coins again.  
  *Parent says:* Now 4 coins. Does it go as far as with 2? What is happening to the same push?  
  *Child mode:* Tape 4 coins on. Do three tries. Write the distances.
  - Ask: Why does a heavier balloon go less far with the same air? (good: The same push has more weight to move.; It takes more push to move something heavy.)
  - Note: If four coins stop the balloon, that is a result, not a failure. It shows that this much air cannot move that much weight.

### a3 · Wind in a sail: a paper boat that moves by breath (20 min)
- **safety:** Pour water carefully and wipe the floor at once. Empty the tray into the sink or onto a plant afterwards.

### a4 · Fill the bus: how much fuel does each person use? (20 min)
- **record:** Draw the four vehicles with their people. Write: kilometres on one litre, how many people, and 'kilometres carried for all the people'.

  *Parent says:* One litre takes a car 15 kilometres, a scooter 45 and a bus only 4. Each grain is a person. Load the plates.  
  *Child mode:* Put one grain for each person on each paper vehicle.
  - Note: The numbers are rough everyday figures. Real distances depend on the road, the load and the driver. The point is to compare, not to be exact.
- **a4s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess which of the four vehicles carries each person farthest on one litre. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* Which vehicle gets each person farthest on one litre? What do you think? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which vehicle carries each person farthest on one litre.
  - Ask: Which will carry each person farthest on one litre, and why? (good: Any guess with a reason. Many children say the scooter or the car.; The one with the most people and the fewest kilometres per person, if they work it out.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most people guess the scooter or the car, because the bus uses more fuel.

  *Child mode:* Multiply the people by the kilometres. Write each answer.
  - Ask: Which has the biggest number, and what does it mean? (good: The bus, with 200. One litre of fuel carries the most people the most distance.; The full bus gets more travel out of one litre than the others.)
  - Note: Use a counting line or the grains to count in 15s, 45s and 4s if multiplying is new. The multiplication tables will help.
- **a4s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Remove 40 grains from the bus plate, so that 10 people ride the bus. Work out again. Ask what happens when a bus runs nearly empty.  
  *Parent says:* Take 40 people off the bus. Now what is the number for the bus?  
  *Child mode:* Take 40 grains off the bus. Work out the number again.
  - Ask: What happens to the bus's number when it is nearly empty, and what does that tell us? (good: 10 times 4 is only 40, which is less than the car with 4 people. An empty bus wastes fuel.; A bus is only a good way to travel when many people ride it.)
  - Note: Full buses are good, and empty ones are not. This is why cities try to have many people using buses.

- **safety:** Watch from the footpath, a safe distance from the road, with a grown-up. Do not cross a railway line.

  *Parent says:* Vehicles wait here for a long time. Let us see how many keep their engines on.  
  *Child mode:* Stand safely on the footpath. Watch the waiting vehicles.

  *Parent says:* How can you tell if an engine is on? Make a mark for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Make a mark for each engine, running or off. Note how long it waits.
  - Ask: How can you tell if an engine is running, and what is it doing while the vehicle does not move? (good: You can hear it, or see the exhaust. It burns fuel but the vehicle goes nowhere.; It uses fuel and makes carbon dioxide and nothing is moving.)

- **a5s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the marks. Ask how many engines could have been switched off, and how many tens of vehicles waiting in a day might add up.  
  *Parent says:* How many engines were running? How many could have been switched off?  
  *Child mode:* Count each column. Which one is bigger?
  - Ask: What could drivers do while waiting, and why might they not? (good: Switch the engine off. Some may think restarting uses more fuel, or may fear the engine will not start.; Many drivers keep it on for the fan or the air conditioning.)
  - Note: For a wait of more than about a minute, switching off saves fuel. A modern engine restarts easily.
  - Think: If every driver switched off at every long wait, how much would a city save in a year?

## Projects
### p1 · Our family's fuel diary for one week (60 min, 7 days)
- **record:** Columns: day, where we went, about how many kilometres, how many people went, and a letter. Write W if the trip could have been walked, C cycled, S shared or J joined with another trip. Leave the letter empty if it could not change.
- **schedule:** day 1: Rule the diary page and talk about which trips you think you take. (15 min); day 2: Write down today's engine trips. (5 min); day 3: Write down today's engine trips. (5 min); day 4: Write down today's engine trips. (5 min); day 5: Write down today's engine trips. (5 min); day 6: Write down today's engine trips. (5 min); day 7: Add up, mark the trips that could change and choose one change to try. (20 min)
- **p1s1 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rule the page into five columns and name each one. Ask your child to guess how many engine trips the family makes in one week. Write the guess at the top.  
  *Parent says:* For one week we write every trip that uses an engine. A trip counts if a scooter, car, auto or bus took us. What do you think the number will be? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Rule five columns. Guess how many engine trips we make in a week.
  - Ask: How many engine trips do you think we make in a week? (good: Any number with a reason, such as 'one every day for school'.; A guess is fine. The diary will check it.)
  - Note: Only the family's own trips go in the diary. It is not shared outside the home. Count a school bus or a shared auto as an engine trip too. Walking and cycling trips are not written.
- **p1s2 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each evening for five days, spend about 5 minutes. Add the day's engine trips to the page. Ask your child to say where you went, how far it was and how many people went. Guess the kilometres if you do not know.  
  *Parent says:* Which trips did we make today? Who was with us? About how far was it?  
  *Child mode:* Add today's trips to the diary. Count the people who went.
  - Ask: Could any trip today have been done another way? (good: A short trip could have been walked or cycled.; Two trips could have been joined into one.)
  - Note: Guess distances by walking time. A walk of 10 minutes is about 1 kilometre. Do not change what you normally do this week, so the diary shows what really happens. Do not make the child feel any trip was wrong.
- **p1s3 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 7, add up the trips and the kilometres. Mark each trip with W, C, S or J if it could change. Count how many trips and kilometres could change. Choose one change to try next week and write it at the bottom.  
  *Parent says:* Let us add up the week. Which trips could we walk, cycle, share or join with another trip? Which one will we try?  
  *Child mode:* Add up the trips. Mark the ones that could change. Pick one to try.
  - Ask: Which trip would you change first, and why? (good: A short trip, because walking or cycling is easy.; One shared with a neighbour, because the car goes anyway.)
  - Ask: If every family changed one trip a week, what would happen to the fuel used by a city? (good: It would fall a lot, because there are so many families.; Any answer with a reason. A lot of small changes add up.)
  - Note: Be honest that some trips cannot change: a late night, heavy shopping, a sick person, no safe footpath. The aim is to see which can, and not to feel guilty about the rest. There is a rebound idea in the lesson: do not spend the saved fuel on extra driving.
  - Think: If your family does change one trip, who gains and who might lose?

## Challenges
### ch1 · Build a cart that carries 10 coins for 1 metre without a hand-push (30 min)
- **problem:** Make a cart that carries 10 one-rupee coins along a flat floor for 1 metre. After the start, nobody may touch or push it.
- **your turn:** Make your own cart and your own way of pushing it. How much load can it carry for 1 metre? Is there one best way, or does the best way depend on what you want?
- **constraints:** Build it from things at home: a small carton, bottle caps, straws, pencils, tape, paper and a rubber band.; It must carry all 10 coins and roll straight for 1 metre on a smooth floor.; Power can come from wind, a balloon or a stretched rubber band. A battery or a motor is not allowed.; Nobody may touch or push it after the start.
- **general:** A paper sail on the cart, pushed from behind by breath or a hand fan.; A long balloon taped on top of the cart with its neck pointing backwards, as in the rocket test.; A rolling start down a slope made from a book, which gives the cart its push from gravity.
- **creative:** A rubber band stretched between the carton and a fixed point, then let go so that it kicks the cart forwards.; A string tied to the cart and led over the table edge to a small weight, so that the falling weight pulls the cart.; A cart shaped like a boat with a large sail, to catch more wind on a smooth floor.; A cart pushed by a tiny second cart that rolls down a slope and bumps it.
- **ch1s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up tapes two straws across the underside of the carton. Slide a pencil through each straw. Push a bottle cap on each pencil end. Check that the cart rolls straight when you push it with a finger.  
  *Parent says:* First, make a cart that rolls. Wheels turn on the pencil axles inside the straws. A wheel that is stuck will not roll.  
  *Child mode:* Make a cart with four wheels. See if it rolls straight.
  - Note: Pierce the bottle caps in the middle with a pointed tool, as an adult job. If the wheels rub on the carton, add a small gap with a bit of tape on each end of the pencil. A cart that wobbles will not go straight for 1 metre.
- **ch1s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the 10 coins in a line inside the carton. Give the cart one gentle push to see how far it rolls with the load. Mark where it stops.  
  *Parent says:* Now the cart carries the load. The push comes from your finger this time. How far does it go?  
  *Child mode:* Load 10 coins. Give it one push. See how far it goes.
  - Ask: Why does a loaded cart need more push than an empty one? (good: More weight needs more push to get going.; The same push moves a heavy thing less far.)
  - Note: This links with the balloon test. A heavier load needs a bigger push.
- **ch1s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try one usual way to give the push. Stand a paper sail upright on the cart's front with tape. Blow from behind through a straw from 30 centimetres away. Or tape a long balloon on top, nozzle pointing backwards, blow it up and let go. Measure how far the cart goes.  
  *Parent says:* Try the usual ways. A sail catches your breath. A balloon pushes air backwards and the cart goes forwards.  
  *Child mode:* Try a sail with your breath. Try a balloon on top of the cart.
  - Ask: Which usual way carried the load farthest, and why? (good: Any honest answer from the test, with a reason.; The balloon, because it gives a longer push, or the sail on a big breath.)
- **ch1s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try one creative way. Stretch a rubber band between two bits of tape at the front of the cart. Hook it behind the cart's back edge and let it spring back to give the cart a kick. Or let the cart roll down a short slope made from a book. Measure each. Write what worked and what did not.  
  *Parent says:* Now invent your own way to push the cart. A stretched rubber band can store a push too. What will you try?  
  *Child mode:* Try a rubber band or a slope. Write what worked.
  - Ask: What was the best way to push the cart, and is there only one best way? (good: Answers will differ. Several ways can carry 10 coins for 1 metre.; The best way depends on what you have and what you want.)
  - Note: Do not rank one design as the right one. Each answer carries the load for some distance, and trade-offs such as time, cost and how easily it repeats are all worth talking about.
### ch2 · Plan the errands: do the day with the fewest engine kilometres (25 min)
- **problem:** A family has five jobs today: drop a child at school, buy vegetables, buy medicine, post a letter and visit a grandmother. Find a plan that does all five with the fewest engine kilometres.
- **your turn:** Draw the map of your own errands for one day. Find a plan that uses fewer engine kilometres. Which plan would your family really choose?
- **constraints:** Home is in the middle of the map. School is 2 kilometres north. The vegetable shop and the medicine shop are side by side, 1 kilometre east. The post office is 1 kilometre west. The grandmother lives 3 kilometres south.; Every job must be done today.; A walk of 1 kilometre takes about 15 minutes. The scooter can go anywhere.; The first plan is to go from home and back for each job on the scooter.
- **general:** Join the vegetable shop and the medicine shop into one trip, since they are side by side.; Walk to the post office, 1 kilometre away.; Do the school drop and come straight back, with no extra trips.
- **creative:** Share the school trip with a neighbour who goes the same way, so one scooter takes two children.; Ask the grandmother to meet the family halfway, or to come to the house.; Walk to the shops too, and carry the shopping home in a bag.; Ask a shop to deliver the medicine along with its other deliveries.
- **ch2s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the map. Write each distance on it. Add up the kilometres if the scooter goes out and back for each job. Remember that each trip is counted twice, there and back.  
  *Parent says:* First plan: a separate scooter trip for every job. How many kilometres is that, there and back, for all five?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the map. Count the kilometres for five separate trips.
  - Ask: How many kilometres does the first plan use? (good: 16 kilometres. School 4, vegetables 2, medicine 2, post 2, grandmother 6.; About 16. The check is 4 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 6.)
  - Note: Adding the shopping stops separately is the usual way. The count of 16 kilometres counts each trip there and back.
- **ch2s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look for a better plan. Join the vegetable and medicine trips, because the shops are side by side. Walk to the post office. Count the scooter kilometres again.  
  *Parent says:* Can two jobs share one trip? Can any job be done on foot?  
  *Child mode:* Find trips to join. Find a trip to walk. Count the kilometres again.
  - Ask: Which jobs can be joined or walked, and how many kilometres does that save? (good: Vegetables and medicine in one trip, and the post office on foot. The scooter then goes 4 + 2 + 6 = 12 kilometres, which is 4 less.; Joining and walking saves 4 kilometres, a quarter of the fuel.)
  - Note: A walk of 1 kilometre each way takes 30 minutes in all. Talk about the time as well as the fuel.
- **ch2s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look for creative ideas. Walk to the shops too. Ask the grandmother to come to you instead, or meet halfway. Ask a neighbour to take the child to school along with their own. Count the kilometres for your plan.  
  *Parent says:* Now think about other ways. Who else could go? Could someone come to you? Could the shops come to you?  
  *Child mode:* Invent two more ways to save. Count the kilometres again.
  - Ask: What other ways could the family save fuel? (good: Share the school trip with a neighbour, walk to the shops, or ask the grandmother to visit.; Any plan with a reason, and a count of the kilometres.)
  - Ask: What would be lost with each plan, and what would be gained? (good: Walking takes more time and gives a walk. Sharing needs a neighbour.; Each plan trades time, money and fuel.)
  - Note: There is no single right plan. A plan that saves the most fuel may take the most time. Let the child weigh up the trade-offs.
  - Think: What would make you choose the fastest plan, and what would make you choose the plan that uses the least fuel?

## Worksheets
### What pushes a vehicle? (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) What does a cycle use in place of petrol from a pump?  
   Options: Wind only / Battery power / The rider's muscles, run on food / Diesel from the rider  
   **Answer:** The rider's muscles, run on food. *Why:* A cycle runs on the rider's muscles, and muscles run on food. A cycle has no engine, no tank and no battery.
2. (fill) A muscle's fuel is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['food']. *Why:* Muscles get their energy from the food we eat. A breakfast of idli moves the legs that push the pedals.
3. (match) Match each vehicle to what makes it go.  
   **Answer:** Cycle → legs and food; Bullock cart → bullocks eating grass and grain; City bus → diesel; Electric scooter → a battery; Green and yellow auto in Delhi → CNG. *Why:* Each vehicle gets its push from a different source. A cycle uses muscles, a cart uses the bullocks, a bus burns diesel, a scooter uses a battery and the auto burns CNG.
4. (sort) Sort each vehicle by how it gets its push.  
   **Answer:** diesel bus → Burns fuel in an engine; cycle → Does not burn fuel in an engine; electric scooter → Does not burn fuel in an engine; CNG auto → Burns fuel in an engine; bullock cart → Does not burn fuel in an engine; petrol scooter → Burns fuel in an engine. *Why:* An engine burns a fuel to make hot gas that pushes a piston. A cycle, a cart and an electric scooter get their push in other ways.
5. (truefalse) Most petrol is made from the bones of dinosaurs.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most oil came from tiny floating sea beings that sank into the mud of warm seas. Their remains were squeezed and heated for millions of years.
6. (order) Put these in order, from the Sun to the moving cycle.  
   **Answer:** ['Sunlight on a rice plant', 'Rice in your breakfast', 'Energy stored in your body', 'Your leg muscles turning the pedals', 'The cycle rolling along']. *Why:* The Sun's light is caught by the plant. The plant's rice becomes breakfast. Your body keeps the energy. Your muscles use it to push the pedals, and the cycle rolls.
7. (write) Write two ways your family could save petrol or diesel this week.  
   **Answer:** Two ideas, each with a reason. For example, walk or cycle to a nearby shop, join two errands, share a ride, switch off at a long wait or pump up the tyres.. *Why:* Fuel is saved by not using an engine, by sharing, and by careful driving. A strong answer says why the idea saves fuel.
8. (draw) Draw a village road and a city road. Put one vehicle on each and label its push.  
   **Answer:** A village road with a cycle, a tractor or a bullock cart, and a city road with a bus, a car or a metro. Each is labelled with legs, bullocks, diesel, petrol or electricity.. *Why:* The two places have different traffic. A strong drawing shows that the village has fewer vehicles, with many cycles and animals, and the city has crowds of vehicles with engines.
9. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Petrol, diesel, CNG, electricity.  
   **Answer:** Electricity, because it is not burned in the vehicle. Petrol, diesel and CNG are burned in an engine.. *Why:* Fuels give out heat when they burn. Electricity from a battery turns a motor and nothing burns inside the vehicle.
10. (perspective) Two ways to go to a shop 2 kilometres away.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks one and says what would change its mind, such as a heavy bag, rain or the time.. *Why:* Each way has good points. The scooter is quick and carries a heavy bag. The cycle burns no fuel and uses little energy of your own.
### Fuels, sharing and electric vehicles (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does a cycle use less energy than walking for the same distance?  
   Options: A cycle has a small engine / The saddle holds you up and the wheels roll / Cyclists always go slower / Pedals are lighter than legs  
   **Answer:** The saddle holds you up and the wheels roll. *Why:* When you walk, your legs lift your body at every step. On a cycle the saddle holds you up and the wheels roll, so your legs only turn the pedals.
2. (truefalse) An electric scooter makes no carbon dioxide anywhere.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The scooter gives out none on the road. The electricity may come from a power station that burns coal, and making the battery also takes energy.
3. (count) A van takes 8 people 10 kilometres on one litre. How many kilometres of travel is that for all 8 together?  
   **Answer:** 80. *Why:* 8 people times 10 kilometres is 80 kilometres of travel from one litre.
4. (fill) Burning one litre of petrol makes about ____ kilograms of carbon dioxide.  
   **Answer:** ['2', '2.3']. *Why:* The petrol joins with oxygen from the air. The new gas weighs more than the petrol, about 2.3 kilograms for one litre.
5. (match) Match each way of saving fuel to why it works.  
   **Answer:** Switch off at a railway gate → The engine stops burning fuel while standing still; Pump up the tyres → Less rubbing on the road; Do three errands in one outing → Fewer trips; Take the school bus → Many children share one vehicle. *Why:* Each idea cuts fuel in a different way. Some stop the engine burning when it is standing still, some cut rubbing, and some share or join trips.
6. (order) Put these in order, from the least fuel for each person to the most.  
   **Answer:** ['Bus with 50 people, 4 kilometres on a litre', 'Scooter with 2 people, 45 kilometres on a litre', 'Car with 4 people, 15 kilometres on a litre', 'Car with 1 person, 15 kilometres on a litre']. *Why:* Multiply people by kilometres on a litre. The bus gives 200, the scooter with 2 gives 90, the car with 4 gives 60 and the car alone gives 15. A bigger number means less fuel for each person.
7. (write) Why do wider roads often fill up with vehicles again?  
   **Answer:** A wider road makes driving easier, so extra people choose to drive and extra vehicles come. The extra space fills up and the jam returns.. *Why:* More road space can mean more driving. A strong answer says that people change what they do when the road changes.
8. (write) Write one good thing and one problem about electric scooters.  
   **Answer:** A good thing, such as no exhaust smoke on the street, a quiet motor or little wasted energy. A problem, such as electricity from coal, mining for battery metals or the cost.. *Why:* Electric scooters help in some ways and not in others. A strong answer gives both and says why.
9. (sort) Sort each one by the kind of place where you see it most.  
   **Answer:** a metro train overhead → Usually city traffic; a bullock cart → Usually village traffic; a flyover over a crossing → Usually city traffic; a bus that comes four times a day → Usually village traffic; a traffic jam at six o'clock → Usually city traffic; buffaloes crossing the road → Usually village traffic. *Why:* A city has many vehicles in little space, with signals and flyovers. A village has fewer vehicles, with more cycles, tractors and animal carts.
10. (perspective) Who should get more of a city road?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says who the road is for, and how it could be shared.. *Why:* Both people have a real need. About one in three Indian workers walk or cycle to work, and most road space still goes to motor vehicles.
### Old sunlight, rebound and the future (challenge) (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Engines now waste less fuel, yet the world burns more. Which idea explains it?  
   Options: Cheaper driving can lead to more driving / Engines no longer need fuel / Petrol has become thicker / People are walking more  
   **Answer:** Cheaper driving can lead to more driving. *Why:* When a vehicle costs less to run, people tend to drive more and buy bigger vehicles. This is called the rebound effect. William Jevons noticed it with coal in 1865.
2. (truefalse) Running a petrol vehicle is running on very old sunlight.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Tiny sea beings caught sunlight millions of years ago. Their remains became oil, and the oil holds that energy.
3. (fill) Ants on a crowded trail avoid jams by ____ down.  
   **Answer:** ['slowing', 'slow']. *Why:* Ants adjust their speed and stop entering a crowded trail. Experiments in 2019 found this. Scientists are still working out the exact rules.
4. (count) A car that stands with its engine running burns half a litre in one hour. How many litres in 4 hours?  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Half a litre each hour for 4 hours is 4 halves, which makes 2 litres.
5. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Petrol, diesel, coal, ethanol.  
   **Answer:** Ethanol, because it is made from plants grown recently. The others are fossil fuels, made from living beings of millions of years ago.. *Why:* Ethanol comes from sugarcane or maize that grew this year, and new plants can be grown. Fossil fuels took millions of years to form.
6. (write) Petrol and breakfast both hold sunlight. How are they different?  
   **Answer:** Breakfast holds sunlight from a few months ago and petrol holds sunlight from millions of years ago. People use petrol far faster than it forms, while crops can be grown again.. *Why:* Both store energy first caught from the Sun. The difference is the age, and how fast we use them compared with how fast they are made.
7. (write) A bullock pulls a cart all day. Write three things a kind owner should do.  
   **Answer:** Give shade and water, let the bullock rest in the midday heat, keep the load light and stop at the end of a working day. A bullock tires, feels heat and needs rest.. *Why:* A working animal is a living being who tires, thirsts and feels heat. Indian rules from 1965 limit work to nine hours and call for rest in the heat.
8. (draw) Draw a chain from the Sun to an electric scooter that is charged from a coal power station.  
   **Answer:** Sunlight on a forest of long ago, coal, a power station, a wire, a battery and the scooter, each with an arrow.. *Why:* The energy in coal was first sunlight caught by trees of long ago. The power station burns the coal to make electricity, and the battery stores it.
9. (mcq) A friend says CNG made Delhi's air clean for ever. Which reply is best?  
   Options: Yes, it made the air clean for ever / It helped at first, but the smoke came back / No, CNG is the same as diesel / CNG caused all of Delhi's smoke  
   **Answer:** It helped at first, but the smoke came back. *Why:* CNG cut black smoke at first. Later there were many more vehicles, and dust, industry and burning, so the air got bad again. One change of fuel does not settle the matter.
10. (perspective) What should most vehicles run on in fifty years?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer gives a reason for each choice and says what could change it.. *Why:* Nobody knows yet. Batteries are ahead for scooters and cars. Hydrogen may suit lorries, but making it takes a lot of electricity.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A bullock cart rolls along a road. What gives it the push?  
   Options: The wheels turning / The road / The bullocks' muscles, run on grass and grain / The weight of the cart  
   **Answer:** The bullocks' muscles, run on grass and grain. *Why:* The wheels roll, but they do not push. The push comes from the bullocks' muscles, and the muscles run on the food they eat. If you chose the wheels, try asking what turns them.
2. (mcq) Which of these needs no fuel tank and no battery?  
   Options: A diesel bus / A cycle / A CNG auto / An electric scooter  
   **Answer:** A cycle. *Why:* A cycle is pushed by the rider's muscles, which run on food. The bus burns diesel, the auto burns CNG and the scooter needs a battery.
3. (mcq) What is a fuel?  
   Options: A liquid kept in a tank / Something very hot / A thing that makes a loud noise / Something that gives out heat when it burns  
   **Answer:** Something that gives out heat when it burns. *Why:* A fuel is anything that gives out heat when it burns. Many fuels are liquids, but CNG is a gas and coal is a solid rock, so 'a liquid' is not the whole answer.
4. (mcq) Which of these is a gas that is squeezed into a cylinder?  
   Options: CNG / Petrol / Diesel / Coal  
   **Answer:** CNG. *Why:* CNG is natural gas squeezed into a strong cylinder. Petrol and diesel are liquids, and coal is a hard black rock.
5. (mcq) What was petrol mostly made from?  
   Options: Melted rocks / Dinosaur bones only / Tiny sea beings of long ago, buried and squeezed / Water and air  
   **Answer:** Tiny sea beings of long ago, buried and squeezed. *Why:* Millions of years ago tiny floating beings died and sank into the mud of warm seas. Heat and squeezing turned their remains into oil. Dinosaurs were not the main source.
6. (mcq) Why will petrol and diesel not last for ever?  
   Options: Engines drink them very slowly / They took millions of years to form / The Sun makes a little more each year / Pumps will be taken away  
   **Answer:** They took millions of years to form. *Why:* The oil took millions of years to form and people are using much of it in a few hundred. It is not being made again at anything like the speed we burn it.
7. (mcq) An electric scooter makes no smoke. Why might some smoke still be made?  
   Options: Its tyres make smoke / Its battery is full of smoke / It makes no smoke anywhere / Coal may be burned at a power station to make its electricity  
   **Answer:** Coal may be burned at a power station to make its electricity. *Why:* The scooter itself gives out no exhaust. But in India most electricity still comes from burning coal, so some smoke has only moved to a chimney far away.
8. (mcq) What does an electric scooter have in place of a tank and an engine?  
   Options: A battery and a motor / A sail and a rope / A bigger exhaust pipe / A cylinder of CNG  
   **Answer:** A battery and a motor. *Why:* The battery stores electricity and the motor uses magnets to turn it into the spinning of the wheel. Nothing is burned inside the scooter.
9. (mcq) A driver waits 3 minutes at a closed railway gate. What saves fuel?  
   Options: Press the pedal now and then / Switch the engine off / Roll forward slowly / Keep it running so it will not stall  
   **Answer:** Switch the engine off. *Why:* A running engine burns fuel even when the vehicle goes nowhere. Switching it off for a wait of a minute or more saves fuel, and a modern engine starts again easily.
10. (mcq) A shop is 500 metres from home. Which way saves the most fuel?  
   Options: Take the scooter with a friend / Drive, and keep the engine running / Walk or cycle there / Go twice, once for each thing  
   **Answer:** Walk or cycle there. *Why:* Walking or cycling burns no fuel at all. Sharing helps, but the best saving is not to use an engine for a short trip.
11. (mcq) Which is more likely on a village road than at a city crossing?  
   Options: A metro train overhead / Hundreds of vehicles in five minutes / A long jam at six o'clock / A bullock cart or a tractor  
   **Answer:** A bullock cart or a tractor. *Why:* A village road has fewer vehicles and more cycles, walkers, tractors and animal carts. A metro, a jam and hundreds of vehicles belong to a city.
12. (mcq) A car carries 1 person and a bus carries 50. Which uses less fuel for each person?  
   Options: The bus, because 50 people share its fuel / The car, because it burns less / They use the same / The faster one  
   **Answer:** The bus, because 50 people share its fuel. *Why:* The bus burns more, but it shares the fuel between 50 people. One litre carries the bus 4 kilometres, which is 200 kilometres of travel altogether, and carries the car only 15.
13. (mcq) Why do we try each amount of air three times in the balloon test?  
   Options: To use up the balloons / One try could be a fluke / So that the string stretches / To make the balloon bigger  
   **Answer:** One try could be a fluke. *Why:* A single try could be lucky or unlucky. Three tries show if the result happens again, and the middle value is less likely to be a fluke.
14. (mcq) 2 breaths sent a balloon 120 centimetres and 4 breaths sent it 200. What can you say?  
   Options: 2 breaths went farther / Double the air gave exactly double the distance / More air went farther, but not double / Air made no difference  
   **Answer:** More air went farther, but not double. *Why:* Double 120 is 240, but the balloon went only 200. More air gave a longer trip, but some of the push was lost to rubbing and to pushing air aside.

## Applications
- At a petrol pump, read the signs on each pump: petrol, diesel or CNG. Watch which vehicles pull up to which pump, and notice that a scooter and a bus drink different fuels.
- Look at the underside of a green and yellow auto in Delhi, Mumbai or Pune, and find the tall cylinder at the back. That cylinder holds CNG, the squeezed natural gas that the engine burns.
- A school bus carries thirty children to school on one tank. Count the children in your street who go to the same school. That is how many vehicles one bus takes off the road.
- The dabbawalas of Mumbai carry about two lakh lunch boxes each day by cycle, handcart and train, and almost no fuel is burned on the way. Look at how they use legs and rails.
- A cycle rickshaw driver in Kolkata or Mathura pedals three passengers along. Watch his legs on a slope, and notice how a rider uses muscle power to move more than his own weight.
- At a railway gate in your town, count the engines that stay on while the train goes by. A driver who switches off for a minute or more saves fuel that would burn for nothing.
- A metro train such as the Chennai Metro or the Kochi Metro runs on electricity and carries hundreds of people at once. Next time you ride it, count the people in one coach and think of how many vehicles they would need on a road.

## Spotted around us
- On the way to school, look at the back of every scooter and car. Find a sticker or a plate that shows petrol, diesel, CNG or electric. Count how many of each you see.
- Wait at a signal and watch the exhaust pipes. Which vehicles are puffing smoke, and which are silent while waiting?
- Find a bullock cart, a tractor or a handcart near your home or on a trip. Ask what gives it its push, and look at the wheels and the axle.
- At the next petrol pump, find the number on the pump that shows the price for one litre. Ask how much one litre costs and how far it could take a scooter.
- Look at the tyres of a parked scooter and a parked bus. Which tyre looks soft and which looks hard? A soft tyre rubs more on the road.
- At a bus stop, look at how many people get on one bus. Ask how many cars it would take to carry the same people, if each car carried only the driver.
- On a walk, count the footpaths. Are they wide and clear for people, or narrow and blocked? Look at who is walking in the road instead.

## Open questions
- **What will most vehicles run on fifty years from now?** Batteries are ahead for scooters and cars. Hydrogen, plant fuels and new kinds of battery are all being tried for lorries, ships and aeroplanes. Which wins depends on discoveries and prices that nobody can predict.
- **Can a battery be made without scarce metals and without harming the people who mine them?** Batteries using sodium, which comes from common salt, have begun to be sold. They hold less energy for their weight. Whether they can replace lithium and cobalt widely is not yet known.
- **What is energy?** Physicists can calculate energy and have found that the total never changes. The physicist Richard Feynman wrote that physics has no knowledge of what energy is. It remains a number that works.
- **How much oil is left, and will it run out before people stop wanting it?** Estimates of oil in the ground keep changing as new methods reach more of it. Whether demand will fall first, because of cleaner vehicles and climate rules, is argued among geologists and economists.
- **How do ants keep their trails flowing without anyone in charge, and can human traffic copy them?** Experiments show that ants adjust their speed and stop entering a crowded trail. The exact rules each ant follows are still being worked out, and human drivers do not behave like ants.
- **Who first made a wheel, and where?** The oldest evidence appears in several regions within a few hundred years of about 3500 BCE. Wood rots, so the first wheels are almost certainly lost, and the dates are too close to tell apart.

## Questions to ask kids
- When you go to school, what gives your vehicle its push? What does that push come from before it was a fuel?
- What is the farthest you have ever walked? What did your body use to get you there?
- If a bullock could talk, what would she or he ask you for after a day of pulling a cart?
- Imagine all the vehicles on your street stopped for one day. What would you notice? What would still work?
- Which trip does your family make by an engine that you think you could make another way? What would change if you did?
- If you could invent a new way to move people, with no fuel and no smoke, what would it use?
- Why do you think a bus feels slow but uses less fuel for each person than a car?
- A pump gives us petrol in a minute. The oil took millions of years to make. What do you think about that?

## Connected maths
- A balloon rocket went 3 ruler lengths with 2 breaths and 5 ruler lengths with 4 breaths. If double the air gave double the distance, how far would 4 breaths have gone? How many ruler lengths short was it? **6 ruler lengths. It was 1 ruler length short.**. Double 3 is 3 + 3 = 6. The balloon went 5, and 6 − 5 = 1 ruler length less. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- One litre takes a bus 4 kilometres. The bus carries 50 people. How many kilometres of travel does one litre give for all of them? Do the same for a car with 4 people that goes 15 kilometres on a litre. Which is bigger? **Bus 200 kilometres, car 60 kilometres. The bus is bigger.**. The bus is 50 groups of 4 kilometres, which is 200. The car is 4 groups of 15 kilometres, which is 60. 200 is more than 60. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- A family goes to the market 3 times a week. Each trip is 2 kilometres there and 2 kilometres back. How many kilometres is that in a week? How many if they join two trips into one? **12 kilometres, and 8 kilometres with joined trips.**. Each trip is 2 + 2 = 4 kilometres. 3 trips are 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. With only 2 trips it is 4 + 4 = 8, which is 4 kilometres less. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L2`)
- From a window a child counts 12 scooters, 9 cars, 6 autos, 3 cycles and 2 buses in five minutes. How many of them have engines? How many more scooters than cycles were there? Which row is longest? **29 have engines. 9 more scooters than cycles. Scooters are the longest row.**. Add the ones with engines: 12 + 9 + 6 + 2 = 29. Cycles have no engine. 12 − 3 = 9. 12 is the biggest number. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- A car goes 15 kilometres on one litre. How many litres does it use to go 45 kilometres? How many to go 90 kilometres? **3 litres, and 6 litres.**. 45 is 3 groups of 15, so 3 litres. 90 is 6 groups of 15, so 6 litres. Doubling the distance doubles the litres. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L2`)

## Warm-up
- What does a cycle use to move, in place of a fuel tank? → The rider's muscles, which run on food.
- What is a fuel? → Something that gives out heat when it burns.
- Name three fuels that vehicles burn. → Petrol, diesel and CNG are three. Coal and wood are fuels too.
- What is the first thing a toy car needs before it moves? → A push or a pull.
- True or false: an electric scooter has an engine that burns petrol. → False. It has a battery and a motor.
- Where does the energy in your breakfast first come from? → The Sun. Plants catch sunlight and make the food.
- Which has more vehicles in five minutes, a city crossing or a village road? → A city crossing.
- Name one way to save petrol. → Walk or cycle short trips, share a ride, join two trips, or switch off the engine when waiting.
