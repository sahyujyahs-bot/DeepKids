# Particles: one picture that explains why solids hold, liquids pour and gases squash
`sci.states.particles.L4`

**Summary.** One drop of red food colour turns a cup of water pink. Pour a spoonful of that pink water into fresh water, and then again, and again, and the colour fades but does not stop at once. Press the plunger of a syringe full of air, and it squashes to less than half. Press one full of water, and it will not move. Your child dilutes colour through a row of cups and squeezes air, water and rice. Then they build one picture, the particle model, that explains what a solid, a liquid and a gas are, and why the air in a pressure cooker can make it whistle.

**Intro.** *Put one drop of red colour into a cup of water, and the whole cup turns pink. Now take a spoonful of that pink water and mix it into a fresh cup. Do it again, and again. How many cups can one single drop colour before the pink is gone?* The answer is surprising, and it tells us something big. A drop is made of far more tiny pieces than anyone can count, and those pieces spread into every cup. In this unit you test how far one drop goes, and you squeeze air, water and rice in a syringe. You find out why air squashes and water does not, and why a steel spoon holds its shape while the steam in a cooker pushes on the lid. One picture explains all of it: everything is made of particles, and what a solid, a liquid and a gas do depends on how close the particles are and how they move.

## Terms
- **Material**: A material is what an object is made of. Many different objects can be made of the same material. *e.g.* A katori and a ladle can both be made of steel. A spoon can be made of wood.
- **Substance**: A substance is one particular kind of stuff. All of it is made of the same kind of particle. *e.g.* Water, salt, sugar and iron are substances.
- **Mixture**: A mixture is two or more substances mixed together, with each keeping its own particles. You can often separate them again. *e.g.* Air, sambar and sugar water are mixtures.
- **Particle**: A particle is a very tiny piece that a substance is made of. It is far too small to see, even with a magnifying glass. *e.g.* A drop of water holds more than a thousand million million million water particles.
- **Atom**: An atom is one of the tiny building blocks that everything is made of. There are about a hundred different kinds. *e.g.* Iron is made of iron atoms. Water has hydrogen atoms and oxygen atoms.
- **Molecule**: A molecule is a small group of atoms joined together. Many substances are made of molecules. *e.g.* A water molecule has three atoms joined: two of hydrogen and one of oxygen.
- **State of matter**: The state of a substance is whether it is a solid, a liquid or a gas. The state depends on how its particles are arranged and moving. *e.g.* Ice, water and steam are three states of the same substance.
- **Solid**: A solid keeps its own shape and its own size. Its particles are close together and held in place. *e.g.* A steel spoon stays a spoon shape in a drawer or in a cup.
- **Liquid**: A liquid keeps its size but takes the shape of its container. Its particles are close together but slide past each other. *e.g.* Water takes the shape of a tumbler, then the shape of a bowl.
- **Gas**: A gas has no shape or size of its own. It spreads to fill the whole space it is in. Its particles are far apart and fly about. *e.g.* The steam in a kettle fills the kettle. The smell of cooking fills the house.
- **Compress**: To compress something is to squeeze it into a smaller space. *e.g.* You can compress the air in a bicycle pump. You cannot compress the water in a bottle.
- **Gas pressure**: Gas pressure is the push that a gas makes on the walls of its container. It comes from particles hitting the walls. *e.g.* The air inside a football pushes outwards on the leather and keeps it round.
- **Diffusion**: Diffusion is the slow spreading of particles through a gas or a liquid, just by their own movement, with no stirring. *e.g.* The smell of an agarbatti slowly reaches the far corner of the room.
- **Dilute**: To dilute a coloured or strong liquid is to add more water, so there is less of the strong stuff in each spoonful. *e.g.* Adding water to a thick squash makes it weaker and paler.
- **Gap**: A gap is the empty space between particles. Nothing at all is in it. *e.g.* The gaps between air particles are why air can be squashed.
- **Model**: A model is a picture, drawing or object that helps us think about something we cannot see. A model is useful, but it is never exactly the real thing. *e.g.* A tray of rice can be a model of particles in a solid, a liquid or a gas.
- **Evidence**: Evidence is something you see, measure or find out that shows an idea is right or wrong. *e.g.* The air in a syringe squashed to half, and that is evidence that there is space between air particles.

## Explanations
### Materials, substances and mixtures · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is what an object is made of. A substance is one kind of stuff, made of one kind of particle. A mixture has two or more substances.

Steel is a material. Salt is a substance, because every salt particle is the same. Air is a mixture of gases, mostly nitrogen with about one fifth oxygen and small amounts of other gases. Sambar is a mixture of water, dal, tamarind and spices. In a mixture, the substances keep their own particles, and often you can get them apart again. In a substance, you cannot get it apart by simple means such as sieving or letting it settle.
- e.g. Sea water is a mixture of water and salt. The salt can be got back by letting the water dry in the sun.
- e.g. Brass is a mixture of copper and zinc, melted together.
- e.g. Distilled water, which is used in car batteries, is very nearly a pure substance.
### Solids: particles packed close and held in place · *defined*
**Definition:** In a solid, the particles touch their neighbours and are held in place by pulls between them. They jiggle on the spot, but they do not move around.

The pulls between particles are strong in a solid. Each particle is held by its neighbours, like a person in a tightly packed row of seats. The particles can shake a little, but they cannot swap places. That is why a solid keeps its shape and its size. In many solids, such as salt and ice, the particles sit in neat repeating rows. Heating a solid makes the particles shake harder.
- e.g. A steel katori keeps its shape when you carry it from the shelf to the table.
- e.g. An ice cube keeps its shape until it warms up.
- e.g. Rock salt grains have flat faces because their particles are stacked in neat rows.
### Liquids: particles close together but sliding · *defined*
**Definition:** In a liquid, the particles touch their neighbours but are not held in place. They slide past each other all the time.

The particles in a liquid are about as close as in a solid, but the pulls between them are weaker. They move around and swap places. That is why a liquid flows and takes the shape of its container. The particles still touch, so a liquid keeps its size. Pour a litre of water into any shape of jug, and it is still a litre.
- e.g. Water poured from a bottle into a tumbler takes the shape of the tumbler.
- e.g. A cup of milk is still a cup of milk if you pour it into a wide plate.
- e.g. Honey slides slowly because its particles stick to each other more.
### Gases: particles far apart and flying · *defined*
**Definition:** In a gas, the particles are very far apart compared with their size. They fly about in straight lines until they hit something.

Gas particles hardly pull on each other at all. Each one flies in a straight line until it bumps into another particle or a wall, and then it bounces off. In air at room temperature, the particles travel about five hundred metres every second. That is faster than the speed of sound, which is about 340 metres a second. A gas has no size of its own, so it spreads out into every corner of whatever holds it. Between the particles there is only empty space.
- e.g. The steam from a kettle spreads out until you cannot see it.
- e.g. The smell of tadka spreads from the kitchen to the bedroom.
- e.g. A balloon is round because gas particles push on its skin from the inside, in all directions.
### Empty space between particles · *defined*
**Definition:** Between the particles of every substance there is empty space. In a gas there is a lot of it. In a liquid or a solid there is much less.

When you squeeze air in a syringe, it squashes to half, because the particles move closer together into the empty space. A liquid or a solid cannot be squeezed much, because their particles are already touching. The same idea explains something odd. Pour 100 millilitres of fine mustard seeds into 100 millilitres of big chana dal, and the total is much less than 200. The small seeds fall into the gaps between the big dal. Particles of different sizes fit into each other's gaps in the same way.
- e.g. Salt dissolved in a full glass of water hardly changes the level, because the salt particles fit between the water particles.
- e.g. A balloon full of air can be squeezed smaller, but a balloon full of water cannot.
- e.g. Bicycle tyres can be pumped up, because more and more air particles can be pushed into the same space.
### One substance, three states · *defined*
**Definition:** A solid, a liquid and a gas can be made of exactly the same particles. Ice, water and steam are all water. What changes is how fast the particles move and how strongly they hold each other.

Heat a solid, and its particles shake harder. At some warmth they break out of their places, and the solid melts into a liquid. Heat the liquid more, and the fastest particles escape and fly off as a gas. Nothing new is made. The water particles in steam are the same as in ice. Each is a molecule of two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Cool the steam on a cold steel plate, and the particles slow down and cling together as drops of water again.
- e.g. A candle's wax melts and runs, and it is still wax when it sets.
- e.g. Steam on a cold plate turns back to water drops.
- e.g. Ghee melts in a pan and sets again as it cools.
### Gas pressure: the push of particles on walls · *defined*
**Definition:** Gas pressure is the push of a gas on a surface. It is made by countless particles hitting the surface and bouncing off.

One particle hitting a wall makes a push far too small to feel. But in a football there are so many particles, hitting so often, that together the push is steady and strong. The air outside pushes on you with about the weight of a one-kilogram bag on every square centimetre. You do not notice it, because the same push is inside your body too. If you heat a gas in a sealed container, its particles move faster, hit the walls harder and more often, and the pressure goes up. A pressure cooker uses this. The sealed steam pushes at about twice the pressure of the outside air.
- e.g. A bicycle tyre is hard because the air inside pushes outwards at about three times the push of the outside air.
- e.g. A balloon left in a hot car can burst, because the hot air inside pushes harder.
- e.g. A cooker's whistle lifts when the steam inside pushes up on the weight.
### Why smells take minutes, not seconds · *defined*
**Definition:** Gas particles fly very fast, but they bump into other air particles billions of times every second. So a smell wanders slowly across a room, spreading by diffusion.

A smell particle leaving an agarbatti is moving at hundreds of metres a second. But it travels less than a ten-thousandth of a millimetre before it collides with an air particle and is knocked in a new direction. It zigzags. After billions of knocks, it has gone only a little way from where it began. Moving air from a fan or an open window carries it far faster than diffusion alone. In a liquid, particles are even closer, so diffusion is far slower still, which is why colour in unstirred water takes hours.
- e.g. A smell reaches the next room mostly because air currents carry it.
- e.g. Colour in unstirred water takes hours to spread through a glass.
- e.g. Warm air spreads smells faster, because its particles move faster.
### How many particles are in a drop? · *defined*
**Definition:** A drop of water holds more than a thousand million million million water particles. That is a 1 followed by 21 zeros. A drop of food colour holds a huge number of colour particles too.

A water particle, one molecule, is about three ten-millionths of a millimetre across. Such sizes are almost impossible to imagine. So scientists use comparisons. If every person alive today counted one particle each second, it would take them more than six thousand years to count the particles in one drop of water. This is why one drop of colour can colour a whole cup and can still be there, in tiny amounts, in a cup that looks clear. A tiny share of a huge number is still a huge number.
- e.g. Even the faintest pink cup still holds millions of colour particles in each teaspoon.
- e.g. A teaspoon of sea water holds many millions of millions of salt particles.
- e.g. A single breath holds more air particles than there are grains of sand in all the beaches on Earth, by some estimates.
### Inside a particle: atoms are mostly empty space · *defined*
**Definition:** Every particle is made of one or more atoms, and an atom is mostly empty space. The middle of the atom, the nucleus, is about a hundred thousand times smaller than the whole atom.

Imagine an atom the size of a big cricket ground. The nucleus in the middle would be about the size of a mustard seed. Around it, tiny particles called electrons zip about. So why does a table feel solid, if its atoms are nearly empty? The electrons of the atoms in your hand push on the electrons of the atoms in the table. This electric push is strong, and it stops them getting closer. What we feel as hardness is this push between atoms.
- e.g. A water molecule has three atoms: two of hydrogen and one of oxygen.
- e.g. A sugar molecule has 45 atoms.
- e.g. A hammer feels hard because the atoms in it push strongly on each other.
### Grains and powders: a solid that flows · *defined*
**Definition:** A heap of rice or sand can pour like a liquid, but each grain is a solid. The particles of a substance are far smaller than the grains.

When you pour rice, the heap flows and takes the shape of the bowl. This makes it look like a liquid. But each grain keeps its own shape, and there is air between the grains. The grains are solid because the tiny particles inside each grain are locked in place. Pouring rice is a nice picture of a liquid, but it is not what a liquid is. A tray of rice is a useful model, but it is not an exact one.
- e.g. Sand runs through an hourglass like a liquid.
- e.g. Atta pours from a tin but a lump of atta dough does not.
- e.g. A sack of cement powder spills across the floor, but each tiny grain is a solid.
### A model: useful, and always a little wrong · *idea*
The particle model is a picture that explains a lot: why things have states, why gases squash, why smells spread. But no model is exactly right. We draw particles as tiny balls, yet real atoms are fuzzy and have no sharp edge. Physicists now describe the smallest things as ripples in fields, not as tiny balls. Even so, the particle model gives correct answers to most questions about solids, liquids and gases, which is why scientists use it. A good scientist keeps asking where a model stops working.
- e.g. A drawing of a particle as a ball is a model.
- e.g. A tray of rice shaken to show a gas is a model.
- e.g. Scientists use computer models of particles to design new materials.

## Mostly agreed
- Everything is made of particles too small to see, and the three states differ in how the particles are arranged and move. *How we know:* The model predicts how gases squash, how they push, how fast smells spread and how solids melt, and the measurements match.
- In a gas, the particles are far apart with empty space between them. That is why gases can be squashed. *How we know:* Air squashes to a fraction of its size under pressure, and the pressure and volume follow a regular rule that the particle model predicts.
- Gas pressure is made by particles hitting the walls, and a hotter gas pushes harder. *How we know:* Measurements of gases at different warmths match the numbers worked out from fast-moving particles.
- Ice, water and steam are the same substance, and only the arrangement and motion of the particles differ. *How we know:* Weighing before and after melting or boiling in closed containers gives the same mass, and cooling steam gives back water.
- Atoms are mostly empty space, with a tiny nucleus in the middle. *How we know:* In the early 1900s, experiments aimed fast particles at thin gold foil, and nearly all went straight through.
- Particles in a solid are never still. They jiggle on the spot, more as the solid is warmed. *How we know:* Heat makes solids expand, and X-ray and neutron measurements show the jiggle of the particles.

## How we found out
**Daniel Bernoulli, a Swiss scientist, and John James Waterston, a Scottish scientist, followed by Rudolf Clausius and James Clerk Maxwell, 1738 (Bernoulli), 1845 (Waterston), the 1850s and 1860s (Clausius and Maxwell).** For a long time, many scientists pictured a gas as particles sitting still, each pushing its neighbours away, like people in a crowd holding out their arms. This picture gave the right rule for squashing a gas, so most people accepted it. In 1738, a Swiss scientist named Daniel Bernoulli wrote that a gas is particles flying about and hitting the walls, and that this makes the pressure. Heat, he said, makes them fly faster. Almost nobody paid attention. In 1845, a Scottish scientist named John James Waterston, who was working in Bombay at the time, sent a paper on the same idea to the Royal Society in London. It was reportedly judged to be nonsense, and the paper lay unread in the archives. In the 1850s and 1860s, Rudolf Clausius and James Clerk Maxwell worked out the maths in full, and it matched measurements. Lord Rayleigh found Waterston's paper in the archives in 1891, and it was published in 1892.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought the particles of a gas sit in fixed places and push each other away. The flying-particle picture explains why heat raises the pressure and why gases spread into each other, which the fixed-place picture cannot.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Particles are tiny hard balls with a clear edge.** (Most school drawings and many textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: Balls explain how particles pack together and slide past each other.  
  Against: Real atoms have no sharp edge. They are better described as fuzzy clouds, and physicists now describe the smallest parts as ripples in fields. Balls are a useful picture but not the truth.
- **Air is nothing. A room with no furniture is empty.** (Many young children, and many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: You cannot see air, and you can walk through it easily.  
  Against: A balloon blown up with air weighs a little more than an empty one. Air squashes in a syringe, pushes on a tyre and carries sound. It is real stuff, with particles far apart.
- **Between the particles of air there is more air, or dust, or tiny invisible stuff.** (A common idea among students; *rejected*)  
  For: It is hard to imagine nothing at all.  
  Against: If something filled the gaps, air could not be squashed, because there would be nothing for the particles to move into. The rule for how the volume of a gas changes with pressure only works if the gaps between the particles are empty.
- **When a substance is heated, its particles get bigger.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Things expand when heated, so the particles must grow.  
  Against: The particles stay the same size. They move faster and push each other further apart, so the whole thing takes up more room.
- **Gas particles sit in fixed places and push each other away, like people in a crowd holding out their arms.** (Many scientists in the 1700s, following Isaac Newton's maths; *rejected*)  
  For: This picture also gives the right rule for how gas volume changes with pressure.  
  Against: It cannot explain why a hot gas pushes harder, or why gases spread into each other. In 1738, Daniel Bernoulli showed that fast-flying particles explain all of these.
- **The particles in a solid do not move at all.** (Some students; *rejected*)  
  For: A solid does not seem to move.  
  Against: The particles jiggle on the spot. Warm a solid and they jiggle more, until the solid melts. Solids that look still are full of movement.
- **A liquid cannot be squeezed at all, not even a tiny bit.** (Many textbooks and many scientists before the 1760s; *rejected*)  
  For: In a syringe, water seems not to move, no matter how hard you press.  
  Against: Very careful experiments show water can be squeezed a very small amount, less than one part in ten thousand for each extra push of one atmosphere. The particles are touching but not perfectly rigid.

## Roots
We have squeezed air and water, and watched one drop of colour spread through cup after cup. Now we ask what it means that everything has a lot of room inside it, and what that says about us.

**Deep truth.** In the syringe, the air squashed to under half its size, and the water hardly moved. Both are made of particles. The difference is the empty space between them. What looks like 'full' or 'nothing' is particles with less room or more room.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a solid is full of stuff and a gas is nothing. That is wrong for the gas, because air is stuff too. It is also wrong for the solid, because it has gaps inside.  
*Evidence:* In the squeeze test, the air in the syringe squashed to about half, the water hardly moved, and the rice moved a little as the grains settled. In the jar test, the mustard seeds slid into the gaps between the dal and filled the jar. If your numbers differ, write them down as they are.

- *who am i:* More than half of your weight is water. Your lungs fill with a gas, and the empty space between air particles is why you can breathe in and out.
- *what is life:* A bee finds a flower by smell particles that drift through the air from far away. Many living beings find food, friends and danger by catching particles in the air or the water.
- *what is this world:* A rock, the sea, the air and the steam from a kettle are made of the same kinds of particles. They differ in how much room the particles have, and how fast they move.

Solid, liquid and gas are not three different kinds of stuff. They are three ways the same particles can behave, depending on how much room they have and how fast they move. Heating or cooling moves a substance from one way to another. A spoon and the steam from a kettle are not as far apart as they look.

- **Democritus of Abdera, as reported by Aristotle in the Metaphysics, Book 1:** Democritus, an ancient Greek thinker, taught that everything is made of atoms and empty space. Atoms cannot be cut further. Different things come from different atoms joined in different ways. (Your syringe test showed the empty space. Air squashed because its particles moved into the room between them.)
- **The Vaisheshika school, traditionally linked to the teacher Kanada:** The Vaisheshika school of India taught that the world is made of tiny indivisible parts called paramanu, and that different things come from them joining in different ways. Scholars do not agree on the date, and some place its first ideas long before the common era. (Steel, water and steam are made of tiny parts joined and moving in different ways. That is the idea you tested with cups and syringes.)
- Science (settled): Gas pressure is made by particles hitting the walls of the container, and a hotter gas pushes harder.
- Science (settled): Atoms are mostly empty space around a tiny nucleus, and the push of electrons between atoms is what makes a solid feel hard.
- Science (open): Physicists describe the smallest things as ripples in fields, not as tiny balls, and they do not agree on what, if anything, lies beneath.
- Other view, Some philosophers of science: The particle picture is a tool for predicting what happens, and we should not say that particles are real in the way a chair is real. A model is good if it predicts well. Whether the particles are really out there is a separate question that experiments may never settle.
- Practice, Three crowds in one breath (3 min): Stand with your feet apart. First, be a solid. Hold your body stiff and shake only a little. Breathe in slowly. Now be a liquid. Sway and let your arms flow. Breathe out slowly. Now be a gas. Spread your arms wide and take up all the room you can. Notice how much space each one needed.
- Ask: If a solid, a liquid and a gas can be the same particles, what makes a spoon a spoon and steam steam?
- Ask: Nobody can see the empty space in air. Is it real? How do you know?
- Ask: Your hand cannot pass through a table, though both are mostly empty space. What does that tell you about what 'solid' means?

- *big:* Nobody has ever seen a gas particle with their eyes. How can we be sure that they are there? (hint: Think about what you tested in this unit, and what it would mean if there were no particles.)

## Story: The Cooker That Did Not Whistle
*A made-up story. Lalitha, her Paati and her uncle are imagined. The science is real. A pressure cooker has a weight on a vent that lifts and whistles when the steam pushes hard enough, and a lid that will not open while there is pressure inside. Always let a cooker cool, and never force the lid.*

On Sundays in Mysuru, the whole family sat down to lunch at one o'clock. This Sunday, Lalitha's Paati had to go to the temple early. She gave Lalitha a big job.

'The dal and rice are in the cooker,' said Paati. 'Put it on the flame. Count the whistles. After three whistles, turn off the gas. Do not touch anything else. I will be back before it is ready.'

Lalitha was ten, and she had counted whistles many times. She lit the stove and sat on a stool, with her chin on her hands. The kitchen smelled of jasmine from the pooja shelf and of fresh coffee.

In a few minutes the cooker began to hiss. The steel weight on top rattled. Pssshh! The first whistle. Lalitha smiled and held up one finger. Pssshh! The second. She held up two.

Then she waited for the third. And she waited.

Nothing came. The cooker was still hissing, but the weight on the top was not moving at all. Lalitha stood up and looked closer. A thin silver thread of steam was slipping out from the side of the lid, with a high thin sound, like a kettle far away.

Her uncle Raghu walked in. He was a big, cheerful man, and he had come from Bengaluru for lunch. He was also very hungry. 'Ah, the cooker,' he said. 'Is lunch ready? Let me open it and look.' He reached for the lid handle with a kitchen cloth.

'Please don't, Mama,' said Lalitha. 'Turn the lid and see. It will not move.'

He tried. He pulled and twisted. The lid did not move. 'Stuck,' said Raghu. 'These old cookers. Give me a spoon, I will force it. Or I will put the whole thing under the tap.'

Lalitha switched off the gas. Then she did something she was proud of for a long time. She stood very still, and she thought about the cooker the way her teacher had taught her to think about everything. She pictured the steam as a crowd of tiny particles, too small to see, flying about at hundreds of metres a second.

'Mama, listen,' she said. 'Inside there is water, and the flame heated it until a lot of it turned into steam. Steam is a gas. Its particles fly in all directions and hit the walls and the lid. The lid is shut, so they have nowhere to go. They are pushing on the lid right now, much harder than the air outside pushes. That is why it will not open.'

Raghu's hand stopped on the handle. 'And the whistle?'

'The weight is supposed to lift when the push gets big enough,' said Lalitha. 'Then steam escapes, and that is the whistle. This one did not lift. Something is blocking the little pipe under it. So the push has built up, and the steam is finding a way out at the side. If you force the lid, the steam will come out all at once.'

Raghu took his hand off the lid. He stepped back, and so did Lalitha. They stood by the door, and they waited.

Five minutes passed. The hiss grew softer. 'The flame is off,' said Lalitha, 'so the steam cools. Its particles slow down. They hit the lid less hard and less often. When the push inside is the same as the push outside, the lid will open easily.'

After fifteen minutes the hiss had stopped. Lalitha put a hand near the lid, not on it. It was only warm. 'Now,' she said. Raghu turned the handle. The lid slid open with a soft sigh, and a cloud of steam rose and spread and was gone.

The dal was perfectly cooked. Lalitha lifted the weight and looked into the little pipe with a torch. A tiny skin of dal had dried inside it, like a plug. She cleaned it with a toothpick while her uncle held the torch.

When Paati came back from the temple, Raghu told her everything. 'Your granddaughter stopped me from opening a cooker full of steam,' he said. 'She explained it better than my physics teacher ever did.'

Paati looked at the pipe, at the toothpick, and at Lalitha. 'Next time,' she said, 'we will clean the pipe before we start. But I am glad you counted the whistles, and glad you noticed when the third did not come.'

That afternoon, over the best dal he had ever eaten, Raghu asked Lalitha to explain it all again, from the beginning.

**For the parent:** Steam is a gas, made of fast-flying particles. In a sealed cooker they hit the walls and the lid, and that push is pressure. Cooling slows the particles, so the push falls and the lid opens.

- Ask: Why would the lid not open while the cooker was hot and sealed?
- Ask: Why did Lalitha switch off the flame and wait, instead of opening the cooker?
- Ask: What happens to the steam particles as the cooker cools, and why does that help the lid open?
- Ask: How could Lalitha tell that the weight was not lifting, and what did she think was wrong?
- Ask: Lalitha explained a cooker to her uncle by picturing particles. Why is a picture of invisible particles useful here?

## Experiments
### e1 · How Far Does One Drop Go? Cup after cup of colour (35 min)
- **question:** One drop of food colour turns a cup of water pink. If you keep mixing a spoonful of that pink water into a fresh cup of water, how many cups can the colour reach before you can no longer see it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many cups will still show some colour? Will a second colour, such as blue, go further or less far than red? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every cup has the same amount of water, ten teaspoons in all. Every spoonful passed on is exactly one level teaspoon, and you stir the same way each time. The cups sit on the same white paper in the same light. Only the colour changes between the red row and the blue row, so any difference comes from the colour.
- **safety:** Food colour stains clothes, skin and kitchen surfaces. Wear old clothes and wipe spills at once. Keep it away from the eyes. The coloured water is only for looking at. Do not drink it. Some children react to food dyes, so a child who does should only watch, and wear gloves or wash hands afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the red row. Then draw the row of cups and colour each cup as dark as you saw it. Do the same for the blue row.
- **results table:** Cup | Colour in this cup compared with cup 1 | Can you see colour? (yes, faint or no) | Red or blue
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the cups, the colour and the spoon. Ask for a guess about how many cups will show colour, and for a reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will put one drop of colour in a cup of water. Then we will pass one spoonful to the next cup, again and again. How many cups will have colour? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* One drop. Six cups. How many will show colour? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: How many cups do you think will show colour, and why? (good: Only two, because the drop is so small.; All six, because colour never goes away.; Three or four, because the colour gets thinner each time.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children guess two or three. Write the guess at the top of the table.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Number the cups 1 to 6. 2. Stand them on the white paper in a row. 3. Put 10 teaspoons of water in cup 1. 4. Put 9 teaspoons in each of the other cups.  
  *Parent says:* Cup 1 gets ten spoons of water. Every other cup gets nine. We will count the spoons carefully, because we want each cup to hold the same amount.  
  *Child mode:* Number the cups. Cup 1 gets 10 spoons of water. The rest get 9.
  - Note: Measure with level teaspoons, not heaped ones. The nine spoons plus the one spoonful that comes in make ten, so every cup holds the same amount in the end.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the dropper above cup 1. 2. Let one drop fall in. 3. Stir with the second spoon for 10 seconds. 4. Look at the colour from above.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the dropper over cup 1 and let one drop fall. Stir for ten seconds. What do you see? How much of the cup has the colour reached?  
  *Child mode:* Let one drop fall into cup 1. Stir. What do you see?
  - Ask: What do you see after you stir? What do you wonder? (good: The whole cup is pink.; One drop made all this colour.; Where did the colour go?)
  - Note: Cup 1 should be a strong pink or red. If it looks pale, add one more drop and write 2 drops in the table.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Use the teaspoon to take one level teaspoon from cup 1. 2. Stir it into cup 2 for 10 seconds. 3. Rinse the spoon. 4. Take one level teaspoon from cup 2 and stir it into cup 3. 5. Keep going to cup 6.  
  *Parent says:* Take one level spoonful from cup 1 into cup 2. Stir. Rinse the spoon. Now one spoonful from cup 2 into cup 3. Keep going, all the way to cup 6.  
  *Child mode:* Move one spoonful from each cup to the next. Stir. Rinse the spoon.
  - Note: The colour in each cup is one tenth of the colour in the cup before. Cup 3 holds one hundredth of cup 1, cup 4 one thousandth, and so on. A rinsed spoon keeps the test fair.
  - Wait: Take your time and rinse the spoon each time, or the colours will mix.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the row from above, against the white paper. 2. For each cup, write yes, faint or no in the table. 3. Put a cup of plain water next to cup 6 and compare. 4. Look from the side too.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the row from above, then from the side. Which cups still have colour? Put plain water next to the last cup. Can you tell them apart?  
  *Child mode:* Look at every cup. Write yes, faint or no. Compare the last cup with plain water.
  - Note: Typically the colour fades out somewhere between cup 3 and cup 5 on white paper, depending on how strong your food colour is, so there is no right number. If the colour has gone by cup 2 or 3, begin again with 3 drops in cup 1 and note it in the table. If cup 6 is still quite bright, add cups 7 and 8. With 2 to 4 children, each runs one row (one colour each) and then you compare the rows; it is a good way to find which colour goes furthest. One child can be the spoon-carrier and one the stirrer, and swap roles for the second row. Hold the cups up to a window or white paper to judge.
  - Check: Cup 6 looks clear. Is there still colour in it? → **Yes, a tiny amount, spread so thinly you cannot see it**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour out the red cups and rinse them. 2. Do the same test with the second colour, from cup 1 to cup 6. 3. Write what you see in a second row of the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now do exactly the same with the blue colour. Does it go further than the red, or less far? Write down what you see.  
  *Child mode:* Repeat with the second colour. Does it go further or less far?
  - Note: Different colours are made from different dyes, and some are stronger. So one colour often stays visible for one more cup than the other.
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why did it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: one drop holds so many colour particles that they spread into many cups. What I saw: the pink faded cup by cup, and the last cup looked clear, perhaps a tiny bit different from plain water. Why: each spoonful carries a tenth of the colour, so there is less and less, but never exactly none.
  - Think: If the colour in cup 6 is still there, how could you prove it without looking?
### e2 · The Squeeze Test: can air, water and a water-and-bubble mix be squashed? (35 min)
- **question:** A syringe is full of air, and another is full of water. You seal the end and push the plunger as hard as you can. Which one squashes, and by how much?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How far will the plunger go in when it is full of air, and when it is full of water? What will happen if the water has a small bubble of air in it? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** You use the same syringe, the same starting amount of 10 millilitres, the same finger seal and the same push each time. You try each test three times and use the middle number. Only what is inside the syringe changes.
- **safety:** Use only a syringe with no needle. Never point a syringe at a face or an eye. Keep the small parts away from children under five. Hold the plunger firmly, because it can spring back. Work over a tray or in the kitchen sink, because the seal may leak and squirt water. Do not use a syringe that has been used for medicine unless it is washed well.
- **record:** Fill in the table with the numbers. Then draw a picture of the air in the syringe before and after you push, showing the particles as dots.
- **results table:** What is inside the syringe | Start amount (millilitres) | Amount after pushing (millilitres) | How much it squashed (millilitres)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the syringe. Pull the plunger out and in a few times. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This is a syringe with no needle. We will seal the end and push the plunger. What will happen with air inside? With water inside? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Seal the syringe. Push. What happens with air? With water? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen when you push the plunger, and why? (good: The air will squash, because it is light.; Neither will squash, because the end is blocked.; The water will squash more, because it is wet.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is good. Write the guesses at the top of the table.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pull the plunger back to the 10 millilitre mark. 2. Press your fingertip tight over the end. 3. Push the plunger a little and let go. Does it spring back?  
  *Parent says:* Pull the plunger back to ten millilitres. Press your finger tight over the end. Push a little and let go. Does the plunger spring back? If it does, your seal is good.  
  *Child mode:* Pull to 10. Seal the end with a finger. Push a little. Does it spring back?
  - Note: If it does not spring back, air is leaking. Press the finger harder, or use a blob of clay over the tip. If the plunger sticks, rub a drop of cooking oil on the rubber.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pull the plunger back to 10 millilitres again. 2. Seal the end. 3. Push the plunger in as far as you can and hold for 3 seconds. 4. Read the mark. 5. Do this three times.  
  *Parent says:* Seal the end and push the plunger in as far as you can. Hold for three seconds. Read the mark. Do it three times. Write each number down.  
  *Child mode:* Seal. Push as hard as you can. Read the mark. Do it three times.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the plunger when you let go? (good: It springs back.; It pushes my thumb out.; It feels like pushing on a spring.)
  - Note: Air usually squashes to between 3 and 6 millilitres, depending on how hard you push and how well your finger seals; a weak seal gives a smaller squash, so compare your three tries and use the middle number. The plunger springs back when you let go. With 2 to 4 children there is one syringe, so give roles: one pushes, one seals the tip, one reads the mark, one writes. Swap roles after each of the three tries so everyone pushes once, and note that a stronger child will squash it more, which is why the same person should push for all three tests in the table.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill a cup with water. 2. Dip the syringe tip in and pull the plunger back to 10 millilitres to draw in water. 3. Hold the tip up, tap out the bubbles, and push the plunger up until there is no air. 4. Seal the end. 5. Push as hard as you can.  
  *Parent says:* Draw up ten millilitres of water. Tap out the bubbles. Seal the end with your finger, and push as hard as you can. What do you see? Do it three times.  
  *Child mode:* Fill with water. Tap out the bubbles. Seal. Push as hard as you can.
  - Note: The plunger may move a tiny amount or not at all, and a finger seal may leak a drop. Write 0 or about 0. If water squirts out the sides, press the tip into a rubber eraser or clay instead of a finger. Tap out every bubble first, or you will be measuring the bubble.
  - Check: The plunger hardly moved when the syringe was full of water. Why? → **The water particles are touching, so there is no empty space to squash them into**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw up 9 millilitres of water. 2. Pull the plunger back to 10 millilitres, to let in a small bubble of air. 3. Seal the end. 4. Push as hard as you can and read the mark. 5. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Now put a small bubble of air in with the water. Seal the end and push. How far does the plunger go this time? What squashed, the water or the bubble?  
  *Child mode:* Water plus a small bubble. Seal. Push. What squashed?
  - Note: The mix should squash by less than the size of the bubble, because only the bubble can squash. This shows that the water was not squashing. The bubble was.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at your three results. 2. Fill in the 'How much it squashed' column. 3. Talk about which squashed and which did not.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the numbers. Which squashed the most? Which squashed the least? What do the particles in each one have to do?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the table. Which squashed most? Which squashed least?
  - Note: Air squashes a lot, water almost not at all, and water with a bubble squashes by about the bubble's size.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened. Then ask them to draw the air particles before and after pushing.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think? What did you see? Why did it happen? Then draw the air particles before and after.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think, what you saw, and why. Then draw the air particles.
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: What I think: air has empty space between its particles. What I saw: the air squashed to about half and sprang back, and the water did not move. Why: air particles are far apart, so they can be pushed closer. Water particles already touch. In the drawing, the particles should be the same size before and after, just closer together after.
  - Think: If you kept squeezing the air in the syringe harder and harder, what do you think would stop you at last?

### Three Crowds of Particles (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which sentence describes the particles in a solid best?  
   Options: They are far apart and fly about / They touch their neighbours and are held in place, jiggling on the spot / They slide past each other  
   **Answer:** They touch their neighbours and are held in place, jiggling on the spot. *Why:* In a solid the particles are close and held in place by pulls between them. Picking the first describes a gas. Picking the last describes a liquid.
2. (mcq) Neha seals the end of a syringe full of air and pushes the plunger. It goes in about half way. What does this tell you about air?  
   Options: Air is made of nothing / There is empty space between the air particles / Air particles are soft  
   **Answer:** There is empty space between the air particles. *Why:* The particles moved closer together into the empty space between them. Air is made of particles, so it is not nothing. The particles do not get squashed, they just get closer.
3. (mcq) Why does the steam from a kettle spread out until you cannot see it?  
   Options: Gas particles fly about and spread into all the space they can reach / Steam is lighter than the kitchen / Steam particles melt away  
   **Answer:** Gas particles fly about and spread into all the space they can reach. *Why:* Gas particles are free to fly in every direction, so a gas spreads out to fill the space it is in. Particles do not melt away.
4. (mcq) Ice, water and steam are three states of one substance. What is the same in all three?  
   Options: The distance between the particles / The kind of particle / How fast the particles move  
   **Answer:** The kind of particle. *Why:* All three are made of the same water particles. What changes is the distance between them and how fast they move.
5. (mcq) Why does a football feel hard when it is pumped up?  
   Options: Nothing is hitting the inside wall / Air particles hit the inside wall very often and push outwards / The air has turned into a solid  
   **Answer:** Air particles hit the inside wall very often and push outwards. *Why:* A huge number of air particles hit the inside wall every second. Together they push outwards, and that makes the ball hard. The air is still a gas.
6. (truefalse) The particles in a steel spoon are completely still.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The particles in a solid jiggle on the spot all the time. They just do not swap places.
7. (truefalse) The water particles in ice are a different kind from the water particles in steam.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. They are exactly the same particles. Only how close they are and how fast they move is different.
8. (fill) A very tiny piece that a substance is made of, far too small to see, is a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['particle']. *Why:* Everything is made of particles, and they are far too small to see with the eyes alone.
9. (fill) The push of a gas on the walls of its container is called gas ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pressure']. *Why:* Gas pressure comes from countless particles hitting the walls and bouncing off.
10. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Material → what an object is made of; Substance → one kind of stuff, made of one kind of particle; Mixture → two or more substances mixed together; Gap → empty space between particles. *Why:* These four words are used all through the unit. A material can be a substance or a mixture.
11. (sort) Sort these things by what state they are in at room temperature.  
   **Answer:** a steel katori → Solid; milk → Liquid; the oxygen you breathe → Gas; a wooden spoon → Solid; coconut oil on a warm day → Liquid; the air inside a balloon → Gas. *Why:* A solid keeps its shape, a liquid takes the shape of its container, and a gas spreads out to fill it.
12. (order) Put the steps of the colour dilution test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Number the cups and measure water into them', 'Put one drop of colour in cup 1 and stir', 'Stir one spoonful into the next cup of water', 'Look along the row and compare with plain water']. *Why:* First set up the cups, then add the drop, then pass the colour on, and last compare the cups.
13. (write) One drop of food colour can turn a whole cup of water pink. Why can one small drop do this?  
   **Answer:** A drop holds a huge number of colour particles. They spread out through the water, so there are some in every part of the cup.. *Why:* A strong answer says the drop is made of a huge number of tiny particles that spread through all the water.
14. (write) Explain to a younger child why you can squash air in a syringe but not water.  
   **Answer:** Air particles are far apart, with lots of empty space between them, so you can push them closer. Water particles already touch, so there is nowhere for them to go.. *Why:* A strong answer names the empty space between air particles and says the water particles are already touching.
15. (draw) Draw the particles in steam. Show how far apart they are and add arrows to show how they move.  
   **Answer:** Small circles far apart, each with a long arrow in a different direction.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the particles far apart with arrows in all directions, because steam is a gas.
16. (mcq) A pressure cooker is heated, and the steam cannot escape. What happens to the push of the steam on the lid?  
   Options: It gets smaller / It stays the same / It gets bigger, because the particles fly faster and hit the lid harder  
   **Answer:** It gets bigger, because the particles fly faster and hit the lid harder. *Why:* Heat makes the steam particles move faster. They hit the lid harder and more often, so the pressure goes up. This is also why the lid will not open until the cooker cools.
### Gaps, Hits and Models (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A jar is full to the top with big chana, and then small mustard seeds slide in between them. What does this show?  
   Options: The chana had gaps between the grains / The chana was soft / The mustard seeds were heavier  
   **Answer:** The chana had gaps between the grains. *Why:* Small things fit into the gaps between big things. In the same way, small particles can fit into the gaps between bigger ones.
2. (mcq) A smell takes minutes to cross a room, though air particles fly at hundreds of metres a second. What is the best reason?  
   Options: The smell particles keep colliding with air particles and zigzag / The smell particles are very slow / Smells are not made of particles  
   **Answer:** The smell particles keep colliding with air particles and zigzag. *Why:* A smell particle hits an air particle billions of times each second, and changes direction each time. So it travels only a little way from where it started. Moving air from a fan carries it much faster.
3. (mcq) A balloon left in a hot car bursts. What is the best reason?  
   Options: The hot air particles fly faster and hit the skin harder / The rubber melts into the air / The air particles grow bigger  
   **Answer:** The hot air particles fly faster and hit the skin harder. *Why:* Heat makes the particles faster, so they hit the balloon harder and more often. The pressure goes up until the skin gives way. The particles do not grow.
4. (mcq) A gas is squashed into half the space. What changes?  
   Options: Each particle is half the size / The particles are closer together and hit the walls more often / There are fewer particles  
   **Answer:** The particles are closer together and hit the walls more often. *Why:* The same particles are now in half the space. They are closer, so they reach the walls twice as often. The particles themselves do not change.
5. (mcq) Which of these is a mixture?  
   Options: A glass of distilled water / A bowl of sambar / A grain of table sugar  
   **Answer:** A bowl of sambar. *Why:* Sambar has water, dal, vegetables, tamarind and spices. The other two are very nearly one substance.
6. (mcq) Which statement about an atom is correct?  
   Options: An atom is a solid ball with no empty space / An atom is mostly empty space, with a tiny nucleus in the middle / All atoms are the same size and kind  
   **Answer:** An atom is mostly empty space, with a tiny nucleus in the middle. *Why:* An atom is mostly empty space. The nucleus is about a hundred thousand times smaller than the whole atom. There are about a hundred different kinds of atom.
7. (truefalse) A heap of dry rice that pours like a liquid is a liquid.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each grain of rice is a solid. A heap of grains can flow, but the grains keep their shape.
8. (truefalse) If you heat a gas in a closed container, the pressure goes up.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Faster particles hit the walls harder and more often.
9. (fill) The slow spreading of particles through a gas or a liquid, with no stirring, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['diffusion']. *Why:* Diffusion happens because particles move and collide, and spread out over time.
10. (sort) Is each description true of the particles in a solid or of the particles in a gas?  
   **Answer:** touch their neighbours → Solid; are far apart → Gas; are held in place → Solid; fly in straight lines until they hit something → Gas; jiggle on the spot → Solid; spread to fill any container → Gas. *Why:* In a solid the particles are close and held in place. In a gas they are far apart and free.
11. (match) Match each thing you can see to the idea it shows.  
   **Answer:** Air squashes in a syringe → Empty space between particles; A football stays round → Gas pressure; Colour spreads through still water → Diffusion; Ice melts into water → Particles jiggle harder and break free. *Why:* Each everyday event is one of the ideas of the particle model.
12. (order) Put the steps of the squeeze test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Pull the plunger back to 10 millilitres', 'Seal the end with a finger', 'Push the plunger in as far as you can and read the mark', 'Write the numbers in the table']. *Why:* The syringe must have its starting amount first. Then you seal it, push it and record.
13. (write) A cup of water looks clear, but it came from the sixth cup of a dilution test. Why can the colour still be there?  
   **Answer:** Each cup carried only a tenth of the colour forward, so the sixth cup has very little. The colour particles are spread so thinly that we cannot see them, but they are not gone.. *Why:* A strong answer says the colour is spread very thin, but a tiny share of a huge number of particles is still some particles.
14. (write) A friend says air is nothing, because you cannot see it. Give one reason why she is wrong.  
   **Answer:** A balloon filled with air feels springy when you squeeze it. An upside-down glass pushed into water keeps the water out. So air takes up space and pushes.. *Why:* A strong answer names a test, and says what it shows about air being real stuff.
15. (write) Explain why heating a gas in a sealed container makes the pressure go up.  
   **Answer:** Heat makes the gas particles move faster. They hit the walls harder and more often, so the push on the walls is bigger.. *Why:* A strong answer says the particles are faster and hit the walls harder and more often.
16. (draw) Draw a closed box of gas. Show the particles and arrows that show what makes pressure on one wall.  
   **Answer:** A box with particles far apart. Arrows show some particles hitting the wall.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows particles hitting the wall, because the hits make the pressure.

   Options: Its particles are far apart, with empty space between them / Its particles are soft / Air has no particles  
   **Answer:** Its particles are far apart, with empty space between them. *Why:* The particles are not squashed. They are pushed closer together, into the empty space.
4. (mcq) A drop of colour is stirred into a cup, and a spoonful of that is stirred into another cup, again and again. The last cup looks clear. What is the best description?  
   Options: The colour is gone / The colour is there, spread so thin you cannot see it / The colour is at the bottom of the first cup  
   **Answer:** The colour is there, spread so thin you cannot see it. *Why:* Each spoonful carries a tenth of the colour onward. Some always goes on, even when too faint to see.
5. (mcq) What makes gas pressure?  
   Options: Particles hitting the walls / Particles being heavy / Particles sticking to the walls  
   **Answer:** Particles hitting the walls. *Why:* A gas pushes on its container because its particles hit the walls and bounce back.
6. (mcq) Sambar is best described as which of these?  
   Options: A substance / A mixture / A gas  
   **Answer:** A mixture. *Why:* Sambar is made of several substances, such as water, dal and spices, mixed together.
7. (mcq) Which change makes the particles of a solid move faster?  
   Options: Cooling it / Heating it / Putting it in a box  
   **Answer:** Heating it. *Why:* Heat is movement of particles. A warmer solid has particles that jiggle harder.
8. (truefalse) The particles in a gas hardly pull on each other at all.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Gas particles are so far apart that they barely pull on each other, so they fly free.
9. (mcq) A model of particles uses tiny balls. What is true about this model?  
   Options: It is useful, but not exactly right / It is exactly right / It is useless  
   **Answer:** It is useful, but not exactly right. *Why:* The balls help us think, but real atoms are fuzzy. All models have limits.
10. (mcq) Which of these makes a smell spread faster across a room?  
   Options: A fan moving the air / Closing all the windows / Cooling the room  
   **Answer:** A fan moving the air. *Why:* Moving air carries the smell particles much faster than they could spread by themselves.
11. (truefalse) A gas can be made of exactly the same particles as a liquid.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Steam and liquid water are both made of water particles.
12. (mcq) Why was the idea of flying gas particles so useful to scientists?  
   Options: It explained why heating a gas raises its pressure / It explained why ice floats / It explained why metals shine  
   **Answer:** It explained why heating a gas raises its pressure. *Why:* Faster particles hit harder, so a hotter gas pushes more. The older idea of fixed particles could not explain this.

## Applications
- Pressure cookers use sealed steam at about twice the push of outside air, so water boils at about 120 degrees Celsius and food cooks faster.
- Bicycle and car tyres hold air at several times the push of the outside air, which keeps them firm.
- LPG cylinders hold gas squeezed so hard that most of it turns into a liquid inside the cylinder.
- Medicine inhalers and spray bottles push a gas to carry a fine mist of medicine into the lungs.
- Perfume, incense and air fresheners work because their particles spread through the air by diffusion and air currents.
- Weather balloons are filled with a gas that is lighter than air, and they swell as they rise, because the air outside gets thinner.
- Scuba divers carry air squeezed into a steel tank, so that they can breathe for an hour underwater.
- Engineers use computer models of particles to design new materials, such as stronger steel and better batteries.
- Chemists make pure substances, such as medicines, by separating one kind of particle from a mixture.
- Hospitals use oxygen gas squeezed into cylinders to help people breathe.
- A refrigerator moves heat by squeezing and letting go of a gas inside its pipes.

## Open questions
- **Is there a smallest thing, with nothing smaller inside it?** Atoms are made of smaller parts, and those parts are described as ripples in fields. Physicists do not know if there is a last, smallest level.
- **Is the empty space between particles really empty?** Physicists describe even empty space as having fields in it. They still argue about what, if anything, that means for how we should picture it.
- **Why do atoms of only about a hundred kinds make everything we know?** We know how atoms are built and how they join. Why the universe has just these kinds is part of a bigger question that nobody has answered.
- **What is the best picture of a particle: a ball, a cloud, or a ripple?** Each picture works for some questions and fails for others. Physicists still use all three, and they do not agree on which is best.
- **How can scientists tell exactly how fast one particle moves, when it is too small to see?** They measure the effect of a huge number of particles, such as pressure. Following one single particle for long is much harder, and the methods are still improving.
- **How do particles of a liquid cool into a glass instead of a crystal?** Scientists understand the broad picture. They are still arguing about the exact way the particles stop moving as a runny liquid turns hard.

## Connected maths
- Pretend cup 1 holds 1,000,000 colour specks. Each spoonful carries one tenth of the specks forward to the next cup. How many specks are in cup 4? In which cup are there only 10 specks? **1,000 specks in cup 4. Cup 6 has 10 specks.**. Cup 2 has 1,000,000 divided by 10, which is 100,000. Cup 3 has 10,000. Cup 4 has 1,000. Cup 5 has 100. Cup 6 has 10. Each cup has one tenth of the one before. A real drop holds far more specks than a million. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A syringe holds 10 millilitres of air. After a hard push, the plunger is at the 4 millilitre mark. By how many millilitres did the air squash? What fraction of the starting amount is that? **6 millilitres. That is 6 tenths, or 3 fifths, of the start.**. 10 take away 4 is 6. The air squashed by 6 out of 10 millilitres, which is the fraction 6/10. Both 6 and 10 divide by 2, so it is the same as 3/5. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In the syringe test, the air squashed from 10 millilitres to 4 millilitres. What percentage of its starting size was the air squashed down to? Is the pressure now more than or less than double the starting pressure? **40 percent. The pressure is more than double.**. 4 out of 10 is 4/10, which is 40 out of 100, or 40 percent. The same particles are in 4 tenths of the space, so there are 10 divided by 4, or 2 and a half, times as many in each millilitre. That is more than double. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A jug held 300 millilitres of chana. Then 85 millilitres of mustard seeds went in, and then 40 millilitres of water. How much stuff is now in the jug? How much is the total more than the 300 millilitres of chana? **425 millilitres. That is 125 more than the chana alone.**. 300 plus 85 is 385. 385 plus 40 is 425. The jug now holds 425 millilitres of stuff. The extra is 425 take away 300, which is 125 millilitres. The extra fitted into the gaps. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- Three tries with air in the syringe gave a squash of 4, 6 and 5 millilitres. What is the middle number? Why do scientists use the middle number and not just the first try? **5 millilitres. It protects against one odd try.**. Put the numbers in order: 4, 5, 6. The middle one is 5. A single try could be odd, for example if the seal leaked. The middle number is less affected by one odd result. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A drop of water holds about a thousand million million million water particles. How many zeros does that number have if you write it out? Write the first part: a thousand is 1,000. **21 zeros.**. A thousand has 3 zeros. A million has 6 zeros. Each 'million' adds 6 zeros, and there are three of them, so 3 plus 6 plus 6 plus 6 is 21 zeros. The number is 1 followed by 21 zeros. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- A tyre holds 2,000 millilitres and starts full of outside air at normal pressure. Each pump stroke pushes in 500 millilitres more outside air. After 4 strokes, how many times as many air particles are in the tyre as at the start? So about how many times the outside pressure is it? **Twice as many. About twice the outside pressure.**. 4 strokes of 500 is 2,000 millilitres of extra outside air. The tyre had 2,000 millilitres, so it now holds 2,000 plus 2,000 which is 4,000 millilitres of outside air squeezed into 2,000. That is twice as many particles in the same space, so about twice the pressure. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Air particles fly at about 500 metres every second. If one could fly in a straight line, how far would it go in one minute? Give your answer in metres and in kilometres. A smell takes about five minutes to cross a 6 metre room. Why is this so much slower? **30,000 metres, which is 30 kilometres. The smell zigzags.**. 500 times 60 is 30,000 metres. 1,000 metres make a kilometre, so 30,000 metres is 30 kilometres. A smell particle is knocked in a new direction billions of times each second, so it zigzags and makes little progress. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a particle? → A very tiny piece that a substance is made of, too small to see.
- What do the particles of a solid do? → They touch and are held in place, jiggling on the spot.
- What do the particles of a liquid do? → They touch but slide past each other.
- What do the particles of a gas do? → They are far apart and fly about.
- Why can air be squashed? → There is empty space between its particles.
- Why can't water be squashed much? → Its particles are already touching.
- What makes gas pressure? → Particles hitting the walls.
- What happens to the pressure when you heat a sealed gas? → It goes up, because the particles fly faster.
- Is a tray of rice a perfect model of particles? → No. It is useful, but the grains are big and touch.
- Are ice, water and steam made of the same particles? → Yes. Only how they are arranged and move is different.
