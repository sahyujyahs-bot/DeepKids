# How your body gets rid of waste: kidneys, breath and sweat
`sci.body.kidneys-and-waste.L4`

**Summary.** Your body makes waste that must leave, and kidneys give most of it back to the body first. How can a filter do that? In this unit you will find your kidneys, test what three different filters hold back, and play a sorting game on how waste leaves.

**Intro.** *You drink clear water, pale buttermilk and brown tea. Yet your urine is always some shade of yellow. Where does the yellow come from, and what else is leaving with it?* Urine is made from your blood, by your two kidneys. The yellow is a waste left over when old red blood cells are broken down. Urine also carries a waste called urea, which your body makes when it uses protein. Your lungs and your skin remove waste too. In this unit you find your kidneys with your thumbs. Then you pour salty tea with flour in it through three filters. You find out what a filter can hold back, and what the kidneys must do after filtering.

## Learning outcomes

## Terms
- **Waste**: Something a cell has made or has left over, which the body cannot use and must send out. *e.g.* Carbon dioxide and urea are wastes. Your cells make them all day and all night.
- **Excretion**: Sending out the wastes that the body's own cells have made. *e.g.* Breathing out carbon dioxide is excretion. Passing urine is excretion too.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas that cells make when they use food for energy. Blood carries it to the lungs. *e.g.* Every breath you blow out carries carbon dioxide away.
- **Urea**: A waste that the liver makes when the body breaks down protein. It dissolves in water. *e.g.* After a meal with dal or egg, your liver makes more urea. Your kidneys send it out.
- **Kidney**: One of two organs in your lower back that clean the blood and make urine. *e.g.* Each of your kidneys is about the size of your fist. A kidney is shaped like a rajma bean.
- **Filter**: Something with small holes. It lets small things pass and holds back things bigger than the holes. *e.g.* A tea strainer is a filter. It lets the tea pass and holds back the leaves.
- **Nephron**: One of the tiny cleaning units in a kidney. Each has a filter and a long thin tube. *e.g.* Each of your kidneys has about a million nephrons, all working side by side.
- **Glomerulus**: The filter of a nephron. It is a tiny ball of very thin blood vessels with holes in their walls. *e.g.* Water, salt, sugar and urea pass through the glomerulus. Blood cells are too big to pass.
- **Reabsorb**: To take something back. The tube of a nephron reabsorbs water, salt and sugar into the blood. *e.g.* Your kidneys reabsorb about 99 of every 100 parts of the liquid they filter.
- **Urine**: The liquid the kidneys make from blood. It is water with urea, salts and other wastes dissolved in it. *e.g.* Urine is about 95 parts water in every 100. The other 5 parts are wastes and salts.
- **Ureter**: A thin tube that carries urine from a kidney down to the bladder. You have two. *e.g.* Urine trickles down your two ureters all day, a few drops at a time.
- **Bladder**: A stretchy bag low in your belly that stores urine until you go to the toilet. *e.g.* When your bladder is about half full, it signals your brain. You feel the need to go.
- **Sweat gland**: A tiny coiled tube in the skin that makes sweat and sends it out through a pore. *e.g.* You have about 2 to 4 million sweat glands. Many are on your palms, soles and forehead.
- **Dialysis**: A treatment that cleans the blood with a machine when the kidneys cannot do it. *e.g.* A person on dialysis usually spends about four hours on the machine, three times a week.

## Explanations
### Your cells make waste all the time · *defined*
**Definition:** Excretion is sending out the wastes that the body's own cells have made. A waste is something the cells cannot use.

Every cell in your body is at work. A cell takes in sugar and oxygen and uses them for energy. This leaves carbon dioxide and water. A cell also uses protein to build and repair itself. When protein is broken down, a poison called ammonia is left over. Your liver quickly changes ammonia into urea, which is far less harmful. Your body also gets more water and more salt than it needs on some days. All of these must leave. If they stayed, they would build up in your blood and make you very ill within days. Stools are a different matter. Most of a stool is food that was never digested, with gut bacteria and water. That food passed through the tube of your gut and never entered your cells. One part of a stool is true waste. It is the brown colour, which your liver made from old red blood cells.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide: made in every cell that uses food for energy.
- e.g. Urea: made in the liver from the leftovers of protein.
- e.g. Extra water and extra salt: what you took in beyond what you need.
- e.g. Colour wastes: made when old red blood cells are broken down.
### There are three main ways out · *defined*
**Definition:** Waste leaves the body through the lungs, the kidneys and the skin. The blood carries each waste to its way out.

Blood flows past every cell and picks up the wastes. Then the blood takes them to three organs. The lungs remove carbon dioxide, which is a gas, and you breathe it out. The kidneys remove wastes that dissolve in water, such as urea and extra salt, and send them out in urine. The skin lets out sweat, which is water with a little salt and a very little urea. The liver helps all three. It changes poisons into forms that the kidneys can remove, and it sends colour wastes into the gut. The kidneys do the largest share of the cleaning. Sweat is mainly for cooling you, and it carries out only a small amount of waste.
- e.g. Lungs: carbon dioxide and some water, in every breath.
- e.g. Kidneys: urea, extra salt, extra water and many other dissolved wastes.
- e.g. Skin: water, a little salt and a very little urea, in sweat.
- e.g. Liver: makes urea, and sends colour wastes out through the gut.
### Where your kidneys are, and how blood reaches them · *defined*
**Definition:** The kidneys are two bean-shaped organs in the lower back. They lie one on each side of the backbone, just under the lowest ribs.

Put your hands on your waist with your thumbs on your back. Slide your hands up until your thumbs touch your lowest ribs. Your kidneys lie under your thumbs. Each kidney is about the size of your own fist. A thick blood vessel brings blood into each kidney. About one fifth of all the blood your heart pumps goes to the kidneys. That is a large share for two small organs. Cleaned blood leaves each kidney by another vessel. The urine leaves by a thin tube called a ureter. The two ureters lead down to the bladder, which is low in your belly. From the bladder, a short tube called the urethra leads outside.
- e.g. A kidney is shaped like a rajma bean. In English, rajma is called the kidney bean.
- e.g. An adult's kidney is about 11 centimetres long. A child's kidney is smaller, about the size of the child's fist.
- e.g. Your lowest ribs cover the top part of each kidney and help to protect it.
### Step one: the kidney filters the blood by size · *defined*
**Definition:** Each kidney has about a million nephrons. A nephron begins with a filter called a glomerulus. It lets small particles out of the blood and holds back large ones.

A glomerulus is a tiny ball of very thin blood vessels. Their walls have holes far too small to see, even with a school microscope. The heart's push presses the blood against these walls. Water passes through the holes. So does everything small that is dissolved in the water: salt, sugar, urea and many others. Red blood cells and white blood cells are too big, so they stay in the blood. Large proteins stay too. The liquid that passes through is called the filtrate. It looks like pale water, and it collects in a cup around the ball. The filter sorts only by size. It lets useful sugar through as easily as harmful urea. An adult's kidneys filter about 180 litres of liquid a day in this way.
- e.g. Passes the filter: water, salt, sugar, urea.
- e.g. Held back: red blood cells, white blood cells, large proteins.
- e.g. A tea strainer works the same way. It holds back leaves and lets the dissolved tea through.
### Step two: the kidney takes back what the body needs · *defined*
**Definition:** To reabsorb is to take back. The long tube of each nephron reabsorbs water, salt and sugar from the filtrate and returns them to the blood.

The filtrate flows from the cup into a long, thin, winding tube. Blood vessels wrap around the tube. The cells in the tube's wall have pumps that grip particular particles and carry them back to the blood. One kind of pump carries sugar. Another carries salt. Water follows the salt. In a healthy person, all the sugar is taken back. So is nearly all the water and salt. About 99 of every 100 parts of the filtrate return to the blood. Urea has no pump that returns all of it, so much of it stays in the tube. The tube also pushes a few extra wastes straight into the liquid. What reaches the end of the tube is urine. This way of working has one great strength. The kidney does not have to recognise a waste. Anything small that is not taken back leaves the body. That is how you get rid of a new medicine that your body has never met before.
- e.g. Taken back fully: sugar.
- e.g. Taken back as needed: water and salt.
- e.g. Left in the tube: much of the urea, extra salt, extra water, used medicines.
- e.g. Of 180 litres filtered in a day, an adult passes only about 1 and a half litres as urine.
### Urine: what is in it, and where it goes · *defined*
**Definition:** Urine is the liquid left at the end of the nephrons. It is about 95 parts water in every 100, with urea, salts and other wastes dissolved in it.

Urine drips from the kidneys into the two ureters all day and all night. The ureters squeeze it down to the bladder. The bladder is a stretchy bag made of muscle. A nine-year-old's bladder holds about 300 millilitres, which is one and a half tumblers. When it is about half full, it signals the brain, and you feel the need to go. The yellow colour of urine has a long history. Old red blood cells are broken down in the liver. Bacteria in your gut then change the leftover colour. Part of it returns to the blood, and the kidneys send it out as a yellow waste called urobilin. Scientists found out how the bacteria do this only in 2024. When there is little water in your urine, the yellow is strong. When there is a lot of water, the yellow is pale.
- e.g. Pale yellow urine: plenty of water is leaving with the wastes.
- e.g. Dark yellow urine: the kidneys are saving water, so the wastes are less thinned out.
- e.g. Pink urine after beetroot: in some people, the beetroot's colour passes through the kidneys.
### The kidneys change their work from hour to hour · *defined*
**Definition:** The kidneys keep the water and salt in the blood steady. They take back more at some times and less at others.

After you drink two tumblers of water, your blood becomes slightly more watery. Your brain notices this within minutes. It then makes less of a messenger chemical called ADH. With less ADH, the tubes of the nephrons take back less water. More water stays in the urine, so you pass more of it, and it is pale. On a hot day with little to drink, the opposite happens. The brain sends out more ADH, and the tubes take back almost all the water. You pass a little dark urine. The kidneys also slow down at night, which is why most children can sleep ten hours without a toilet visit. Salt is handled in the same way. After salty chips, the kidneys leave more salt in the urine.
- e.g. After a long drink: more urine, and paler.
- e.g. After playing in the sun with no water: less urine, and darker.
- e.g. At night: the kidneys make less urine than by day.
### The lungs remove carbon dioxide, and other things too · *defined*
**Definition:** The lungs are a way out for wastes that can turn into a gas. Carbon dioxide passes from the blood into the air in the lungs and leaves in the breath.

Blood carries carbon dioxide from every cell to the lungs. There the blood flows very close to the air, with only a thin wall between them. Carbon dioxide passes through the wall, and you breathe it out. An adult breathes out about 1 kilogram of carbon dioxide a day. Breath also carries out water, about 300 millilitres a day. You see it as mist when you breathe on a cold steel tumbler. Other things in the blood can leave this way if they turn into a gas easily. After a person eats raw garlic, one of the garlic's smelly chemicals enters the blood from the gut. For hours it leaves through the lungs, however well the person brushes their teeth.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide: about 1 kilogram a day from an adult.
- e.g. Water: the mist on a cold window when you breathe on it.
- e.g. Garlic breath: a smell that comes from the blood, by way of the lungs.
### Sweat cools you, and carries a little waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Sweat is a liquid made by sweat glands in the skin. It is about 99 parts water in every 100, with salt and a very little urea.

A sweat gland is a tiny coiled tube deep in the skin. It draws water and salt from the liquid around it. The sweat rises through a duct to a pore on the skin. On the way up, the duct takes back much of the salt. This is the same method the kidney uses: let the liquid out first, then take back what is needed. On the skin, the water evaporates, which means it turns into a gas and goes into the air. Evaporating water carries heat away, and that is the main job of sweat. The salt cannot evaporate, so it stays on your skin and your clothes. Sweat does carry out a little urea. But the amount is tiny beside what the kidneys remove. A person cannot clean their blood by sweating.
- e.g. A white line on a dark cap after a hot cricket match: salt left by dried sweat.
- e.g. A salty taste on your upper lip after running.
- e.g. People who sweat often in hot places make less salty sweat, because their ducts take back more salt.
### When kidneys stop working: dialysis and transplants · *defined*
**Definition:** Kidney failure means the kidneys can no longer clean the blood. Dialysis is cleaning the blood with a machine. A transplant is giving a person a healthy kidney from someone else.

When both kidneys fail, urea, salt and water build up in the blood. Without treatment a person dies within days or weeks. In 1943 the Dutch doctor Willem Kolff built the first artificial kidney that worked. He used sausage skin made of cellophane, a wooden drum and a tub of salty water. The patient's blood flowed through the cellophane tube. Cellophane has holes too small to see. Urea passed out through the holes into the tub, and the blood cells stayed in. Kolff's first patients died. In 1945 his machine saved Sofia Schafstadt, a woman of 67. Today's dialysis machines work on the same idea. A person can also live well with one kidney. So a healthy person can give one kidney to someone whose kidneys have failed. The first kidney transplant that lasted was done in 1954 in Boston, between twin brothers. The kidney that stays behind in the giver grows larger and does more work.
- e.g. Dialysis: about four hours on a machine, usually three times a week.
- e.g. A transplant: one healthy kidney, often given by a parent, a sister or a brother.
- e.g. Some people are born with one kidney and never notice.
### Why do we have two kidneys when one is enough? · *idea*
A person with one healthy kidney can live a full life. So why does the body build two? Nobody is sure. Scientists have ideas, and each explains part of it.
- e.g. Eyes, ears, lungs and kidneys all come in pairs.
- e.g. After one kidney is given away, the other grows and filters more.
- **Scientists who study how bodies form:** A body grows as two matching halves, left and right. Many organs form once on each side, and the kidneys are among them. On this idea, two kidneys are a result of how a body is built.
- **Scientists who study survival:** A spare kidney helps an animal survive an injury, an illness or old age. Over a long life, each kidney slowly loses nephrons. On this idea, the second kidney is a reserve.

## Mostly agreed
- The kidneys make urine by filtering the blood and then taking back most of what was filtered. *How we know:* In 1924 Joseph Wearn and Alfred Newton Richards drew liquid from one glomerulus of a frog. They used a very fine glass pipette. It held salt and sugar but no protein. The frog's urine held no sugar.
- An adult's kidneys filter about 180 litres a day and pass about 1 and a half litres as urine. *How we know:* Doctors measure filtering with a harmless marker that passes the filter and is never taken back. The amount of marker in the urine shows how much liquid was filtered.
- Carbon dioxide leaves the body through the lungs. *How we know:* Breathed-out air holds about 100 times more carbon dioxide than breathed-in air. This has been measured since the time of Antoine Lavoisier in the 1780s.
- Urea is made in the liver and removed by the kidneys. *How we know:* When the kidneys fail, urea builds up in the blood, and dialysis brings it down. When the liver fails, ammonia builds up in the blood.
- Sweat is mainly for cooling, and it removes only a small amount of waste. *How we know:* Measurements of sweat show it is about 99 parts water in every 100. The urea in a day's sweat is a small fraction of the urea in a day's urine.
- A person can live a healthy life with one kidney. *How we know:* Doctors have followed thousands of people who gave a kidney, for many years. Most stay as healthy as other people of their age.

## How we found out
**William Bowman in England, Carl Ludwig in Germany, and Joseph Wearn and Alfred Newton Richards in the United States, Bowman published his drawings in 1842. Ludwig proposed filtering in the 1840s. Wearn and Richards published their frog experiment in 1924..** For a long time, many doctors thought a kidney made urine the way a gland makes spit. On this idea, the kidney's cells picked each waste out of the blood and pushed it into the urine. In 1842 the English doctor William Bowman drew the kidney's tiny tubes under a microscope. Each tube began as a cup around a ball of blood vessels. In the 1840s the German scientist Carl Ludwig proposed that the ball is a filter. He said that the push of the blood drives water and small particles through it. The tube then takes most of them back. Few people accepted this, and the argument lasted eighty years. In 1924, in Philadelphia, Joseph Wearn and Alfred Newton Richards settled it. They worked on a frog who had been put to sleep. They slid a glass pipette, finer than a hair, into one cup and drew out a tiny drop. The drop held salt and sugar, as blood does, but no protein. The urine in the frog's bladder held no sugar. So sugar had passed the filter, and the tube had taken it back.  
*What people believed before:* That the kidney's cells pick each waste out of the blood and push it into the urine. Wearn and Richards showed that the liquid just past the filter holds sugar and salt, like blood without its proteins. The kidney filters first and takes back afterwards.

## Challenging perspectives
- **You can sweat out toxins in a hot room or with hard exercise.** (Many advertisements for saunas, hot yoga and 'detox' wraps.; *rejected*)  
  For: Sweat does hold tiny traces of some pollutants, and people feel fresh after sweating.  
  Against: In 2018 Pascal Imbeault and his team worked out the amounts. Even hard sweating removes far less of these pollutants than a day's food brings in. The liver and kidneys do the cleaning.
- **Urine is sterile, which means it has no living microbes in it.** (Doctors and textbooks for more than a hundred years.; *accepted*)  
  For: The usual hospital test grew no bacteria from the urine of healthy people.  
  Against: In 2014 Evann Hilt, Alan Wolfe, Linda Brubaker and their team used better ways to grow bacteria. They found living bacteria in the bladders of healthy women. The bladder has its own community of microbes.
- **Everyone must drink eight glasses of water a day.** (Health magazines, apps and many school posters.; *rejected*)  
  For: The body does lose water all day, and too little water makes the kidneys' work harder.  
  Against: In 2002 the kidney scientist Heinz Valtin searched for the evidence behind the number and found none. Food also gives water. Needs differ with size, weather and play. Thirst and urine colour are better guides.
- **Drinking your own urine is good for your health.** (Some old health traditions. Morarji Desai, a prime minister of India, said in public that he did it.; *rejected*)  
  For: People who do it say they feel well, and urine from a healthy person is mostly water.  
  Against: No fair test has shown any benefit. Urine holds the very wastes that the kidneys worked to remove. Drinking it gives them back to the kidneys to remove again.
- **The salt in our blood is left over from the ancient sea where animals first lived.** (The Canadian scientist Archibald Macallum, in 1926.; *debated*)  
  For: Blood is salty, and the first animals did live in the sea. The mix of salts in blood is a little like sea water.  
  Against: Blood is about three times less salty than today's sea. Nobody knows exactly how salty the ancient sea was. The idea is attractive, but it is hard to test.

## Roots
In this unit you poured salty tea through three filters and learned how the kidneys clean the blood. Here we ask what it means to clean something, and where a waste goes after it leaves you.

**Deep truth.** In the Three Filters Test, every filter let the salt and the tea colour pass with the water. The filters in your kidneys also let salt, sugar and urea out of the blood together. Then the kidneys take back the water, salt and sugar that your body needs. Whatever they do not take back leaves as urine, even a waste your body has never met.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of cleaning as finding the dirt and taking it out. To do that, the cleaner must know every kind of dirt. The kidneys clean the blood by knowing what the body needs, and they let everything else go.  
*Evidence:* In the Three Filters Test the tissue held back the leaves and the flour. The liquid stayed brown and salty. In 1924 Wearn and Richards found sugar in the liquid just past a frog's filter. They found none in the frog's urine. A healthy person's urine holds no sugar, though sugar passes the filter all day.

- *who am i:* About half the water in your body is replaced every week or two. Your body keeps its shape while the water and salt in it keep changing.
- *what is life:* A living body takes things in, keeps what it needs for a while and lets the rest go. A body that stopped letting go would die within days.
- *what is this world:* There is no "away" to throw things to, because everything stays inside the same world. The urea and the water that you let go are taken up by soil bacteria, plants, rivers and clouds.

The water in your urine was rain, river water and tap water before it was blood. The nitrogen in your urea was in the protein of a dal seed. Before that it was in the soil and the air. After the urea leaves you, bacteria in the soil turn the urea into food for plants. So the same atoms pass through cloud, plant, animal and soil in turn. The teacher Thich Nhat Hanh called this interbeing, which means that each thing is made of other things.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.5.1 to 6.5.2:** “अन्नमशितं त्रेधा विधीयते / तस्य यः स्थविष्ठो धातुस्तत्पुरीषं भवति । यो मध्यमस्तन्मांसम् । योऽणिष्ठस्तन्मनः / आपः पीतास्त्रेधा विधीयन्ते / तासां यः स्थविष्ठो धातुस्तन्मूत्रं भवति । यो मध्यमस्तल्लोहितम् । योऽणिष्ठः स प्राणः” Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu that food, once eaten, becomes three things. The coarsest part becomes stool, the middle part becomes flesh and the finest part becomes mind. Water, once drunk, also becomes three things: urine, blood and breath. (This is a very old attempt to follow what the body keeps and what it lets go. Science agrees that the body sorts what it takes in. It differs on the details. Urine is made from the blood, by the kidneys.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), the chapter 'Roses and Garbage':** A rose becomes garbage within a few weeks, and garbage becomes compost that feeds the next rose. A good gardener looks at a rose and sees the garbage, and looks at garbage and sees a rose. (Urea leaves your body as a waste and reaches a plant as food. So the word 'waste' only describes what a thing is for one body at one moment. For another living being, the same thing can be food.)
- Science (settled): Atoms are not destroyed in the body. Every atom of nitrogen that leaves in urea goes on to the soil, the water or the air. Plants take up many of them.
- Science (idea): In 1953 the kidney scientist Homer Smith wrote about what decides what the blood is made of. He said it is decided mostly by what the kidneys keep, and less by what goes in through the mouth. He also argued that this steady inner liquid allowed animals to leave the sea for the land.
- Other view, Carl Ludwig and other scientists of the 1840s, and many scientists today: There is no choosing or knowing in a kidney. The push of the blood drives liquid through holes. Pumps in the tube's cells move particles by the rules of physics and chemistry. On this view, words such as 'keeps' and 'needs' are only a short way to describe a mechanism.
- Practice, One breath out, one sip in (3 min): Sit together with a tumbler of water. Breathe out slowly onto the back of your hand. Feel the warmth and the damp. That is water and carbon dioxide leaving your blood. Now take one sip and swallow it slowly. In a few minutes this water will be in your blood. Sit still for one minute. Your kidneys are at work behind you, and they make no sound.
- Ask: Your kidneys do not need to know what a waste is. They need to know what your body needs. How might a kidney know that?
- Ask: The urea that leaves you today may be part of a tomato plant next year. When did it stop being yours?
- Ask: In the story What Fell Through the Sieve, Yazhini threw away things she could not name. Could something useful have been among them? Could a kidney make the same mistake?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your kidneys can remove a new medicine that no human body ever met before. Could a cleaner that only hunts for known dirt do that? (hint: Think about the Three Filters Test. The kidneys only need to know what to take back.)
- *deep:* Urea is a waste to you and a food to a plant. Is anything a waste for everyone? (hint: Farmers put urea on fields. Bacteria in the soil turn the urea into a form that roots take in.)
- *deep:* Food that you could not digest passes through your gut and never enters a cell. Was it ever inside you? (hint: The gut is a tube that runs through the body, open at both ends.)
- *big:* About half the water in your body is replaced every week or two. Are you the same body you were last year? (hint: The water changes, but the pattern of your body stays. Which one is you?)
- *big:* There are more people who need a kidney than there are kidneys given. How should we decide who gets one? (hint: Some say the person who has waited longest. Some say the person the kidney fits best. Some say children first.)
- *big:* Astronauts drink water that was cleaned from their own urine. Is that different from what the Earth does with all water? (hint: The water in your tumbler has passed through many animals, including humans, plants and clouds before.)

## Story: What Fell Through the Sieve
*Yazhini, Selvi Chithi, Kathir and the stall are made up. It is true that many tailors work in small stalls in the lanes around the Meenakshi temple in Madurai. The way Yazhini cleans the heap is the way a kidney cleans blood. A filter holds back the big things, everything small passes through, and only what is needed is taken back.*

The day before the wedding order was due, the button tin fell off the top shelf.

Yazhini heard it from the back of the stall. First there was a bang, and then a sound like heavy rain on a tin roof. Then came the small voice of her cousin Kathir, who was five. "I only wanted to see the bells," he said.

Her aunt's tailoring stall stood in a lane near the Meenakshi temple in Madurai. On one side sat a bangle seller. On the other was a shop that sold sieves, winnowing trays and brass pots. The stall was four steps wide, and every wall was shelves.

The tin was an old biscuit tin as big as a pillow. Selvi Chithi had been filling it for twenty years. It held shell buttons and brass buttons, hooks and eyes, press studs, sequins and glass beads. It held the tiny silver bells that are sewn on the hem of a little girl's pavadai.

Now all of it lay on the floor. And the floor of a tailoring stall is never empty. There were thread ends and snips of cloth, chalk dust and pins. There were three spools that had rolled under the machine, pieces of a broken bangle from next door, and the sand that comes in on everybody's feet.

Chithi looked at the heap. Then she looked at the clock. "Forty shell buttons and sixty silver hooks for the wedding blouses," she said. "They are wanted by six. And I have nine hems still to do." She sat down at her machine. "Yazhi, you clean it. Take out the rubbish and put the rest back in the tin."

Yazhini knelt on the floor and began. She picked out a thread end. Then she picked out a snip of blue cloth, and a piece of bangle glass, carefully, by its smooth side. Then a burnt matchstick. Then something grey and soft that she did not want to name.

After half an hour she had filled a paper cone with rubbish, and the heap looked exactly the same.

Every handful held a new kind of rubbish. There was a toffee wrapper, a curl of pencil shaving and a dry neem leaf. There was a fish scale, and nobody could say how a fish scale had come to a tailor's stall. Some things she could not name at all. How do you pick out a thing when you do not know what it is?

And then there was the sand. No fingers can pick out sand.

"I can blow on it," said Kathir. He blew. Sand flew up, and forty sequins flew with it and went under the shelves.

"Thank you, Kathir," said Yazhini. "Please do not help."

She sat back on her heels. Her knees hurt. The clock said a quarter past three. At her machine, Chithi dropped a needle. Chithi did not look at the dust or the threads on the floor. She looked for one bright thing, found it, picked it up and went on sewing. It took her two seconds.

Yazhini watched that, and then she looked at her paper cone.

The rubbish had no end of kinds. She would never learn them all. But the things the stall wanted were few, and she knew every one of them. Buttons. Hooks and eyes. Press studs. Sequins. Beads. Bells. She could count them on six fingers.

She stood up and went next door. "Akka, may I borrow a sieve for one hour? The one with the big holes, for cleaning groundnuts."

Back in the stall she spread a clean white veshti on the floor. She held the sieve over it and shook the whole heap through, a few handfuls at a time.

The big things stayed in the sieve. There were the three spools, the measuring tape and some pieces of cloth large enough to use as patches. They were easy to see, and nearly all of them belonged to the stall. She put them back on the shelf.

Everything small had fallen through. It lay on the white cloth in a thin layer, the buttons and the sand together, the bells and the dust and the fish scale.

Now Yazhini did not look for rubbish at all. She set out six steel tumblers. She looked only for the six kinds of things, and her fingers found them quickly. A button feels like a button. A hook catches on the skin. She gave Kathir the tumbler for bells, because bells shine and ring, and even a boy of five can find them.

"Fifty-one bells!" said Kathir, much later, and nobody checked.

When the tumblers were full, Yazhini looked at what was left on the veshti. Sand. Dust. Thread. Glass. The grey soft thing. Things with names and things without names. She did not sort them. She did not need to know a single one of them. She gathered the four corners of the cloth, carried it to the bin at the end of the lane and shook it out.

At five o'clock she counted forty shell buttons and sixty silver hooks into two paper packets and put them beside the machine.

Chithi stopped the wheel with her hand. "Finished? I thought you would still be picking out threads at Pongal."

"I stopped picking out the rubbish," said Yazhini. "I let everything small fall through. Then I took back what we wanted. The rest left by itself."

Chithi turned a shell button over in her fingers. "So if a new kind of rubbish falls on my floor tomorrow?"

"Then it goes out with the sand," said Yazhini. "I never have to know what it is."

Chithi laughed, bit off her thread and reached for the first blouse. Kathir sat under the shelves and looked for sequins, one bright thing at a time.

**For the parent:** Yazhini could not finish while she tried to pick out the rubbish, because rubbish comes in endless kinds. She finished when she let everything small fall through the sieve and took back only what the stall needed. Your kidneys clean your blood in the same way.

- Ask: Why did picking out the rubbish take so long, while picking out the buttons was quick?
- Ask: What stayed in the sieve, and what fell through? Which things in your blood are like each of these?
- Ask: A new kind of rubbish falls on the floor the next day. Why does Yazhini's method still work?

  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Shuffle the cards. Take three tokens: STOPPED, BACK and OUT.
- **s2 (7 min)**

  - Ask: How did you decide between BACK and OUT? (good: I asked if the body needs it.; I looked at what the person had just done, such as drinking a lot.; Wastes like urea go out.)

- **s4 (3 min)**

  - Check: Water is in the BACK pile and also in the OUT pile. Why? → **The kidney takes back what the body needs**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Three Filters Test: what can a filter hold back? (35 min)
- **question:** A mixture holds tea leaves, flour, tea colour and salt. Which of these four can a tea strainer hold back? Which can a cloth hold back? Which can tissue paper hold back?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which of the four things will the tissue paper hold back: the tea leaves, the flour, the tea colour, the salt? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use one mixture for all three filters, and stir it just before each pour. Pour the same amount through each filter, about a third of the tumbler. Use the same tea strainer each time, and rinse it between pours. Check every bowl in the same four ways. Only one thing changes: the size of the holes in the filter.
- **safety:** A grown-up heats and pours the hot water. Let the mixture cool until it is just warm before the child handles it. Use drinking water and clean things, because the child tastes one drop from each bowl.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows: strainer, cloth and tissue. For each row write yes or no four times: tea leaves, spoon seen, brown colour, salty taste.
- **results table:** Filter | Tea leaves in the bowl? | Can you see the spoon at the bottom? | Brown colour? | Salty taste?
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put 1 teaspoon of tea leaves in a steel tumbler. A grown-up pours in one tumbler of hot water. Wait 5 minutes. Then stir in 1 level teaspoon of salt until it is gone.  
  *Parent says:* This is our mixture. The tea has turned the water brown. Now the salt goes in. Stir, and tell me where the salt has gone.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the tea turn brown. Stir in the salt. Where did the salt go?
  - Note: While the tea stands, read the question together. The salt has dissolved. It is still there, as particles too small to see.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let the tea cool until it is just warm. Stir in 1 level teaspoon of atta. Then ask for the guess and the reason for each of the four things. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* The mixture now holds four things: tea leaves, flour, brown colour and salt. We will pour it through three filters. Which of the four will the tissue hold back? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Stir in the flour. Guess which of the four things the tissue will hold back.
  - Ask: Why do you think the tissue will hold that back? (good: The leaves are big.; The tissue has very small holes.; A filter cleans water, so it takes out the salt.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect the tissue to take out the salt or the colour. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stir the mixture well. At once pour about a third of it through the bare tea strainer into bowl 1. Rinse the strainer.  
  *Parent says:* First filter: the strainer alone. Stir, then pour quickly, before the leaves sink. What stayed in the strainer?  
  *Child mode:* Stir. Pour a third through the strainer into bowl 1.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Line the strainer with one layer of cotton cloth. Stir the mixture and pour half of what is left into bowl 2. Then line the strainer with two tissues. Stir, and pour the rest slowly into bowl 3.  
  *Parent says:* Second filter: the cloth. Third filter: the tissue. The tissue is slow, so pour a little at a time. Do not squeeze it.  
  *Child mode:* Pour through the cloth into bowl 2. Pour through the tissue into bowl 3.
  - Note: The tissue may take 3 to 5 minutes and may clog. If it is still dripping after 5 minutes, lift it away. A few spoonfuls in bowl 3 are enough. A squeezed tissue tears, and the test is spoilt.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Check each bowl in four ways. Look for leaves. Lower a clean spoon to the bottom and see if it shows. Look at the colour. Taste one drop from a clean spoon. Write yes or no in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now we check each bowl. Are there leaves? Can you see the spoon at the bottom? Is it brown? Is one drop salty? Use a fresh spoon for each taste.  
  *Child mode:* Check each bowl. Leaves? Spoon seen? Brown? Salty? Write yes or no.
  - Note: In most homes the strainer stops the leaves and the cloth stops some flour. The tissue stops nearly all the flour. All three bowls are brown and salty. Bowl 3 may still be slightly hazy. Write what you really see.
  - Check: In the Three Filters Test, why do you stir the mixture before every pour? → **So each filter gets the same mixture**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your table. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Which things did no filter hold back?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Which things got through every filter?
  - Ask: Why did the salt pass through every filter? (good: It dissolved into particles smaller than the holes.; It is far too small to be caught.)
  - Ask: Your blood holds useful salt and sugar. What happens to them at a filter in your kidney? (good: They pass through with the water.; The kidney must get them back somehow.)
  - Note: The reason: a filter sorts only by size. Leaves are bigger than the strainer's holes. Flour grains are bigger than the tissue's holes. Dissolved salt and tea colour are single particles, thousands of times smaller than any hole in a tissue. The filters in a kidney have much finer holes. They hold back blood cells and large proteins. But salt, sugar and urea pass through together. So filtering alone cannot clean blood. The kidney needs a second step, which is to take back the salt, the sugar and most of the water.
  - Think: If no filter can separate salt from water, how might the kidney get its salt back?
### e2 · After Two Tumblers: how soon do your kidneys answer? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** You drink two tumblers of water at 3 o'clock. How long is it until the extra water leaves as urine? How do the colour and the amount of urine change, compared with an ordinary afternoon?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many minutes after the two tumblers will you first need the toilet? Will the urine be paler, darker or the same as usual? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Do both afternoons at home, with the same kind of lunch and the same kind of quiet play. On both days, use the toilet at 3 o'clock, and then record every visit until 6 o'clock. On the ordinary day, drink only a few sips if you are thirsty. On the water day, drink two tumblers at 3 o'clock. Only one thing changes: the two tumblers of water.
- **record:** A colour chart with four boxes: 1 nearly clear, 2 pale yellow, 3 yellow, 4 dark yellow. A table for each day with the time, the colour shade and the count for every toilet visit.
- **results table:** Day | Time of each toilet visit | Colour shade, 1 to 4 | How long the stream lasted, in counts
- **schedule:** day 1: The ordinary afternoon. Draw the colour chart. Record every toilet visit from 3 o'clock to 6 o'clock. (10 min); day 2: The water afternoon. Drink two tumblers at 3 o'clock. Record every visit until 6 o'clock, then compare the two days. (15 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. Make the colour chart with your child: colour four boxes from nearly clear to dark yellow, or pick four yellow things at home to match the shades. Ask for the guess about tomorrow, and the reason. Write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Tomorrow at 3 o'clock you will drink two tumblers of water. How many minutes later will you first need the toilet? Will the urine look different? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Colour four shades of yellow, or pick four yellow things. Guess how many minutes until you need the toilet.
  - Ask: Why do you think it will take that long? (good: The water has to go from the stomach into the blood first.; The kidneys need time to notice.; It goes straight through, so it will be quick.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children think water runs straight from the stomach to the bladder.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. At 3 o'clock your child uses the toilet. This visit is not recorded. Until 6 o'clock they record every visit: the time, the shade, and how long the stream lasted, counted slowly.  
  *Parent says:* This is the ordinary afternoon. Use the toilet now, to start with an empty bladder. After each visit until 6 o'clock, write the time, the shade and your count.  
  *Child mode:* Use the toilet at 3 o'clock. After that, write down every visit.
  - Note: Counting 'one, two, three' during the stream is a simple measure of the amount. There may be no visit at all in three hours. Write that down. It is a result.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2. Give the same kind of lunch. At 3 o'clock your child uses the toilet, then drinks two tumblers of water within 10 minutes. Until 6 o'clock they record every visit as before.  
  *Parent says:* This is the water afternoon. Use the toilet, then drink both tumblers. Now watch the clock. Tell me the moment you first need to go.  
  *Child mode:* Use the toilet. Drink two tumblers. Write down every visit until 6 o'clock.
  - Note: Two tumblers are a safe amount for a child of nine. Do not give more. Most children first need the toilet 30 to 60 minutes after drinking, and go two or three times.
  - Wait: Do this the day after the ordinary afternoon
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2, after 6 o'clock. Put the two tables side by side. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. Then read them the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at both days. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. How many minutes passed before the first visit on the water day?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both tables. Which day had paler urine?
  - Ask: The water did not come out at once. Where was it during that time? (good: In the stomach and gut, then in the blood.; The kidneys had to filter it out of the blood.)
  - Ask: Why was the urine paler on the water day? (good: More water left with the same wastes.; The yellow was thinned out.)
  - Note: The reason: water goes from the gut into the blood in a few minutes. The brain senses that the blood is more watery and sends less of the messenger ADH. The tubes in the kidneys then take back less water. The extra water reaches the bladder within about half an hour to an hour. If your child was short of water before the test, less comes out. The body kept what it needed. Vitamin tablets can turn urine bright yellow, and beetroot can turn it pink.
  - Check: After two tumblers of water, why does urine become paler? → **The same wastes leave in more water**

## Applications
- Dialysis machines clean the blood of people whose kidneys have failed. The blood flows past a thin sheet with tiny holes, and urea passes out through the holes.
- A urine test at a clinic can find illness early. Sugar in the urine points to diabetes, and protein points to damaged filters.
- Many home water purifiers push water through a sheet with very fine holes. Like a kidney's filter, the sheet needs pressure to work.
- On the International Space Station, machines clean the crew's urine and sweat and turn them back into drinking water.
- Traffic police use a breath test to find drivers who have drunk alcohol. It works because alcohol leaves the blood through the lungs.
- Farmers spread urea on their fields as food for crops. Factories make this urea from the nitrogen in air.

- A dog panting on a hot afternoon in Nagpur or Vijayawada. Dogs have few sweat glands, so they lose heat and water from the tongue.

## Open questions
- **Why can humans not grow new nephrons after birth?** A baby is born with all the nephrons they will ever have. Zebrafish grow new ones all their lives. Scientists do not yet know how to make a human kidney do this.
- **Can a kidney grown in a laboratory be made to work in a person?** In 2015 Melissa Little and her team in Australia grew tiny kidney-like organs from human cells. They have nephrons, but no way yet to carry urine out or to connect to a blood supply.
- **Can a pig's kidney work for years inside a human?** In 2024 surgeons in the United States placed kidneys from specially bred pigs into living patients. Nobody yet knows how long such a kidney can last.
- **Why are so many farm workers in some hot places losing their kidneys?** Many farm workers have kidney disease with no known cause. This is seen in the Uddanam area of Andhra Pradesh, in Sri Lanka and in Central America. Heat, too little water and something in the water or soil are all suspected.
- **How much water does a person really need each day?** It depends on body size, weather, food and work. Studies give a wide range, and no single number fits everyone.

## Connected maths
- An adult's kidneys filter 180 litres a day. The watery part of an adult's blood is about 3 litres. About how many times a day is it filtered? **About 60 times a day.**. 180 divided by 3 is 60. The same 3 litres pass through the filters again and again, about 60 times in a day. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- An adult's kidneys filter 180 litres a day and pass 1 and a half litres as urine. How many litres are taken back? Is that more or less than 99 parts in 100? **178 and a half litres are taken back. That is a little more than 99 parts in 100.**. 180 minus 1 and a half is 178 and a half. One part in 100 of 180 litres is 1.8 litres. The urine is less than that, so more than 99 parts are taken back. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In the Three Filters Test, the strainer stopped leaves only. The cloth stopped leaves and some flour. The tissue stopped leaves and nearly all the flour. A fourth filter has even finer holes than tissue. What might it stop? Could any filter with holes stop salt? **It might stop the last of the flour. A filter could stop dissolved salt only if its holes were smaller than a salt particle.**. Each finer filter stopped smaller things than the one before. Dissolved salt particles are thousands of times smaller than the holes in tissue. The pattern says that a filter stops whatever is bigger than its holes. (queues `math.logic.if-then.L4`)
- On a water afternoon, two tumblers were drunk at 3:00. The toilet visits were at 3:45, 4:20 and 5:10. How long was it until the first visit? Which gap between visits was longest, and what might that show? **45 minutes until the first visit. The longest gap was the last one, 50 minutes. It suggests that the extra water was nearly gone.**. From 3:00 to 3:45 is 45 minutes. From 3:45 to 4:20 is 35 minutes. From 4:20 to 5:10 is 50 minutes. As the extra water leaves, the bladder fills more slowly. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- A packet of chips weighs 50 grams. Its label says 800 milligrams of sodium in 100 grams. Salt is 2 and a half times the sodium. How much salt is in the packet? How many such packets hold 5 grams of salt? **1 gram of salt in one packet. Five packets hold 5 grams.**. 50 grams is half of 100 grams, so the packet has 400 milligrams of sodium. 400 times 2 and a half is 1,000 milligrams, which is 1 gram of salt. 5 times 1 gram is 5 grams, about one teaspoon. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- One tissue filter clears a tumbler of flour water in 8 minutes. How long would 2 such filters take, sharing the water? And 4 filters? A kidney has about a million filters. What does the pattern tell you? **2 filters take 4 minutes. 4 filters take 2 minutes. With a million filters, even very slow, fine filters clear a lot of liquid quickly.**. Twice as many filters halve the time. 8 divided by 2 is 4, and 8 divided by 4 is 2. The time keeps falling as filters are added, and the filters stay just as fine. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A nine-year-old's bladder holds about 300 millilitres. By day the kidneys send it about 40 millilitres each hour. In how many hours would it fill? Most children sleep 10 hours without a toilet visit. How can that be? **It would fill in about 7 and a half hours. The kidneys must make less urine at night.**. 300 divided by 40 is 7 and a half. Ten hours of sleep is longer than that. So the night rate must be lower than 40 millilitres an hour, and it is. At night the tubes take back more water. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
