# Sun, Moon and stars: what lights and warms our sky
`sci.sky.sun-moon-stars.L1`

**Summary.** Hold your hand in a patch of sunshine, and it soon feels warm. The Sun gives us light and heat, even though it is very far away. At night the Sun's light is gone from our sky, and we can see the Moon and the stars. The Moon makes no light of its own: it shines because the Sun lights it up. The stars are there in the daytime too. In a torch test, your child finds out why we cannot see them by day.

**Intro.** *Hold your hand in a patch of sunshine for a slow count of twenty. Now hold it in the shade. What do you feel?* In the sunshine your hand gets warm. In the shade it stays cooler. The Sun is very, very far away, but its light still reaches your hand and warms it. At night that sunlight is gone from our sky, and the Moon and the stars show up. But where are the stars in the daytime? In this unit you find out.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that the Sun gives us light and heat.
1. By the end I can show that things in sunshine get warmer than things in the shade.
2. By the end I can say that the Moon makes no light of its own: the Sun lights it up.
3. By the end I can say that the stars are in the sky in the daytime too, and that the bright day sky hides them.
4. By the end I know I must never look straight at the Sun.

## Terms
- **Sun**: The Sun is a star. It is a huge ball of very hot gas that gives us light and heat. *e.g.* Sunlight on a balcony railing makes the steel warm to touch.
- **Star**: A star is a huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light. The stars at night are so far away that they look like tiny dots. *e.g.* On a clear night you can see a few bright stars from a balcony, even in a city.
- **Moon**: The Moon is a big round ball of rock that goes round the Earth. It makes no light of its own. It shines because sunlight falls on it. *e.g.* On a full Moon night, the Moon looks like a bright round plate.
- **Shade**: Shade is a place where sunlight is blocked, so it is cooler and less bright. *e.g.* On a hot day, people wait for the bus in the shade of a tree.

## Explanations
### The Sun gives light and heat · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sun is a star. It gives out light and heat all the time. When its light falls on something, that thing gets warmer.

In the ice test, the ice cube in the sunshine melted much faster than the one in the shade. The sunshine brought heat to it. The Sun is about 150 million kilometres away. That is so far that a car would take more than a hundred years to drive there. Its light still reaches us in about 8 minutes, and it warms everything it falls on: the floor, the water in a tank, your skin and the leaves of plants. Plants use sunlight to make their food, and humans and other animals eat plants.
- e.g. In the ice test, the ice cube in the sunshine melted first.
- e.g. Clothes on a sunny balcony dry faster than clothes in the shade.
- e.g. A crow sits in the morning sun with puffed-out feathers to get warm.
### The Moon shines with the Sun's light · *defined*
**Definition:** The Moon makes no light of its own. Sunlight falls on the Moon, and some of it bounces off and comes to our eyes.

The Moon looks bright at night, so many children think it makes light, like a lamp. It does not. The Moon is a ball of grey rock. The Sun shines on it, the way a torch shines on a steel plate. Some of that light bounces off the Moon and reaches us. That is why the Moon looks bright even when it is night where we are. We often see the Moon in the day too, as a pale white shape in the blue sky.
- e.g. A steel plate looks bright only when a lamp shines on it. The Moon looks bright only because the Sun shines on it.
- e.g. On some mornings you can see a pale Moon in the blue sky.
- e.g. In 2023, India's Chandrayaan-3 landed on the Moon and found grey rock and dust there.
### The stars are in the sky in the daytime too · *defined*
**Definition:** The stars shine all the time, by day and by night. In the day, sunlight makes the whole sky so bright that we cannot see the stars' small light.

In the torch test, the little torch star was hard to see in the bright spot by the window. In the dark room, the same torch star shone brightly. The torch was the same both times. Only the light around it changed. The real stars work the same way. In the day, the Sun's light lights up the air, and the whole sky glows blue. The stars are still there, but their light is too faint to see in that bright sky. When the Sun sets and the sky goes dark, we can see them again.
- e.g. In the torch test, the star was hard to see by the sunny window and bright in the dark room.
- e.g. During a total eclipse of the Sun, the Moon covers the Sun for a few minutes. The sky goes dark and bright stars appear in the middle of the day.
- e.g. Big telescopes can find bright stars even in the daytime.
### Why a city sky shows fewer stars · *defined*
**Definition:** Street lights, shop signs and lit windows light up the air over a city at night. Then only the brightest stars can be seen.

On a clear night in a big city, you may see only a few stars. In a village far from city lights, the sky can be full of thousands of them. The stars are the same. The city lights make the night air glow a little, the way the Sun makes the day sky glow a lot. The faint stars get hidden, and only the brightest ones show. Scientists call this light pollution: too much light at night, in places where it is not needed.
- e.g. From a city balcony you may count only five or ten stars.
- e.g. Far from towns, in the Thar desert or the hills of Ladakh, the sky can be full of stars.
- e.g. Switch off the lights near a window at night, and a few more stars show.

## Mostly agreed
- The Sun is a star. It gives the Earth light and heat. *How we know:* Anyone can feel the Sun's warmth in a patch of sunshine. Scientists have measured the Sun's light from the ground and from spacecraft, such as India's Aditya-L1, which was launched in 2023 to study the Sun.
- The Moon makes no light of its own. It shines with sunlight. *How we know:* The Moon's lit side always faces the Sun. Spacecraft that landed on the Moon, such as India's Chandrayaan-3 in 2023, found grey rock and dust, not anything that glows.
- The stars are in the sky in the daytime too. *How we know:* During a total eclipse of the Sun, bright stars can be seen in the daytime sky. Big telescopes can see bright stars in the day.

## How we found out
**Annie Maunder, an astronomer from Ireland, 22 January 1898.** For a long time, many people thought the stars went away in the daytime, or only came out at night. But people who watched a total eclipse of the Sun saw something strange. For a few minutes, the Moon covered the whole Sun. The sky went dark in the middle of the day, and bright stars and planets appeared. They had been there all along. One person who travelled far to see an eclipse was Annie Maunder, an astronomer from Ireland. In January 1898 she came to India, to a village called Talni, where the eclipse could be seen. She carried a small camera, bought with a little money from her old college. When the Moon covered the Sun, she photographed the Sun's faint outer glow, which can be seen only in an eclipse. Her picture showed a streamer of glow longer than anyone had photographed before. For many years, her pictures were often printed with her husband's name instead of hers. Today big telescopes can find bright stars even in the daytime sky.  
*What people believed before:* People thought the stars went away in the daytime. Total eclipses of the Sun showed that when the Sun's light is blocked, the stars can be seen at midday, so they are there all the time.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Moon makes its own light, like a lamp.** (A common idea among young children, and many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: The Moon is the brightest thing in the night sky, and it can light up a path at night.  
  Against: The Moon is a ball of grey rock. Its bright side always faces the Sun. When the Moon goes into the Earth's shadow in an eclipse of the Moon, it goes dark red and dim, because sunlight cannot reach it.
- **The stars go away in the daytime, and come out at night.** (A common idea among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: We see stars only at night. In the day the sky looks empty of them.  
  Against: In the torch test, the torch star was still shining by the sunny window. It was only hard to see. During a total eclipse of the Sun, stars appear in the middle of the day, because the sky goes dark.

## Roots
We have felt the Sun's heat, and found that a small light can shine without being seen. Now we ask what that tells us about the sky, about ourselves and about the world.

**Deep truth.** By the sunny window, our torch star was hard to see. In the dark room, it shone brightly. The torch did not change, and the real stars do not change from day to night either. They shine all day, but we see them only when the sky around them is dark.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say the stars 'come out' at night, as if they switch on like street lights. The torch test shows that a star can stay the same while the light around it changes.  
*Evidence:* In the torch star test, the same star was faint by the bright window and bright in the dark room. In a total eclipse of the Sun, stars appear at midday.

- *who am i:* You are someone who lives in sunshine. The warmth on your skin and the light in your eyes come from the Sun.
- *what is life:* Almost all life on Earth runs on the Sun. Plants turn their leaves to the sunshine and make their food from it, and humans and other animals eat the plants.
- *what is this world:* The world is under a sky full of stars, all day and all night. Our Sun is one of them, the one close enough to warm us.

The Sun is one star among countless others. In the day, its light fills our sky so brightly that all the other stars are hidden. At night, our side of the Earth turns away from the Sun, and the other stars can be seen again. The same Sun lights the Moon, warms the ice, dries the chillies and feeds the plants. The Sun, the Moon and the stars are aware in their own way, like every part of the world.

- **Rig Veda 1.50.2 (a hymn to Surya, the Sun):** The stars slip away like thieves, together with their beams, before the all-seeing Sun. (The old hymn says what everyone sees: the stars vanish at sunrise. The torch test shows that they do not really go anywhere. The Sun's bright light hides them.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 15:** The face of truth is covered by a golden lid. Open it, so that I can see. (Something very bright can hide what is behind it. The golden light of the Sun covers the day sky, and the stars behind it cannot be seen.)
- Science (settled): The stars shine all the time. Sunlight lights up the air and makes the day sky bright and blue, and that hides the much fainter light of the stars.
- Science (settled): The Sun is a star. The Moon makes no light of its own, and shines with sunlight.
- Other view, Many scientists today: They say the Sun, the Moon and the stars are not aware of anything. To them, the Sun is only very hot gas, and the Moon is only rock. They think awareness belongs to living beings with nerves or brains.
- Practice, One star, five breaths (3 min): On a clear evening, stand at the window with your child and switch off the room light. Find one star together. Breathe in slowly, and out slowly, five times, looking at that star. Then say: this star was shining all day, while we could not see it. If no star shows, sit by a sunny window in the day instead. Close your eyes and feel the warm light on your eyelids.
- Ask: The stars were there all day, though nobody could see them. Is something real even when nobody sees it?
- Ask: The Sun's light hides the other stars. Can something very bright stop us seeing something else?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the torch test, the star was still shining by the sunny window, but you could hardly see it. What else might be there all the time that we cannot see? (hint: Think of the stars in the day, the Moon on a cloudy night, or the air around you.)
- *deep:* The Moon has no light of its own, but it lights up the night. Is it right to call the Moon bright? (hint: Think of a steel plate next to a lamp. Where does its brightness come from?)
- *deep:* The ice in the sunshine melted first. How did the heat get from the Sun, so far away, to the ice? (hint: Nothing touched the ice except sunlight. The heat came with the light.)
- *big:* Every star at night is a sun. Could a child far away, near another star, be looking at our Sun as a tiny dot tonight? (hint: Our Sun is a star too. From very far away, it would look like a small dot.)

## Story: Tara and the Hidden Stars
*Ajji means grandmother in Kannada. Tara, Manu, Amma and Ajji are made up. Villages far from towns have much darker night skies than cities, so many more stars can be seen there.*

Tara was six. She lived in a tall block of flats in Bengaluru, with her Amma and her little brother Manu.

From their balcony, Tara could see the Moon on many nights. But she could only ever count three or four stars. "Where are all the stars from my picture books?" she asked.

In the summer holidays, Amma took them on a long bus ride to a village in Karnataka. Their grandmother, Ajji, lived there in a small house with a flat roof.

The days in the village were hot and bright. Every morning, Ajji spread red chillies on a mat in the sunshine. "The Sun will dry them for us," she said.

Tara put her hand on the mat at noon. It was warm, almost hot. Then she put her hand on the floor inside the house, where no sunlight fell. It was cool.

"The sunshine warmed the mat," said Tara.

"Yes," said Ajji. "The Sun is very far away. But its light comes all the way here, and it brings heat with it."

That night, after dinner, Ajji took the children up onto the roof. She switched off the bulb by the stairs.

Tara looked up, and gasped. The sky was full of stars. There were big bright ones and tiny faint ones, more than she could ever count.

"Ajji!" said Tara. "The village has so many more stars than Bengaluru!"

Ajji smiled. "It is the same sky, Tara. The same stars are over Bengaluru tonight. But the city has so many lights that the night air glows. The faint stars get hidden. Here it is dark, so we can see them all."

Manu pointed at the Moon. It was a half Moon, high over the coconut trees. "Is the Moon a big lamp?" he asked.

"No," said Ajji. "The Moon makes no light of its own. The Sun shines on the Moon, the way a lamp shines on your steel tumbler. The Moon sends some of that sunlight down to us."

The next morning, Tara ran up to the roof. The sky was bright blue. There was not a single star.

"Ajji, where did the stars go?" she called.

Ajji came up with a small torch and a piece of cardboard. She fixed the cardboard over the front of the torch with a rubber band. She poked one tiny hole in it with a pencil point, and taped a bit of thin paper over the hole.

"This is our star," she said. She switched on the torch and stood in the bright sunshine, a few steps away. "Can you see it?"

Tara looked hard. She could only just see a dull little dot. Manu could not see it at all.

Then Ajji took them down into the dark storeroom, where the rice sacks were kept. She shut the door. The little torch star shone bright and clear.

"It was shining in the sunshine too!" said Tara slowly. "We just couldn't see it."

"That is right," said Ajji. "The real stars are like that. They shine all day. But the Sun lights up the whole sky, so it is too bright to see them."

"So the stars are up there right now?" asked Manu.

"Right now," said Ajji.

Back in Bengaluru, on the first night home, Tara stood on the balcony. She counted four stars. Then she switched off the balcony light and waited. She counted six.

"The others are there too," she told Manu. "They are just hidden."

**For the parent:** Tara finds that the stars never go away. The bright day sky hides them, and city lights hide the faint ones at night. On the way she also feels that sunshine brings heat, and learns from Ajji that the Moon shines only with the Sun's light.

- Ask: Why was the mat in the sunshine warm, and the floor inside cool?
- Ask: Why could Tara see so many more stars from Ajji's roof?
- Ask: Where does the Moon's light come from?
- Ask: In the morning, where were the stars?

## Experiments
### e1 · Where Do the Stars Go in the Day? The torch star test (22 min)
- **question:** Does a small light still shine in a bright place? Can we see it there?
- **predict:** We will look at the torch star by a bright window, and then in a dark room. Where will you see it best? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same torch, the same tiny hole and the same thin paper every time. You stand the same distance away, three big steps, every time. Only one thing changes: how bright it is around the star.
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun. Keep the Sun off to one side, out of your child's view.
- **record:** Draw the torch star twice: once by the bright window, and once in the dark room. Draw it as big and bright as it looked each time.
- **results table:** Where the star was | My guess: will I see it? | What I saw: bright, faint, or not at all
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child where the stars are in the daytime. Listen, and don't correct the answer.  
  *Parent says:* At night we can see stars in the sky. Where do you think the stars are in the daytime?  
  *Child mode:* Where do the stars go in the day? What do you think?
  - Ask: Where are the stars in the day? (good: They go away.; They go to sleep.; They are still there.; They are on the other side of the sky.)
  - Note: Many children say the stars go away, go to sleep or switch off. Any answer is fine. The test will answer it.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the cardboard over the front of the torch. 2. Hold it on with a rubber band or tape. 3. Poke one tiny hole in the middle with a sharp pencil point. 4. Tape one small square of thin paper over the hole. 5. Switch the torch on to check that only a dim dot of light comes out.  
  *Parent says:* We are making a pretend star. The torch light can only come out of this tiny hole, through a thin paper. That makes one small dim dot of light, like a star.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the grown-up make the star. Light comes out of one tiny hole.
  - Note: Push the pencil gently, with the torch lying on a table, away from fingers. The paper makes the dot dim, as real stars are. If the cardboard glows all over, use thicker cardboard or two layers.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess. Write or draw the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* First we will hold the star by the bright window. Then we will take it into the dark bathroom. Where will you see it best? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Where will you see the star best? By the window, or in the dark? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: It is the same torch, so it will look the same.; It will look brighter in the dark.; In the sunshine it will get lost.)
  - Note: Wait for a guess and a reason. Do not say what will happen.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand at the brightest window, or on the balcony, in the day. 2. Your child stands three big steps away, facing you. 3. Switch the star on and point it at your child. 4. Ask what they see.  
  *Parent says:* Here is our star, shining. Look at the hole in the cardboard. Can you see the star? Is it bright, faint, or can you not see it at all?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the hole in the cardboard. Can you see the star?
  - Ask: What do you see? (good: I can only just see it.; It is very faint.; I can't see it.)
  - Note: Stand so the Sun is off to one side, not in your child's view. By a bright window, the dim star should look very faint or vanish. If your child still sees it clearly, tape on another square of paper and look again. Use that same star in the dark room. Let your child walk one step closer and back again.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go into a dark room, such as a bathroom, and shut the door. 2. Switch off the light. 3. Stand three big steps apart again. 4. Switch the star on.  
  *Parent says:* Same torch, same hole, same three steps. Now we are in the dark. Can you see the star now? Is it bright or faint?  
  *Child mode:* Same star, now in the dark. Can you see it? Bright or faint?
  - Ask: What is different now? (good: Now it is bright.; I can see it clearly.; It looks like a real star.)
  - Note: In the dark, the same dim dot looks bright and sharp. This is the surprise. If you cannot see it in the dark either, take off one square of paper, then look again by the window with the same star. A little light under the door is fine.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Keep the star shining. Switch the room light on, then off, then on again. Ask your child to watch the star each time.  
  *Parent says:* I am switching on the room light. Watch the star. Now I am switching it off. What happens to the star?  
  *Child mode:* Watch the star. The room light goes on. Then off. What happens?
  - Ask: Did the torch stop shining when the room light came on? (good: No, it was still on.; It was still shining, but hard to see.)
  - Note: The room light is like the Sun coming up. The star does not stop shining. The light around it makes it hard to see.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Back in the bright room, look at the guesses. Ask your child what they think now, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Was the star shining by the window? Why was it hard to see there? So where do you think the real stars are in the daytime?  
  *Child mode:* Was the star shining by the window? Where are the real stars in the day?
  - Ask: Where are the real stars in the day? (good: Still in the sky, shining.; They are there, but the sky is too bright to see them.)
  - Check: Nila, a girl in Class 1, holds the torch star by a sunny window. Her brother can hardly see it. Why? → **The bright light around it hides the star**
### e2 · Sun or Shade: which ice cube melts first? (20 min)
- **question:** Does an ice cube melt faster in sunshine or in shade?
- **predict:** One ice cube goes in the sunshine, one in the shade. Which will melt first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use two ice cubes of the same size, from the same tray. We use two bowls that are the same. We put them down at the same time, and we look at them at the same times.
- **safety:** Never look at the Sun. A sunny floor or railing can be hot, so touch it gently first.
- **record:** Draw the two bowls after 10 minutes. Show how big each ice cube was, and how much water was in each bowl.
- **results table:** Time | Ice in the sunshine | Ice in the shade
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find a patch of sunshine and a shady spot nearby. Let your child touch the floor in both places.  
  *Parent says:* Put your hand on the floor in the sunshine. Now in the shade. Which feels warmer?  
  *Child mode:* Touch the floor in the sun. Now in the shade. Which is warmer?
  - Note: If no sunshine comes into your home, use a sunny windowsill or balcony. On a cloudy day, try again on a sunny one.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the two ice cubes in their bowls. Ask your child to guess, and why.  
  *Parent says:* One ice cube will sit in the sunshine. One will sit in the shade. Which one will melt first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which ice cube will melt first? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The Sun is hot.; Sunshine makes things warm.; They will melt the same, because it is the same ice.)
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put one bowl in the sunshine and one in the shade, at the same time. Note the time.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? We put them down together. Now we wait five minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Put them down together. Now we wait five minutes.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at both bowls after 5 minutes and after 10 minutes. Each time, your child says which ice is smaller and which bowl has more water.  
  *Parent says:* Look into both bowls. Which ice cube is smaller? Which bowl has more water in it?  
  *Child mode:* Look in both bowls. Which ice is smaller? Which has more water?
  - Ask: What do you see? (good: The ice in the sunshine is smaller.; The sunny bowl has more water.)
  - Note: In strong sunshine, the ice in the sun is clearly smaller after 5 to 10 minutes. In winter or weak sunshine, wait a little longer.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what they found and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which ice melted first? Nothing touched it except sunshine. So what did the sunshine bring?

## Applications
- Solar panels on roofs and at railway stations turn sunlight into electricity.
- Families dry clothes, papad, vadam and chillies in the sunshine, because the Sun's heat dries them.
- Solar water heaters on many flat roofs use the Sun's heat to warm bath water.
- India's Aditya-L1, launched in 2023, is a spacecraft that watches the Sun all the time. Its project director was Nigar Shaji, a scientist from Tenkasi in Tamil Nadu.
- Some cities and parks keep their lights low at night so that people, birds and insects can see the stars and the dark again.

## Spotted around us
- At Pongal in Tamil Nadu, families cook sweet pongal outside in the morning sunshine, to thank the Sun for the harvest.
- At Chhath Puja in Bihar, people stand in rivers and ponds at sunset and sunrise to greet the Sun.
- At the Sun Temple in Konark, Odisha, the temple is built like a giant chariot for the Sun, with huge stone wheels.
- On Sharad Purnima, many families in Gujarat and Maharashtra sit out under the full Moon and share sweet milk.
- In the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, far from town lights, the night sky is full of stars.
- On rooftops in Kerala and Karnataka, people spread coconut pieces and chillies on mats to dry in the sun.
- At Hanle in Ladakh, India has an observatory and a dark-sky reserve, where lights are kept low so the stars can be seen.

## Open questions
- **How many stars are there?** Our galaxy alone has hundreds of billions of stars, and there are billions of galaxies. Nobody can count them all, and scientists' best guesses still differ.
- **Is anyone living near another star, looking at our Sun?** Scientists have found thousands of planets going round other stars. Nobody knows yet if any of them has life.
- **Why does the Sun's outer glow get hotter than the Sun's surface?** The Sun's faint outer glow, the corona, is much hotter than the bright surface below it. Scientists have ideas about why, and spacecraft like Aditya-L1 are studying it, but they do not know for sure yet.
