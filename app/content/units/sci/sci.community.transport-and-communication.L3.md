# Transport and communication: how people, goods and messages travel
`sci.community.transport-and-communication.L3`

**Summary.** The rice in your kitchen may have come hundreds of kilometres by truck or train. A phone call reaches Ammamma in Mysuru in less than a second. Transport is how people and goods move from place to place: by road, rail, water, air and pipeline. Communication is how messages and ideas travel: by speech, signs, letters, phones, radio, TV and the internet. In this unit you follow how both grew, from footpaths and boats to metros and satellites. You learn what road signs and traffic lights mean, and how transport dirties the air. Then you test, in a dark room, which clothes a driver can see from furthest away.

**Intro.** *Pick up one packet from your kitchen shelf, such as rice, salt or tea. Read where it was packed. Who carried it to your home, and how?* Almost nothing in your home was made in your home. Salt may come from Thoothukudi or Gujarat. Tea may come from the Nilgiris or Assam. It came by truck, train or ship, and then by a shop's van or a delivery scooter. People also need to send messages: to order the rice, to pay for it, to say 'it has arrived'. In this unit you find out how people learned to carry things and messages further and faster. You also find out how roads keep people safe, and test whether a driver can really see you at night.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can tell how transport grew, from walking and boats to wheels, trains, ships and aeroplanes.
1. By the end I can name the kinds of transport (road, rail, water, air and pipeline) and say what each is best for.
2. By the end I can explain how communication grew, and tell one-way communication from two-way communication.
3. By the end I can read common road signs and traffic lights, and say what their shapes and colours mean.
4. By the end I can explain, using my dark-room test, why white clothes and reflective strips keep a walker safe at night.
5. By the end I can say how transport harms the air and our health, and choose ways to travel that pollute less.

## Terms
- **Transport**: Transport means moving people or goods from one place to another. It can be by road, rail, water, air or pipeline. *e.g.* A train carrying rice from Andhra Pradesh to Kerala is transport. So is your walk to the shop.
- **Communication**: Communication means sharing messages, feelings and ideas with others. It can be by talking, signs, writing, phones, radio, TV or the internet. *e.g.* Waving goodbye, writing a letter and making a video call are all communication.
- **Vehicle**: A vehicle is a machine that carries people or goods, such as a bus, a cycle, an auto, a boat or an aeroplane. *e.g.* An auto-rickshaw is a three-wheeled vehicle.
- **Wheel and axle**: A wheel is a round frame that turns. The axle is the rod at its centre that the wheel turns around. Together they let a heavy load roll instead of being dragged. *e.g.* A bullock cart has two big wooden wheels joined by one axle.
- **Pipeline**: A pipeline is a long pipe that carries a liquid or a gas over a long distance, such as water, petrol or cooking gas. *e.g.* In some cities, cooking gas comes to the kitchen through a pipe, not in a cylinder.
- **Traffic signal**: A traffic signal is a set of lights that tells vehicles when to stop and go. Red means stop. Amber means get ready to stop. Green means go. *e.g.* At a busy junction in Hyderabad, cars stop at the red light.
- **Road sign**: A road sign is a board by the road that gives a message with a shape, a colour and a picture. Most road signs use no words, so anyone can understand them. *e.g.* A red circle with a crossed-out horn means 'Do not sound your horn here'.
- **Zebra crossing**: A zebra crossing is a set of wide white stripes painted across a road. It shows where people on foot may cross, and vehicles must stop for them. *e.g.* Children cross at the zebra crossing outside a school in Madurai.
- **Reflect**: To reflect is to send light back. Light from a lamp hits a thing, and some of it bounces off. We see the thing when that light reaches our eyes. A white cloth reflects much more light than a black cloth. *e.g.* A white shirt reflects a lot of a car's headlight. A black shirt reflects very little.
- **Reflective strip**: A reflective strip is a shiny band that sends light straight back towards the lamp it came from. It shines brightly to the driver whose headlights hit it. *e.g.* Many school bags, helmets and cycle pedals have reflective strips.
- **One-way communication**: In one-way communication, the message goes from the sender to the receiver, and the receiver cannot answer back straight away. *e.g.* A radio programme, a road sign and a newspaper are one-way.
- **Two-way communication**: In two-way communication, both people can send and receive messages, so the listener can ask questions. *e.g.* A phone call and a talk at the dinner table are two-way.
- **Mass communication**: Mass communication sends one message to a very large number of people at once. *e.g.* A cricket match on TV reaches millions of homes at the same time.
- **Satellite**: A satellite is something that goes round the Earth again and again. People send machines up into space to be satellites. Communication satellites pass on phone, TV and internet signals. *e.g.* Your TV dish points up at a satellite far above the Earth.
- **PIN code**: A PIN code is the 6-digit number in an Indian address. PIN stands for Postal Index Number. It tells the post office which area a letter must go to. *e.g.* The PIN code 600001 is for an area in Chennai.
- **Pollution**: Pollution is anything harmful that we add to air, water or land. Vehicle smoke and loud horns are two kinds of pollution. *e.g.* A bus with an old engine leaves a cloud of black smoke behind.
- **Exhaust**: Exhaust is the smoke and gas that come out of a vehicle's engine when it burns petrol or diesel. It has carbon dioxide and tiny specks of soot in it. *e.g.* You can smell the exhaust behind a bus at a bus stop.

## Explanations
### Why a dark shirt is hard to see at night · *defined*
**Definition:** We see a thing only when light from it reaches our eyes. At night, a driver sees a walker only by the headlight that bounces off the walker and comes back. White and pale colours reflect much more light than black and dark colours.

In the dark-room test, the same dim torch shone on every cloth. The black cloth vanished a few steps away, because it reflected very little of the light. The white cloth reflected most of the light, so it stayed visible much further away. A reflective strip is special. It sends light straight back towards the lamp, so it shines brightly to the person holding the torch, or the driver behind the headlights. A driver going fast needs to see a walker from far away, to have time to slow down. So at night, walkers should wear pale clothes and something reflective, and carry a torch.
- e.g. A walker in a white mundu or a white school shirt is seen from much further away than one in a black shirt.
- e.g. A cycle's red back reflector shines brightly in a car's headlight, though it has no battery.
- e.g. Traffic police at night wear jackets with reflective stripes.
### How transport grew · *defined*
**Definition:** Transport grew from walking and carrying, to boats on rivers, to animals and carts with wheels, to engines that burn coal, petrol or diesel, and to electric trains and aeroplanes.

The first transport was walking. People carried loads on their heads and backs, and made footpaths by walking the same way again and again. Boats are older than wheels. A wooden canoe found in the Netherlands is about 10,000 years old. The wheel was invented about 5,500 years ago. Clay toy carts with wheels, about 4,500 years old, have been found at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro. Animals pulled carts for thousands of years. Then steam engines came, and India's first passenger train ran from Bombay to Thane in 1853. Cars, buses, aeroplanes and metros followed. Each change carried more people and goods, faster and further.
- e.g. The first passenger train in South India ran from Royapuram in Madras in 1856.
- e.g. Kattumaram, the Tamil name for a raft of logs tied together, gave English the word 'catamaran'.
- e.g. Kochi started India's first water metro, with electric boats, in 2023.
### The kinds of transport · *defined*
**Definition:** There are five main kinds of transport: road, rail, water, air and pipeline. Each is best for different journeys.

Road transport, such as cycles, autos, buses and trucks, goes almost everywhere, right up to the door. Rail transport carries many people and heavy goods a long way, cheaply, but only where there are tracks. Water transport by boats and ships is the cheapest way to carry very heavy goods across seas and along rivers, but it is slow. Air transport is the fastest over long distances, but it costs the most and uses a lot of fuel. Pipelines carry water, petrol and gas without any vehicle at all. Most goods travel by more than one kind. Rice may go by train to a city, then by truck to a shop.
- e.g. A fisherwoman in Kollam takes her fish to the market in an auto: road transport.
- e.g. Coal reaches power stations in long goods trains: rail transport.
- e.g. Big ships carry cars and machines into Chennai port: water transport.
### How communication grew · *defined*
**Definition:** Communication grew from gestures and speech, to drums, smoke and messengers, to writing and the post, and then to electric messages: the telegraph, the telephone, radio, TV and the internet.

People first shared messages face to face, with sounds, faces and hand movements. To reach further, they beat drums, lit fires and sent runners and pigeons. Writing let a message travel without the person who wrote it. India's postal department began in 1854. The telegraph sent messages as short and long pulses of electricity along wires. Telephones carried the voice itself. Radio and TV sent sounds and pictures through the air to millions of homes. India's last telegram was sent in 2013. Today a phone sends messages as radio waves to a tower, and from there through cables or satellites to almost anywhere in the world in under a second.
- e.g. The Radio Club of Madras began radio broadcasts in Chennai in 1924.
- e.g. Odisha police kept a pigeon post service running until 2008.
- e.g. PIN codes began in India on 15 August 1972.
### One-way and two-way communication · *defined*
**Definition:** In one-way communication, the receiver cannot answer back straight away. In two-way communication, both sides can send and receive, so the listener can ask questions.

A road sign, a radio programme and a newspaper are one-way. They can reach very many people at once. But if the message is unclear, nobody can ask what it meant. A phone call or a talk is two-way. The listener can say, 'Do you mean the big circle or the small one?' Two-way talk usually gets the message across more correctly, but it takes longer. This is why road signs must be very simple. A driver going past at speed cannot ask the sign a question.
- e.g. A teacher on a video lesson cannot hear your question. A teacher in the room can.
- e.g. A letter is two-way, but slow: the answer may take a week.
- e.g. A WhatsApp message is two-way, but the reply may come late.
### Road signs and traffic lights · *defined*
**Definition:** Road signs give their message by shape and colour, so they can be read from far away and without words. In India, a circle gives an order, a triangle gives a warning, and a rectangle gives information.

A red circle says what you must not do, such as 'No horn' or 'No entry'. A blue circle says what you must do, such as 'Go straight only'. A triangle with a red border warns you of something ahead, such as a school, a speed breaker or a sharp bend. A blue rectangle gives information, such as the way to a hospital or a petrol pump. The STOP sign is a red eight-sided shape. Traffic lights use red for stop, amber for get ready to stop, and green for go. People on foot have their own lights: a red standing person and a green walking person. These rules let thousands of strangers share a road without crashing into each other.
- e.g. A triangle with two children on it means 'School ahead: slow down'.
- e.g. A red circle with '40' inside means 'Do not go faster than 40 kilometres an hour'.
- e.g. A blue rectangle with a fuel pump picture means 'Petrol pump this way'.
### How transport affects the air and our health · *defined*
**Definition:** Vehicles that burn petrol or diesel give out exhaust. Exhaust has carbon dioxide, which warms the Earth, and tiny specks of soot and harmful gases, which hurt our lungs. Horns and engines also cause noise pollution.

In big cities, much of the dirty air comes from vehicles. Breathing it for years can make people ill, especially children and old people. Carbon dioxide from engines adds to the gases that are warming the whole Earth. Walking and cycling make no exhaust at all. A full bus or metro carries many people with much less fuel for each person than if they all drove. Electric vehicles give out no exhaust on the road, but their electricity often comes from power stations that burn coal. So the cleanest journey is often the one we walk, cycle or share.
- e.g. A Chennai Metro train carries hundreds of people at once on electricity.
- e.g. A cycle ride to the shop makes no smoke and no noise.
- e.g. Some hospital and school zones have 'No horn' signs to cut noise.
### How a radio message gets past the curve of the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Some radio waves bounce off a layer of charged air high above the Earth, called the ionosphere. This lets them reach places far beyond the horizon. Today, satellites also pass on signals across the world.

Radio waves travel in straight lines, like light. The Earth is round, so a straight line soon leaves the ground and heads into space. In 1901, Guglielmo Marconi reported a radio signal sent across the Atlantic Ocean, much further than straight lines should allow. Later, scientists showed that a layer high in the air, the ionosphere, sends some radio waves back down. Now communication satellites go round the Earth high above us. They catch signals from one place and send them down to another, far away.
- e.g. Short-wave radio from other countries can be heard at night in India because of the ionosphere.
- e.g. A live cricket match in Australia reaches TVs in India through satellites and undersea cables.
- e.g. India's INSAT satellites pass on TV and weather signals.
### How other living beings send messages · *debated*
People are not the only beings who communicate. A honeybee who finds flowers comes back and dances on the comb. The direction of her dance tells the other bees which way to fly, and its length tells them how far. Ants leave a trail of smell from food to the nest, and others follow it. Crows give loud calls when they see a cat or a snake, and other crows gather. Plants send messages too. When insects chew a plant's leaves, the plant releases smells into the air, and nearby plants make their own defences ready.
- e.g. Put a grain of sugar near an ant line and watch others come along the same path.
- e.g. A crow cawing loudly at a cat on the wall brings other crows.
- e.g. Freshly cut grass smells strong: those smells are messages the grass sends.
- **Many biologists who study plants:** A plant senses an attack and sends out smells that warn their neighbours, who get ready. That is communication, like a crow's warning call.
- **Some other biologists:** The smells may be meant for the plant's own other leaves, or for insects that eat the attackers. The neighbours may simply be 'overhearing'. They prefer the word 'signalling'.

## Mostly agreed
- At night, drivers see pale clothes and reflective strips from much further away than dark clothes. *How we know:* You can test it with a dim torch in a dark room. Road safety studies with real drivers find the same.
- Boats are much older than wheels. *How we know:* Log canoes about 10,000 years old have been dug up. The oldest wheels found are about 5,500 years old.
- Vehicle exhaust makes city air unhealthy and adds carbon dioxide, which warms the Earth. *How we know:* Air-quality stations in Indian cities measure soot and gases near roads, and doctors find more lung illness where the air is dirtiest.
- Two-way communication usually gets a message across more correctly than one-way communication, but it takes longer. *How we know:* Studies since the 1950s have tested this with people describing shapes, just like the Words Only test.
- Some radio waves are bent back to the ground by the ionosphere, a charged layer high in the air. *How we know:* Edward Appleton measured radio echoes from this layer in 1924, and radio stations use it every day.

## How we found out
**Guglielmo Marconi, Arthur Kennelly, Oliver Heaviside, Edward Appleton and Miles Barnett, 1901 (the signal across the Atlantic), 1902 (the idea of a reflecting layer), 1924 (Appleton and Barnett's proof).** By the 1890s, scientists knew that radio waves travel in straight lines, like light. The Earth is round, so a straight line soon leaves the ground and heads off into space. Most experts therefore thought radio could never reach much beyond the horizon, perhaps a few hundred kilometres. Guglielmo Marconi, a young Italian inventor, tried anyway. In December 1901 he set up a receiver on a hill in Newfoundland, with its wire held up by a kite. He reported hearing three faint clicks, the letter S in Morse code, sent from Cornwall in England, about 3,400 kilometres away across the Atlantic. Some people doubted he really heard it. But within a few years his stations were sending messages across the ocean every day. In 1902, Arthur Kennelly and Oliver Heaviside suggested a reason: a layer of charged air high above the Earth that bends radio waves back down. In 1924, Edward Appleton and Miles Barnett proved it. They sent radio waves up from a BBC transmitter and timed the echoes coming back from this layer. Today we call it the ionosphere.  
*What people believed before:* Experts thought that because radio waves travel in straight lines, they could never follow the curve of the Earth to faraway places. Marconi's signal across the Atlantic showed that radio could go much further. The reason was a charged layer high in the air, the ionosphere, which sends some radio waves back down to the ground.

## Challenging perspectives
- **People travelled on wheels before they travelled in boats.** (A common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: The wheel is often called the greatest invention, so many people think it came first.  
  Against: Log canoes about 10,000 years old have been found. The oldest wheels are about 5,500 years old. People also crossed the sea to reach Australia more than 50,000 years ago, so they must have had boats or rafts.
- **Bright neon colours, like fluorescent green or orange, keep you safe at night.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Fluorescent colours look very bright in the daytime and at dusk.  
  Against: Fluorescent colours glow using some of the invisible light in sunlight. Most headlights give out almost none of it. At night a fluorescent jacket is seen about as well as a plain pale one. Reflective strips work far better.
- **Marconi invented radio.** (Many school books; *debated*)  
  For: Marconi built the first radio system that worked over long distances and sold it to ships and governments. He shared the Nobel Prize in 1909.  
  Against: Many people worked on radio at the same time. In Kolkata in the 1890s, J.C. Bose made and detected very short radio waves. Nikola Tesla, Alexander Popov and others also built radio devices. Most historians say radio had many inventors.
- **Electric scooters and cars cause no pollution.** (A common belief; *debated*)  
  For: They give out no exhaust on the road, so the air on the street is cleaner.  
  Against: Their electricity is often made by burning coal in power stations. Making their batteries uses a lot of energy. Their tyres and brakes still make dust.
- **If I can see the car, the driver can see me.** (A common belief, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: The car's headlights look very bright to you.  
  Against: You see the bright lamp itself. The driver sees you only by the little light that bounces off you. In the dark-room test, the black cloth vanished a few steps away while the torch was still shining.

## Roots
We have learned how people, goods and messages travel, and found in the dark that a driver sees us only by the light we send back. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** With the same dim torch, the black cloth vanished a few steps away. The white cloth was still clear across the room. Both were there the whole time. A driver knows you are on the road only when light from you reaches their eyes.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think that being somewhere is the same as being seen there, and that if we can see a car, its driver can see us. The test showed that being seen depends on how much light comes back from us. Road safety needs both: we must see, and we must be seen.  
*Evidence:* In the Seen in the Dark test, every cloth stayed on the chair and got the same torch light. Only the light reflected back to the eye changed, and the black cloth vanished first. A reflective strip, which sends light straight back to the lamp, shone brightest of all.

- *who am i:* Others see you only by the light that bounces off you into their eyes. Your face reaches your friend as light.
- *what is life:* Bees dance, ants leave smell trails, crows call and plants release smells that warn their neighbours. Living beings are always sending messages.
- *what is this world:* Roads, rails, wires and radio waves join far places, so rice from Andhra Pradesh reaches a kitchen in Kochi.

Every message needs a sender, something that carries it, and a receiver. In the dark test, the torch, the cloth and your eye made one chain, and if any part was missing, nothing was seen. Roads, rails, wires and radio waves are longer chains of the same kind. Light, sound and signals pass between things all the time, so no part of the world is ever fully cut off from the rest. One world shows up as many separate things, and they stay in touch through what passes between them.

- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** A great net hangs over the world, with a jewel at every knot. Each jewel shines with the reflected light of all the others. (In the dark test, the cloths had no light of their own. Each one was seen only by the torch light it sent back. The things around us are seen the same way, by light passed between them.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, the idea of interbeing (in The Heart of Understanding, 1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain, the tree and the logger in the paper. Nothing exists by itself alone. (In the Kitchen Journey, one packet of rice held a farmer, a truck driver, a train, a shopkeeper and many messages. Transport and communication are how these many parts reach each other.)
- Science (settled): Apollo 11 left a reflector on the Moon in 1969. Scientists still bounce laser light off it to measure how far away the Moon is.
- Science (settled): Radio messages from the Voyager 1 spacecraft, now beyond the planets, take nearly a day to reach Earth.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India: The Charvakas taught that perception, what our senses show us, is the only sure way to know anything. In the dark, you could not see the black cloth. Yet you said it was still there, because you remembered putting it on the chair. A Charvaka would ask: can you really know something that your senses cannot show you right now?
- Practice, Borrowed light (3 min): Let's sit still in a dim room with only one small light on. Look slowly around. Name one thing you can see. Now find where its light comes from. Is it giving out its own light, or sending back light from the lamp? Do this for three things. Then close your eyes and breathe slowly five times. Open them again and notice the light coming back to you.
- Ask: The black cloth was there, but you could not see it. How did you know it was still there?
- Ask: When you see a friend's face, does anything go from you to them, or only from them to you?
- Ask: If a message is sent and nobody receives it, was anything really sent?

- *deep:* If someone sends a message and nobody receives it, has anything been communicated? (hint: Think of a letter that gets lost in the post, or a road sign that nobody passes.)

## Story: Paati's Night Bus
*Women drive buses in Kerala today. Saraswathi, Kavya, Arjun and this evening in Kochi are made up. Reflective strips, cycle reflectors and road signs work exactly as the story says.*

Kavya was nine, and her cousin Arjun was six. Every summer they stayed with their grandmother, Saraswathi Paati, in a small flat in Kochi. Paati had driven buses for thirty years. She had driven up the ghat roads to Munnar, and through the night to Thrissur.

One evening the power went off. The lane outside was dark, except for the lights of passing autos. Arjun pulled on his black T-shirt. 'I will run across to the shop and buy the milk,' he said.

'Wait,' said Paati. 'First come to the balcony. I want to show you something.'

The three of them stood at the rail and looked down at the lane. An auto came round the corner, its one headlight shining ahead. In front of it walked a man in a white mundu. Kavya saw him clearly, long before the auto reached him.

'There is someone else down there too,' said Paati quietly. 'Can you see him?'

Kavya looked and looked. She saw nothing. Then the auto came close, and suddenly there was a boy in a dark blue shirt, right in front of it. The driver honked and swerved. The boy jumped to the side. Arjun gasped.

'He was there the whole time,' said Paati. 'The driver could not see him until the very last moment.'

'But the headlight was so bright,' said Arjun. 'I could see it from up here.'

'You could see the lamp,' said Paati. 'That is different from the driver seeing the boy. The driver sees him only by the light that bounces off him and comes back. A white mundu sends back a lot of light. A dark shirt sends back very little.'

Next came a girl on a cycle. Her cycle had no lamp. But as the next auto came up behind her, a red spot on the back of her cycle flashed brightly.

'Her reflector,' said Kavya. 'It has no battery. How does it shine?'

Paati went inside and came back with a torch and Kavya's school bag. She held the torch right next to her own eye and pointed it at the bag across the balcony. A grey strip on the bag blazed silver.

'Now you hold the bag, and I will stand to the side,' said Paati. She asked Kavya to point the torch at the bag again. From where Paati stood, the strip looked dull. From where Kavya held the torch, it shone.

'This strip sends light straight back to wherever it came from,' said Paati. 'So it shines brightest for the person holding the lamp. On the road, that person is the driver.'

'Like the road signs on the ghat road,' said Kavya. 'They light up in the headlights.'

'Yes,' said Paati. 'When I drove the night bus to Munnar, nobody had switched on those signs. The bend signs and the speed breaker signs glowed only when my own headlights hit them. They were made to send my light back to me. A road sign is a message. It cannot shout. It can only send back the light that falls on it.'

Arjun looked down at his black T-shirt. Then he went inside and came back in his white school shirt.

'Better,' said Paati. She gave him the torch. 'Carry this, and shine it down at the road in front of you. Then the driver will see your light. You will see the road too.'

Kavya went with him, holding his hand. At the edge of the lane they stopped, looked right, then left, then right again. An auto was coming. They waited until it passed. Then they crossed.

When they came back with the milk, Arjun was thinking hard. 'Paati,' he said, 'when I see you, does anything go from my eyes to you?'

Paati laughed. 'Now that is a question even I never asked on the night bus. What do you think?'

**For the parent:** For the parent: a driver sees a walker only by the headlight that bounces off the walker and returns to the driver's eyes. A white mundu reflects much of that light, so the man was seen from far away. The dark blue shirt reflected very little, so the boy was seen only when the auto was very close. Seeing the bright lamp does not mean the driver can see you. A reflector, like the red one on a cycle or the strip on a school bag, sends light straight back towards its source, so it shines brightly for the driver and much less for someone standing to the side. Many road signs are covered with the same kind of sheeting. Arjun's last question has a plain answer: nothing goes out from our eyes to make us see. We see when light comes into them.

- Ask: Why could Kavya see the man in the white mundu long before the auto reached him?
- Ask: Arjun could see the auto's headlight from the balcony. Why did that not mean the driver could see the boy?
- Ask: Why did the strip on the bag shine for Kavya, who held the torch, but not for Paati, standing to the side?
- Ask: What three things did Arjun do to be safe when he crossed the lane?
- Ask: Paati says a road sign 'can only send back the light that falls on it'. What did she mean?

## Experiments
### e1 · Seen in the Dark: which clothes can a driver see from furthest away? (40 min)
- **question:** At night, how far away can a driver see a black cloth, a dark cloth, a white cloth and a yellow cloth?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which cloth will you still see from furthest away? Which one will vanish first? How many foot-steps away do you think each will vanish? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same torch, covered the same way, for every cloth. Hang every cloth on the same chair in the same place, with the same dark room behind it. Keep the room just as dark each time. The same person looks every time, holding the torch next to their eye like a car's headlight. Only the cloth changes. Test each cloth twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up stays in the room the whole time. Clear the floor before switching off the lights, because the child walks backwards in the dark. Walk slowly, heel to toe. Never shine the torch into anyone's eyes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar for each cloth, as long as the number of foot-steps.
- **results table:** Cloth | My guess (foot-steps) | Try 1 (foot-steps) | Try 2 (foot-steps)
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find the longest straight path in your home, ending at an open doorway. 2. Put the chair in the doorway, with the next room dark behind it. 3. Hang the black cloth over the chair back. 4. Fold the handkerchief into 4 layers and fix it over the torch with the rubber band.  
  *Parent says:* Tonight you are a driver, and this chair is a person walking on a dark road. The torch is your headlight. We make it dim, because a car's light is weak by the time it reaches someone far away.  
  *Child mode:* Put the chair in a doorway. Keep the room behind it dark. Hang the black cloth on it. Cover the torch with the folded hanky.
  - Note: The room behind the chair must be dark, with nothing pale behind the cloth, like a real road at night. If the wall behind is white, the black cloth shows up as a dark patch against it, and the test fails. If your home has no free doorway, hang a dark bedsheet behind the chair.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child all the cloths in the light. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Your child writes a guess in foot-steps for each cloth in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which cloth do you think you will still see from furthest away? Which will vanish first? Why do you think so? Write a guess for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Look at all the cloths. Which will you see from furthest away? Why? Write a guess for each one.
  - Ask: Why do you think that cloth will be seen from furthest away? (good: It is bright.; White is easy to see.; Black looks like the dark.)
  - Note: Do not give the answer. Any guess with a reason is good. A foot-step is one shoe-length: put the heel of one foot against the toes of the other.
- **s3 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off all the lights and close the curtains. 2. Wait 2 minutes so your eyes get used to the dark. 3. Your child stands 2 foot-steps from the chair, holding the dim torch against their cheek, pointing at the cloth.  
  *Parent says:* Let's wait two minutes in the dark. Your eyes need time to get used to it. Hold the torch right by your eye, like a headlight next to a driver.  
  *Child mode:* Switch off the lights. Wait two minutes for your eyes. Hold the torch by your cheek. Point it at the cloth.
  - Note: Phone screens and room lights must stay off, so every try has the same darkness.
- **s4 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child steps backwards, heel to toe, counting foot-steps, keeping the torch on the cloth. 2. They stop when they can no longer tell the cloth is there. 3. Write the number. 4. Switch on the light, change the cloth, switch off, and wait 1 minute. 5. Test every cloth this way.  
  *Parent says:* Step back slowly, heel to toe, and count. Keep the torch on the chair. Stop when you can't tell the cloth is there. How many foot-steps?  
  *Child mode:* Step back, heel to toe. Count each step. Keep the torch on the cloth. Stop when it vanishes. Say the number.
  - Ask: Which cloth vanished first? How far away were you? (good: The black one, after only a few steps.; The dark one went quickly.)
  - Note: Walk beside your child and keep one hand ready. If even the black cloth is still easy to see at the far end, add 2 more layers of hanky and start again. In many homes the black cloth vanishes within 2 to 5 foot-steps, and the white one may stay visible right to the far wall. If so, write 'still seen at' and the number.
- **s5 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Test each cloth a second time, in the same way. 2. Write the second numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Scientists try each thing twice, because one try can be a fluke. Let's do every cloth again.  
  *Child mode:* Test each cloth again. Write the new numbers. Are they close to the first ones?
- **s6 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. If you have a reflective strip or a cycle reflector, put it on the chair. 2. Shine the dim torch from your eye. 3. Then hold the torch at arm's length to the side and look again. 4. Try a piece of kitchen foil the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a reflective strip. Shine the torch from your eye. Now hold the torch far out to one side. Does the strip look the same?  
  *Child mode:* Shine the torch at the strip from your eye. Now hold the torch far to the side. What changed?
  - Note: The strip blazes when the torch is by the eye, and looks much duller when the torch is held to the side. It sends light back towards the lamp. Foil can glint brightly or look dark, depending on how it is tilted. Skip this step if you have no strip or reflector.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch the lights on. 2. Read the table together. 3. Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? Which cloth could a driver see from furthest away? What is your evidence? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Which cloth did you see from furthest away? Which vanished first? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: The same torch shone on every cloth. Why did some vanish sooner? (good: The black cloth sends back very little light.; The white cloth sends back more light to my eyes.)
  - Ask: What should you wear when you walk on a road at night? (good: White or pale clothes.; Something with a reflective strip.; Carry a torch.)
  - Check: Leela, a girl in Class 3, walks home at night in a black dress. Which change helps drivers see her from furthest away? → **A white dupatta and a reflective strip on her bag**
  - Think: You could see the torch's light the whole time. Why could you not see the black cloth?
### e2 · Words Only: can you send a picture by talking? (30 min)
- **question:** Does a picture sent by words alone come out more correctly when the listener can ask questions, and does it take longer?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the drawing come out better when the listener stays silent, or when the listener can ask questions? Which way will be quicker? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two pictures that are equally hard: each has the same 4 kinds of shapes in different places. The same person describes both times. Use a clock to time each round. Score each drawing in the same way, 2 points for each shape: 1 for the right shape and size, 1 for the right place. Then swap roles and do it again, using the pictures the other way round.
- **safety:** No special safety needs.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Put each drawing next to its picture card.
- **results table:** Round | Listener may ask questions? | Time taken (minutes) | Score out of 8
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put two chairs back to back. 2. The describer gets picture A and picture B, face down. 3. The listener gets paper and a pencil. 4. Ask for guesses and reasons.  
  *Parent says:* You will describe a picture using only words. I will draw it without seeing it. First, guess. Will my drawing be better if I stay silent, or if I can ask questions? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Sit back to back. Hide your pictures. Guess: silent listener or questions allowed? Which will be better? Why?
  - Note: If you cannot print, draw 4 cards. Each has 4 shapes: a circle, a square, a triangle and a straight line, in different sizes and places. Two cards are for the first go and two for the swap. Keep them simple.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Round 1: the describer describes picture A. 2. The listener draws and says nothing at all. 3. Time the round. 4. Stop when the describer says 'done'.  
  *Parent says:* Round 1. I must stay silent. I cannot ask anything. Describe picture A as clearly as you can. Tell me when you are done.  
  *Child mode:* Describe picture A. Your listener must stay silent. Say 'done' at the end.
  - Note: Do not nod, sigh or ask. This is one-way communication, like a radio programme.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Round 2: the describer describes picture B. 2. This time the listener may ask as many questions as they like. 3. Time the round.  
  *Parent says:* Round 2. Now I can ask questions. Describe picture B. I will ask whenever I am not sure.  
  *Child mode:* Describe picture B. Now your listener can ask questions. Answer each one.
  - Note: Ask real questions: 'How big is the circle?' 'Is the line touching the triangle?' This is two-way communication, like a phone call.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn the cards over. 2. Score each drawing: 2 points for each shape, 1 for the right shape and size, 1 for the right place. 3. Write the scores and times.  
  *Parent says:* Let's score them. For each shape, one point if it is the right shape and size, and one if it is in the right place.  
  *Child mode:* Put each drawing next to its card. Give points for each shape. Write the scores and times.
  - Ask: Which round had the higher score? Which was quicker? (good: The round with questions scored higher.; The silent round was quicker.)
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap roles: now the child draws. 2. Use the other two cards. Do one silent round and one round with questions. 3. Score and time both.  
  *Parent says:* Now I describe and you draw. We do it again, once silent and once with questions. Does the same thing happen?  
  *Child mode:* Now you draw. Stay silent in the first round. Ask questions in the second. Score both.
  - Note: Swapping checks that the result is not just because one of you is a better describer.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a claim, the evidence and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* What did you find? What is your evidence? Why do you think questions made such a difference?  
  *Child mode:* Which way gave better drawings? Which was quicker? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: A road sign cannot answer questions. What does that mean for how it must be made? (good: It must be very simple.; Everyone must understand it at once.; It uses shapes and pictures, not long words.)
  - Check: Which of these is two-way communication? → **A phone call with Ammamma**

## Open questions
- **Who really sent the first radio signals, and who deserves the most credit?** Bose, Marconi, Tesla, Popov and others all did important early work at about the same time. Historians still argue about how to share the credit.
- **Did Marconi really hear the signal across the Atlantic in 1901?** Only Marconi and his assistant heard the faint clicks, and nothing was recorded. Some engineers have argued that he may have heard noise. Later tests by his company did work across the ocean.
- **How can our cities move everyone quickly without dirtying the air?** Metros, buses, electric vehicles, cycle lanes and working from home all help, but every city is different and nobody has found one answer that works everywhere.
- **Exactly how do nearby plants respond to the warning smells of a plant being eaten?** Scientists have found that neighbours get their defences ready, but they are still working out which smells matter and how plants sense them.
- **Where did the wheel first come from?** Very old wheels have been found in Mesopotamia, near the Black Sea and in Europe, all about 5,000 to 5,500 years old. Nobody knows for sure where the first one was made.

## Connected maths
- In the Seen in the Dark test, Kavya still saw the white shirt from 15 foot-steps away. The black T-shirt vanished at 3 foot-steps. How many times further away was the white shirt seen? **5 times further away.**. The white shirt was seen at 15 foot-steps and the black T-shirt at 3. 15 divided by 3 is 5. So the white shirt was seen 5 times further away. Check: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 15. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Kavya's yellow dupatta was seen at 12 foot-steps on try 1 and 14 foot-steps on try 2. What is the middle value of her two tries? **13 foot-steps.**. 12 + 14 = 26. Half of 26 is 13. So the middle value is 13 foot-steps. This is called the average of the two tries. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- Farhan watched the road for 10 minutes. He counted 20 cars with 2 people in each, and 1 bus with about 40 people. Which carried more people: all the cars together, or the one bus? **They carried the same number: 40 people each.**. 20 cars with 2 people each is 20 × 2 = 40 people. The bus carried about 40 people too. So one bus carried as many people as 20 cars, using one engine instead of 20. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- India's first passenger train ran in 1853. Kochi's water metro began in 2023. How many years apart are they? **170 years.**. 2023 − 1853 = 170. Count up: from 1853 to 1953 is 100 years, and from 1953 to 2023 is 70 more. 100 + 70 = 170. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)
- In the Words Only test, Revathi's silent round took 2 minutes and scored 3 out of 8. Her question round took 5 minutes and scored 7 out of 8. How many more points did questions give? How many more minutes did they take? **4 more points, and 3 more minutes.**. 7 − 3 = 4 more points. 5 − 2 = 3 more minutes. So questions gave a much better drawing, but took longer. Whether it is worth it depends on the job. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
