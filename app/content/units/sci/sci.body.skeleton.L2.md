# Bones and joints: the hard frame inside you, and where it bends
`sci.body.skeleton.L2`

**Summary.** Press on your shin, then on your tummy. One feels hard and one feels soft. The hard part is bone. A grown-up has about 206 bones inside. A place where two bones meet is a joint, and that is where you bend. Your child shines a torch on the wall and moves one joint at a time, to find which joints can draw a circle and which can only draw a line.

**Intro.** *Bend one finger slowly. How many places does it bend? Why does it not bend everywhere, like a piece of string?* Hold out your hand and wiggle your fingers. Now press the back of one finger. It feels hard inside. That hard part is bone. Your finger can only bend in a few places. In this unit you find your bones by feeling them. You find your joints, where bones meet. Then you test which joints can move in a circle.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that I have bones inside me, and find some of them by pressing gently.
1. By the end I can say what a joint is, and show the joints in my finger, arm and leg.
2. By the end I can say that some joints, like my shoulder, move in a circle, and some, like my elbow, move only one way, like a door.
3. By the end I can say three jobs of bones: holding me up, keeping soft parts safe, and letting me move.
4. By the end I can say that muscles pull on bones to move them, and feel a muscle get hard when it works.
5. By the end I can say that bones are alive, and that a broken bone can mend.

## Terms
- **Bone**: A bone is a hard, strong part inside your body. Bones are alive, and they grow as you grow. *e.g.* Press the front of your leg below the knee. The hard part you feel is your shin bone.
- **Skeleton**: Your skeleton is all your bones together. It is the hard frame inside your body. *e.g.* A grown-up has about 206 bones in their skeleton. A crow, a cat and a fish have skeletons too.
- **Joint**: A joint is a place where two bones meet. Most joints let you bend or turn. *e.g.* Your elbow, knee, wrist and knuckles are joints.
- **Hinge joint**: A hinge joint moves only one way and back, like a door on its hinges. *e.g.* Your elbow and knee are hinge joints. They bend and straighten, but cannot make a circle.
- **Ball and socket joint**: In a ball and socket joint, the round end of one bone sits in a cup in another bone. It can move in almost every direction. *e.g.* Your shoulder and hip are ball and socket joints. You can swing your straight arm round in a big circle.
- **Muscle**: A muscle is a soft part of your body that can get shorter and pull. Muscles pull on bones to move them. *e.g.* When you bend your arm to lift a water bottle, the muscle at the front of your upper arm gets short, fat and hard.
- **Tendon**: A tendon is a strong cord that joins a muscle to a bone. *e.g.* Stand on tiptoe and feel the back of your ankle. The hard cord there is a tendon. It joins your calf muscle to your heel bone.
- **Cartilage**: Cartilage is a smooth, bendy material in the body. It is softer than bone. *e.g.* The tip of your nose and the flap of your ear are cartilage. That is why they bend when you press them.
- **Skull**: Your skull is the bone of your head. It is a hard case round your brain. *e.g.* Tap gently on the top of your head. You feel your skull under your hair.
- **Ribs**: Ribs are curved bones round your chest. Together they make a cage that keeps your heart and lungs safe. *e.g.* Put your hands on your sides and breathe in. You can feel your ribs move out and in.
- **Backbone**: Your backbone is a long row of small bones down the middle of your back. It holds you up and lets you bend. *e.g.* Bend forward and ask a grown-up to run a finger down your back. The row of bumps is your backbone.
- **X-ray**: An X-ray is a special kind of light that goes through skin and muscle. Bone stops more of it, so bones show up white on an X-ray picture. *e.g.* When someone falls and hurts their wrist, a doctor takes an X-ray to see if a bone is broken.

## Explanations
### We have bones inside us · *defined*
**Definition:** Bones are the hard, living parts inside the body. All the bones together make the skeleton.

Press gently on your shin, your knuckles or your head, and you feel something hard. That is bone. A grown-up has about 206 bones. Some are long, like the leg bones. Some are tiny, like the three little bones deep inside each ear.
- e.g. The longest bone in your body is your thigh bone. It goes from your hip to your knee.
- e.g. The smallest bone is deep inside your ear. It is smaller than a grain of rice.
- e.g. Each hand has 27 bones, and each foot has 26.
### What bones do for us · *defined*
**Definition:** Bones hold the body up, keep soft parts safe, and give muscles something to pull on.

Without bones, you would flop like an empty sock. Your leg bones carry your weight when you stand. Your skull is a hard case round your soft brain. Your ribs make a cage round your heart and lungs. Muscles are joined to bones, and they pull on them to move you.
- e.g. The skull keeps the brain safe.
- e.g. The ribs keep the heart and lungs safe.
- e.g. The backbone holds you up and keeps the soft cord of nerves inside it safe.
- e.g. Leg bones carry your weight when you walk.
### Joints are where bones meet · *defined*
**Definition:** A joint is a place where two bones meet. Most joints let the body bend or turn.

A bone is stiff. It does not bend. So you can only bend where two bones meet. Each finger has three bones and bends in three places. Smooth cartilage covers the bone ends, and a slippery liquid helps them slide, like oil in a door hinge.
- e.g. Each finger has 3 bones. The thumb has 2 bones you can feel, and another inside the palm.
- e.g. Your elbow, knee, wrist, ankle, shoulder and hip are joints.
- e.g. Your skull is made of many bones that joined together. Those joints do not move.
### Hinge joints and ball and socket joints · *defined*
**Definition:** A hinge joint moves one way and back, like a door. A ball and socket joint can move in almost every direction, because a round ball of bone turns in a cup.

At your elbow, the bones fit together like a hinge. So the torch light could only go up and down in a line. At your shoulder, the round top of your arm bone sits in a shallow cup. So the light could go round in a whole circle. Your knee and the middle joints of your fingers are hinges. Your hip is a ball and socket joint. Your wrist and big knuckles are in between, and draw a small circle.
- e.g. Hinge joints: elbow, knee, the middle and end joints of your fingers.
- e.g. Ball and socket joints: shoulder and hip.
- e.g. In between: wrist, ankle, and the big knuckles where your fingers join your hand.
### Muscles pull bones · *defined*
**Definition:** Muscles move bones by getting shorter and pulling on them. A muscle can pull, but it cannot push.

Put your hand on the front of your upper arm and bend your elbow. The muscle there gets shorter, fatter and harder. It is pulling your forearm up. To straighten your arm, a muscle at the back of your upper arm pulls instead. Muscles are joined to bones by strong cords called tendons. You have more than 600 muscles.
- e.g. The muscle at the front of your upper arm bends your elbow.
- e.g. Your calf muscle pulls your heel up when you stand on tiptoe.
- e.g. Your heart is a muscle too. It squeezes blood round your body.
### Bones are alive · *defined*
**Definition:** Bone is living. It has living bone cells and blood running through it.

Bone looks hard like stone, but it is alive. Inside are bone cells that build new bone and take away old bone, all through your life. When a bone breaks, the bone cells build new bone across the crack. A doctor puts on a plaster cast only to keep the pieces still, so they join straight. Many bones also make blood inside them, in the soft middle called marrow.
- e.g. A child's broken arm usually mends in a few weeks.
- e.g. Bone cells build more bone where your body pushes on it most, for example in a tennis player's hitting arm.
- e.g. Astronauts in space lose some bone, because nothing presses on their bones there.
### Children have more bones than grown-ups · *defined*
**Definition:** A newborn baby has about 300 bones and bits of bone. Many of them join together as the child grows, until a grown-up has about 206.

A baby's skull is made of plates with soft gaps between them. You may have felt the soft spot on a baby's head. The plates join up as the child grows. A baby's kneecaps are soft cartilage, and they slowly turn into bone during the first few years. Bones keep growing longer until the teenage years.
- e.g. The soft spot on top of a baby's head closes by about the age of 2.
- e.g. A baby's lower jaw starts as two halves, which join in the first year.
- e.g. The bones at the bottom of the backbone join together by the time a person is grown up.
### Skeletons in other animals · *defined*
**Definition:** Most fish, and all frogs, lizards, birds and mammals, have a skeleton of bone inside, with a backbone. Humans are one of these animals.

A crow, a cat, a house lizard and you all have a backbone, a skull and ribs. The bones are the same kinds, in different shapes and sizes. A giraffe has 7 bones in her neck, the same number as you, but each one is very long. Ants, spiders and crabs have no bones. Their hard skeleton is on the outside, like a coat of armour.
- e.g. A giraffe and a human both have 7 neck bones.
- e.g. A bird's leg seems to bend backwards. That bend is really the ankle. The knee is hidden up in the feathers.
- e.g. An ant's hard skeleton is on the outside of their body.

## Mostly agreed
- A grown-up human has about 206 bones. A newborn baby has more separate pieces, about 300, and many of them join as the child grows. *How we know:* Doctors have counted bones in skeletons and watched them on X-rays of children of every age.
- Bones are alive. Bone cells build and remove bone all through life, and a broken bone can mend. *How we know:* X-ray pictures taken weeks apart show new bone growing across a break. Under a microscope, bone is full of living cells and tiny blood tubes.
- Muscles move bones by pulling. A muscle cannot push. *How we know:* When a muscle works, you can feel it get shorter and harder. To move a joint back again, a different muscle on the other side pulls.
- Most fish, and frogs, reptiles, birds and mammals, including humans, have a backbone and a skeleton of bone inside. *How we know:* Scientists have compared the skeletons of thousands of animals. The same kinds of bones turn up again and again, in different shapes.

## How we found out
**Galen, Andreas Vesalius, and Wilhelm Röntgen with Anna Bertha Röntgen, About 170 CE (Galen), 1543 (Vesalius) and 1895 (the first X-ray pictures).** About 1,850 years ago, a famous Greek doctor called Galen worked in Rome. People there were not allowed to cut open human bodies to study them. So Galen studied the bodies of monkeys, pigs, dogs and other animals instead. He wrote that the human lower jaw is two bones, joined at the chin. In dogs and many other animals, it is. For about 1,300 years, doctors in Europe learnt from Galen's books, and few checked. In 1543, a young doctor named Andreas Vesalius, who taught in Padua in Italy, published a big book of drawings made from real human skeletons. He showed that a grown-up's lower jaw is one bone. A baby's jaw starts as two halves, but they join in the first year. Vesalius showed that the only way to know is to look at the real thing. Then, in 1895 in Germany, Wilhelm Röntgen found X-rays. One of his first X-ray pictures was of his wife Anna Bertha's hand. For the first time, people could see the bones inside a living person.  
*What people believed before:* That the human lower jaw is two bones, because Galen studied the jaws of other animals. Vesalius looked at real human skeletons and showed that an adult's jaw is one bone.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Every person has exactly 206 bones.** (Many school books; *accepted*)  
  For: Most grown-ups do have 206 bones when doctors count them in the usual way.  
  Against: Babies have about 300 pieces. Some grown-ups have an extra rib, or extra small bones in the hands and feet. Old Indian books counted differently: the Sushruta Samhita counts 300 bones and the Charaka Samhita 360, because they counted teeth, nails and some cartilage too.
- **Cracking your knuckles gives you joint illness when you are old.** (A common warning from grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: The popping sound is loud, and it seems as if something must be getting hurt.  
  Against: A doctor named Donald Unger cracked the knuckles of only his left hand for about 60 years. When he checked, both hands were just as healthy. Larger studies have found no clear link either.
- **The pop when you crack a knuckle is a bubble forming in the joint liquid.** (Some scientists who filmed knuckles cracking inside a scanner, in 2015; *debated*)  
  For: Their pictures showed a gap of gas appearing in the joint liquid at the moment of the pop.  
  Against: Earlier scientists, and some who made computer models later, said the sound comes from a bubble collapsing, partly or fully.
- **Bones are dead, like stones, once they have finished growing.** (A common idea, because a skeleton looks hard and dry; *rejected*)  
  For: The bones you see in a museum are hard and dry, and they do not change.  
  Against: Bones in a living body have blood and living cells. They mend when broken and get thicker when used more.

## Roots
We have felt our bones, found our joints and tested which joints can draw a circle. Now we ask what the torch test tells us about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** When you moved only your elbow, the torch light could only go up and down. When you moved only your shoulder, the light drew a whole circle. The elbow fits together like a door hinge, and the shoulder like a ball in a cup. So the ways your body can move are set by the shapes where its bones meet.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of moving as something we simply decide to do, or something our muscles do. But muscles can only pull a bone the ways its joint allows. The shape where the bones meet decides which paths are possible.  
*Evidence:* In the torch test, the shoulder and hip could draw circles, while the elbow, knee and middle finger joints could only go back and forth. In an X-ray or a skeleton, the top of the arm bone is a round ball in a cup, and the elbow is a hinge.

- *who am i:* You are a frame of about 206 living bones, joined at many joints, and moved by muscles that pull.
- *what is life:* Your bones are alive. Bone cells feel the pushes of your running and jumping, and build bone thicker where it is pushed most.
- *what is this world:* A crow, a cat, a fish and you all have a backbone, a skull and ribs. Many animals share one plan of bones, in different shapes.

Bone cells feel the pressing of your weight and the jolts of your jumping. They answer by building bone where it is needed and taking it away where it is not. So the shape of your bones is a record of how you have lived and moved. Some scientists call this an automatic response to pressure.

- **Sushruta Samhita, Sharira Sthana, chapter 5:** Sushruta, a surgeon in ancient India, named the joints of the body by their shapes. He called the joints of the fingers, knees and elbows kora, like a hinge. He called the joints of the shoulders and hips ulukhala, like a mortar with its pestle. (This is what your torch test found: the hinge joints swing one way, and the shoulder and hip turn in a cup like a pestle in a mortar.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 76:** The Tao Te Ching, an old Chinese book, says that a living person is soft and bending, and a dead body is stiff and hard. A living plant is soft and bends, and a dead one is dry and breaks. (Both link life with moving and changing. A living body bends at its joints, and living bone is always being rebuilt. A museum skeleton is hard and still, because its living cells have gone.)
- Science (settled): Bone is living. Bone cells build and remove bone all through life, and build more bone where it is pressed and pushed.
- Science (open): Exactly how bone cells sense pressure and pass the message to other cells is still being worked out.
- Other view, Many biologists: Many biologists say bone cells do not listen or answer in any way like a person does. They say the cells follow chemical rules when they are squeezed, the way a switch turns on when it is pressed.
- Practice, Joint by Joint (3 min): Sit or stand comfortably. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out. Now wiggle only your toes. Now draw a slow circle with one ankle. Now bend and straighten one knee. Now draw a big slow circle with your arm from the shoulder. Bend your elbow and straighten it. Last, curl your fingers and open them. Breathe out. Which joints went round? Which only bent?
- Ask: If your joints were all ball and socket joints, would that be better or worse? Why?
- Ask: Your bones change shape a little with how you move. Are your bones shaping your life, or is your life shaping your bones?
- Ask: A skeleton in a museum has the same bones as a living body. What has gone?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* Your bones are made from the food you ate. When does the food stop being food and start being you? (hint: Think of the milk, ragi and greens you ate last week. Where are they now?)
- *deep:* A bone is stiff, but your body bends. How can a body made of stiff parts be so bendy? (hint: Think about how many places your finger bends, and what is at each one.)
- *deep:* Why would a body need some joints that move only one way? (hint: Think of standing on one leg. Would you want your knee to swing round in every direction?)
- *deep:* Bone cells build more bone where your body is pushed the most. How do you think they can tell where that is? (hint: Scientists know part of the answer. Bone cells sense pushes and send signals to each other.)

## Story: Thatha's Wrist and the Picture of Bones
*A made-up story. It is true that bones show up white on an X-ray, that bones are alive, and that a broken bone mends when it is kept still.*

Revathi lived with her Amma and her Thatha in a flat on the third floor in Madurai. Thatha was her grandfather. Every morning he made filter coffee. He poured it from the tumbler to the davara and back again, high in the air, to make it frothy.

One Monday the bathroom floor was wet. Thatha slipped. He put out his right hand to stop his fall, and he landed hard on his wrist. "Aiyo!" he cried. His wrist began to swell, and it hurt when he tried to turn his hand.

Amma called an auto, and the three of them went to the hospital. A doctor with grey hair and kind eyes looked at Thatha's wrist. Her name was Dr Fathima. "We need to see inside," she said. "We will take an X-ray."

"What is an X-ray?" Revathi asked.

"It is a special kind of light that goes through skin and muscle," said Dr Fathima. "Bone stops more of it. So on the X-ray picture, the bones show up white."

A nurse named Selvi asked Thatha to rest his hand flat on a table and keep very still. Then she took the picture. Later, Dr Fathima held a large grey sheet up against the bright window.

Revathi gasped. There was Thatha's hand. But it was a hand made of white bones! Each finger had a row of long thin bones. Near the wrist was a bunch of small round bones, packed together like pebbles in a bag.

"Look here," said Dr Fathima. She pointed to a thin dark line across a bone just above the wrist. "This bone has a crack. It is broken."

Thatha sighed. "Then my wrist is finished," he said. "A bone is like a stone. When a stone cracks, it stays cracked."

Dr Fathima smiled. "A stone is not alive, Sir. Your bone is. Inside it there are living bone cells, and blood runs through it. The bone cells will build new bone across the crack. Our job is only to hold the pieces still, so they join straight."

Nurse Selvi wrapped Thatha's arm in a hard white plaster cast. It went from his knuckles almost to his elbow. "Can you wiggle your fingers?" asked Dr Fathima. Thatha wiggled them. "Can you bend your elbow?" He bent it. "Can you bend your wrist?" He tried, but the cast held it straight.

"Why can he move his fingers but not his wrist?" asked Revathi.

"Because you bend only where two bones meet," said Dr Fathima. "Those places are called joints. The cast covers the wrist joint, so it keeps it still. The finger joints are outside the cast."

At home, Revathi sat beside Thatha on the sofa and looked at her own hand. She bent one finger slowly. It bent in three places. "Thatha, my finger has three bones, like the white ones in your picture!" She bent her thumb. It bent in two places. "And my thumb has two."

"How do you know they are bones?" asked Thatha.

Revathi pressed the back of her finger. "Feel. It is hard inside, between the bends. And it bends only at the joints." Thatha pressed her finger with his good hand. "You are right," he said. "I have had this hand for seventy years, and I never counted."

Every day for six weeks, Thatha drank his milk and ate his ragi kali and keerai. Dr Fathima had said that bone cells need good food to build new bone. Revathi drew a calendar and crossed off each day with a red pencil.

Then they went back to the hospital. Nurse Selvi took a new X-ray. This time the dark line was gone. In its place was a slightly thicker white patch on the bone. "The bone cells have done their work," said Dr Fathima. "The crack is filled with new bone."

The cast came off. Thatha's wrist was stiff, and his arm looked thin, because he had not used it for weeks. "Move it gently every day," said Dr Fathima. "Bones and muscles grow stronger when you use them."

A week later, Thatha made filter coffee again. He poured it from the tumbler to the davara, not quite as high as before. Revathi watched his wrist turn. "Your bone mended itself, Thatha," she said.

Thatha laughed. "My doctor and my granddaughter have taught an old man two things," he said. "Bones are not stones. They are alive. And I bend only where my bones meet."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Thatha thinks a bone is like a stone, so a cracked bone stays cracked. The X-ray and the doctor show that bone is alive and builds new bone across a crack. Revathi also finds, by bending her own finger, that a body bends only at its joints.

- Ask: Why do bones show up white on an X-ray picture?
- Ask: Why did Thatha think his wrist would stay broken?
- Ask: What was the plaster cast for, if the bone cells did the mending?
- Ask: Why could Thatha wiggle his fingers in the cast but not bend his wrist?

## Experiments
### e1 · Torch on the Wall: which joints can draw a circle? (25 min)
- **question:** If you move only one joint, which joints can make the torch light draw a whole circle on the wall, and which can only make it go back and forth in a line?
- **predict:** Guess first, for each joint: shoulder, elbow, wrist, the big knuckle of your finger, and the middle joint of your finger. Will it draw a circle or only a line? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Only one joint moves each time, so we know which joint made the shape. A grown-up gently holds the part above the joint still. You stand at the same spot each time. You try each joint three times, because one try can be a mistake.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Next to each joint, draw the shape the light made: a circle or a line.
- **results table:** Joint | My guess: circle or line? | What the light drew | Was my guess right?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table on paper with five rows: shoulder, elbow, wrist, big knuckle, middle finger joint. 2. Ask your child to guess for each joint, and to give a reason. 3. Write each guess in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We will hold a torch and move only one joint at a time. For each joint, will the light draw a circle or only a line? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess for each joint. Will the light draw a circle, or a line? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The shoulder can go round, because I can swing my arm.; The elbow only bends, so it will make a line.; All of them can make circles, because I can move my arm round.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine, as long as there is a reason. Do not tell your child the answers. Many children guess that the elbow can draw a circle.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Close the curtains and switch off the lights. 2. Your child stands about two big steps from a plain wall. 3. Switch the torch on and give it to your child.

### e2 · Bone Hunt: where is bone close to your skin? (20 min)
- **question:** On which parts of your body can you feel hard bone just under the skin, and on which parts can you feel only soft muscle and fat?
- **predict:** Look at the list of 14 places. For each one, guess: will you feel hard bone, or only soft parts? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** You press every place the same way: gently, with two fingertips. You press each place on a grown-up too, to see if bodies are the same. You only count it as bone if it feels hard and does not squash.
- **record:** Colour the body outline: a red dot where you felt hard bone, a blue dot where you felt only soft parts. Count the red dots and the blue dots.
- **results table:** Place | My guess: hard or soft? | What I felt on me | What I felt on a grown-up
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a simple body outline on paper. 2. Read out the 14 places on the list. 3. Ask your child to guess hard or soft for each one, with a reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will hunt for bones by pressing gently. Will the tip of your nose be hard or soft? What about your tummy? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess for each place. Hard bone, or soft? Why?
  - Note: The 14 places are: top of the head, forehead, top of the nose, tip of the nose, ear flap, collarbone, sides of the chest, front of the tummy, point of the elbow, middle of the upper arm, kneecap, front of the shin, back of the calf, and the knobs at the ankle. Write the guesses in the table. Do not say the answers yet.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child presses each place gently with two fingertips, one at a time. After each one, they put a red or a blue dot on the body outline.  
  *Parent says:* Press gently, like you are feeling a ripe mango. Is it hard and does not squash? Then it is bone. Red dot. Is it soft? Blue dot.  
  *Child mode:* Press gently with two fingers. Hard? Red dot. Soft? Blue dot.
  - Ask: Which place surprised you? (good: The tip of my nose bends!; My tummy has no bone at the front.; My ear is bendy, not hard.)
  - Note: Hard bone is easy to feel on the head, the top of the nose, the collarbone, the ribs, the elbow, the kneecap, the shin and the ankle knobs. The tummy and the middle of the upper arm and calf feel soft. The nose tip and the ear flap bend, because they are cartilage, not bone. Press gently on the chest and tummy, never hard.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child presses the same places on you. Talk about whether your body felt the same.  
  *Parent says:* Now press on me, gently. Is my body the same as yours?  
  *Child mode:* Now press on the grown-up, gently. Is it the same as you?
  - Note: The bones are in the same places on everyone. On a grown-up some may be harder to feel, because there is more muscle or fat over them.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the red dots and the blue dots. Talk about what you found.  
  *Parent says:* How many red dots? How many blue dots? Why do you think there is bone over your chest but not over your tummy?  
  *Child mode:* Count the red dots and the blue dots. Why is there no bone on your tummy?
  - Ask: Why is there bone over your chest but not at the front of your tummy? (good: The ribs keep my heart and lungs safe.; My tummy has to bend and get bigger after I eat.)
  - Note: Most children find about 9 or 10 red places and 4 or 5 blue ones. There is bone at the back of the tummy: the backbone. The front needs to bend and stretch when you bend over, breathe and eat.
  - Check: Pooja, a girl of 7, presses the tip of her nose and it bends. Why? → **The tip of the nose is cartilage, which is bendy**

### c1 · Make a Paper Finger That Bends (25 min)
- **problem:** Can you make a paper finger with three bones and three joints, which curls up when you pull a thread, like a real finger?

Pull the string, and the finger curls.

## Worksheets
### Bones and Joints (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (label) Label the skeleton: skull, ribs, backbone, thigh bone.  
   **Answer:** ['skull: the bone of the head', 'ribs: the curved bones round the chest', 'backbone: the row of small bones down the back', 'thigh bone: the long bone from the hip to the knee']. *Why:* These are four of the biggest parts of the skeleton. A strong answer puts each label on the right part.
2. (match) Draw a line from each bone to the job it does.  
   **Answer:** skull → keeps the brain safe; ribs → keep the heart and lungs safe; leg bones → carry your weight when you stand; backbone → holds you up and lets you bend. *Why:* Bones hold the body up and keep soft parts safe. The skull is a case for the brain, and the ribs are a cage round the heart and lungs.
3. (truefalse) True or false: the bones inside you are alive.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Bones have living cells and blood inside them. That is why a broken bone can mend.
4. (draw) Remember the Bend and Dot activity. Draw one of your fingers. Put a dot at every place it bends.  
   **Answer:** A finger with three dots: at the big knuckle, the middle and near the tip.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows three bends and three bones between them, because a finger bends only where bones meet.
5. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: why does your finger bend in some places and not in others?  
   **Answer:** A bone is stiff. My finger bends only at the joints, where two bones meet.. *Why:* A strong answer says that bones do not bend, and that bending happens at joints, between the bones.
6. (sort) Remember the Bone Hunt. Sort these places into hard bone near the skin, and soft.  
   **Answer:** front of the shin → Hard bone near the skin; front of the tummy → Soft; kneecap → Hard bone near the skin; back of the calf → Soft. *Why:* The shin bone and kneecap are just under the skin. The tummy has no bone at the front, and the calf is a thick muscle with the bone deep inside.
7. (fill) A place where two bones meet is called a ___.  
   **Answer:** joint. *Why:* A joint is where two bones meet. Most joints let you bend or turn.
8. (draw) In the torch test, what did the light do when you moved only your elbow? Draw it on the wall.  
   **Answer:** A straight or curved line going up and down, not a circle.. *Why:* The elbow is a hinge joint, like a door. It moves one way and back, so the light makes a line.
9. (odd) Which one is not a joint? Elbow, knee, shin, wrist.  
   Options: Elbow / Knee / Shin / Wrist  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* The shin is a bone. The elbow, knee and wrist are joints, the places where two bones meet.
10. (truefalse) True or false: a crow has a backbone, like you.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Birds, cats, fish and humans all have a skull, a backbone and ribs inside, in different shapes.
### How Joints Move (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (write) In the Floppy Sock test, the sock with one long ruler inside could point but could not bend. What did it need to bend?  
   **Answer:** It needed two bones with a joint between them, like the two pencils.. *Why:* A strong answer says that stiff bones hold you up, but bending needs a joint where two bones meet.
2. (perspective) Three cousins are arguing about which joint is the best.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason. Kavya is right that the shoulder moves the most. Meena is right that a hinge is steady: a shoulder can slip out of its cup more easily than a knee or an elbow. Ammu is right that each joint fits what it does.. *Why:* A ball and socket joint moves more ways, but it is less steady. A hinge moves fewer ways, but it is steadier. Both kinds are useful in different places.
3. (order) Put these bones in order, from the longest to the shortest.  
   **Answer:** ['the thigh bone', 'the upper arm bone', 'a finger bone', 'a tiny bone in the ear']. *Why:* The thigh bone is the longest bone in the body. The upper arm bone is shorter. Finger bones are small, and the tiniest bone, in the ear, is smaller than a grain of rice.
4. (truefalse) True or false: a muscle can push a bone as well as pull it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A muscle can only get shorter and pull. To move a joint back, a different muscle on the other side pulls.
5. (draw) Remember the Muscle Feel activity. Draw your upper arm muscle when your arm is straight, and when you lift the bottle.  
   **Answer:** A long thin muscle when the arm is straight, and a short fat bump when the arm is bent.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the muscle getting shorter and fatter as it pulls the forearm up.
6. (fill) The strong cord that joins your calf muscle to your heel bone is a ___.  
   **Answer:** tendon. *Why:* A tendon joins a muscle to a bone. You can feel this one at the back of your ankle when you stand on tiptoe.
7. (write) A pigeon's leg seems to bend backwards. Which of your joints is that bend like? How can you show it with your own leg?  
   **Answer:** It is like my ankle. If I stand on tiptoe, my heel goes up and my ankle bends like the pigeon's leg.. *Why:* A strong answer names the ankle and says that birds stand on their toes with the heel up.
8. (label) Label the arm: shoulder, elbow, wrist. Then write H next to each hinge joint and B next to each ball and socket joint.  
   **Answer:** ['shoulder: B', 'elbow: H', 'wrist: neither, it is in between']. *Why:* The shoulder is a ball and socket joint and the elbow is a hinge. The wrist moves up and down and side to side, so it is in between.
9. (truefalse) True or false: to bend your elbow, the muscle at the back of your upper arm gets short and hard.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The muscle at the front of your upper arm gets short and hard and pulls the forearm up. The muscle at the back pulls to straighten the arm.
10. (mcq) A tennis player's hitting arm has thicker bones than her other arm. Why?  
   Options: Bone cells build more bone where the body pushes on it most / She eats more than other people / Bones grow thicker if you do not use them  
   **Answer:** Bone cells build more bone where the body pushes on it most. *Why:* Bone is alive. Bone cells build more bone where it is pushed and pressed, so a hard-working arm gets stronger bones.

   **Answer:** The ribs keep my heart and lungs safe. My tummy needs to bend when I bend over, and to stretch when I eat a big meal.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason for both: soft parts that need guarding get bone, and parts that must bend and stretch do not.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What do we call the hard, living parts inside your body?  
   Options: Bones / Muscles / Skin  
   **Answer:** Bones. *Why:* Bones are the hard parts inside you. Muscles are soft and can pull. Skin covers the outside.
2. (mcq) About how many bones does a grown-up have?  
   Options: About 206 / About 20 / About 2,000  
   **Answer:** About 206. *Why:* A grown-up has about 206 bones. A newborn baby has more pieces, about 300, which join as they grow.
3. (mcq) What is a joint?  
   Options: A place where two bones meet / The middle of a bone / A kind of muscle  
   **Answer:** A place where two bones meet. *Why:* A joint is where two bones meet. Your body bends at its joints.
4. (mcq) Your elbow bends and straightens like a door. What kind of joint is it?  
   Options: A hinge joint / A ball and socket joint / It is not a joint  
   **Answer:** A hinge joint. *Why:* A hinge joint moves one way and back, like a door. The elbow and knee are hinge joints.
5. (mcq) Which of these joints can move round in a circle?  
   Options: The hip / The knee / The elbow  
   **Answer:** The hip. *Why:* The hip is a ball and socket joint, like the shoulder. The knee and elbow are hinges.
6. (mcq) What do your ribs do?  
   Options: Keep your heart and lungs safe / Help you see / Make your hair grow  
   **Answer:** Keep your heart and lungs safe. *Why:* The ribs make a cage of bone round the heart and lungs.
7. (mcq) What would a body be like with no bones at all?  
   Options: Floppy, like an empty sock / Just the same / Stiff, like a ruler  
   **Answer:** Floppy, like an empty sock. *Why:* Bones hold the body up. Without them, a body would flop, like the empty sock in the Floppy Sock test.
8. (mcq) How does a muscle move a bone?  
   Options: It gets shorter and pulls / It pushes the bone away / It gets longer and pushes  
   **Answer:** It gets shorter and pulls. *Why:* Muscles can only pull. They get shorter and harder as they pull a bone.
9. (mcq) Selvi, a nurse, puts a plaster cast on a broken arm. Why?  
   Options: To keep the pieces still while the bone cells mend it / Because the cast makes new bone / To keep the arm warm  
   **Answer:** To keep the pieces still while the bone cells mend it. *Why:* Bone is alive. Bone cells build new bone across the crack. The cast only holds the pieces still so they join straight.
10. (mcq) Why can a broken bone mend, when a cracked stone cannot?  
   Options: Bone is alive and has living cells / Bone is softer than stone / Bone is made of water  
   **Answer:** Bone is alive and has living cells. *Why:* Living bone cells build new bone. A stone has no living cells, so a crack in a stone stays.

## Applications
- Doctors take X-rays to see if a bone is broken, and to check that it has mended straight.
- Bone doctors, called orthopaedic doctors, put plaster casts on broken bones to keep them still while they heal.
- Doctors can replace a worn-out hip or knee with a new joint made of metal and plastic.
- Engineers copy the hinge and ball and socket joints of the body in door hinges, robot arms and the joints of lamps and camera stands.
- Physiotherapists help people move stiff joints and make weak muscles strong again after an injury.
- Scientists who study fossils use the shapes of bones to work out how animals that lived long ago moved.

## Spotted around us
- A steel door hinge in your flat moves just like your elbow and knee: one way and back.
- A Bharatanatyam or Odissi dancer bends at the neck, wrists, fingers and ankles. Every pose uses many joints at once.
- A cricket bowler swings their straight arm round in a big circle from the shoulder, a ball and socket joint.
- A crow or a myna walking on a balcony rail, standing on their toes, with the heel and ankle bent backwards.
- An X-ray film in an envelope from a hospital, which a family member keeps after a fall.
- A kalaripayattu student in Kerala doing deep stretches and kicks that swing the leg round from the hip.

## Open questions
- **How exactly do bone cells feel the pushes of running and jumping, and pass the message on?** Scientists know that bone cells sense pressure and send signals to build more bone. Exactly how they sense it is still being worked out.
- **Why does a broken bone heal with new bone, while a deep cut in skin often leaves a scar?** Bone can rebuild itself very well. Scientists are still learning why bone mends so well and skin does not, and hope to use this to help other parts heal.
- **Why do almost all mammals, from mice to giraffes, have 7 neck bones?** Only a few mammals, such as sloths and manatees, have a different number. Scientists have ideas about why, but nobody is sure.
