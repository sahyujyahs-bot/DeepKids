# How life began: the oldest life and the RNA idea
`sci.evolution.story-of-life.L3`

**Summary.** Every living thing comes from another living thing. So how did the very first life begin? Nobody knows for sure yet. The Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. The oldest signs of life we have found are rocks about 3.5 billion years old, built in layers by mats of tiny microbes. These rocks are called stromatolites, and some are found in India too. Before life, the young sea held simple chemicals. Experiments show that some of the building blocks of life can form from these chemicals without any living thing. Many scientists think the first life was RNA, a chain molecule that can carry instructions and can also help make copies of itself. Later, tiny green microbes began to make oxygen. Their oxygen rusted the iron in the sea and changed the air for ever.

**Intro.** *You came from your mother. She came from her mother, and so on, back and back. A curry leaf plant came from a seed, which came from another curry leaf plant. Every living thing comes from another living thing. So where did the very first living thing come from, when there was nothing alive to come from?* This is one of the biggest questions in science, and it is still open. In this unit you meet the oldest life we know, which left striped rocks behind. You test what the first oxygen did to iron, with nails in jars of water. You see the white threads of DNA from a banana. And you learn the leading idea about how life began: the RNA idea. Scientists are still testing it, and you can weigh the evidence too.

## Terms
- **Cell**: The smallest living unit. Every living thing is made of one cell or many cells. Each cell has a thin skin around a watery inside. *e.g.* A bacterium is one cell. Your body is made of tens of trillions of cells.
- **Microbe**: A living thing too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria are microbes. *e.g.* The bacteria that turn milk into curd are microbes.
- **Molecule**: A tiny group of atoms joined together. Everything around you is made of molecules. *e.g.* Water is made of water molecules. Each one is two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom.
- **Billion**: A thousand million. Written out, it is 1 followed by 9 zeros: 1,000,000,000. *e.g.* The Earth is about 4.5 billion years old. In Indian numbers, one billion is 100 crore.
- **Fossil**: Any trace of a living thing from long ago, kept in rock. It can be a bone, a shell, a print, or a shape left by tiny living things. *e.g.* A shell turned to stone, or a dinosaur footprint, is a fossil.
- **Stromatolite**: A rock built up in thin layers by mats of microbes in shallow water. The microbes trap mud and sand, then grow up through it, again and again. *e.g.* Stromatolites about 3.5 billion years old are found in Western Australia. Ones about 2 billion years old are found near Vempalle, in the Kadapa district of Andhra Pradesh.
- **Cyanobacteria**: Tiny bacteria that make their food from sunlight, water and air, as plants do. As they do this, they give out oxygen. *e.g.* The dark green or black film on a damp wall in the monsoon often holds cyanobacteria.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air that we need to breathe. Almost all of it was made by living things that use sunlight, such as cyanobacteria, algae and plants. *e.g.* Every breath you take brings oxygen into your body.
- **Rust**: The orange-brown stuff that forms when iron joins with oxygen. Water helps rust form. *e.g.* An old iron gate that stays out in the rain turns rusty.
- **DNA**: A very long chain molecule inside every cell. It holds the instructions for building and running that living thing. Its chain uses four kinds of chemical letters. *e.g.* Your DNA came half from your mother and half from your father.
- **RNA**: A chain molecule, a close cousin of DNA. Its four chemical letters are called A, U, C and G. In every cell, RNA carries copies of instructions from the DNA and helps build proteins. *e.g.* In your cells, RNA copies of instructions travel from the DNA to the cell's protein-building machines.
- **Protein**: A molecule made of a chain of smaller molecules called amino acids, folded into a shape. Proteins do most of the work in a cell. *e.g.* Your hair and nails are mostly a protein called keratin. Dal is rich in protein.
- **Amino acid**: A small molecule. Living things join amino acids into chains to make proteins. *e.g.* Stanley Miller made amino acids in a glass flask with gases, water and electric sparks.
- **Ribozyme**: A piece of RNA that does a job in the cell, such as cutting or joining other molecules. It is RNA acting like a tool. *e.g.* Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman found ribozymes in the early 1980s.
- **Hydrothermal vent**: A crack in the sea floor where hot water full of minerals pours out from deep inside the Earth. *e.g.* Vents were first seen in 1977, deep in the Pacific Ocean near the Galápagos Islands.
- **Meteorite**: A rock from space that falls to the ground on Earth. *e.g.* The Murchison meteorite fell in Australia in 1969. It holds amino acids that formed in space.
- **Hypothesis**: An idea that tries to explain something, and that can be tested. *e.g.* 'Life began as RNA' is a hypothesis. Scientists test it with experiments.
- **Ancestor**: A living being that someone came from, long ago. Your grandmothers and great-grandmothers are your ancestors. *e.g.* All living things today share very old ancestors: the first cells.

## Explanations
### Every living thing comes from another living thing · *defined*
**Definition:** Today, every living thing comes from another living thing of its own kind. A cell comes only from another cell.

Puppies come from dogs. A mango tree grows from a mango seed, and the seed came from another mango tree. Even bacteria come only from other bacteria, by splitting in two. Scientists tested this for hundreds of years, and it has always held. That is what makes the first life such a puzzle. If life comes only from life, how did the first life begin? Something must once have been different.
- e.g. You came from your parents, and they came from theirs.
- e.g. Curd sets only when you add a little old curd, because the curd bacteria come from other curd bacteria.
### The young Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. It had oceans of liquid water by about 4.4 billion years ago. The air then had almost no oxygen.

At first the Earth was very hot, and rocks from space crashed into it often. As it cooled, water fell as rain and filled the oceans. We know there was liquid water very early because of tiny crystals called zircons, found in Western Australia. Some are about 4.4 billion years old, and their chemistry shows they formed with water around. The young sea had no fish, no seaweed and no living thing at all. It held salts, iron, and simple chemicals made of carbon, nitrogen, hydrogen and oxygen.
- e.g. Zircon crystals from the Jack Hills in Western Australia are about 4.4 billion years old.
- e.g. A 4.5 metre time string, with one metre for each billion years, shows how long the Earth had no life.
### The oldest life we know: stromatolites · *defined*
**Definition:** Stromatolites are rocks built in layers by mats of microbes in shallow water. The oldest ones we are fairly sure of are about 3.5 billion years old.

A mat of microbes is a slimy carpet of millions of tiny living things. Mud and sand settle on the mat and stick to it. The microbes then grow up through the mud towards the light, and make a new mat on top. Year after year, this builds up a dome of thin layers. Over time the domes turn to stone. The oldest stromatolites we know are in the Pilbara, in Western Australia. Abigail Allwood, an Australian geologist, and her team showed in 2006 that a reef there, about 3.4 billion years old, was built by living things. Living stromatolites still grow today at Shark Bay, in Australia, where the water is too salty for snails that would eat the mats.
- e.g. Stromatolites about 2 billion years old are found near Vempalle in the Kadapa district of Andhra Pradesh.
- e.g. Jhamarkotra near Udaipur and Bhojunda near Chittorgarh, in Rajasthan, are stromatolite parks you can visit.
### How the first oxygen rusted the sea · *defined*
**Definition:** Cyanobacteria were among the first living things to make oxygen. Their oxygen joined with iron in the sea and made rust, which sank in layers. Later the oxygen filled the air.

Iron rusts only when oxygen reaches it. The young sea had almost no oxygen, so iron could stay dissolved in the water, the way salt stays dissolved. Then cyanobacteria began making oxygen from sunlight and water. Where their oxygen met the iron, rust formed and settled on the sea floor. Layer after layer, it made striped rocks called banded iron formations. Most scientists think much of this rust formed because of the microbes. By about 2.4 billion years ago, the extra oxygen also began to fill the air. Scientists call this the Great Oxidation Event. Much of the iron ore mined in India, at Kudremukh, Sandur and Bailadila, comes from banded iron rocks.
- e.g. In the nail test, the nail in open water rusted, but the nail sealed under oil stayed almost shiny.
- e.g. Banded iron rock has red stripes of rust between grey or black stripes.
### The building blocks of life can form without life · *defined*
**Definition:** Some molecules that living things are made of, such as amino acids, can form from simple chemicals with no living thing involved.

People once thought that only living things could make the chemicals of life. In 1828, Friedrich Wöhler made urea, a chemical found in urine, from chemicals that were not alive. In 1952, Stanley Miller filled a glass flask with water and the gases methane, ammonia and hydrogen. He made electric sparks in it, like lightning, for a week. The water turned brown, and in it he found amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. Amino acids have also been found in meteorites, such as the Murchison meteorite that fell in Australia in 1969. In 2008, Zita Martins and her team found uracil, one of the letters of RNA, in the same meteorite.
- e.g. Miller's flask made the amino acid glycine, which is also in your body.
- e.g. Meteorites show that some building blocks of life form even in space.
### RNA: a chain of four letters · *defined*
**Definition:** RNA is a chain molecule. Its chain is made of four kinds of small parts, written as A, U, C and G. The order of the letters can carry instructions.

Think of a long bead necklace with beads in four colours. The order of the colours is a message. RNA is like that, but the beads are molecules. The letters also pair up: A pairs with U, and C pairs with G. So a chain can attract loose letters to make a partner chain, letter by letter. Copy the partner, and you get the first chain back. DNA works in the same way, with T in place of U. In your cells today, RNA carries copies of instructions from the DNA to the cell's protein-building machines.
- e.g. The chain A-U-G-C has the partner chain U-A-C-G.
- e.g. Copy the partner U-A-C-G again, and you get A-U-G-C back.
### RNA that does jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Some RNA molecules fold into shapes that do jobs, such as cutting or joining other molecules. These are called ribozymes.

For a long time, scientists thought only proteins could do the jobs in a cell. Then, in the early 1980s, Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman found RNA molecules that do jobs too. They won the Nobel Prize in 1989. Then came an even bigger find. Every cell has machines called ribosomes, which join amino acids into proteins. In 2000, scientists showed that the part of the ribosome that does the joining is made of RNA, not protein. Venkatraman Ramakrishnan, born in Chidambaram in Tamil Nadu, Ada Yonath from Israel and Thomas Steitz from the United States worked out the ribosome's shape. They won the Nobel Prize in 2009. Ada Yonath worked on it for about 20 years, while many scientists said it could not be done.
- e.g. Every protein in your body was put together by ribosomes, at a working centre made of RNA.
- e.g. Some medicines kill harmful bacteria by jamming their ribosomes.
### The RNA idea · *idea*
This is an idea, not a settled fact. In every cell today, DNA holds the instructions, and proteins do most of the work. But each needs the other: proteins are needed to copy DNA, and DNA is needed to make proteins. So which came first? RNA can do both jobs. It can carry instructions, like DNA, and some RNA can do jobs, like proteins. So many scientists think the first life was RNA that could help copy itself. Later, DNA took over keeping the instructions, and proteins took over most of the jobs. Francis Crick, Leslie Orgel and Carl Woese suggested this in the 1960s, and Walter Gilbert named it 'the RNA world' in 1986. In 2009, Tracey Lincoln and Gerald Joyce made two RNA molecules in the lab that copy each other. But nobody has yet made RNA that copies itself from scratch, the way the idea needs.
- e.g. The ribosome's working centre is made of RNA, which may be left over from an RNA world.
- e.g. In the lab, RNA molecules can be made that join other RNA pieces together.
- **Scientists who favour the RNA idea:** RNA can carry instructions and do jobs, and the ribosome's working centre is made of RNA. So RNA could have come before DNA and proteins.
- **Scientists who favour 'metabolism first':** RNA is hard to make and breaks easily. They think chains of chemical reactions, maybe on mineral surfaces at vents, came first, and RNA came later.
### Cell skins that form by themselves · *defined*
**Definition:** Molecules called fatty acids can gather in water to make tiny hollow bubbles with a thin skin. Many scientists think the first cells had skins like these.

A fatty acid molecule has one end that mixes with water and one end that stays away from water. In water, many of them line up side by side, with their water-shy ends tucked in. They close up into tiny hollow bubbles, each with a skin two molecules thick. In 1985, David Deamer showed that fatty acids taken from the Murchison meteorite form bubbles like these. In 2003, Jack Szostak's team showed that clay helps these bubbles form, and that RNA can stick to the same clay. A bubble with RNA inside might have been the first cell. Soap is made from fatty acids too.
- e.g. Soap bubbles get their thin skin from soap molecules lining up.
- e.g. A cell's skin today is made of similar molecules, called lipids.
### Copying with small mistakes makes new kinds · *defined*
**Definition:** When a chain is copied, a few letters sometimes come out wrong. These changes are called mutations. Copies that last and copy well become more common.

No copying is perfect. If a copy has one changed letter, its own copies carry that change too. Most changes make no difference, and some make a copy worse. A few make a copy better at lasting or copying. Over many copies, the better ones become more common. This is how one kind of life slowly becomes many kinds. It still happens today, in every living thing. Cells now have ways to check and fix their copies, so their mistakes are very rare.
- e.g. Bacteria that change in a way that lets them survive a medicine become more common in a body that takes the medicine.
- e.g. In the copy-mistakes activity, a chain copied ten times ends up with letters that differ from the first chain.
### Where did life begin? · *debated*
Nobody knows where life began. There are a few main ideas, and each has some evidence for it.
- e.g. At hot vents on the sea floor, where hot water full of minerals meets cold sea.
- e.g. In warm pools on land, which fill with rain and dry out again and again.
- e.g. Some think life, or its building blocks, came from space.
- **Scientists who favour sea-floor vents, such as Michael Russell and Nick Lane:** Vents give warmth, minerals and a steady flow of energy. Deborah Kelley found a field of vents called the Lost City in 2000, and its chemistry may be like that of the first life.
- **Scientists who favour warm pools on land, such as David Deamer and John Sutherland:** On land, pools dry out and fill up again. Drying helps small molecules join into chains, and fatty acid bubbles form well in fresh water. Charles Darwin guessed a 'warm little pond' in a letter in 1871.
- **A few scientists, such as Fred Hoyle and Chandra Wickramasinghe:** They suggested that life itself came to Earth from space. Most scientists do not accept this. It would also not explain how life began in the first place.
### All life shares one family · *defined*
**Definition:** All living things today share the same ancestors, far back in time. Scientists call the most recent ancestor of all life today LUCA, the Last Universal Common Ancestor.

Bacteria, mushrooms, mango trees, crows and you all use DNA. All use RNA to carry instructions, and ribosomes to build proteins. All read the same three-letter code to choose amino acids. It is very unlikely that all of this matched by chance. So scientists agree that all life today comes from one shared family of early cells. Nobody knows exactly when LUCA lived. It was probably more than 3.5 billion years ago.
- e.g. Your cells and a banana's cells both hold DNA, as the banana DNA test shows.
- e.g. A bacterium's ribosome and your ribosome are built in a very similar way.

## Mostly agreed
- The Earth is about 4.5 billion years old. *How we know:* Scientists measure the age of rocks and meteorites from the slow, steady change of some atoms into others, called radioactive decay.
- Life has been on Earth for at least 3.5 billion years. *How we know:* Stromatolites and the chemistry of very old rocks in Australia, and later ones in South Africa, India and elsewhere.
- The first life was made of single cells, and it lived in water. *How we know:* The oldest fossils are all of microbes. Fossils of animals with many cells come much later, in the last 600 million years or so.
- Oxygen in the air was made by living things that use sunlight. *How we know:* Very old rocks show no sign of oxygen in the air. Rocks younger than about 2.4 billion years show it. Banded iron rocks record when oxygen met iron in the sea.
- Some building blocks of life can form without living things. *How we know:* Miller's spark experiment, many later lab experiments, and amino acids and RNA letters found in meteorites.
- All living things today share the same ancestors. *How we know:* All life uses DNA, RNA, ribosomes and the same genetic code.

## How we found out
**Friedrich Wöhler, a German chemist, and Stanley Miller, an American student working with Harold Urey, 1828 (Wöhler makes urea) and 1952 (Miller's spark experiment, published in 1953).** For a long time, many chemists thought the chemicals in living bodies were special. They believed only a living thing, with a 'life force' inside it, could make them. In 1828, Friedrich Wöhler, a German chemist, heated a salt called ammonium cyanate, which is made from non-living chemicals. To his surprise, he got urea, a chemical found in urine. He wrote to a friend that he had made urea without needing a kidney. It took many more experiments to convince everyone, but the old idea began to crack. More than a hundred years later, in 1952, Stanley Miller was a young student working with Harold Urey in Chicago. He wanted to test an idea of Alexander Oparin in Russia and J.B.S. Haldane in Britain: that the young sea was a soup in which life's chemicals could form. He filled a flask with water, methane, ammonia and hydrogen, and made electric sparks in it for a week. The water turned brown. In it were amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. Haldane later moved to India and became an Indian citizen. He worked in Kolkata and Bhubaneswar until he died in 1964.  
*What people believed before:* People thought that the chemicals of living things could only be made by living things, with a special life force. Wöhler made urea from non-living chemicals, and Miller made amino acids from gases, water and sparks.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only living things can make the chemicals of life.** (Many chemists until the 1800s; *rejected*)  
  For: For a long time, nobody could make sugar, urea or other chemicals of living bodies in the lab.  
  Against: Wöhler made urea in 1828, and Miller made amino acids in 1952. Chemists now make many such chemicals from non-living ones.
- **Life began as RNA, before DNA and proteins.** (Many scientists who study how life began; *debated*)  
  For: RNA can carry instructions and do jobs, and the ribosome's working centre is made of RNA.  
  Against: RNA is hard to make without a cell, and it breaks easily. Nobody has yet made RNA that copies itself from scratch.
- **Life began at hot vents on the sea floor.** (Michael Russell, Nick Lane and others; *debated*)  
  For: Vents give warmth, minerals and energy, all day and night, safe from the harsh light of the young Sun.  
  Against: RNA and other chains break apart easily in hot water, and vents do not dry out, so it is hard to join small pieces into long chains there.
- **Life began in warm pools on land.** (David Deamer, John Sutherland and others; *debated*)  
  For: Pools dry and wet again, which helps small pieces join into chains. Lab chemistry with sunlight makes RNA letters.  
  Against: There was little dry land on the young Earth, and we have no rocks from the first pools.
- **Life came to Earth from space.** (Svante Arrhenius in 1903, and later Fred Hoyle and Chandra Wickramasinghe; *minority*)  
  For: Meteorites carry amino acids and RNA letters, and some microbes can survive a long time in space.  
  Against: No living thing has ever been found in a meteorite. And the idea only moves the question: how did life begin out there?
- **All life today comes from one shared family of early cells.** (Nearly all biologists; *accepted*)  
  For: All living things use DNA, RNA, ribosomes and the same genetic code.  
  Against: Some think life may have begun more than once, and the other kinds died out. Even so, all life alive today shares one family.

## Roots
We have learned about the oldest life, the first oxygen, and the RNA idea of how life began. Now we ask what this story tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your nail in open water rusted, but the nail sealed under oil stayed almost grey. Iron rusts only where oxygen reaches it. Earth's early sea had almost no oxygen until tiny microbes began making it. Their oxygen rusted iron in the sea, and much of that rust became the iron ore India mines today.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of the Earth as a stage, and living things as the actors who live on it. But the oldest life changed the stage itself: the air, the sea and the rocks. The planet we live on is partly made by life.  
*Evidence:* In the nail test, the nail with oxygen rusted and the nail without oxygen did not. Banded iron rocks, at Kudremukh, Sandur and Bailadila, hold red layers of rust that formed when oxygen made by microbes met iron in the sea.

- *who am i:* Your cells hold DNA and RNA written in the same four-letter way as a banana's and a microbe's. You come from the same first cells as every living thing.
- *what is life:* Life began small and stayed tiny for most of the Earth's story. Those tiny lives changed the sea, the rocks and the air.
- *what is this world:* The air you breathe, and even the iron in a nail, carry the work of microbes from billions of years ago.

Before life, the young sea held only a few kinds of simple molecules. Some joined into chains, and chains that could be copied made more of themselves. Small copying mistakes made new kinds, and those made more kinds still. From a few simple molecules came every living thing, from microbes to mango trees to you. And the living things, in turn, remade the sea, the air and the rocks they came from.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1:** From the Self came space, from space air, from air fire, from fire water, from water earth. From earth came plants, from plants food, and from food the person. (Like the story science tells, it says the living came step by step out of simpler things, with water and earth before plants and people.)
- **Rig Veda 10.121.7, the Hiranyagarbha hymn:** When the great waters came, holding the seed of everything and giving birth to fire, the one life-breath of the gods arose from them. (The hymn places the first life-breath in the waters. Science too finds that the oldest life we know lived in water, and for most of the Earth's story, life lived only in water.)
- **Thales of Miletus, about 600 BCE, as reported by Aristotle in Metaphysics, Book 1 (983b):** Water is the origin of all things. (Thales, one of the first Greek philosophers, guessed that water was first. The oldest life we know did grow in water, though not everything comes from water.)
- Science (settled): Life has been on Earth for at least 3.5 billion years, and all life today shares one family.
- Science (settled): Oxygen in the air was made by living things, beginning with cyanobacteria.
- Science (idea): Life may have begun as RNA that could help copy itself.
- Science (open): How and where life began is not known.
- Other view, Many chemists and biologists, and many faiths: Many scientists see the start of life as chemistry following its own rules, with no aim or awareness behind it. Many faiths teach that life was made by God. Both views are held by thoughtful people, and the science does not settle that question either way.

The first ones were tiny microbes, billions of years ago. Breathe out slowly. Your breath goes back to the plants and the sea. Let's take five slow breaths like that, in silence.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If a chain of RNA could copy itself in the sea, would that chain be alive? (hint: Think about what living things do. Which of those things would the chain do, and which would it not?)
- *big:* Did life begin only once on Earth, or many times? (hint: All life today shares one family. Could other kinds have begun and died out without leaving fossils?)

## Story: The Stripes in the Hill
*Stromatolites about 2 billion years old really are found near Vempalle in the Kadapa district of Andhra Pradesh, and banded iron rock really is found near Sandur in Karnataka. Nila, Karthik, Revathi and this trip are made up.*

Nila was nine, and her brother Karthik was seven. In the summer holidays they went to stay with their aunt, Revathi Athai, in Kadapa. Athai was a geologist. That means she studied rocks. Her house was full of them. There were rocks on the shelves, rocks on the windowsills, and one big striped rock that held the bathroom door open.

"Tomorrow," said Athai at dinner, "I'll show you the oldest living thing you have ever seen." Karthik stopped eating his curd rice. "A tortoise?" he asked. "A banyan tree?" Athai only smiled.

Early the next morning, they drove out past fields of groundnut to a low grey hill near Vempalle. There were no trees on the hill, only rock. Athai walked along slowly, looking down. Then she knelt and brushed away some dust with her hand. "Here," she said.

Nila looked. The rock had thin wavy lines in it, hundreds of them, one on top of the other. In some places the lines curved up into little domes, like the tops of mushrooms. "It looks like a stack of dosas," said Karthik. Athai laughed. "It does. But a living thing made this."

"These are called stromatolites," Athai said. "About two billion years ago, this hill was the floor of a warm, shallow sea. On the sea floor lived a slimy mat of microbes, too small to see one by one. Mud settled on the mat. The microbes grew up through the mud towards the sunlight, and made a new mat on top. Then more mud came, and they grew up again." She ran her finger along one line. "Each of these lines is one layer of that mat."

"So the microbes are still in there?" asked Karthik. "Their bodies are long gone," said Athai. "But the shape of their work is kept in the stone. It is like a footprint in dried mud. The foot has gone, but you can still see where it was."

Nila counted the lines on one dome. She got to forty and lost count. "Two billion years," she said quietly. "Is that the oldest life there is?" "Not quite," said Athai. "In Australia there are stromatolites about three and a half billion years old. They are the oldest signs of life we are fairly sure of."

That evening, Athai took the big striped rock from the bathroom door and put it on the table. It had stripes of rusty red and stripes of shiny dark grey. "This one is from Sandur, in Karnataka," she said. "It is called banded iron. The red stripes are rust."

"Rust? Like on our gate?" asked Karthik. "Exactly like that," said Athai. "Long ago, the sea had almost no oxygen in it. Iron could stay in the water without rusting. Then microbes like the ones in the hill began to make oxygen from sunlight. Their oxygen met the iron, and rust formed. It sank to the sea floor in layers. So the microbes did not only make the stromatolites. They changed the whole sea, and in the end the air too. The oxygen you are breathing now was made by living things."

Karthik took a big breath and held it, as if it might run away. Nila was still thinking. "Athai," she said, "the microbes came from other microbes, right? Like curd comes from curd. But who did the very first microbes come from?"

Athai was quiet for a moment. Then she got up and fetched a pencil and a strip of paper. She wrote four letters on it: A, U, C, G. "Nobody knows yet," she said. "That is the honest answer. But here is one idea that many scientists like. Inside every cell there is a chain molecule called RNA. Its chain is made of four kinds of letters. And the letters pair up: A with U, and C with G."

She wrote a short chain: A U G C. Under it, she wrote the partners: U A C G. "Look. The first chain can make a partner chain, letter by letter. Copy the partner, and you get the first chain back. So a chain like this can make copies of itself, with a little help from the chemicals around it. Some RNA can also do jobs, like a tiny tool. Many scientists think life began as chains of RNA in the young sea, copying themselves."

"Did that really happen?" asked Karthik. "We don't know," said Athai. "Scientists are testing it. They have made some pieces of it work in the lab, but not the whole thing yet. Maybe one of you will find out."

Nila took the pencil and wrote a chain of her own. Then she made its partner, and the partner's partner. Her third chain matched her first one exactly, except for one letter, where she had written C instead of G. "Oops," she said. "A mistake."

"Keep it," said Athai. "Mistakes like that are how one kind of life slowly becomes many kinds." Nila looked at the striped rock, and at her paper chain, and at her brother, who was still holding his breath. "Breathe out, Karthik," she said. "The microbes will make more."

**For the parent:** For the parent: the wavy lines in the hill are stromatolites, layers built by mats of microbes that trapped mud and grew up through it. The red stripes in the banded iron are rust that formed when the first oxygen, made by microbes, met iron dissolved in the sea. The paper chain shows how RNA letters pair up, A with U and C with G, so that a chain can make a partner and the partner can make the first chain again. Nila's mistake shows how copies slowly change. Athai's honest answer is the real state of science: the RNA idea is a hypothesis, still being tested.

- Ask: How did tiny microbes make the wavy lines in the rock?
- Ask: What are the red stripes in the banded iron rock, and how did they form?
- Ask: Why could iron stay in the young sea without rusting?
- Ask: Nila's third chain had one letter wrong. Why did Athai say to keep it?
- Ask: Athai said 'Nobody knows yet.' Why is it good for a scientist to say that when it is true?

### e1 · The Rusting Nails: what did the first oxygen do to the sea? (30 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Does an iron nail in water rust when very little oxygen can reach it?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which nail will rust most: the nail in open tap water, the nail in boiled water sealed under oil, or the nail in open salty water? Which will rust least? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use three nails of the same kind and size, three jars of the same size, and the same amount of water in each. Keep the jars side by side in the same place. Only one thing changes in each jar: open water, water with little oxygen, or salty water.
- **safety:** A grown-up boils the water and pours it. Let it cool until just warm before anyone touches the jar. Nails can be sharp at the tip.
- **record:** Fill in the table each time you look. Draw each nail on day 3, colouring any rust orange.
- **results table:** Jar | What is in it | After 3 hours | After 1 day | After 2 days | After 3 days
- **schedule:** day 1: Boil water for jar B. Set up the three jars. Make your guess. Look again after 3 hours. (20 min); day 2: Look at all three nails. Write and draw what you see. (5 min); day 3: Look again, take the nails out, and say what you found. (5 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the three nails. 2. Ask them to guess which nail will rust most, and which least, and why.  
  *Parent says:* We'll put these iron nails in three jars of water. In one jar we'll keep oxygen away. Which nail do you think will rust most? Which will rust least? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the three nails. Which will rust most? Which will rust least? Why do you think so?
  - Note: Write the guess and the reason down before you start. Don't say what you expect. Any guess with a reason is good.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Boil about one glass of water in a small vessel for 5 minutes. 2. Put the lid on and let it cool for about 10 minutes, until warm.  
  *Parent says:* Boiling drives most of the air out of water. Air has oxygen in it. So boiled water has very little oxygen.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up boils some water. Boiling pushes the air out of it. So it has very little oxygen now.
  - Note: Keep the lid on while it cools, so less air gets back in. You can set up jars A and C while it cools.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the jars A, B and C. 2. Fill jar A half full of tap water. 3. Fill jar C half full of tap water and stir in 1 teaspoon of salt. 4. Drop one nail into each.  
  *Parent says:* Jar A is plain water with air. Jar C is salty water with air, a bit like the sea.  
  *Child mode:* Label the jars A, B and C. Put tap water in A. Put salty water in C. Drop a nail into each one.
- **s4 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Gently pour the warm boiled water into jar B, half full, without splashing. 2. Drop in the third nail. 3. Slowly pour 2 tablespoons of oil on top, so it makes a layer. 4. Put a lid on jar B.  
  *Parent says:* The oil layer stops air from getting back into the water. So the nail in jar B gets very little oxygen, like the iron in the young sea.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours the boiled water into jar B. Drop in the nail. Pour oil on top. The oil keeps the air out.
  - Note: Pour gently, because splashing mixes air back in. The oil should cover the whole surface. Put the three jars side by side, out of direct sun.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After about 3 hours, look closely at each nail. Write what you see in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look. Has anything changed yet?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each nail closely. Any orange yet? Write it down.
  - Note: The nail in salty water often shows an orange haze first. Tap water may show nothing yet. That is fine. Rust takes time.
  - Wait: Leave the jars for 3 hours
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at each nail on day 2. 2. Write and draw what you see. 3. Do not stir or move the jars.  
  *Parent says:* Which nail has changed most? Which has changed least?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the nails again. Which one has the most orange? Which has none? Draw them.
  - Check: In jar B, the nail sat in boiled water under a layer of oil. Why did the oil matter? → **The oil kept air, with its oxygen, out of the water**
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 3, look at all three nails. 2. Take them out with a spoon and lay them on a paper. 3. Compare them.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's see all three side by side. What do they tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Take the nails out with a spoon. Lay them side by side. Which rusted most? Which least?
  - Note: Usually C has rusted most, A has clear rust, and B is grey or has only a tiny spot or two. If B rusted a little, air got in, maybe from splashing or a gap in the oil. That is still a useful result: less oxygen gave less rust.
  - Wait: Come back the day after
- **s8 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: iron rusts when oxygen reaches it, and salt makes it rust faster. What I saw: nails A and C turned orange, but the nail in boiled water under oil stayed grey. Why: the oil kept oxygen away from nail B. Afterwards, tell them this. The young sea had almost no oxygen, so its iron did not rust. When microbes began making oxygen, the iron in the sea rusted and sank in layers.
  - Check: Long ago, the sea had almost no oxygen. Then tiny microbes began making oxygen. What happened to the iron in the sea? → **It joined with the oxygen, made rust and sank in layers**
### e2 · Banana Threads: can we see the instruction molecule? (25 min)
- **question:** Do a banana and green peas both hold white threads of DNA, and does a glass with no food give any?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will you see anything in the glass with no food? Will the banana or the peas give more threads? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same amounts of salt, washing liquid, water and spirit for every glass. Only the food changes: banana, green peas, or none.
- **safety:** Spirit catches fire easily and is poisonous to drink. A grown-up keeps the bottle, pours it, and keeps it far from the stove, lamps and flames. Wash hands afterwards. Don't eat anything used in the test.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw what you saw in each glass.
- **results table:** Glass | Food | What I saw in the spirit layer | How much (none, a little, a lot)
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the spirit in the freezer 30 minutes before you start. 2. Show your child the banana, the peas and an empty glass. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Every cell holds a long chain molecule called DNA. It holds the instructions for that living thing. Do you think we can see it? Will the empty glass show anything? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Every cell has instructions inside, in a chain called DNA. Can we see it? What will the empty glass show? Why?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mix 1 cup warm water, 2 teaspoons salt and 4 teaspoons washing liquid. Stir gently, without making foam. 2. Mash the banana in one bag and the peas in another, with 2 spoons of the mix each, for 2 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Mashing breaks the cells apart. The washing liquid breaks their thin skins. Salt helps the long DNA strands clump together, so we can see them.  
  *Child mode:* Mash the banana well. Mash the peas well. You are breaking open millions of cells.
  - Note: Keep the third portion of the mix with no food in it. That glass is the control. It shows what happens with no cells at all.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add 4 more spoons of the mix to each bag. Squish gently for 1 minute. 2. Strain each one into its own glass, about a quarter full. 3. Pour the same amount of plain mix into the third glass.  
  *Parent says:* Now we strain out the lumps. The DNA is in the runny liquid now.  
  *Child mode:* Strain each mash into its own glass. Pour plain mix into the third glass.
- **s4 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tilt each glass. 2. Pour the cold spirit very slowly down the inside wall, until there is a layer as deep as the liquid below. 3. Stand the glasses still.  
  *Parent says:* Pour gently, so the spirit floats on top as its own layer. Don't stir.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours cold spirit slowly down the side. It floats on top. Don't shake the glass.
  - Note: Pouring slowly is the most important step. If it mixes in, the threads are harder to see. Keep the spirit bottle closed and away from the stove.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wait 3 to 5 minutes. 2. Look where the two layers meet. 3. Gently twirl the toothpick there in each glass.  
  *Parent says:* Look where the two layers meet. Can you see anything white and cloudy rising up?  
  *Child mode:* Wait and watch where the layers meet. See white threads? Twirl the toothpick there.
  - Note: White, cloudy, stringy stuff rises into the spirit layer in the banana and pea glasses. It winds onto the toothpick like wet cotton. The glass with no food stays clear. If you see little, the spirit may not be cold enough, or there may be too much liquid. Try again with less liquid and colder spirit.
  - Check: The glass with no food stayed clear. The banana and pea glasses made white threads. What does this show? → **The threads came from the cells of the banana and the peas**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for three short sentences: what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: banana and peas both have DNA in their cells. I saw white threads in both, and none in the glass with no food. The cells broke open and the DNA clumped in the cold spirit. Then tell them this. Your cells have DNA too, made of the same four kinds of letters. Every living thing uses this same chain. RNA is its close cousin, and many scientists think RNA came first.

## Applications
- Searching for life on Mars: the Perseverance rover is collecting rock samples from Jezero Crater, an old lake bed, to look for signs of ancient microbes.
- Space missions to the icy moons Europa and Enceladus, which have oceans under their ice, look for the chemistry life might need.
- mRNA vaccines, such as some COVID-19 vaccines, use RNA to give cells instructions. Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman won the 2023 Nobel Prize for the work behind them.
- Some antibiotics, such as streptomycin and tetracycline, work by jamming the ribosomes of bacteria.
- Painting, oiling and coating iron, such as gates, bridges and ship hulls, keeps oxygen and water away so it rusts less.
- Geologists use stromatolites and banded iron rocks to find limestone, phosphate and iron ore. Jhamarkotra in Rajasthan is a phosphate mine near stromatolites.
- DNA tests in hospitals and in forensic labs start with pulling DNA out of cells, as the banana test does.
- Chemists who study how life began help design new ways to make medicines and materials from simple chemicals.

## Spotted around us
- A rusty iron gate, grille or bicycle chain after the monsoon. Oxygen and water reached the iron.
- Green or black streaks on damp walls and old clay pots in the rainy season, made by cyanobacteria and algae.
- Red, orange and grey striped rocks, used as building stone or seen on hillsides in Karnataka, Goa and Odisha, where iron ore is mined.
- Shooting stars on a clear night. They are tiny bits of space dust burning up. Some bigger pieces land as meteorites.
- The sea at a beach such as Marina in Chennai or Kovalam in Kerala. Life lived only in water for most of the Earth's story.
- Curd setting overnight. Today, new microbes come only from living microbes.

## Open questions
- **How did life begin?** There are good ideas, but nobody has made life from non-living chemicals, and the first life left no fossils we have found.
- **Was RNA really first?** RNA can carry instructions and do jobs, but it is hard to make without a cell and breaks easily. Some scientists think other chemistry came first.
- **Where did life begin: at sea-floor vents, in warm pools on land, or somewhere else?** Each place has evidence for and against. There are no rocks left from the exact time and place.
- **When exactly did life begin?** The oldest sure signs are about 3.5 billion years old. Some scientists think life is older, but older rocks are rare and changed by heat.
- **Did life begin once, or many times?** All life today is one family. Other kinds may have begun and died out without a trace.
- **Is there life on other worlds?** No life has been found beyond Earth yet. Mars, Europa and Enceladus are being studied.
- **How did the genetic code, the rules for turning RNA letters into proteins, begin?** All life uses nearly the same code, but nobody knows how it first came about.

Two strips of 50 cm make 1 metre. 4 metres need 4 lots of 2, which is 8 strips. Half a metre more needs 1 more strip. 8 plus 1 is 9. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Meenakshi counted rust spots on her nails after 2 days. The nail in open tap water had 10 spots. The nail in salty water had 30. The nail under oil had 1. How many times as many spots did the salty-water nail have as the tap-water nail? What does this tell you? **3 times as many. Salt makes iron rust faster.**. 30 divided by 10 is 3, so the salty-water nail had 3 times as many spots. Both nails had air and water. The only difference was salt, so salt made the rust form faster. The nail under oil had far fewer spots than either, because little oxygen reached it. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Imagine one RNA chain that makes one copy of itself every hour, and every copy does the same. Start with 1 chain. How many chains are there after 5 hours? **32 chains.**. The number doubles every hour: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. After 1 hour there are 2, and after 5 hours there are 32. Doubling grows very fast. After 10 hours there would be 1,024. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
