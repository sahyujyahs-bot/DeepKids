# Three things a force can change: shape, speed and direction
`sci.forces.changing-motion.L2`

**Summary.** Roll a marble round the inside of a curved card wall, and it follows the curve. Where the wall ends, the marble goes off in a straight line. So the wall was pushing it sideways all the way round. A force is a push or a pull, and it can change three things: the shape of a thing, its speed and its direction. Your child crumples foil, races a ball down ramps and bends a marble's path, to see each change happen.

**Intro.** *Roll a ball across the floor. Without touching the ball, can you make it turn a corner?* A rolling ball goes in a straight line until something pushes it sideways. A wall, a hand or a puff of air can turn it. Pushes and pulls can also squash things and stretch them. They can make things go faster or slower. In this unit you find all three things a force can change: shape, speed and direction. Then you find out what a ball does when the wall that is turning it suddenly ends.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that a force is a push or a pull, and that a force can change the shape, the speed or the direction of a thing.
1. By the end I can change the shape of foil, a bottle or a rubber band with a push or a pull, and say whether the new shape stays.
2. By the end I can show that a ball rolls faster and further from a steeper ramp, and say that gravity pulls it down the slope.
3. By the end I can make a rolling ball change direction with a push from the side or a wall.
4. By the end I can say that a ball going round a bend needs a push all the way round, and goes straight on when the push stops.
5. By the end I can guess what will happen, give a reason, test it, and say if my guess was right.

## Terms
- **Force**: A force is a push or a pull. You cannot see a force, but you can see what it does. *e.g.* When you push a door shut, your hand puts a force on the door.
- **Shape**: The shape of a thing is its outline: round, flat, long, bent or twisted. *e.g.* A ball of dough is round. Roll it out, and its shape becomes flat.
- **Squash**: To squash something is to push on it so that it gets flatter or smaller. *e.g.* When you sit on a cushion, you squash it.
- **Stretch**: To stretch something is to pull it so that it gets longer. *e.g.* Pull a hair rubber band between two fingers, and it stretches.
- **Twist**: To twist something is to turn one end one way and the other end the other way. *e.g.* When you wring out a wet towel, you twist it.
- **Speed**: Speed is how fast something is moving. *e.g.* A cycle going downhill has more speed than a cycle going uphill.
- **Direction**: The direction of a moving thing is the way it is going: forwards, back, left, right, up or down. *e.g.* An auto-rickshaw turning left at a corner changes its direction.
- **Spring back**: A thing springs back when it goes back to its old shape by itself after a push or a pull stops. *e.g.* Squeeze a sponge and let go. It springs back.

## Explanations
### A force is a push or a pull · *defined*
**Definition:** A force is a push or a pull on a thing.

You push a door to close it. You pull a drawer to open it. Pushes and pulls are both called forces. You cannot see a force. You see what it does to a thing. A force can change three things: its shape, its speed and its direction. A force needs something to push or pull, like a hand, a wall, the wind or the Earth.
- e.g. Your foot pushes a football.
- e.g. The Earth pulls a ball down a slide. This pull is called gravity.
- e.g. The wind pushes a kite up into the sky.
### A force can change a thing's shape · *defined*
**Definition:** When you squash, stretch, bend or twist a thing, a force changes its shape.

Crumple a sheet of foil, and your push makes it a ball. Pull a rubber band, and it gets longer. Foil and dough keep their new shape when you stop. A sponge and a rubber band spring back. So does a capped bottle, because the air inside pushes back. Why some things spring back and others do not depends on how the tiny particles inside them are joined.
- e.g. A potter pushes wet clay into the shape of a pot, and it keeps that shape.
- e.g. You stretch a rubber band round a box, and it springs back when you take it off.
- e.g. You squeeze a lemon, and it stays squashed.
- **Prashastapada, a thinker of the Vaisheshika school in India, about 1,500 years ago:** He gave a name to the way some things go back to their shape after a push. He called it sthitisthapaka, which means 'putting back the state'.
### A force can change a thing's speed · *defined*
**Definition:** A force can make a moving thing go faster or slower.

A ball let go at the top of a ramp starts slowly. The Earth pulls it down the slope, and it goes faster and faster. This pull is called gravity. On a steeper ramp, gravity speeds the ball up more, so it rolls further across the floor. On a cloth or a rug, the cloth rubs against the ball and slows it down. This rubbing is called friction.
- e.g. A cycle goes faster downhill because gravity pulls it down the slope.
- e.g. A cyclist squeezes the brakes, and the brakes rub the wheels to slow the cycle.
- e.g. A football slows down when it rolls onto grass.
### A force can change a thing's direction · *defined*
**Definition:** A push or a pull from the side can make a moving thing turn.

A rolling ball goes straight until something pushes it. Push it from behind, and it goes faster. Push it from the front, and it slows down. Push it from the side, and it turns. A wall can push too. Roll a ball straight at a wall, and it comes back towards you. Roll it at a slant, and it bounces off at a slant the other way.
- e.g. A cricket bat pushes the ball back towards the bowler.
- e.g. In carrom, the striker bounces off the wooden frame.
- e.g. A crow pushes the air with her wings and tail to turn in the sky.
### Going round a bend needs a push all the way round · *defined*
**Definition:** A moving thing goes in a straight line unless a force pushes or pulls it sideways. To go round a curve, it needs a sideways push all the way round.

A marble rolled round the inside of a curved wall. The wall pushed it sideways at every moment, so its path bent. Where the wall ended, nothing pushed it sideways any more. So it went off straight, in the direction it was going at that moment. Turning is a change in motion, just like speeding up or slowing down. So turning needs a force too.
- e.g. The rim of a steel plate pushes a marble inwards, so the marble runs round and round inside it.
- e.g. On a curve, the rails push a train's wheels sideways, so the train turns.
- e.g. The Earth's gravity pulls the Moon all the time, so the Moon goes round the Earth instead of flying off straight.
### Why does a moving thing keep going straight? · *idea*
Isaac Newton wrote it down as a rule in 1687: a moving thing keeps going straight at the same speed until a force changes it. Space probes far from any planet do almost exactly this. But why the world works this way is still a question. Nobody knows for sure.
- e.g. A marble rolling off the end of a curved wall goes straight.
- e.g. A space probe far out in space keeps going for years without an engine.
- **Many physicists:** It is simply how the world works. Newton's rule describes it, and we may never find a deeper reason.
- **Ernst Mach, an Austrian scientist, in 1883:** He suggested that the way things keep moving might depend on all the stars and other matter in the universe. Scientists still argue about this idea.

## Mostly agreed
- A force can change the shape, the speed or the direction of a thing. *How we know:* Anyone can test it at home: crumple foil, roll a ball down a ramp, or roll a ball at a wall.
- A moving thing goes in a straight line unless a force pushes or pulls it sideways. *How we know:* A marble leaving a curved wall goes straight. Space probes far from planets travel in nearly straight lines for years.
- Gravity pulls things down a slope, so they speed up as they roll down. *How we know:* A ball let go on a steeper ramp rolls faster and further. People have measured this since Galileo rolled balls down ramps in Italy, about 400 years ago.
- The Moon goes round the Earth because the Earth's gravity pulls the Moon all the time. *How we know:* Isaac Newton showed in 1687 that the Earth's gravity gives just the bend in the Moon's path that people had measured. Satellites sent up by ISRO and others follow the same rules.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, a Greek thinker; Robert Hooke and Isaac Newton, English scientists; and Michael McCloskey, Alfonso Caramazza and Bert Green, scientists in the United States, About 350 BCE, 1679, 1687 and 1980.** For about 2,000 years, many people followed the ideas of Aristotle, a Greek thinker. He taught that the Moon, the Sun and the planets move in circles naturally. Circles, he said, were the perfect motion of the sky, and needed no cause. In 1679, Robert Hooke, an English scientist, wrote a letter to Isaac Newton with a new idea. A planet, he said, would move in a straight line if nothing pulled it. Its path bends into a curve because the Sun pulls it all the time. Newton worked out the maths. In 1687, in his book called the Principia, he showed that one pull, gravity, bends the Moon's path round the Earth and the planets' paths round the Sun. A moving thing goes straight unless a force turns it. In 1980, three scientists in the United States asked university students what a ball does when it shoots out of a curved tube. Many of them guessed it would keep curving. So the old idea, that curving can carry on by itself, is still in many people's heads today.  
*What people believed before:* Things that move in circles, like the Moon and the planets, keep curving by themselves, without any force.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A ball that has been going round a curve keeps curving after the curve ends.** (A common guess among children, and among many grown-ups too; *rejected*)  
  For: The ball was going round, so it seems it should keep going round for a while.  
  Against: In the Bend Wall test, the marble went straight the moment the wall ended. In 1980, Michael McCloskey, Alfonso Caramazza and Bert Green in the United States asked university students the same question with a curved tube. Many of the students guessed wrong too.
- **Turning a corner needs no force, as long as the speed stays the same.** (A common idea, because a turning ball does not get faster or slower; *rejected*)  
  For: When a ball goes round a curve at the same speed, it does not look as if anything is changing.  
  Against: The direction changes, and that is a change in motion. A ball only turns when a wall, a hand or a pull pushes it sideways. Take the wall away, and it goes straight.
- **Only soft things change shape when you push them.** (A common first idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Dough, sponge and foil change shape easily, but a steel spoon or a stone floor does not seem to.  
  Against: A steel spoon bends if you push hard enough, and a car's steel body dents in a crash. Hard things change shape too, but a much bigger force is needed, or the change is too small to see.
- **Gravity is not a pull at all. Big things like the Earth bend space and time, and the Moon follows the bend.** (Albert Einstein's theory of gravity, from 1915; *accepted*)  
  For: Einstein's idea explains things Newton's pull cannot, like light from far stars bending as it passes the Sun. This was measured during an eclipse in 1919.  
  Against: For the Moon, a ball on a ramp and a satellite, thinking of gravity as a pull works perfectly well. Nobody yet knows how to join Einstein's idea with the physics of tiny particles.

## Roots
We have changed shapes, sped a ball up on a ramp and turned a marble with a card wall. Now we ask what the Bend Wall test tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your marble ran round inside the curved wall. Where the wall ended, the marble went off straight. So the wall was pushing it sideways all the way round. Going round a curve needs a push every moment, even when the speed stays the same.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a force is needed only to start, stop, speed up or slow down. Turning seems to cost nothing, because the marble goes no faster or slower. But a change of direction is a change in motion too, and it needs a force all the way round.  
*Evidence:* In the Bend Wall test, the marble followed the wall and went straight on the moment the wall ended, with a long wall and a short one. A finger held on the outside of the wall felt the marble knocking against it.

- *who am i:* You change direction many times as you walk round your home. Each turn happens because your feet push the floor sideways, and the floor pushes you back.
- *what is life:* Animals turn by pushing on something. A crow pushes the air, an ant pushes the ground, a fish pushes the water, and you push the floor. The crow chooses when to turn, and the air answers the push of her wings.
- *what is this world:* The Moon goes round the Earth, and the Earth goes round the Sun, because something pulls them all the time. Without that pull, each would fly off in a straight line.

Nothing in the Bend Wall test turned by itself. The marble turned because the wall pushed it, and the wall felt the marble push back. Every change of shape, speed or direction in this unit came from two things meeting and pushing on each other. A rolling marble, a turning crow and the circling Moon are all changed by what they meet. Everything that took part, the marble and the wall too, is part of one conscious world, sensing and answering what it meets.

- **Prashastapada, Padarthadharmasangraha (Vaisheshika school, India):** Prashastapada described three kinds of samskara, a tendency a thing keeps after it is acted on. Vega is the tendency of a moving thing to keep moving. Sthitisthapaka is the tendency of a bent thing to spring back. Bhavana is the impression an experience leaves in the mind, which lets us remember. (Vega is the marble rolling straight on after the wall ends. Sthitisthapaka is the rubber band springing back in Foil Shapes. Prashastapada put both in the same family as memory.)
- **Dhammapada, verse 277:** The Buddha taught that all conditioned things are impermanent. A conditioned thing is one that comes about through causes, and so it changes when the causes change. (In every test in this unit, a change had a cause you could find: a hand, a wall or the pull of the Earth. When the wall's push ended, the marble's path changed too.)
- Science (settled): A moving thing keeps going straight at the same speed unless a force acts on it. Isaac Newton wrote this down as his first law in 1687.
- Science (open): Why moving things behave this way has no agreed deeper reason. Ernst Mach suggested in 1883 that it depends on all the matter in the universe.
- Other view, Many physicists: Many physicists say there is no awareness in a marble or a wall. They describe the push between them as tiny electric forces between particles, and say nothing more is going on.
- Practice, The Turning Walk (3 min): Stand at one side of the room. Walk slowly in a straight line. Just before the wall, turn round and walk back. Do it three times, very slowly. Feel your feet as you turn. Which way do they push on the floor? Now stand still and breathe out slowly. Your body went straight until your feet pushed. Where else did something push you today?
- Ask: The marble went straight when the wall ended. Is going straight what a thing does when nothing changes it?
- Ask: When you lean on a wall, you push the wall and the wall pushes you. Which one is doing the pushing?
- Ask: The Moon has gone round the Earth for billions of years. What would happen to the Moon if the Earth's pull stopped?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The Moon turns a little every moment as it goes round the Earth. What is pulling it, and why does it not fall onto the Earth? (hint: Think of the marble in the Bend Wall test. It was moving sideways fast, and the wall kept bending its path.)
- *deep:* Foil stayed crumpled, but a rubber band sprang back. What is different inside them? (hint: Think of tiny particles holding on to each other. Do they go back to their old places, or find new ones?)
- *deep:* Can anything move, stop or turn without being pushed or pulled at all? (hint: Try to think of one example. Then look for the push or the pull you missed.)
- *big:* The Earth carries you round the Sun very fast, and turns you all the time. Why do you not feel it? (hint: The turn is very gentle, and everything around you turns with you. You feel a turn in a bus because the bus turns sharply.)
- *big:* Your body changes shape a little every time you sit, jump or smile. Are you the same shape as you were yesterday? (hint: Think of your cheeks when you smile, and your feet when you stand on them.)

## Story: Paati's Corner Shot
*A made-up story. Kavya, Arjun and Paati are made up. Paati means grandmother in Tamil. Chennai really does get most of its rain from October to December. Everything the striker does in the story is what a real carrom striker does.*

It had rained in Chennai for three days. The roads were full of puddles, and the school had a holiday. Kavya was seven. She stood at the window of their flat and watched the rain fall.

Her cousin Arjun had come from Pune for the holidays. He was eight. He was bored, and he said so every ten minutes.

"Then we will play carrom," said Paati. Paati was Kavya's grandmother. She pulled the big square board out from under the bed and blew the dust off it.

Paati sprinkled a pinch of white carrom powder on the board. She rubbed it in with her palm. "Now the coins will slide well," she said.

She set the coins in a flower shape in the middle. The red queen went in the centre. The black and white coins went round her in a ring.

Arjun went first. He put the striker on the line in front of him. He flicked it as hard as he could. The striker shot across the board. Coins flew everywhere. One white coin dropped into a pocket.

"I won!" shouted Arjun.

"You got one coin," said Kavya. "There are nineteen."

Arjun flicked again, very gently this time. The striker crept forward and stopped before it reached any coin.

"Too soft," said Paati. "A big flick gives a big push. A small flick gives a small push. Your finger decides how fast the striker goes."

Arjun looked at his finger.

"Now you, Kavya," said Paati. "Press your finger on the board, the way you do before you flick." Kavya pressed. The tip of her finger went flat and white. "Your finger changed shape," said Paati. "The board pushed back on it. When you lift it, it comes back to its round shape."

Now it was Kavya's turn. A black coin sat right next to the far pocket. But the red queen was in the way. There was no straight path to the black coin.

"I cannot hit it," said Kavya. "The queen is in front."

Paati smiled. "Then do not go straight. Go round."

"Round? A striker cannot go round corners," said Arjun.

"Can it not?" said Paati. She put her own fingers on the striker. She did not aim at the black coin. She aimed at the side of the board, at the wooden frame.

She flicked. The striker raced at a slant to the side of the board. It hit the wooden frame with a little knock. Then it shot off at a slant the other way. It slid past the queen and tapped the black coin. The black coin dropped into the pocket.

Arjun's mouth fell open. "How did it turn?"

"Put your hand on the frame, here, where it hit," said Paati. She flicked the striker at the frame again. Arjun felt a small knock against his palm.

"I felt the striker push the frame," he said.

"And the frame pushed the striker back," said Paati. "That push turned it. A striker goes straight until something pushes it."

"Your finger pushes it to start it. A coin pushes it when they bump. The frame pushes it when it hits the side. Each push changes where it goes, or how fast."

"But the frame is not alive," said Arjun. "It cannot push."

"Lean on the wall," said Kavya. Arjun leaned on the wall of the flat. "Why do you not fall over?" she asked.

Arjun thought. "The wall is holding me up."

"It is pushing you," said Kavya. "Paati just said it. The frame pushed the striker back."

Paati laughed. "Kavya listens better than I thought."

They played all afternoon. Arjun practised bouncing the striker off the frame. Kavya drew a picture of the board with arrows to show where the striker went. Each time it hit the frame at a slant, it came off at a slant the other way.

By the evening the rain had stopped. Arjun stood at the window. An auto-rickshaw drove down the wet road. At the corner, the driver turned the handle, and the auto swung round to the left.

"Kavya," said Arjun. "What pushed the auto round the corner? There is no wall there."

Kavya looked at the auto's front wheel, turned to the left. She looked at the road. She was not sure.

"Paati!" they both called.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Arjun thinks a striker can only go straight, and that a wooden frame cannot push anything. Paati shows him that the frame pushes the striker and turns it. Kavya adds that a wall pushes him when he leans on it. Every change in the striker's speed or direction comes from a push: a finger, a coin or the frame. The open question at the end has an answer the child can work out: the turned wheel pushes sideways on the road, and the road pushes the wheel back, which turns the auto.

- Ask: Why did the striker go faster when Arjun flicked it harder?
- Ask: How did Paati get the striker to the black coin when the queen was in the way?
- Ask: What pushed the striker when it changed direction?
- Ask: What do you think pushed the auto-rickshaw round the corner?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bend Wall: where does the marble go when the wall ends? (20 min)
- **question:** A marble rolls round the inside of a curved wall. When the wall ends, will the marble keep curving, go straight on, or swing out wide?
- **predict:** Look at the three paths drawn on the paper. Path A keeps curving. Path B goes straight on. Path C swings out wide. Which path will the marble take when the wall ends? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Start the marble from the same mark each time, with the same gentle push. Try each wall three times, because one try can go wrong. Change only how long the wall is.
- **safety:** Marbles are a choking risk for children under 3. Keep them away from babies and toddlers, and pick them all up afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw the wall from above, and draw the path the marble really took with an arrow.
- **results table:** Wall | My guess: A, B or C | Try 1 | Try 2 | Try 3 | Was my guess right?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the marble in the steel plate on the floor. 2. Your child moves the plate in small circles until the marble runs round the rim. 3. Then hold the plate still and watch.  
  *Parent says:* Swirl the plate gently. What is the marble doing? What keeps it from rolling away?  
  *Child mode:* Swirl the plate gently. Watch the marble run round. What keeps it in?
  - Ask: What keeps the marble going round? (good: The edge of the plate.; The rim stops it going out.; The rim pushes it back in.)
  - Note: The marble runs round the rim many times. Do not explain yet. Just agree that the rim keeps it in.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the newspaper flat on the floor. 2. A grown-up cuts the card strip. 3. Stand the strip on its long edge and bend it into a half circle. 4. Hold it up from outside with tumblers and tape.  
  *Parent says:* This card is a wall, like the rim of the plate. But this wall stops halfway round.  
  *Child mode:* Help stand the card up in a half circle. It is a wall.
  - Note: The wall must stand firm, so the marble can run along it without knocking it over. Tape the bottom edge to the paper on the outside.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Mark a start dot at one end of the wall, on the inside. 2. At the far end, draw three paths with the sketch pen. 3. A keeps curving, B goes straight on, C swings out wide. 4. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* The marble will run round the wall. Then the wall ends. Will it take path A, B or C? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* When the wall ends, will the marble go A, B or C? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: A, because it was going round, so it keeps going round.; B, because nothing is pushing it any more.; C, because it was going fast and flies out.)
  - Note: Every guess is fine if it has a reason. Many children, and many grown-ups, guess A. Write the guess in the table.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the marble on the start dot, touching the wall. 2. Your child gives it a gentle push along the wall. 3. Watch where it goes when the wall ends. 4. Put a small dot where it stops. 5. Try three times.  
  *Parent says:* Push it gently along the wall. Watch the end of the wall. Which path did it take?  
  *Child mode:* Push the marble gently along the wall. Which path did it take?
  - Note: The marble follows the curve and then goes off straight, along path B. If it jumps over the wall, push more gently. If it stops before the end, push a little harder.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fold the last part of the wall flat outwards, so the curve is only a quarter circle. 2. Ask for a new guess. 3. Try three times and mark each path.  
  *Parent says:* Now the wall ends sooner. Where will the marble go now? Try it three times.  
  *Child mode:* Now the wall is shorter. Guess, then try three times.
  - Note: The marble again goes straight on, but now in a different direction. It goes the way it was heading at the moment the wall ended.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child holds the middle of the wall lightly from outside with one finger. 2. Roll the marble again. 3. Then talk about what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the wall lightly from outside. Did you feel the marble knock against it? So what was the wall doing to the marble all the way round?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the wall lightly. Do you feel a little knock? What did the wall do?
  - Ask: Why did the marble go straight when the wall ended? (good: Nothing was pushing it round any more.; The wall was pushing it, and then the push stopped.; It only turns when something pushes it sideways.)
  - Note: The knock is small with a marble. A heavier ball, such as a small rubber ball, gives a bigger one. A strong answer says: 'I thought A. It went B. The wall was pushing it round, and when the wall ended, it went straight.' Then read the card on going round a bend.
  - Check: A marble runs round inside a curved wall. The wall ends. Which way does the marble go? → **Straight on, the way it was going**
### e2 · The Ramp Race: does a steeper ramp make a faster ball? (20 min)
- **question:** If you let a ball roll down a ramp without pushing it, does a steeper ramp make it go faster and roll further?
- **predict:** Will the ball roll furthest from a ramp on 1 book, 2 books or 3 books? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same ball and the same ramp each time. Let the ball go from the same top mark, and never push it. Change only the number of books. Try each height three times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Circle the height that sent the ball furthest.
- **results table:** Books under the ramp | My guess | Try 1 (hand-spans) | Try 2 (hand-spans) | Try 3 (hand-spans)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table. 2. Ask your child to guess which height will send the ball furthest, and why.  
  *Parent says:* We will roll a ball down a ramp, without pushing it. Which ramp will send it furthest: 1 book, 2 books or 3? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which ramp will send the ball furthest? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: 3 books, because it is steeper.; 3 books, because the ball goes faster downhill.; They will be the same, because it is the same ball.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one end of the ramp on 1 book. 2. Spread the cloth flat at the bottom. 3. Mark the top of the ramp. 4. Your child holds the ball on the mark and lets go. 5. Mark where it stops. 6. Try three times.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the ball on the mark. Open your fingers and let it go. Do not push it. Where did it stop?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the ball on the mark. Let go. Do not push.
  - Note: The cloth slows the ball down, so it stops in a few hand-spans instead of rolling across the room. Let the bottom of the ramp rest on the cloth, so the ball does not bounce. If the ball stops in less than one hand-span, use a thinner cloth. If it rolls off the cloth, fold the cloth double.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add a second book and do three tries. 2. Add a third book and do three tries. 3. Each time, count the hand-spans from the end of the ramp to the ball.  
  *Parent says:* Now the ramp is steeper. Watch the ball at the bottom of the ramp. Is it going faster? Count your hand-spans.  
  *Child mode:* Add a book. Let go again. Count the hand-spans.
  - Note: From 3 books the ball comes off the ramp faster and rolls further. Count in your child's hand-spans, so the numbers are theirs.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask what they think, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Which ramp sent the ball furthest? Nobody pushed the ball. So what made it go faster?  
  *Child mode:* Which ramp won? Nobody pushed the ball. What made it go?
  - Ask: What made the ball go faster on the steep ramp? (good: The Earth pulled it down.; Gravity pulled it down the slope.; It was going downhill more steeply.)
  - Note: Gravity, the pull of the Earth, speeds the ball up as it rolls down. On a steeper ramp it speeds the ball up more. The cloth then slows it down. So one force sped it up and another slowed it.
  - Check: Nobody pushed the ball, but it went faster down the ramp. What pulled it? → **Gravity, the pull of the Earth**

   Options: A push or a pull / A kind of ball / How fast a thing goes  
   **Answer:** A push or a pull. *Why:* A force is a push or a pull. Speed is how fast a thing goes.
2. (mcq) You crumple a sheet of foil into a ball. What did your push change?  
   Options: Its shape / Its colour / Nothing  
   **Answer:** Its shape. *Why:* Your hands squashed the foil, so its shape changed.
3. (mcq) Which of these goes back to its old shape when you let go?  
   Options: A stretched rubber band / Crumpled foil / Dough pressed flat  
   **Answer:** A stretched rubber band. *Why:* A rubber band springs back. Foil and dough keep their new shape.
4. (mcq) The same ball is let go on a ramp on 1 book and on a ramp on 3 books. Which rolls further?  
   Options: The ball from 3 books / The ball from 1 book / Both the same  
   **Answer:** The ball from 3 books. *Why:* The steeper ramp lets gravity speed the ball up more, so it rolls further.
5. (mcq) A ball rolls off the ramp onto a rug and slows down. What slows it?  
   Options: The rug rubbing against it (friction) / Gravity pushing it up / Nothing  
   **Answer:** The rug rubbing against it (friction). *Why:* The rug rubs against the ball. This rubbing is called friction, and it slows the ball down.
6. (mcq) A ball is rolling across the floor. You push it gently from its side. What happens?  
   Options: It turns / It goes faster in the same direction / It changes shape  
   **Answer:** It turns. *Why:* A push from the side changes the ball's direction.
7. (mcq) You roll a ball straight at a wall. What does it do after it hits?  
   Options: It comes back towards you / It goes through the wall / It goes off to the side  
   **Answer:** It comes back towards you. *Why:* The wall pushes the ball back the way it came.
8. (mcq) A marble rolls round the inside of the rim of a steel plate. What keeps it going round?  
   Options: The rim pushes it inwards all the time / The marble likes going round / The plate is heavy  
   **Answer:** The rim pushes it inwards all the time. *Why:* The rim pushes the marble inwards at every moment. That push bends its path into a circle.
9. (mcq) In the Bend Wall test, you held a finger on the outside of the wall. What did it feel?  
   Options: The marble knocking against the wall / The wall pulling the marble / Nothing at all  
   **Answer:** The marble knocking against the wall. *Why:* The wall pushes the marble sideways, and the marble pushes back on the wall, so a finger on the wall feels a small knock.
10. (truefalse) True or false: a bus going round a corner at the same speed needs no force.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The bus changes direction, and that needs a force. The road pushes on its turned wheels.
11. (mcq) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 2, guessed the marble would keep curving. It went straight. What is the best thing for her to do?  
   Options: Write down what really happened, and think about why / Change her guess in the table so it looks right / Stop doing the test  
   **Answer:** Write down what really happened, and think about why. *Why:* A wrong guess is useful. Writing what really happened, and thinking about why, is how scientists learn.

## Applications
- Cycles, scooters and buses use brakes to slow down, and turned wheels to change direction.
- Potters, cooks and tailors change the shape of clay, dough and cloth with pushes, pulls and twists.
- On a curve, the rails push a train's wheels sideways, and that push turns the whole train.
- In 2014, ISRO's Mangalyaan fired its engine near Mars to slow down, so that Mars's gravity could bend its path into a loop round Mars. Ritu Karidhal was one of the women scientists who led the mission's work.
- Carrom, cricket and football players use a wall, a bat or the ground to change which way the ball goes.

## Spotted around us
- An auto-rickshaw at a street corner turns its front wheel, and the road pushes it round the bend.
- On a curve in a Mumbai local train, you sway to the side. Your body tries to go straight while the train turns.
- In a kitchen in Kolkata, a cook presses balls of dough flat to make luchis.
- On Uttarayan in Gujarat, a flier pulls the kite string, and the kite swoops and turns.
- At the Thiruvarur temple car festival in Tamil Nadu, thousands of people pull thick ropes together to start the huge wooden chariot rolling.

## Open questions
- **Why does a moving thing keep going in a straight line when nothing pushes it?** Isaac Newton described this rule, and it works everywhere we look. But nobody knows why the world works this way. Some scientists think it may depend on all the matter in the universe.
- **Is gravity a pull, or is it the Earth bending space and time?** Albert Einstein's idea of bent space and time explains gravity very well. But scientists have not yet found how to fit it together with the physics of the tiniest particles.
- **Exactly how much force will make a material crack instead of bend?** Engineers can test materials and make good guesses. But a tiny hidden crack can change the answer, so the exact moment a thing breaks is still hard to predict.

## Connected maths
- Imagine a Ramp Race where the ball rolled 3 hand-spans from 1 book, 5 from 2 books and 7 from 3 books. If the pattern goes on, how far will it roll from 4 books? **About 9 hand-spans.**. Each extra book adds 2 hand-spans: 3, 5, 7. The next number is 7 and 2 more, which is 9. A real ball may not follow the pattern exactly, so test it to check. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L2`)
- Imagine a Change Spotter tally with 4 shape changes, 6 speed changes and 5 direction changes. Which kind was seen most? How many changes were there in all? **Speed changes, with 6. 15 changes in all.**. 6 is more than 5 and more than 4. 4 and 6 make 10, and 10 and 5 make 15. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- The Bend Wall was a card strip 60 centimetres long. For the second test, the curved part was half as long. How long was it then? **30 centimetres.**. Half of 60 is 30, because 30 and 30 make 60. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L2`)
- In Ball Hockey Round the Corner, Divya, a girl in Class 2, used 4 pushes on Course 1 and 7 pushes on Course 3. Why do you think Course 3 needed more pushes? **Course 3 has a corner, so the ball must be turned. Turning takes extra pushes from the side.**. On Course 1 the ball only goes straight. On Course 3 it must also change direction, and each change needs a push. 7 is 3 more than 4. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- On squared paper, draw a ball's path: 3 squares forward, turn right, 2 squares forward, turn right, 3 squares forward. Where does the ball end up? **2 squares to the right of where it started.**. The two 3-square parts go forward and then back, so they cancel out. Only the 2 squares to the right are left. (queues `math.position.directions-and-grids.L2`)
