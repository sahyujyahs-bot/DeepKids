# Sound travels: how a sound gets from where it is made to your ears
`sci.sound.sound-moves.L1`

**Summary.** A grown-up rubs two fingers together. Close by, you hear a soft rustle. Step back a few steps, and the rustle is gone, though the fingers are still rubbing. A sound travels out from its source, through the air, to your ears. It spreads out to every side as it goes, so it gets softer the further it travels. Your child tests how far three sounds can travel in your home, finds that a sound reaches them even from behind and round a corner, and sends their voice one way with a paper cone.

**Intro.** *Shut your eyes. A grown-up will tap a spoon on a steel tumbler somewhere in the room. Point to where the sound is. How did the sound find you?* You did not touch the tumbler, and you did not see it. Still, the sound reached you. A sound starts at its source, the thing that makes it. Then it travels through the air, all the way to your ears. In this unit you find out how far a sound can travel, which way it goes, and why far-away sounds are soft.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that a sound travels from its source, through the air, to my ears.
1. By the end I can show that a sound gets softer the further it travels, by testing how far away I can still hear it.
2. By the end I can show that a sound spreads out to every side, so I can hear it from in front, from behind and round a corner.
3. By the end I can use a paper cone to send more of my voice one way.
4. By the end I can say that sound takes a little time to travel, which is why thunder comes after the lightning.

## Terms
- **Sound source**: The sound source is the thing that makes a sound. The sound starts there. *e.g.* When a pressure cooker whistles, the pressure cooker is the sound source.
- **Travel**: To travel is to go from one place to another. A sound travels from its source to your ears. *e.g.* The cooker's whistle travels from the kitchen to your bedroom.
- **Spread out**: To spread out is to go to every side at once. A sound spreads out from its source, like ripples spread out on water. *e.g.* Everyone around a temple bell hears it, in front, behind and on both sides.
- **Megaphone**: A megaphone is a cone you speak into. It sends more of your voice one way, so people far away in front of you hear you better. *e.g.* You can make a megaphone by rolling a sheet of newspaper into a cone.

## Explanations
### A sound travels from its source to your ears · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound starts at its source. It travels through the air until it reaches your ears. Then you hear it.

When you tapped the tumbler, it moved very fast. It pushed the air next to it. That air pushed the air next to it, and so on, all the way to your ears. You cannot see the air doing this, but you hear the result. Air is not the only thing a sound can travel through. That is why you can hear the neighbour's mixie through the wall. In Class 4 you will test which things carry sound best.
- e.g. The cooker's whistle travelling from the kitchen to the bedroom
- e.g. A koel's call travelling from a tree to your window
- e.g. The neighbour's mixie heard through the wall
- e.g. Your friend's voice travelling across the room to you
### A sound spreads out, so it gets softer as it travels · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound spreads out to every side from its source. The further it travels, the more it has spread out, so less of it reaches each ear. That is why far-away sounds are soft.

In the Walk Away test, the rubbing fingers made the same sound every time. Close by, you heard it well. A few steps back, it was gone. The sound had spread out into the whole room, so only a little of it reached you. A loud sound starts big, so it can spread a long way before it fades. That is why you heard the spoon on the tumbler from much further away than the rubbing fingers. Because a sound spreads to every side, you can hear it from in front, from behind, and even round the corner of a door.
- e.g. Rubbing fingers heard only from close by
- e.g. A spoon on a steel tumbler heard across the flat
- e.g. A temple bell heard by everyone around it, on every side
- e.g. The cooker heard from round the corner of the kitchen door
### A cone sends more of a sound one way · *defined*
**Definition:** A megaphone is a cone that keeps a sound from spreading out to the sides. More of the sound goes out of the wide end, in one direction.

Without a cone, your voice spreads out to every side. Some goes to the walls, some to the ceiling, some behind you. A cone sends more of it forward. So a person in front of you, far away, hears you better. A person behind you hears you less. Many festival loudspeakers are shaped like cones for the same reason.
- e.g. A rolled newspaper megaphone
- e.g. Cone-shaped loudspeakers on poles at festivals
- e.g. Cupping your hands round your mouth to call someone
### Sound takes a little time to travel · *defined*
**Definition:** A sound does not arrive the moment it is made. It takes a little time to travel. The further away the source, the longer the wait.

In a thunderstorm, lightning and thunder are made at the same moment. The light of the flash reaches you almost at once. The sound of the thunder travels much more slowly. So you see the flash first, and hear the thunder after. Count slowly after you see a flash. A long count means the storm is far away. A short count means the storm is close.
- e.g. Seeing lightning first, then hearing the thunder
- e.g. Seeing a cricket bat hit a ball across a big ground, then hearing the knock a moment later
- e.g. Seeing fireworks burst in the sky, then hearing the bang

## Mostly agreed
- A sound travels from its source to our ears, through the air or through other things. *How we know:* You can hear a tumbler being tapped without touching it or seeing it. And you can hear a neighbour's mixie through a wall.
- A sound gets softer the further it travels from its source. *How we know:* In the Walk Away test, the same finger rub was clear close by and could not be heard a few steps back.
- A sound spreads out to every side from its source. *How we know:* In All Around the Tumbler, the sound reached the child from in front, from behind, from the sides and round the door.
- Sound takes time to travel, so far-away sounds arrive late. *How we know:* Thunder comes after the lightning. In the 1630s, Marin Mersenne and Pierre Gassendi in France timed the bang of guns fired far away, after seeing the flash.

## How we found out
**Marin Mersenne (1588 to 1648) and Pierre Gassendi (1592 to 1655), French scientists, The 1630s.** For a very long time, people knew that thunder comes after lightning. But many thought a sound reaches us the moment it is made. The Greek thinker Aristotle also taught that high, sharp sounds travel faster than deep ones. In the 1630s, two French scientists, Marin Mersenne and Pierre Gassendi, tested these ideas. They stood far away from someone firing a gun. They saw the flash and smoke at once. The bang came later. They timed the wait. The further away they stood, the longer the wait. So a sound takes time to travel. Gassendi also compared a big gun, with a deep boom, and a small gun, with a sharp crack. From far away, both bangs arrived after the same wait. So high and deep sounds travel at the same speed. Aristotle was wrong about that. You can test the same idea in any monsoon storm: count from the flash to the thunder.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle taught that high sounds travel faster than deep sounds. Gassendi timed a deep boom and a sharp crack from far away, and both arrived after the same wait.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Sound only goes forward, the way you are facing.** (A common idea among young children; *rejected*)  
  For: When we talk, we face the person we are talking to.  
  Against: A sound spreads out to every side. In All Around the Tumbler, the child heard the tap from behind and round a door. A cone can send more of it one way, but without one it goes everywhere.
- **A sound arrives the moment it is made.** (A common idea, because nearby sounds seem to arrive at once; *rejected*)  
  For: When someone claps next to you, you hear it straight away.  
  Against: Sound takes time to travel. Close by, the wait is too short to notice. From far away, you can notice it: thunder comes after the lightning.
- **High sounds travel faster than deep sounds.** (The Greek thinker Aristotle, more than 2,300 years ago, and many people after him; *rejected*)  
  For: High, sharp sounds can seem to reach us quickly and clearly.  
  Against: In the 1630s, Pierre Gassendi timed the deep boom of a big gun and the sharp crack of a small gun from far away. Both arrived after the same wait.
- **A far-away sound is soft because the sound gets tired on the way.** (A common idea among children; *rejected*)  
  For: Far-away sounds are softer, and people get tired when they travel far.  
  Against: The sound spreads out to every side as it travels. So only a little of it reaches each ear far away. A cone stops some of the spreading, and the far sound becomes louder.

## Roots
We have walked away from sounds, listened from behind a door and called through a paper cone. Now we ask what a travelling sound tells us about the world, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In All Around the Tumbler, one tap reached you in front, behind and round the door. The sound spread out to fill the whole room at once. Far away, it had spread over more space, so less of it reached you. So one sound is shared out among every place around it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a sound going from one thing to one ear, like a ball thrown to a catcher. But a sound spreads out to every side, and every ear around it catches a share of the same sound.  
*Evidence:* In All Around the Tumbler, the same tap reached every spot in the room and round the door. In the Walk Away test, the same finger rub was clear close by and gone a few steps back, where its share had become too small to hear.

- *who am i:* You are a listener in one spot. Every sound you hear has travelled to that spot, from somewhere else, to reach you.
- *what is life:* Many living beings send sounds across distances to stay together: a calf calling her mother, crows calling across the city, people calling each other home.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of sounds spreading out from many places at once, crossing each other in the air, reaching everyone a little differently.

One bell rings in one place. Its sound spreads out on every side and reaches everyone around it: a child near the bell, a grandmother across the pond, a crow on the roof. One sound becomes a different sound in each ear, louder or softer. The air that carries it is aware in its own way, and so are the bell and every listener. Many scientists disagree: they say only beings with nerves are aware.

- **The Nyaya philosophers of India, as summed up by Vishvanatha in the Bhasha-pariccheda (17th century), in the verses on sound:** A sound makes the next sound next to it, and that one makes the next, until a sound reaches the ear. It spreads like ripples on a pond, one ring after another. Some teachers said it spreads like a kadamba flower, whose buds open on every side at once. (In All Around the Tumbler, the tap reached you in front, behind and at the sides. In Be the Air, the tap was passed from person to person. These old thinkers described the same spreading and passing on, by thinking about ripples and flowers.)
- **The Udana 6.4, a Buddhist text, which tells the story of the blind men and the elephant (Jain teachers tell this story too):** Several blind men each touched one part of an elephant. One felt the trunk, one the ear, one the leg. Each one described a different elephant, and each was right about the part he touched. (In Who Hears It Louder?, Meena by the bell heard a loud sound, and her friend at the end of the street heard a soft one. It was one bell. Each of them told the truth about what reached their own ears.)
- Science (settled): A sound spreads out to every side from its source. The further it travels, the more it has spread out, so it sounds softer.
- Science (settled): Sound takes time to travel through air: about three seconds to go one kilometre.
- Other view, Many scientists today: They say air, bells and tumblers are not aware of anything. To them, sound is only air pushing on air. They think awareness belongs to living beings with nerves or brains.
- Practice, The furthest sound (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. First, listen to the closest sound you can hear. Maybe your own breathing. Now listen a little further, to a sound in this room. Now further, to a sound outside the room. Now as far as you can. What is the furthest sound you can hear? Every one of those sounds has travelled all the way to you.
- Ask: When the finger rub was too far away for you, could someone closer still hear it? Was the sound loud or soft, then?
- Ask: One bell, many listeners, each hearing it a little differently. Which one hears the real sound?
- Ask: Where does a sound go after it has passed you?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* When the finger rub was too far away to hear, was there still a sound in the room? (hint: The fingers were still rubbing. Could someone closer still hear it? Could an ant or a cat?)
- *deep:* How does a sound find its way round the corner of a door? (hint: The sound spreads out to every side. Some of it goes through the doorway and spreads out again on the other side.)
- *deep:* Why can you hear a cooker whistle in every room, but not a whisper? (hint: Think about how big each sound is when it starts, and how much it spreads out on the way.)
- *big:* When one bell rings, everyone around it hears it at once. Is it one sound, or many sounds, one for each person? (hint: There is one bell. But the sound reaches each ear, and each person hears it a little louder or softer.)
- *big:* If you could follow one sound as it travels, where would it go after it passes you? (hint: It keeps spreading: to the walls, out of the window, into the street, getting softer and softer.)

## Story: Ammamma's Paper Cone
*Ammamma means grandmother, mother's mother, in Malayalam. Chechi means big sister. Nila, Kannan, Zoya, Amma and Ammamma are made up.*

Nila was six. She lived on the fifth floor of a tall building in Kochi, with Amma, her little brother Kannan, and her grandmother, Ammamma.

Across the road stood another tall building. On the balcony right opposite lived Nila's best friend, Zoya.

Every evening, Nila and Zoya waved to each other. Then they tried to talk.

"ZOYA!" Nila shouted. "I GOT A NEW BOOK!"

Zoya cupped a hand behind her ear. She shook her head. Down on the road, the autos honked and the buses roared. Nila's words got lost somewhere in the middle.

"She can't hear me," said Nila. "The road is too wide."

Ammamma was folding clothes behind her. "The road is not too wide," she said. "Your voice is going everywhere."

"Everywhere?" said Kannan, who was four. "Where is everywhere?"

"When you shout, your voice goes out on every side," said Ammamma. "Some goes to Zoya. Some goes up to the sky. Some goes down to the autos. Some goes back into our flat. So only a little bit gets to Zoya."

Ammamma picked up an old newspaper. She rolled it into a long cone, wide at one end and narrow at the other. She stuck it with a strip of tape.

"When I was a girl, our house stood at the edge of the paddy fields," she said. "My mother used a cone like this to call us home for lunch. Every child in the fields heard her."

Nila put the narrow end to her mouth. She pointed the wide end at Zoya's balcony.

"Zoya!" she called. She did not shout this time. She only used her ordinary calling voice. "I got a new book!"

Across the road, Zoya jumped. She gave a big thumbs up. Then she ran inside.

A minute later, Zoya came back with her own rolled-up newspaper. "What book?" she called through it.

Nila heard her clearly, over the honking. She laughed. "A book about the sea!"

Kannan pulled Ammamma's sari. "Why does the cone work?"

"Without the cone, your voice spreads out on every side," said Ammamma. "The cone keeps it from going to the sides. So more of it goes straight to Zoya."

Kannan took the cone. He turned round and pointed it at Amma in the kitchen. "Amma, I'm hungry!" he called.

"I can hear you, Kannan," said Amma, laughing. "Dinner is coming."

That night it rained, the way it rains in Kochi in June. The whole sky went white for a moment.

"Lightning!" said Nila.

"Now count with me, slowly," said Ammamma. "One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six."

BOOM. The thunder rolled over the buildings.

"Why did the thunder come so late?" asked Nila. "Did it get lost?"

"The lightning and the thunder were made at the same moment, far away over the sea," said Ammamma. "The light reached us almost at once. The sound had to travel all the way here. Sound takes a little time to travel."

There was another flash. They counted together. One. Two. Three. BOOM.

"Only three this time," said Nila. "So the storm is coming closer."

"Yes," said Ammamma. "A short count means a close storm."

Kannan pulled his blanket up to his nose. "Will it come all the way here?"

They counted the next flash. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven. BOOM, much softer.

"Seven!" said Nila. "It's going away again."

"And the thunder is softer," said Kannan. "Because it spread out on the way?"

Ammamma smiled. "You have both learned something today. A sound starts in one place. Then it travels, and it spreads out on every side as it goes."

Nila looked out at the dark balcony across the road. Zoya's light was still on. Nila picked up the paper cone and whispered into it. "Good night, Zoya."

It was only a whisper. But across the road, the light blinked off and on again.

**For the parent:** A sound starts at its source and spreads out on every side as it travels. A cone sends more of it one way, so a friend far away hears it better. Sound also takes time to travel, so thunder comes after the lightning, and a longer wait means the storm is further away.

- Ask: Why couldn't Zoya hear Nila's shout across the road?
- Ask: How did Ammamma's paper cone help Zoya hear Nila?
- Ask: Why did the thunder come after the lightning?
- Ask: When the count got longer, what did Nila know about the storm?

## Games
### g1 · Whisper Walk (10 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the cards and put them face down.; The whisperer stands at one end of the room or the hallway.; The walkers stand at the far end, next to each other.; The whisperer picks a card and whispers the word.; If no walker can hear it, every walker takes one step closer, and the whisperer whispers the same word again.; The first walker to say the word right keeps the card.; Everyone goes back to the far end for the next card. After four cards, swap who whispers.
- **Printable cards: 12 Whisper Walk word cards:** Elephant; Peacock; Coconut; Banana; Dosa; Mango; Rain; Kite; Parrot; Butterfly; Auto; Boat
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the cards. Read the rules together. You whisper first.  
  *Parent says:* I will whisper a word from far away. If you can't hear it, take one step closer. Let's see how close you need to come.  
  *Child mode:* Listen for the whisper. Can't hear it? Take one step closer.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play four cards. Whisper at the same softness each time. After each card, count how many steps the walkers took.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a word. Can you hear it? No? Take a step. Now?  
  *Child mode:* Can you hear the word? Say it! Or take a step.
  - Ask: Why did you have to come closer? (good: The whisper was too soft from far away.; Close up, more of the whisper reaches me.)
  - Note: Keep the whisper equally soft each time, so the game is fair.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap. Your child whispers, and you are the walker.  
  *Parent says:* Now you whisper, and I walk. Do you think I will need more steps or fewer than you?  
  *Child mode:* Now you whisper. Make it soft!
  - Check: The walkers could not hear the whisper. What made it easier to hear? → **Coming closer**
### g2 · Who Hears It Louder? A card game (10 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the cards and put them face down.; A grown-up reads a card out loud.; The players answer. Then they act it out if they can: ring, clap, whisper or whistle.; The grown-up turns the card and reads the answer.; A good answer with a reason keeps the card. The most cards at the end wins.
- **Printable cards: 10 Who Hears It Louder cards:** A temple bell rings. Meena stands next to the bell. Her friend is at the end of the street. Who hears it louder? (back: Meena. She is close, so more of the sound reaches her.); The cooker whistles in the kitchen. Arjun is in the bedroom. Can he hear it? (back: Yes. The whistle travels through the air, out of the kitchen door, to the bedroom.); Paati claps behind Kavya's back. Can Kavya hear the clap? (back: Yes. A sound spreads out to every side, so it reaches you from behind too.); A storm is far away. You see a flash of lightning. What comes next? (back: The thunder, a little later. The sound takes time to travel to you.); Lakshmi whispers to Farhan from across a big room. He can't hear. What can he do? (back: Come closer. A whisper fades as it travels, so it is easier to hear close by.); Riya calls through a paper cone. Should the wide end point at her friend, or at the floor? (back: At her friend. The cone sends more of the sound out of the wide end.); A koel calls from a tree far away, and another koel calls from the window. Which call sounds louder? (back: The koel at the window. Her call has travelled less far.); The neighbour's mixie is running behind the wall. Can you hear it? (back: Yes. Sound can travel through walls, as well as through air.); Two drums are hit equally hard. One is next to you. One is down the road. Which sounds louder? (back: The drum next to you. The sound of the far drum has spread out more on the way.); Ammu stands round the corner of a door. Amma rings a bell in the room. Can Ammu hear it? (back: Yes, a little softer. The sound spreads out through the doorway and round the corner.)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the cards. Read the rules together.  
  *Parent says:* I will read a card. You tell me who hears the sound, or what happens. Then we try it.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the card. Who hears it? Then try it.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the cards one by one. After each answer, ask why. Act out the ones you can at home.  
  *Parent says:* Who hears it louder? Why do you think so? Let's try it.  
  *Child mode:* Who hears it louder? Why? Let's try!
  - Ask: Why does the person far away hear it more softly? (good: The sound spreads out on the way.; Less of it reaches them.)
- **s3 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the cards each player kept.  
  *Parent says:* Which card was the trickiest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your cards. Which was the hardest?
  - Check: Paati claps behind your back. Can you hear it? → **Yes, because sound spreads out to every side**

## Experiments
### e1 · Walk Away: how far can you hear it? (25 min)
- **question:** Does every sound travel the same distance before you stop hearing it? And does a hard tap travel further than a gentle one?
- **predict:** The grown-up will rub fingers, crinkle a plastic bag and tap a spoon on a steel tumbler. Which sound will you hear from furthest away? Which will fade first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The grown-up stays on the same spot and holds each sound at the same height, next to their shoulder. They make each sound the same way each time. You take the same small steps, one heel touching the other toe, and you close your eyes before each sound.
- **record:** Draw the hallway as a long box. Draw the grown-up at one end. Draw a chappal for each sound where it faded, with a small picture of the sound next to it: fingers, a bag, a tumbler.
- **results table:** Sound | My guess: near, middle or far? | Steps until I could not hear it
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off the fan, the TV and any music. 2. Find the longest straight line in your home. 3. Stand at one end with the bag, the tumbler and the spoon.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to find out how far a sound can travel in our home. First, let's make the house quiet.  
  *Child mode:* Help make the house quiet. Switch off the TV and the fan.
  - Note: A hallway, or two rooms joined by an open door, works well. Other noises make sounds fade sooner, so a quiet time of day helps.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make each sound once while your child stands next to you. Rub your thumb and finger together beside your ear. Crinkle the bag. Tap the tumbler gently with the spoon.  
  *Parent says:* Here are our three sounds. Listen to each one. Which will you hear from furthest away? Which will fade first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the three sounds. Which will you hear from far away? Why?
  - Ask: Which sound do you think will go furthest? Why? (good: The tumbler, because it is loudest.; The bag, because it is crinkly.; The fingers, because I can hear them well now.)
  - Note: Write the guesses in the table. Any guess with a reason is good. Don't say what will happen.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child closes their eyes. 2. Rub your fingers beside your ear, three times. 3. If they say yes, they take one heel-to-toe step back. 4. Repeat until they say no twice. 5. Put a chappal on that spot.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes. Say yes if you hear it. Then take one step back. Say no if you can't hear it.  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Can you hear it? Say yes, then step back.
  - Ask: How many steps did you take before the finger rub faded? (good: Only a few steps.; Four steps, then I could not hear it.)
  - Note: Keep your fingers at the same height each time, and rub the same way. The finger rub usually fades after a few small steps. Sometimes say 'now', and make no sound. This checks that your child is listening, not guessing.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child comes back to your side. 2. Do the same with the crinkling bag. 3. Then with the spoon tapped gently on the tumbler. 4. Put a chappal where each sound faded.  
  *Parent says:* Now the bag. Same rules. Close your eyes, say yes, and step back. Now the tumbler.  
  *Child mode:* Now the bag. Then the tumbler. Step back each time you hear it.
  - Ask: Which sound did you hear from furthest away? (good: The tumbler.; The spoon on the steel.)
  - Note: If your child reaches the wall and can still hear the sound, put the chappal at the wall. That sound travels further than your home is long. The tumbler often goes the whole way.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand your child at the chappal for the bag. 2. Crinkle the bag gently, then hard. 3. Ask which one they heard.  
  *Parent says:* You are standing where the bag faded. I will crinkle it gently. Now hard. Which one could you hear?  
  *Child mode:* Listen. A gentle crinkle. Now a hard one. Which can you hear?
  - Ask: Which crinkle reached you? (good: The hard one.; I heard the hard one, not the gentle one.)
  - Check: Which sound can travel further before it fades? → **A hard crinkle**
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the three chappals together. 2. Count the steps to each one. 3. Ask your child to tell you three things, and write them in their words.  
  *Parent says:* Look at our chappals. Which sound went furthest? Tell me what you think now, what you heard, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the chappals. Which sound went furthest? Why?
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this. 'The fingers faded first and the tumbler went furthest. Far away, the sounds got softer. A big sound can travel further before it fades.' Later you can add: the sound spreads out to every side, so less of it reaches you far away.
### e2 · The Paper Cone: can we send a sound one way? (15 min)
- **question:** Does a paper cone make a voice easier to hear from far away? Does it matter which way the cone points?
- **predict:** The grown-up will say the same words three ways: with no cone, with the cone pointing at you, and with the cone pointing at the wall. Which way will be clearest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The grown-up stays on the same spot and uses the same quiet voice each time. You stay at the same far spot. The words are different each time, so you cannot guess them.
- **record:** Draw the grown-up speaking three ways: no cone, cone pointing at you, cone pointing at the wall. Draw a smiling ear next to the clearest one.
- **results table:** How the grown-up spoke | My guess: clear, a little, or not at all? | What I heard: clear, a little, or not at all?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay two sheets of newspaper on top of each other. Roll them into a cone, wide at one end and narrow at the other. Tape it.  
  *Parent says:* We are making a megaphone. A megaphone is a cone you speak into. Let's see what it does.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the newspaper into a cone. Make one end wide. Tape it.
  - Note: Make the narrow end about as wide as your mouth, and the wide end about as wide as a plate. A cone about as long as your arm works well.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands far away, at the spot where a quiet voice is only just clear. 2. Ask them to guess which way will be clearest, and why.  
  *Parent says:* I will say some words three ways: no cone, cone at you, and cone at the wall. Which will be clearest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Which way will you hear best? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Which way do you think will be clearest? (good: The cone pointing at me.; The cone, because it is like a loudspeaker.)
  - Note: Find the spot by talking quietly and asking your child to step back until your words are only just clear. A whisper works too.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say a short sentence quietly with no cone. 2. Say a new sentence the same way through the cone, pointing at your child. 3. Say a third sentence with the cone pointing at the side wall. 4. Each time, your child says what they heard.  
  *Parent says:* Listen. Tell me the words you heard. Was it clear, a little, or not at all?  
  *Child mode:* Listen. Say the words you heard. Was it clear?
  - Ask: When was the voice clearest? (good: When the cone pointed at me.; With the cone, facing me.)
  - Note: Keep your voice at exactly the same loudness. Use new words each time, like 'Idli for breakfast', 'The crow is on the wall', 'Ten red bangles'. Usually the cone pointing at your child is clearest, and the cone pointing at the wall is the hardest to hear.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to tell you three things. Write them down in their words.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you hear? Why do you think the cone helped?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think. Say what you heard. Say why.
  - Note: A strong answer sounds like this. 'With the cone pointing at me, the words were clearest. Pointing at the wall, they were hard to hear. The cone sends the voice one way, instead of to every side.'
  - Check: Your friend is far away, in front of you. Which way should the wide end of the cone point? → **At your friend**

### a1 · All Around the Tumbler (8 min)

  *Parent says:* Now stand behind me. Can you hear it? Now at the side. Now go round the door, where you can't see me. Can you still hear it?  
  *Child mode:* Stand behind. Now at the side. Now round the door. Can you hear it?
  - Ask: Where could you hear the tap? (good: Everywhere.; Even behind and round the door.)
  - Note: Your child will hear the tap in every spot, even round the door. It may sound a little softer there. Don't explain yet. Let them notice.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what they found. Then ask them to draw it.  
  *Parent says:* The tumbler is in one place. But you heard it in front, behind, at the sides and round the door. What does that tell you about the sound?  
  *Child mode:* You heard it everywhere. Which way did the sound go?
  - Ask: Which way does a sound go from the tumbler? (good: Every way.; It spreads out to all sides.)

### a2 · Count After the Lightning (10 min)
- **safety:** Watch from inside, through a window or from a covered balcony. Stay away from open terraces, water and tall trees in a storm.
- **record:** Draw a line of lightning flashes. Under each one, write or draw the count you got: for example, five dots for a count of five.
- **s1 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* On a stormy evening, sit with your child by a closed window or on a covered balcony.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the sky. When you see a flash, we will count slowly together until the thunder comes.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the sky with a grown-up. Wait for a flash.
  - Note: This activity needs a storm, so do it when one comes. During the monsoon that is often. If there is no storm this week, keep it for later.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After each flash, count slowly together: one, two, three. Stop when the thunder comes. Write the number. Do this for three or four flashes.  
  *Parent says:* Flash! One. Two. Three. Four. There's the thunder. What number did we get?  
  *Child mode:* Flash! Count slowly. Stop at the thunder. What number?
  - Ask: Why does the thunder come after the flash? (good: The sound takes time to come.; The thunder has to travel to us.)
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the numbers together.  
  *Parent says:* Our numbers got bigger. Is the storm coming closer, or going away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Are the numbers getting bigger or smaller? What does that mean?
  - Ask: What does a bigger count mean? (good: The storm is further away.; The sound had further to travel.)
  - Note: Lightning and thunder are made at the same moment. The light arrives almost at once. The sound takes about three seconds to travel one kilometre. So a longer count means a storm further away.
### a3 · Be the Air: pass the sound along (8 min)
- **record:** Draw your family in a line, with a drum at one end and a big ear at the other. Draw arrows from each person to the next.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a big ear on a paper and tape it to a wall. Everyone else stands in a line between the steel plate and the ear, facing the ear.  
  *Parent says:* The plate is the sound source. The paper is an ear. We are the air in between.  
  *Child mode:* The plate makes the sound. The paper is an ear. We are the air.
  - Note: With only two people, the child can be the air and you can walk the tap along to the ear yourself.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The first person taps the plate once, then gently taps the shoulder of the next person. Each person passes the tap on. The last person touches the ear and says 'I hear it!'  
  *Parent says:* Tap the plate, then tap the next shoulder. Pass it on. When it reaches the ear, say 'I hear it!'  
  *Child mode:* Feel the tap on your shoulder. Pass it on gently.
  - Ask: Did anyone walk to the ear? (good: No.; We stayed in our places. Only the tap moved.)
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about what happened.  
  *Parent says:* Nobody walked to the ear. But the tap got there. How is that like a sound going through the air?  
  *Child mode:* Nobody walked. But the tap reached the ear. How?
  - Ask: How does a sound get from the plate to your ear? (good: The air passes it along.; The air next to the plate pushes the next air, all the way to my ear.)
  - Note: This is a model, a way of showing what we cannot see. The air does not rush from the plate to your ear. Each bit of air pushes the next bit, and the push travels.

### Sound Travels (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Kavya taps a steel tumbler in the kitchen. Her brother hears it in the hall. How did the sound get to him?  
   Options: It travelled through the air / He saw the tumbler / The tumbler was in his hand  
   **Answer:** It travelled through the air. *Why:* A sound travels from its source, through the air, to our ears. He did not need to see or touch the tumbler.
2. (draw) Draw a bell in the middle of the page. Draw sound lines going out from the bell. Which ways do they go?  
   **Answer:** Sound lines going out on every side of the bell: up, down, left and right.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the sound spreading out to every side, not only one way.
3. (mcq) In the Walk Away test, which sound faded first, after only a few steps?  
   Options: The rubbing fingers / The spoon on the tumbler / Both faded at the same spot  
   **Answer:** The rubbing fingers. *Why:* The finger rub is a very small sound to start with. It spreads out and fades after only a few steps. The tumbler makes a bigger sound, so it travels further.
4. (truefalse) Paati claps her hands behind you. You cannot hear the clap, because you are not facing her.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A sound spreads out to every side. You can hear a clap from behind you, as you heard the tumbler from behind in All Around the Tumbler.
5. (sort) A grown-up reads each sound. Is it near or far? Draw a line to the right box.  
   **Answer:** A friend whispering into my ear → Near me; Thunder from a storm over the sea → Far away; A clap right next to me → Near me; A train horn heard at night from far off → Far away; The fan above my head → Near me; A koel calling from a tree down the road → Far away. *Why:* Close sounds have travelled only a short way. Far sounds have travelled a long way and spread out, so they often sound soft.
6. (label) Look at the picture of a flat. The cooker is whistling in the kitchen. A grown-up points to each room. Say 'loud' or 'soft', and the grown-up writes it.  
   **Answer:** ['loud', 'loud', 'soft']. *Why:* Close to the cooker, the whistle is loud. In the far bedroom it has spread out more, and the shut door also makes it softer.
7. (write) Say it out loud, and a grown-up writes it down. In the Walk Away test, why did you stop hearing the finger rub?  
   **Answer:** I was too far away. The sound spread out, so not enough of it reached my ears.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the sound was still being made, but it was too soft to hear from far away, because it spread out on the way.
8. (match) Draw a line from each problem to the best fix.  
   **Answer:** I can't hear a whisper from far away → Come closer; My friend across the road can't hear me → Use a paper cone; Paati can't hear the doorbell in the far room → Open the doors on the way. *Why:* Coming closer means the sound travels less far. A cone sends more of your voice one way. With the doors open, the sound has an open path and does not have to go round them.
9. (odd) Three of these people will hear the temple bell. One is too far away to hear it. Circle the one who is too far.  
   Options: Meena, standing by the bell / Her friend at the temple gate / Her aunt across the temple pond / Her cousin in another town  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The bell's sound spreads out and gets softer as it travels. In another town, it is far too soft to hear. The others are close enough.
10. (perspective) Kavya wants her grandmother on the balcony to hear her. Three children have ideas.  
   **Answer:** Any choice with a good reason. A shout is loud but can hurt your throat and disturb others. A cone sends more of the sound to the balcony without shouting. Walking over is clearest, because the sound has only a short way to travel, but it takes longer.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason that uses how sound travels, and says fairly what is good about another idea.
### Sound Travels (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)

   **Answer:** ['Guess which sound will go furthest', 'Close your eyes and step back each time you hear it', 'Put a chappal where the sound faded']. *Why:* First we guess, then we test by stepping back and listening, then we mark where each sound faded.
2. (mcq) Farhan says the words through a paper cone. Where should his friend stand to hear him best?  
   Options: In front of the wide end / Behind Farhan / Next to the narrow end  
   **Answer:** In front of the wide end. *Why:* The cone sends more of the sound out of the wide end. So the friend in front of the wide end hears best. Behind Farhan, the voice is softer.
3. (mcq) Nila sees a flash of lightning. She counts to 7 before the thunder. Then she sees another flash and counts to 3. What happened to the storm?  
   Options: It came closer / It went further away / It stayed in the same place

4. (truefalse) Lightning and thunder are made at the same moment.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. They are made together. We see the flash first because light travels much faster than sound. The thunder's sound takes longer to reach us.

   **Answer:** softer. *Why:* As a sound travels, it spreads out. So less of it reaches each ear, and it sounds softer.

   **Answer:** A child speaking into the narrow end of a cone, with most sound lines going out of the wide end towards the friend.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows most of the sound lines going one way, out of the wide end, towards the friend.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A bell rings in the next room. How does the sound reach your ears?  
   Options: It travels through the air / Your ears go to the bell / It does not reach you  
   **Answer:** It travels through the air. *Why:* The sound starts at the bell and travels through the air to your ears. Your ears stay where they are and catch it.
2. (mcq) What is the sound source when a pressure cooker whistles?  
   Options: The pressure cooker / Your ears / The kitchen door  
   **Answer:** The pressure cooker. *Why:* The sound source is the thing that makes the sound. The whistle starts at the cooker, then travels to you.
3. (mcq) You can hear the neighbour's mixie through the wall. What does that show?  
   Options: Sound can travel through a wall / The wall is making the sound / The mixie is in your flat  
   **Answer:** Sound can travel through a wall. *Why:* The mixie is the source, in the neighbour's flat. Its sound travels through the wall to you. Sound can travel through things, as well as through air.
4. (mcq) You walk away from someone rubbing their fingers. What happens to the sound?  
   Options: It gets softer, then you can't hear it / It gets louder / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It gets softer, then you can't hear it. *Why:* The further the sound travels, the more it spreads out, so it gets softer. A few steps away, the finger rub is too soft to hear.
5. (mcq) Which sound can you hear from furthest away?  
   Options: A spoon tapped hard on a steel plate / Fingers rubbing together / A soft whisper  
   **Answer:** A spoon tapped hard on a steel plate. *Why:* A hard tap on steel makes a big sound to start with. It can spread out a long way and still be loud enough to hear.
6. (mcq) Why is a far-away drum softer than a drum next to you?  
   Options: Its sound has spread out more on the way / The far drum is broken / Far drums are always smaller  
   **Answer:** Its sound has spread out more on the way. *Why:* A sound spreads out to every side as it travels. By the time it has come a long way, only a little of it reaches your ears.
7. (mcq) A grown-up taps a tumbler in the middle of the room. Where can you hear it?  
   Options: In front, behind, at the sides and round the door / Only in front / Only if you can see it  
   **Answer:** In front, behind, at the sides and round the door. *Why:* A sound spreads out to every side from its source. In All Around the Tumbler, the tap reached every spot.
8. (mcq) Why can you hear a sound from round a corner?  
   Options: The sound spreads out, through the doorway and round the corner / Sounds can see round corners / You can't hear round a corner  
   **Answer:** The sound spreads out, through the doorway and round the corner. *Why:* A sound spreads out to every side. Some goes through the doorway and spreads out again on the other side, round the corner.
9. (mcq) What does a paper cone do to your voice?  
   Options: It sends more of it one way / It makes it disappear / It sends it behind you  
   **Answer:** It sends more of it one way. *Why:* The cone stops your voice spreading out to the sides. More of it goes out of the wide end, so people in front hear you better.
10. (mcq) You see lightning, then hear thunder. Why does the thunder come later?  
   Options: The sound takes time to travel to you / Thunder is made after the lightning / Your ears are slow  
   **Answer:** The sound takes time to travel to you. *Why:* Lightning and thunder are made at the same moment. The light reaches you almost at once. The sound takes longer to travel.
11. (mcq) After a flash, you count to 2 before the thunder. Is the storm near or far?  
   Options: Quite near / Very far away / You can't tell  
   **Answer:** Quite near. *Why:* A short count means the sound did not have far to travel. So the storm is quite near. A long count means it is far.

## Applications
- Ambulances and fire engines use loud sirens, so that the sound travels far ahead and people move out of the way.
- Festival loudspeakers are often shaped like cones, so they send the sound one way, towards the crowd.
- Lifeguards and police use megaphones to call people far away.
- Weather watchers count from lightning to thunder to tell whether a storm is coming closer.
- Schools ring one loud bell in the middle of the building, so the sound spreads to every classroom.

## Spotted around us
- In Chennai, vegetable and fish sellers walk down the street calling out what they sell. People hear them from the fourth and fifth floors of a building.
- During the Kerala monsoon in June, families see the lightning over the sea first and hear the thunder a few moments later.
- At Ganesh Chaturthi in Mumbai and Pune, cone-shaped loudspeakers on poles send music and announcements along the street.
- During Durga Puja in Kolkata, people blow the conch shell, and the sound carries across the whole neighbourhood.
- At night, in towns and villages all over India, people hear a train horn from a station far away.

## Open questions
- **Why does a sound sometimes carry much further on a cool, still night than on a hot afternoon?** Scientists know that wind and warm and cool layers of air bend a sound up or down as it travels. Working out exactly how far a sound will carry on a given day is still hard, and people are still studying it.
- **How do our ears and brain tell which way a sound came from?** Scientists know that having two ears helps, because a sound reaches one ear a tiny moment before the other. Some parts of how the brain works this out, especially for sounds from above or behind, are still being studied.
- **Why do some places carry sound much better than others?** The shape of a building, the walls and even the air temperature change how a sound travels. Builders and scientists still test old forts, temples and halls to understand why some carry sound so well.

## Connected maths
- In the Walk Away test, Lakshmi heard the finger rub for 3 steps, the crinkly bag for 6 steps, and the tumbler for 10 steps. Put the sounds in order, from the one that travelled least far to the one that travelled furthest. **The finger rub (3 steps), then the bag (6 steps), then the tumbler (10 steps).**. 3 is less than 6, and 6 is less than 10. So the finger rub went least far and the tumbler went furthest. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- Nila counted to 6 after the first lightning flash and to 3 after the second. Was the storm closer the first time or the second time? How many fewer did she count the second time? **Closer the second time. She counted 3 fewer.**. A shorter count means the thunder had less far to travel, so the storm was closer. Count down from 6 to 3: 5, 4, 3. That is 3 fewer. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L1`)
- Zoya can hear the whisper from 2 steps away. Her brother is 5 steps away. How many steps must he walk to hear it? **3 steps.**. He is at 5. He needs to get to 2. Count down from 5: 4, 3, 2. That is 3 steps. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L1`)
- Look at your cooker map. Count the rooms you coloured red, yellow and blue. Which colour has the most rooms? **It depends on your home. For example: red 2 rooms, yellow 2 rooms, blue 1 room. Red and yellow are equal, and blue has the fewest.**. Count each colour and write the number. Then compare the numbers. The biggest number has the most rooms. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L1`)

- Inverse-square spreading: sound intensity from a small source in open air falls with the square of distance (about 6 decibels per doubling of distance). Indoors, reflections from walls make the fall-off slower.
- Speed of sound in air at about 20 degrees Celsius: about 343 metres per second, so about 3 seconds per kilometre.
- Marin Mersenne, Harmonie universelle (1636), gave a speed of sound from gun-flash timing. Pierre Gassendi's timings (1630s) found loud and soft, high and low gun sounds travelled at the same speed, against Aristotle's view that high notes travel faster.
- Horn and cone megaphones increase forward sound by directing it and matching the voice to the air better; the gain is modest for a paper cone but audible.
- Nyaya-Vaisheshika on sound propagation: vichi-taranga-nyaya (the maxim of waves and ripples) and kadamba-mukula-nyaya (the maxim of kadamba buds), Vishvanatha's Bhasha-pariccheda (Karikavali). Verse numbers not checked.
- Udana 6.4 (Tittha Sutta), the parable of the blind men and the elephant.
