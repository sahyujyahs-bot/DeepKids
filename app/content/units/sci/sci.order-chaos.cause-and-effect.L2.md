# Cause and effect: how one push knocks down a whole line
`sci.order-chaos.cause-and-effect.L2`

**Summary.** Push the first domino in a line, and the last one falls too, even though you never touched it. Each domino falls because the one before it hits it. What makes something happen is called the cause. What happens is called the effect. In a line of dominoes, every domino is the effect of the one before and the cause of the next. If one gap is too wide, the chain of causes stops there, and the rest of the line stays standing.

**Intro.** *Stand five books, matchboxes or dominoes on their ends in a row, a little apart. Push only the first one. What happens to the last one? You did not touch it. So what made it fall?* Everything that happens is made to happen by something. A push makes a domino fall. Rain makes a puddle. Heat makes ice melt. In this unit you test how far apart dominoes can be and still knock each other down. You send a push through a line of coins and watch only the last coin move. You also find out how people learned that flies come from eggs, and not from rotting meat.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what a cause and an effect are, and give examples from my home.
1. By the end I can show that a push passes along a line of dominoes, from each one to the next.
2. By the end I can say why a chain stops when one gap is too wide or one domino is missing.
3. By the end I can show that a push passes through a line of coins and moves the last coin.
4. By the end I can say that some effects come much later than their cause.
5. By the end I can say why something that happens first is not always the cause of what comes next.

## Terms
- **Cause**: A cause is what makes something happen. *e.g.* Your push is the cause of the first domino falling. Heat from the stove is the cause of the milk boiling.
- **Effect**: An effect is what happens because of a cause. *e.g.* The domino falling is the effect of your push. A puddle is an effect of rain.
- **Chain of causes**: A chain of causes is when each effect becomes the cause of the next thing, one after another. *e.g.* Dark clouds bring rain. The rain fills the lane with water. The water makes the school van drive slowly. Each one causes the next.
- **Energy**: Energy is what lets something move or change. A moving thing can pass some of its energy on to what it hits. *e.g.* A falling domino has energy. When it hits the next domino, it passes some energy on, and that one falls too.
- **Gap**: A gap is the empty space between two things. *e.g.* In the domino test, the gap is the space between one domino and the next.
- **Fair test**: A fair test changes only one thing at a time, so you know what made the difference. *e.g.* In the domino test, we change only the gap. We use the same dominoes, the same floor and the same gentle push each time.

## Explanations
### Cause and effect: every happening is made to happen · *defined*
**Definition:** A cause is what makes something happen. An effect is what happens because of it. The cause always comes first, and the effect comes after.

When you switch on the fan, the curtain starts to move. Switching on the fan is the cause. The moving curtain is the effect. When you drop a steel spoon on a steel plate, the clang is the effect. Every action you do has an effect, even a small one. When you clap, the air shakes and someone in the next room may hear it. Scientists look for causes because, once they know the cause, they can make something happen again, or stop it.
- e.g. Switching on the fan makes the curtain move.
- e.g. Stirring sugar into water makes the sugar disappear and the water sweet.
- e.g. Heat from the gas makes the water in a pot boil.
- e.g. Rain makes puddles on the road.
### A chain of causes: why the last domino falls · *defined*
**Definition:** In a chain of causes, each effect becomes the cause of the next thing. A push can travel a long way like this, but only if every link in the chain passes it on.

In the domino test, your finger pushed only the first domino. As it fell, it hit the second one and passed on some of its energy. The second one fell and hit the third, and so on to the end. When the gap was small, every domino could reach the next one, so the whole line fell. When the gap was as wide as a domino is tall, the first domino lay down without touching the next one. The chain stopped there. Taking one domino out of the middle did the same. The push reached the hole and stopped, so everything after it stayed standing.
- e.g. A line of dominoes falling one after another
- e.g. A line of coins passing a push to the last coin
- e.g. Rain, then a full lane, then a slow school van
- e.g. A message passed from person to person in a game
### Some effects come much later · *defined*
**Definition:** Some effects happen straight after their cause. Others take hours, days or years to show.

A domino falls at once when it is hit. But many causes take time. When dry chana is soaked in water at night, the seeds slowly drink the water. By morning they are much bigger. A mango seed planted today becomes a tree only after some years. Because the effect comes so late, people sometimes do not notice what caused it. A good scientist keeps asking: what happened before this, maybe a long time before?
- e.g. Chana soaked tonight is big and soft tomorrow morning.
- e.g. A seed planted now becomes a tree years later.
- e.g. Clothes put out in the sun dry after a few hours.
- e.g. Brushing your teeth every day keeps them healthy for years.
### Something that comes first is not always the cause · *defined*
**Definition:** Two things can happen one after the other without the first one causing the second. To find the real cause, we test: we change one thing and see what happens.

A rooster crows every morning, and then the Sun rises. Does the rooster make the Sun rise? No. On a day when the rooster stays quiet, the Sun still rises. The rooster crows because morning is coming. So both have the same cause: the Earth turning towards the Sun. This is why scientists do tests. In the domino test, we changed only the gap. Then we knew that the gap was what made the chain stop.
- e.g. A rooster crows and then the Sun rises, but the rooster does not cause the sunrise.
- e.g. People carry umbrellas and then it rains, but umbrellas do not cause rain.
- e.g. You put on your school shoes and then the van comes, but your shoes do not bring the van.
### Does everything have a cause? · *debated*
Everything big that we see, like a falling domino, a boiling pot or a puddle, has causes we can find. But some very tiny things are harder. Inside some materials, tiny bits called atoms break apart all by themselves. Nobody can find anything that decides the exact moment one of them breaks. Scientists do not agree about what this means.
- e.g. A domino falls because it is pushed.
- e.g. A tiny atom breaks apart at a moment nobody can predict or explain.
- **Many physicists today:** For the tiniest things, there may be no cause for the exact moment something happens. It just happens, by chance.
- **Albert Einstein, and some physicists since:** There may be hidden causes that we have not found yet. Einstein wrote in a letter in 1926 that he was sure nature 'does not throw dice'.
- **Many thinkers in India and elsewhere:** Nothing happens without causes, but every happening has so many causes that nobody can know them all.

## Mostly agreed
- A push can pass along a line of dominoes, each one knocking over the next. *How we know:* In the domino test, a single gentle push on the first domino made the whole line fall when the gaps were small.
- A chain of causes stops where a link is missing or a gap is too wide. *How we know:* In the domino test, the chain stopped when the gap was as wide as a domino is tall, and when one domino was taken out of the middle.
- A push can travel through things that hardly move. *How we know:* In the coin chain test, the coins in the middle of the line hardly moved, but the last coin slid away.
- Some effects come long after their cause. *How we know:* Dry chana soaked at night only becomes big and soft by the next morning.
- Living things do not appear by themselves from rotting food. Flies come from eggs laid by other flies. *How we know:* In 1668, Francesco Redi showed that maggots grew only on meat that flies could reach.

## How we found out
**Francesco Redi, an Italian doctor, and Maria Sibylla Merian, a German artist and scientist, Redi's jar test was published in 1668. Merian's first book on caterpillars came out in 1679..** For about two thousand years, most people in Europe believed that small creatures had no parents. They thought maggots grew by themselves out of rotting meat, and caterpillars out of mud and leaves. In 1668, an Italian doctor called Francesco Redi tested this. He put pieces of meat in jars. He left some jars open. He covered others with fine cloth, so air could get in but flies could not. Flies landed on the open meat, and soon it was full of maggots. The covered meat rotted too, but no maggots grew on it. Instead, flies laid their eggs on top of the cloth. So the maggots came from fly eggs, not from the meat. At the same time in Germany, Maria Sibylla Merian was raising caterpillars at home. She fed them leaves and watched them every day. She drew each stage: the egg, the caterpillar, the pupa and the moth. Her book of 1679 showed that every caterpillar hatches from an egg laid by a moth or butterfly.  
*What people believed before:* That maggots, caterpillars and other small creatures grow by themselves from rotting meat or mud, with no parents. Redi's covered jars showed that maggots grow only where flies lay eggs, and Merian's drawings showed every stage from egg to moth.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Maggots and flies grow by themselves out of rotting meat.** (Most people in Europe believed this for about two thousand years, and many great thinkers wrote it down.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rotting meat left out soon had maggots on it, and nobody saw where they came from.  
  Against: In 1668, Francesco Redi covered some jars of meat with fine cloth. No maggots grew on that meat. Flies had laid their eggs on the cloth instead. Maria Sibylla Merian raised insects from eggs and drew every stage.
- **The rooster's crowing makes the Sun rise.** (Some old stories say this, and young children often think it.; *rejected*)  
  For: Every morning the rooster crows first, and then the Sun comes up.  
  Against: On days when a rooster is ill or quiet, the Sun still rises. Places with no roosters at all still have sunrise.
- **A small push can only ever make a small effect.** (Many people think this.; *rejected*)  
  For: A gentle push on one domino seems far too small to knock down a whole line.  
  Against: In the domino test, one gentle push knocked down ten dominoes. A scientist called Lorne Whitehead showed in 1983 that a domino can knock over one about one and a half times bigger than itself.
- **Every effect has just one cause.** (We often talk this way, for example 'the wind knocked it over'.; *debated*)  
  For: It is easy to name the last thing that happened before the effect.  
  Against: In the domino test, the last domino fell because of the domino before it, and that one because of the one before it, and so on back to your finger. A cut in any link would have stopped it. So every link was a cause.

## Roots
We have knocked down dominoes, sent a push through a line of coins and watched chana swell overnight. Now we ask what chains of causes tell us about the world, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In your domino line, your finger pushed only the first domino. Yet the last one fell too, because each domino pushed the next. When one gap was too wide, the push stopped there, and the rest stayed standing. So one small push can reach far away, as long as every link passes it on.  
*Habit it breaks:* We usually name one cause for an effect, such as 'my finger knocked them down' or 'the wind did it'. The domino test shows that every link in between is a cause too, and taking away any one of them stops the effect.  
*Evidence:* In the domino gap test, one gentle push knocked down all ten dominoes when the gaps were small. With the widest gap, or with one domino taken out, the chain stopped at that spot and the rest stayed standing.

- *who am i:* You are the effect of a very long chain: your parents and grandparents, the food you have eaten and the air you have breathed. And everything you do starts new chains.
- *what is life:* Living beings take in causes and pass them on. A chana seed drinks water and swells. An ant finds sugar and tells the others.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of chains of causes that cross each other everywhere. A coconut falling in the night can reach all the way to a new friendship.

Before the push, the line of dominoes stood still, ready to fall. One push, one single action, became ten falls, one after another. In the same way, one coconut falling became a line of stopped cars, a late van and a new friend. One cause keeps becoming many effects. Everything is conscious, the dominoes and the coins too, each one taking in the push and passing it on in their own way. Many scientists disagree, and say only living beings with brains are aware.

- **Samyutta Nikaya 12.21, in the Pali Canon, the oldest written collection of the Buddha's teachings:** When this is, that is. When this arises, that arises. When this is not, that is not. When this stops, that stops. (When the fifth domino was there, the push went on. When it was taken away, the push stopped there. Each domino falls only because the one before it does.)
- **Samkhya Karika, verse 9, by Ishvarakrishna, written more than 1,500 years ago:** An effect comes only from a cause that is able to make it. Not everything can come from everything. Teachers explaining this verse say that oil comes from sesame seeds, never from sand. (Francesco Redi showed that maggots come only from fly eggs, never from the meat by itself. Chana swell only when there is water for them to drink.)
- Science (settled): When a domino or a coin hits the next one, it passes on some of its energy. That is how one push can travel down a whole line. A scientist called Lorne Whitehead showed in 1983 that a domino can knock over one about one and a half times bigger.
- Science (debated): Some tiny atoms break apart at moments nobody can predict. Physicists still argue whether anything at all causes the exact moment.
- Other view, David Hume, a Scottish thinker, in his book An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, 1748: Hume said we never actually see a cause making an effect happen. We only see one thing, and then another thing, again and again. Because we are used to it, we expect the second whenever we see the first. So 'cause' may be partly a habit of our minds.
- Practice, Follow the warmth back (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Breathe out slowly onto your palm. Feel the warm air. Your body made that warmth. What gave your body the energy? The food you ate. Where did the food come from? A farmer, the soil, the rain and the Sun. Breathe out once more and feel the warmth again.
- Ask: Your finger pushed one domino, and ten fell. Did you knock down ten dominoes, or did they knock each other down?
- Ask: If you follow the causes back from your lunch today, how far back does the chain go?
- Ask: Is there anything at all that has no cause?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Your finger touched only the first domino. Did your finger knock down the last one, or did the domino next to it? (hint: What would have happened if one domino in the middle had been taken away?)
- *deep:* When the gap was too wide, the first domino fell but the second one stayed up. Where did the push go? (hint: Think about what the falling domino hit instead.)
- *deep:* Can anything happen with no cause at all? (hint: Think of something that seemed to happen all by itself. Was there something before it?)
- *big:* Everything you do today is the cause of something tomorrow. How far could one small thing you do today reach? (hint: Think of the line of dominoes, and how far one push travelled.)
- *big:* If you follow causes back and back, where does the chain begin? (hint: What made the first domino fall? What made you push it? What came before that?)

## Story: Meenu and the Coconut That Made the Van Late
*Meenu, Selvi Akka, Fathima and the man on the scooter are made up. Akka means elder sister in Tamil, Kannada and Telugu, and children use it for a young woman they know.*

Meenu was seven, and she lived in a tall block of flats in Thiruvananthapuram. Every morning, a yellow school van came to the gate at half past seven. Selvi Akka drove it. She knew every road in the city.

One morning, the van did not come. Meenu waited at the gate with her bag. Ten minutes went by. Then twenty. At last the van came round the corner, very slowly.

'Why are you so late, Akka?' asked Meenu, as she climbed in.

'Because of a coconut,' said Selvi Akka.

Meenu laughed. 'A coconut can't make a van late!'

'Wait and see,' said Selvi Akka. She drove out of the gate.

On the main road, the cars were stuck in a long line. Nobody could move. A man on a scooter beside them pressed his horn again and again. 'What is going on?' he shouted. 'Why is nobody moving?'

Selvi Akka leaned out of her window. 'There was a strong wind last night,' she told him. 'The wind shook the coconut trees by the temple. A big coconut fell onto the road. A bus braked hard so as not to hit it. The car behind the bus stopped too. Now every car behind that car has stopped. That is why nobody is moving.'

The man stared at her. 'All this because of one coconut?'

'One coconut, and the wind before it,' said Selvi Akka. 'Each thing made the next thing happen. Honking will not move the coconut.'

The man stopped honking. He even smiled a little.

Meenu looked out of the window. Far ahead, a policewoman was rolling the coconut to the side of the road. Then the bus moved. Then the car behind the bus moved. Then the next car, and the next. The moving went down the line of cars like a wave, until at last it reached their van.

'It is like the dominoes my Amma set up for me,' said Meenu. 'When she pushed the first one, all of them fell, one after another.'

'Just like that,' said Selvi Akka. 'Except now the push is making everyone go, instead of stop.'

Meenu reached school twenty minutes late. The first bell had already rung, and the gate was nearly shut. Standing at the gate, all alone, was a girl Meenu had never seen before. She was holding a new bag very tightly.

'Are you lost?' Meenu asked.

'It's my first day,' said the girl. 'I am Fathima. Our family just moved here from Kozhikode. I don't know which class to go to.'

'You're in Class 2? So am I!' said Meenu. 'Come with me.'

They walked in together. At lunch, they shared Meenu's idiyappam and Fathima's banana chips. By the end of the day, they were friends.

That evening, Meenu sat with Amma on the balcony. She told her about the coconut, the bus, the cars and the man on the scooter.

'If the wind had not blown,' said Meenu slowly, 'the coconut would not have fallen. If the coconut had not fallen, the van would not have been late. If the van had not been late, I would not have been at the gate when Fathima was there. And then she would not be my friend.'

Amma nodded. 'So what caused your new friend?'

Meenu thought hard. 'The wind? The coconut? Selvi Akka? Fathima's family moving here? Me, for asking if she was lost?' She frowned. 'All of them?'

'All of them,' said Amma. 'And many more things that we will never know about.'

Meenu looked down at the road below. A gust of wind was shaking the coconut trees. She wondered what it would make happen tomorrow.

**For the parent:** One coconut falling in the night was the start of a long chain of causes. Each thing that happened made the next one happen, and the chain reached all the way to Meenu's new friend.

- Ask: Why were all the cars stuck, when only the bus had to stop for the coconut?
- Ask: When the coconut was moved, the cars did not all start at once. Why did they start one after another?
- Ask: Meenu found many causes for her new friendship. Can you think of one more?
- Ask: What small thing happened to you today that might cause something tomorrow?

## Games
### g1 · Chain Builder: a card game of causes (15 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 18 cards. Find the three cards that say 'card 1' on the back. Lay them face up in a row. These start the three chains.; Shuffle the other 15 cards. Give each player 4 cards. Put the rest face down in a pile.; On your turn, put down one card that can come next in any chain. Say the chain out loud with 'so': 'The tawa gets hot, so Amma pours the batter.'; The other players turn your card over. If the back shows the right chain and the right number, you keep it there. If not, take it back.; If you cannot put down a card, take one from the pile.; The first player with no cards left wins. Playing alone, build all three chains, then check the backs.

(back: Rain chain, card 2 of 6); Water fills the lane outside the flats. (back: Rain chain, card 3 of 6); The school van has to drive very slowly.   *Parent says:* Each card is something that happens. You add a card that the last card makes happen. Then you say it with 'so', to show the cause and the effect.  
  *Child mode:* Add a card that the last card makes happen. Say it with 'so'.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take turns adding cards. 2. Say each new link out loud with 'so'. 3. Check the back of each card.  
  *Parent says:* The rain falls all afternoon, so what happens next? Which card shows the effect?  
  *Child mode:* Find the card that comes next. Say the link with 'so'.
  - Ask: Why can't 'Asha reaches school late' come straight after 'Dark clouds blow in'? (good: Something has to happen in between.; The clouds don't make her late by themselves. The rain and the water in the lane do.)
  - Note: If a player's card could fit in a sensible way, even if the back says otherwise, let them explain it. A good reason counts.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. When all the chains are finished, look at the mango chain. 2. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the mango chain. What does the last card lead to?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the last mango card. What does it lead to next?
  - Ask: What does the last mango card lead to? (good: A ripe mango falls again, so the chain starts again.; It goes round in a circle.)
  - Note: A chain that comes back to the start is called a loop. Many chains in nature are loops: mango, seed, tree, mango.
  - Check: In the dosa chain, what is the cause of the tawa getting hot? → **Amma lighting the gas**
### g2 · Pass the Push: a human chain game (10 min)
- **rules:** Stand in a line, one behind the other, each facing the back of the person in front.; The timer says 'Go' and pats the shoulder of the person at the back of the line.; When you feel a pat, pat the shoulder of the person in front of you. Do not pat before you feel it.; The person at the front puts a hand up as soon as they feel the pat. The timer stops the phone's stopwatch.; Write the time on the sheet. Try again with eyes closed.; Last, one person in the middle steps out and does not pat. Does the pat still reach the front?
- **Printable score sheet: Pass the Push timing sheet:** Try 1, eyes open: seconds from the first pat to the last person's hand going up; Try 2, eyes open: seconds; Try 3, eyes closed: seconds; Try 4, eyes closed: seconds; Try 5, one person in the middle steps out: did the pat reach the end?
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand in a line. 2. Read the rules out loud. 3. Have one practice try.  
  *Parent says:* You can only pat when you feel a pat. Each person is one domino. Let's see how fast the push can travel.  
  *Child mode:* Pat the person in front only when you feel a pat.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do two tries with eyes open and two with eyes closed. 2. Write each time on the sheet.  
  *Parent says:* How many seconds did it take? Will it be faster or slower with eyes closed? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* How many seconds did it take? Try with eyes closed. Is it faster or slower?
  - Ask: Why does the pat take some time to get to the front? (good: Each person needs a moment to feel it and pat.; It has to go through every person, one at a time.)
  - Note: Each person takes a short moment to feel the pat and pass it on. More people in the line means a longer time.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. One person in the middle steps out to the side and does not pat. 2. Play once more.  
  *Parent says:* Now one link is missing. Will the pat still reach the front?  
  *Child mode:* One person steps out. Does the pat still get to the front?
  - Ask: Why did the pat stop? (good: The person who stepped out did not pass it on.; There was a missing link, like the missing domino.)
  - Note: The pat stops at the gap, just like the domino line with one domino taken out.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Domino Gap Test: how far apart can they be? (30 min)
- **question:** How far apart can dominoes stand and still knock each other down, all the way to the end of the line?
- **predict:** We will try three gaps: one finger wide, half a domino tall, and one whole domino tall. Which gaps will make all ten dominoes fall? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We change only the gap. We use the same ten dominoes on the same floor each time. We give the first domino the same gentle push with one finger. We switch off the fan, because moving air can knock over light dominoes. We try each gap twice, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** If you use matchboxes, take all the matches out first. A grown-up keeps the matches away from children.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then draw the line of dominoes for the widest gap, showing which ones fell and which stayed up.
- **results table:** Gap | First try: how many fell (out of 10)? | Second try: how many fell (out of 10)?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand one domino on its short end on the paper strip. 2. Your child marks the strip level with the top of the domino. 3. Cut the strip at the mark. 4. Fold it in half and mark the fold.  
  *Parent says:* This strip is exactly as long as a domino is tall. Folded in half, it shows half a domino's height. We will use it to set the gaps.  
  *Child mode:* Stand a domino on the paper. Mark how tall it is. Cut the paper there. Fold it in half.
  - Note: Measuring with the strip means the test works with any dominoes, matchboxes or blocks, whatever their size.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off the fan. 2. Show your child the three gaps: one finger, half the strip and the whole strip. 3. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* We will try three gaps. One finger wide. Half a domino tall. One whole domino tall. For which gaps will all ten fall? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* There are three gaps to try. Which gaps will make all ten fall? Why?
  - Ask: Which gaps will make all ten dominoes fall? Why? (good: The small gaps, because each domino can reach the next one.; The big gap, because the domino falls harder from further away.; All of them, because a push always goes on.)
  - Note: Write down your child's guess and their reason. Do not say who is right. Many children think a bigger gap gives a harder hit.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands ten dominoes in a straight line, one finger apart. 2. Start at the far end, so a slip knocks over only a few. 3. Push the first domino gently with one finger. 4. Count how many fell. 5. Do it once more.  
  *Parent says:* Put one finger between each domino and the next. Now give the first one a gentle push. Don't push hard. How many fell?  
  *Child mode:* Stand ten dominoes in a line, one finger apart. Push the first one gently. Count how many fall.
  - Note: If some fall while your child is setting them up, that is normal. Stand them up again. With a one-finger gap, all ten usually fall.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the ten dominoes again, using the half strip to set each gap. 2. Push the first one gently. 3. Count how many fell. 4. Do it once more.  
  *Parent says:* Now use the half strip for each gap. The gaps are bigger this time. Will they all fall? Push the first one gently.  
  *Child mode:* Use the half strip to make each gap. Push the first one. Count how many fall.
  - Note: With half a domino's height as the gap, all ten usually still fall. Each one hits the next one high up, near the top.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the ten dominoes again, using the whole strip to set each gap. 2. Push the first one gently. 3. Watch where the first domino lands. 4. Count how many fell. 5. Do it once more.  
  *Parent says:* Now each gap is as wide as a domino is tall. Push the first one. Watch where it lands. How many fell this time?  
  *Child mode:* Make each gap as wide as the whole strip. Push the first one. Watch where it lands.
  - Ask: Where did the first domino land? Did it touch the second one? (good: It lay down flat and just reached the bottom of the next one.; It did not reach the next one, so that one stayed up.)
  - Note: Usually only the first one falls. Lying flat, it just reaches the bottom of the next domino, too low to tip it. If your dominoes slide on a smooth floor, try a rough tile or a table.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the line again with the half-strip gaps. 2. Take out the fifth domino. 3. Push the first one and count how many fall. 4. Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why. 5. Then read the card on why the last domino falls.  
  *Parent says:* This time we take one domino out of the middle. What will happen? Push. How many fell? Why do you think the chain stopped where it did?  
  *Child mode:* Take out the fifth domino. Push the first one. Where does the chain stop? Why?
  - Ask: Why did the chain stop? (good: The fourth domino had nothing to hit.; There was a big hole, so the push could not get across.; Each domino only falls if the one before it hits it.)
  - Note: Four dominoes fall, then the chain stops at the hole. Let your child explain it before you read the card. A strong answer says that each domino falls only when the one before it hits it.
  - Check: In the domino test, why did the last domino fall when the gaps were small? → **The domino next to it hit it**
### e2 · The Coin Chain: which coin moves? (20 min)
- **question:** If you slide a coin into one end of a line of coins, which coin will move away?
- **predict:** When the sliding coin hits the line, which coin will move? The first one, the middle ones, the last one, or all of them? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All the coins are the same kind, so they are the same size and weight. They touch each other in a straight line. We slide the coin the same way each time, on the same smooth surface. We try each test three times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **record:** Draw the line of coins before and after each test. Draw an arrow on each coin that moved.
- **results table:** Test | Which coins moved away? | Did the middle coins move?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the ruler on the board. 2. Your child puts five coins along its edge, all touching. 3. Take the ruler away gently. 4. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* These five coins are all touching. We will slide another coin into this end. Which coin will move away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put five coins in a line, touching. Which coin will move when another coin hits? Why?
  - Ask: Which coin will move away when the sliding coin hits? (good: The first coin, because it gets hit.; All of them, because they all get pushed.; The last coin, because the push goes along.)
  - Note: Write down your child's guess and reason. Most children guess the first coin, or all of them.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put a sixth coin about one hand-length from the end of the line, in a straight line with it. 2. Your child flicks it with a finger so it slides straight into the line. 3. Watch which coin moves. 4. Set up again and do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Flick this coin so it slides straight into the end of the line. Watch carefully. Which coin moved?  
  *Child mode:* Flick the coin into the line. Watch closely. Which coin moved away?
  - Ask: Which coin moved? What did the middle coins do? (good: The last coin slid away.; The middle coins hardly moved at all.)
  - Note: On a smooth board, the coin you flick stops, the middle coins hardly move, and the last coin slides away. If all the coins scatter, the line was not straight or the coins were not touching. Set them up again.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Set up the line of five again. 2. Put two coins touching each other, one hand-length away. 3. Your child flicks the back coin, so both slide into the line together. 4. Watch how many coins move away at the far end.  
  *Parent says:* This time, two coins hit the line together. How many coins will move away at the other end? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Two coins hit the line together. How many move away at the end?
  - Note: Usually two coins slide away at the far end. If only one moves, check that the two coins were touching as they slid. Try again.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they think, what they saw and why. 2. Then read the card on why the last domino falls.  
  *Parent says:* The middle coins hardly moved. But the last coin flew off. How did the push get to the end?  
  *Child mode:* The middle coins hardly moved. How did the push get to the end coin?
  - Ask: How did the push get from the first coin to the last coin? (good: Each coin pushed the next one.; The push went through the coins, like through dominoes.; The coins passed it on without moving much.)
  - Note: The push travels through the coins as a quick squeeze from one to the next. The middle coins pass it on and stay almost still. The last coin has nothing to pass it to, so it moves away.

## Activities
### a1 · Do It and See: six actions, six effects (10 min)
- **record:** Draw one action and its effect, with an arrow from the action to the effect.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child drops the spoon onto the plate. 2. Holds the paper strip in front of the fan and switches the fan on. 3. Stirs a spoon of sugar into the water and tastes it. 4. Breathes on the steel tumbler. 5. Claps once. 6. Switches the light off and on.  
  *Parent says:* Do this, and tell me what happened because of it. What you did is called the cause. What happened is called the effect.  
  *Child mode:* Do each action. Then say what happened. What you did is the cause. What happened is the effect.
  - Ask: What was the effect of breathing on the tumbler? (good: The steel went cloudy.; A misty patch came on it, and then it went away.)
  - Note: Your breath is warm and wet. The cold steel turns some of that water into tiny drops, so it looks cloudy.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to find an action that has no effect at all. 2. Talk about each one they try.  
  *Parent says:* Can you find an action that has no effect at all? Try something. Now, did anything change, even a tiny bit?  
  *Child mode:* Try to find an action with no effect at all. Did anything change, even a little?
  - Ask: Was there an action with no effect at all? (good: No. Even blinking moves the air a tiny bit.; Waving my hand moved the air.; When I whispered, Amma heard me.)
  - Note: Any action changes something, even if only a little: it moves air, makes a sound or warms what you touch. Let your child test their ideas. There is no need to settle it now.
### a2 · Cause Detectives: what made this happen? (12 min)
- **record:** For each effect, draw it and write one word for its cause.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Before you start, without your child seeing, spill a little water near a tumbler. 2. Leave a book on the floor near a table edge. 3. Leave biscuit crumbs on a plate. 4. Put one chappal upside down by the door.  
  *Parent says:* Something happened in four places in our home. Your job is to find out what caused each one. Look for clues.  
  *Child mode:* Four things happened at home. Find them. Look for clues. What caused each one?
  - Note: Choose effects with clear clues. The wet tumbler is a clue for the water. The table edge is a clue for the book.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child finds each effect. 2. For each one, ask the questions below.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think caused this? What clue tells you? Could anything else have caused it?  
  *Child mode:* What caused this? What clue tells you? Could it be something else?
  - Ask: What caused the crumbs on the plate? How do you know? (good: Someone ate a biscuit there, because the crumbs are biscuit crumbs.; A biscuit broke on the plate.)
  - Note: Praise the clue more than the answer. 'The tumbler is wet on the outside' is real evidence. Some effects have more than one possible cause, and that is a good thing to notice.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch on a light just as your child walks into the room. 2. Ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* The light came on just as you walked in. Did your walking in make the light come on?  
  *Child mode:* The light came on as you walked in. Did you make it come on?
  - Ask: Did walking in make the light come on? (good: No, you pressed the switch.; It only happened at the same time.)
  - Note: This shows that two things can happen together, or one after the other, without one causing the other.
### a3 · The Ant Message Chain (15 min)
- **record:** Draw the paper each time you look, with a dot for each ant. Write the time and the number of ants.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Find a place where you have seen ants, such as a kitchen counter, a window sill or a balcony. 2. Put a drop of sugar water on the paper there. 3. Count the ants on the paper now.  
  *Parent says:* Ants walk here sometimes. Let's put a drop of sugar water on this paper and count the ants. Then we wait.  
  *Child mode:* Put a drop of sugar water on the paper, near the ants. Count the ants now.
  - Note: If no ants come within an hour, try again near a different wall or window. Some flats have no ants at all, and then this activity is for another day.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look again and count the ants. 2. Look once more ten minutes after that.  
  *Parent says:* How many ants are there now? Where are they coming from? Do they walk in a line?  
  *Child mode:* Count the ants now. Are they walking in a line?
  - Ask: How did so many ants know where the sugar was? (good: The first ant told the others.; They followed each other's path.; They smelled it.)
  - Note: Many kinds of ants leave a trail of smell on the way home from food. Other ants smell the trail and follow it, so one ant's find brings many ants.
  - Wait: 10 to 20 minutes later
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Talk about the chain together. 2. Afterwards, move the paper outside or wipe the sugar away, without hurting the ants.  
  *Parent says:* First the sugar. Then one ant found the sugar. Then she left a smell trail. Then many ants came. What started the chain?  
  *Child mode:* One ant found the sugar. Then many came. What started the chain?
  - Note: Ants sense, decide and tell each other where food is. Some scientists call the trail an automatic response. Either way, one ant's find was the cause of all the others coming.

## Challenges
### c1 · The Chain Reaction Machine: ring a bell with one push (25 min)
- **problem:** Make a machine where one gentle push starts a chain of at least four causes, and the last one rings a steel bowl or drops a coin into a cup.
- **your turn:** Draw your machine first, with an arrow from each cause to its effect. Then build it. If it stops, find the link that did not pass the push on, fix it, and try again.
- **constraints:** You may touch only the first part, once.; There must be at least four steps, each causing the next.; Use things from home, and put everything back afterwards.
- **general:** A line of dominoes falls onto a ruler lying across a pencil, like a see-saw. The other end of the ruler flips up and hits the steel bowl.; The last domino pushes a marble down a ramp made from a book. The marble rolls into a steel tumbler and rings it.
- **creative:** Make the dominoes grow in size, from a matchbox to a small book. Each one can knock over one a little bigger than itself.; Make the marble knock a coin off the edge of a table into a cup.; Add a turn in the domino line, around a corner of a book.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the problem together. 2. Your child draws a plan with arrows.  
  *Parent says:* One push has to start at least four causes in a row. The last one must ring the bowl. Draw your plan first. Put an arrow from each cause to its effect.  
  *Child mode:* Draw your machine. Draw an arrow from each cause to its effect.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child builds the machine. 2. Test it. 3. When it stops, look for the link that failed. 4. Fix it and try again.  
  *Parent says:* Where did the chain stop? Which link did not pass the push on? What could you change there?  
  *Child mode:* Push the first part. Where did it stop? Fix that link and try again.
  - Note: Chains nearly always fail a few times. Help your child find the exact link that failed, rather than starting again from scratch.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Run the finished machine. 2. Your child names each cause and effect in order.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me the chain, from the first push to the ring. Use 'so' between each step.  
  *Child mode:* Say your chain from start to end. Use 'so' between each step.
  - Ask: Which link was the hardest to get right? Why? (good: The ramp, because the marble rolled off the side.; The dominoes, because the gap was too big.)

## Worksheets
### Causes, Effects and Dominoes (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (match) Draw a line from each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Cause → what makes something happen; Effect → what happens because of a cause; Chain of causes → each effect causes the next thing; Gap → the empty space between two things. *Why:* These are the words the child needs to talk about the domino test. A cause makes something happen, and the effect is what happens.
2. (mcq) In the domino test, what happened when each gap was as wide as a domino is tall?  
   Options: All ten fell / Only the first one fell / None of them fell  
   **Answer:** Only the first one fell. *Why:* The first domino fell when it was pushed, but it lay down without tipping the next one. Choosing 'all ten fell' means thinking a bigger gap gives a harder hit. The test shows the domino cannot reach.
3. (sort) Each pair below is a cause and its effect. Sort each one into 'cause' or 'effect'.  
   **Answer:** switching on the fan → Cause; the curtain moving → Effect; heat from the gas → Cause; the water boiling → Effect; a push on the first domino → Cause; the first domino falling → Effect. *Why:* In each pair, the cause comes first and makes the effect happen. The fan moves the curtain, the heat boils the water, the push tips the domino.
4. (draw) Draw your domino line with the widest gap, after you pushed the first one. Show which dominoes fell and which stayed standing.  
   **Answer:** One domino lying flat, its top just reaching the bottom of the next one, and nine dominoes still standing.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the fallen domino not reaching high enough to tip the next one. That is the evidence for why the chain stopped.
5. (truefalse) In the domino test, your finger touched every domino in the line.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Your finger touched only the first domino. Each of the others was knocked over by the domino before it.
6. (write) Say out loud, and a grown-up writes it down: when we took the fifth domino out, why did the chain stop?  
   **Answer:** The fourth domino had nothing to hit, so the push could not get past the hole.. *Why:* A strong answer says that each domino falls only when the one before it hits it. Saying 'because it was broken' does not say which link was missing.
7. (order) Put this chain of causes in order.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3]. *Why:* Each one causes the next. The clouds bring rain, the rain fills the lane, and the water makes the van go slowly.
8. (count) Kavya stood up 10 dominoes with the widest gap. When she pushed, only 1 fell. How many stayed standing?  
   **Answer:** 9. *Why:* 10 take away 1 is 9. Nine dominoes stayed standing, because the first one could not reach the second.
9. (perspective) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 2, and her brother Arjun watch the last domino fall.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason, then sees the other side. For example: Arjun is right that the ninth domino hit the last one. Meenakshi is right that without her push, nothing would have fallen.. *Why:* Both are partly right. The last domino's nearest cause was its neighbour. But the push started with the finger. Every link in a chain is a cause.
10. (fill) What makes something happen is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** cause. *Why:* The cause comes first and makes something happen. What happens is the effect.
### Coins, Chana and Hidden Causes (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) In the coin chain test, one coin slid into the line. Which coin moved away?  
   Options: The first coin in the line / All the coins together / Only the last coin  
   **Answer:** Only the last coin. *Why:* The push travelled through the coins, and only the last one moved away. Choosing 'the first coin' makes sense, because it was hit. But it passed the push on instead of moving.
2. (write) The middle coins hardly moved. So how did the push get to the last coin?  
   **Answer:** Each coin pushed on the next one, and passed the push along without moving much. The last coin had nothing to pass it to, so it moved away.. *Why:* A strong answer says that each coin passed the push to the next. Saying 'it jumped over' misses that the coins touched and passed it on.
3. (draw) Draw the coin line before and after you slid two coins in together. Put an arrow on each coin that moved away.  
   **Answer:** A line of five coins with two coins coming in at one end, and then two coins sliding away from the far end.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the same number of coins leaving as came in, with the middle coins staying in place.
4. (odd) In three of these, the first thing causes the second. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Switching on the fan, then the curtain moves / The rooster crows, then the Sun rises / Stirring in sugar, then the water tastes sweet / Lighting the gas, then the tawa gets hot  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* The rooster crows before sunrise, but the Sun would rise anyway. Both happen because morning is coming. In the other three, the first thing really causes the second.
5. (truefalse) If one thing happens just before another, the first thing must be its cause.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A rooster crows before sunrise, but does not make the Sun rise. To find a real cause, we test by changing one thing.
6. (perspective) Meenu, a girl in Class 2, made a new friend after her school van was late because a coconut fell on the road. Her cousins Revathi and Joel talk about it.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason and sees the other side. For example: Joel is right that Meenu chose to speak. Revathi is right that Meenu would not have been there without the coconut.. *Why:* Both are real causes. A happening usually has many causes, some that just happen and some that people choose.
7. (fill) Soaked overnight in water, dry chana become ____ by the morning.  
   **Answer:** bigger. *Why:* The chana seeds soak up water through the night. The effect takes hours to show.
8. (label) Label the two pictures: write 'cause' under one and 'effect' under the other.  
   **Answer:** ['a rain cloud pouring rain: cause', 'a puddle on the road: effect']. *Why:* The rain comes first and makes the puddle. So the rain is the cause, and the puddle is the effect.
9. (count) Before soaking, Kavya's line of 10 chana was 8 centimetres long. In the morning, it was 12 centimetres long. How much longer was it?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 12 take away 8 is 4. The line was 4 centimetres longer, because each chana had soaked up water.
10. (mcq) Why did the chain stop when the gap was as wide as a domino is tall?  
   Options: The falling domino could not reach high enough on the next one / The dominoes were too heavy / The push got tired  
   **Answer:** The falling domino could not reach high enough on the next one. *Why:* Lying flat, the first domino just reached the bottom of the next one, which is too low to tip it. The dominoes were the same as in the other tests, so weight was not the reason.
### Chains, Loops and Proof (challenge) (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (write) Does the floor change how well a domino chain works? Say how you would test it, and what you would keep the same.  
   **Answer:** Set up the same ten dominoes with the same gap, first on a smooth tile and then on a mat. Push the same way. Keep the dominoes, the gap and the push the same, and change only the floor.. *Why:* A strong answer changes only the floor and keeps everything else the same, so any difference must be caused by the floor.
2. (perspective) Nila, a girl in Class 2, and her grandmother watch a domino line fall.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason. For example: Nila is right that the dominoes touched and pushed. Her grandmother is right that what our eyes see is one movement after another.. *Why:* This is a real question that thinkers have argued about. The Scottish thinker David Hume said, in 1748, that we never see a cause itself, only one thing following another.
3. (draw) Draw a plan for a machine where one push starts four causes in a row, and the last one rings a steel bowl. Put an arrow from each cause to its effect.  
   **Answer:** A drawing with at least four parts, such as dominoes, a ruler see-saw, a rolling marble and a steel bowl, each joined by an arrow.. *Why:* A strong plan has every link able to reach the next one. Each arrow shows which thing makes the next one happen.
4. (mcq) In 1668, Francesco Redi put meat in jars. Some were open and some were covered with fine cloth. What grew on the covered meat?  
   Options: Lots of maggots / No maggots / Small flies with no eggs  
   **Answer:** No maggots. *Why:* Flies could not reach the covered meat, so no maggots grew on it. This showed that maggots come from fly eggs, not from the meat itself.
5. (order) Put the ant message chain in order.  
   **Answer:** [0, 1, 2, 3]. *Why:* The sugar comes first. The first ant's find, and her smell trail, cause many other ants to come.
6. (truefalse) A falling domino can knock over a domino that is a little bigger than itself.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In 1983, a scientist called Lorne Whitehead showed that a domino can knock over one about one and a half times its size. So a chain of growing dominoes can end with a very big one.
7. (sort) Sort these effects: does each one come straight after its cause, or much later?  
   **Answer:** a domino falling when pushed → Straight after; a clang when a spoon hits a plate → Straight after; soaked chana getting bigger → Much later; a mango seed growing into a tree → Much later; a curtain moving when the fan is on → Straight after; wet clothes drying in the sun → Much later. *Why:* Some effects happen in a moment. Others take hours or years. A late effect is easy to miss, so we keep asking what happened before.
8. (write) The domino line and the coin line are different. Say one way they are the same.  
   **Answer:** In both, a push went from one thing to the next, all the way to the end. Only the first one was touched.. *Why:* A strong answer names the chain: each part passing the push to the next. Saying 'they both fell' is not true, because the coins slid.
9. (fill) A chain of causes stops where one ____ is missing.  
   **Answer:** link. *Why:* Each link passes the push to the next. When one is missing, as when we took out the fifth domino, the push stops there.
10. (odd) Which of these chains is broken, so the effect at the end will not happen?  
   Options: Push, domino, domino, domino, bell / Push, domino, a wide empty gap, domino, bell / Pat, person, person, person, hand goes up  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* The wide empty gap means the domino before it has nothing to hit. In the other two, every link can reach the next.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a cause?  
   Options: What makes something happen / What happens at the end / Something very small  
   **Answer:** What makes something happen. *Why:* A cause makes something happen. The effect is what happens because of it.
2. (mcq) You switch on the fan, and the curtain moves. What is the effect?  
   Options: Switching on the fan / The curtain moving / The fan's switch  
   **Answer:** The curtain moving. *Why:* The curtain moving is what happens because of the fan. Switching on the fan is the cause.
3. (mcq) Ten dominoes stand one finger apart. You push only the first one. What usually happens?  
   Options: Only the first one falls / All ten fall, one after another / They all fall at the same moment  
   **Answer:** All ten fall, one after another. *Why:* Each domino hits the next one, so they fall one after another. They do not fall together, because the push takes a moment to travel down the line.
4. (mcq) Which domino knocks over the last domino in a line?  
   Options: The first domino / The domino just before it / Nobody knows  
   **Answer:** The domino just before it. *Why:* Each domino is knocked over by its neighbour. The first one starts the chain, but it never touches the last one.
5. (mcq) A domino in the middle of the line is taken away. What happens when you push the first one?  
   Options: The whole line still falls / The dominoes fall up to the gap, and then the chain stops / None of them fall  
   **Answer:** The dominoes fall up to the gap, and then the chain stops. *Why:* The domino before the gap has nothing to hit. A chain of causes stops where a link is missing.
6. (mcq) Why can a gap be too wide for a domino chain?  
   Options: The falling domino cannot reach the next one high enough to tip it / Dominoes get tired / The floor stops the push  
   **Answer:** The falling domino cannot reach the next one high enough to tip it. *Why:* A domino can only tip the next one if it hits it well above the bottom. If the gap is as wide as the domino is tall, it just reaches the bottom.
7. (mcq) A coin slides into a line of touching coins. What do the coins in the middle do?  
   Options: They fly off in all directions / They hardly move, but pass the push on / They all move together  
   **Answer:** They hardly move, but pass the push on. *Why:* The middle coins pass the push to the next coin and stay almost still. Only the last coin slides away.
8. (mcq) Two coins slide together into a line of touching coins. What usually happens at the far end?  
   Options: One coin slides away / Two coins slide away / No coins move  
   **Answer:** Two coins slide away. *Why:* Usually the same number of coins leaves the far end as came in. Two in, two out.
9. (mcq) Dry chana are put in water at night. When can we see the effect?  
   Options: At once / By the next morning / Never  
   **Answer:** By the next morning. *Why:* The chana soak up water slowly, so they become big only after many hours. Some effects come long after their cause.
10. (mcq) Which of these effects takes years to come?  
   Options: A clang from a dropped spoon / A mango tree growing from a seed / A curtain moving in the fan's wind  
   **Answer:** A mango tree growing from a seed. *Why:* A seed takes years to grow into a tree. The clang and the moving curtain happen in a moment.
11. (mcq) Every morning a rooster crows, and then the Sun rises. What is true?  
   Options: The rooster makes the Sun rise / The rooster crows because morning is coming / The Sun makes the rooster quiet  
   **Answer:** The rooster crows because morning is coming. *Why:* The Sun rises even when the rooster is quiet. Coming first does not make something the cause.
12. (mcq) Long ago, many people thought maggots grew by themselves out of rotting meat. What did Francesco Redi's covered jars show?  
   Options: Maggots come from eggs that flies lay / Meat turns into maggots when it gets old / Cloth makes maggots  
   **Answer:** Maggots come from eggs that flies lay. *Why:* No maggots grew where flies could not reach the meat. The real cause was the flies' eggs.

## Applications
- Doctors look for the cause of an illness, such as a germ or a lack of a vitamin, so they can treat the cause and not only the effects.
- Weather scientists at the India Meteorological Department follow chains of causes, from warm seas to winds to clouds, to say when the monsoon will reach Kerala.
- Engineers who build lifts, bridges and trains test what would happen if one part broke, so one broken link does not stop or harm everything.
- Mechanics and electricians follow a chain back, step by step, to find which part stopped a scooter or a fan from working.
- Farmers know that what they do now, such as adding compost to the soil, has effects on the crop months later.

## Spotted around us
- Traffic on a busy road in Bengaluru or Kolkata: when the front car stops, each car behind it stops one after another, like dominoes.
- A carrom board: the striker hits one coin, which hits another, which goes into the pocket. A chain of causes in one shot.
- The monsoon reaching Kerala in early June: warm sea, then winds, then clouds, then rain, then full rivers and green fields.
- A line of ants on the kitchen wall, following a smell trail one ant left on the way back from food.
- Soaking rice and urad dal for idli and dosa batter in the evening, so it is ready to grind the next day.

## Open questions
- **Does everything have a cause?** For big things, we can almost always find causes. But some tiny atoms break apart at moments nobody can predict or explain. Physicists still argue whether there is any cause for when that happens.
- **Where does a chain of causes begin?** If you follow causes back, each one has a cause before it. Scientists trace chains back to the beginning of the universe, but nobody knows what, if anything, came before that.
- **When you choose to do something, what caused your choice?** Your choices have causes, such as what you saw, what you felt and what you remember. Scientists and thinkers still argue about how free our choices are.

- Can you think of an effect that comes a long time after its cause?
- If you took one step out of your morning, which other things would not happen?

## Connected maths
- Kavya stood 10 dominoes in a line and took out the fifth one. When she pushed the first, 4 fell. How many dominoes were still standing? **5 dominoes.**. She took 1 domino out, so 9 were in the line. 4 fell. 9 take away 4 is 5. So 5 stayed standing after the gap. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Each of Kavya's dominoes is 5 centimetres tall. A gap must be less than one domino's height for the chain to work. Which of these gaps will work: 2 centimetres, 4 centimetres or 6 centimetres? **2 centimetres and 4 centimetres.**. 2 and 4 are both less than 5, so the falling domino can reach the next one. 6 is more than 5, so the falling domino lies flat before it reaches the next one. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In the morning, 10 soaked chana made a line 12 centimetres long. About how long would a line of 20 soaked chana be? **About 24 centimetres.**. 20 chana is two lots of 10. Each lot of 10 makes about 12 centimetres. 12 and 12 make 24. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- In Pass the Push, a line of 4 people passed the pat to the front in about 2 seconds. If 8 people stand in the line, about how long will it take? **About 4 seconds.**. 8 people is twice as many as 4. Each person needs about the same moment to feel the pat and pass it on. So it takes about twice as long: 2 and 2 make 4 seconds. Your real time may be a little more or less. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L2`)
