# Life began in water: from the first tiny cells to us
`sci.evolution.story-of-life.L2`

**Summary.** Dry baking soda and dry citric acid can sit side by side for months, and nothing happens. Add a few drops of water, and they fizz at once. Water lets chemicals move about, meet and change. That is why life began in water, more than 3.5 billion years ago. The first living beings were tiny cells in the sea, far too small to see. Very slowly, over a very long time, they became every kind of living being on Earth, including you.

**Intro.** *Put a pinch of baking soda and a pinch of citric acid side by side on a dry steel plate. Push them together with a dry spoon. Watch for one minute. Is anything happening?* Dry powders can sit together for months and stay just the same. Yet a few drops of water change them in a second. In this unit you find out what water does to them. Then you find out why every living being began in water, long ago, and how long the story of life really is. Before you read on, make a guess. Will the powders fizz if you pour cooking oil on them instead of water? You are going to test it.

## Terms
- **React**: When two or more chemicals meet and change into new chemicals, we say they react. *e.g.* Baking soda and citric acid react in water and make bubbles of gas.
- **Dissolve**: To spread out into a liquid in pieces too small to see, the way sugar seems to vanish in tea. *e.g.* Water dissolves citric acid, so the citric acid can spread and meet the baking soda.
- **Cell**: The smallest living unit of any living being. Some living beings are one cell. You are made of a huge number of cells. *e.g.* The first living beings on Earth were each one single cell, living in water.
- **Fossil**: The remains or the mark of a living being from long ago, kept in rock. *e.g.* A shell pressed into stone, or the layers a mat of tiny cells left behind, are fossils.
- **Stromatolite**: A rock built up in thin layers by a mat of tiny cells living in shallow water. Sand sticks to the mat, and the cells grow up through it again and again. *e.g.* The oldest stromatolites we know are about 3.5 billion years old. Some still grow today, at Shark Bay in Australia.
- **Ancestor**: Anyone you come from: a parent, a grandparent, a great-grandparent, and on and on, back and back. *e.g.* Your great-grandmother is your ancestor. So, very far back, were tiny cells in the sea.
- **Timeline**: A line that shows things in the order they happened, the oldest at one end and the newest at the other. *e.g.* The Year of Life calendar is a timeline of the story of life squeezed into one year.
- **Billion**: A thousand million. Written out, it is 1 followed by nine zeros: 1,000,000,000. *e.g.* The Earth is about four and a half billion years old.
- **RNA**: A long, thin chain of chemicals inside every cell. RNA carries instructions, and some kinds of RNA can help make copies of other chains. *e.g.* Many scientists think the very first life may have used RNA to store its instructions.

## Explanations
### Why the powders fizzed only in water · *defined*
**Definition:** Two chemicals react when their tiny pieces meet. Baking soda and citric acid can only meet when water dissolves them.

A grain of dry powder is a solid. Its tiny pieces stay stuck in place, so the two powders hardly meet at all. Water dissolves the grains. Then their tiny pieces spread out through the water and bump into each other. When baking soda meets citric acid, they react and make a gas called carbon dioxide. That gas makes the bubbles. Oil does not dissolve these powders, so nothing happened in the oil bowl. As soon as you added water to the oil bowl, the powder fizzed. It had been ready all along.
- e.g. Fruit salt sits quietly in a dry packet, and fizzes in a tumbler of water.
- e.g. Cooks in Gujarat add fruit salt or soda to wet dhokla batter, so it fills with bubbles and rises.
- e.g. Sea water made the powders fizz just like tap water, because the sea is water too.
### Life began in water · *defined*
**Definition:** The chemicals of life move about, meet and react in water. So life began in water, more than 3.5 billion years ago, and every living being still needs water inside them.

Inside every living cell, the chemicals of life are dissolved in water, like the powders in your bowl. There they meet and react all day long. That is how a cell grows, uses food and makes copies of itself. So scientists think life could only begin where there was liquid water. The oldest clear signs of life come from rocks that formed in shallow sea water about 3.5 billion years ago, in Australia. In 2006, the Australian scientist Abigail Allwood and her team showed that some of these rocks are stromatolites, built by mats of tiny cells. Your own cells are mostly water too. More than half of your body is water.
- e.g. The oldest stromatolites formed in shallow sea water about 3.5 billion years ago.
- e.g. A moong seed stays dry and still for months, and begins to grow only when it gets water.
- e.g. When people look for life on Mars, they look first for signs of water.
### Where in the water did life begin? · *debated*
Scientists agree that life began in water. They do not agree on which water. Each idea has some evidence, and nobody has found the answer yet.
- e.g. Hot springs on the deep sea floor still pour out warm water full of chemicals.
- e.g. Hot spring pools on land, in places like Iceland, dry out and fill up again.
- e.g. Big craters made by rocks from space filled with water on the young Earth.
- **Scientists who study the deep sea, such as Michael Russell and William Martin:** Life began at hot springs on the deep sea floor. There, warm water full of chemicals meets cold sea water, and rocks have tiny holes where chemicals can gather.
- **Scientists who study hot springs on land, such as David Deamer and Bruce Damer:** Life began in warm pools on land that dried out and filled up again. Drying pushes chemicals close together, so they can join into long chains. Charles Darwin guessed something like this in 1871, when he wrote of a warm little pond.
- **Sankar Chatterjee, a scientist born in India who works in the United States:** Life began in lakes inside craters made by rocks from space. The rocks brought chemicals, and the crater floors had hot springs.
### The first living beings were tiny cells · *defined*
**Definition:** A cell is the smallest living unit of any living being. The first living beings were each one single tiny cell.

The first living beings were far too small to see. About 50 of them, lying side by side, would just stretch across the width of one hair. Each cell had a thin skin around a watery inside. Each cell took in food from the water, grew, and split in two to make more cells. Tiny cells like these sense food and swim towards it, and the first cells sensed their world too. They lived in mats and films on the sea floor. We know about them from stromatolites and from chemicals they left in old rocks. For nearly 3 billion years, almost all life on Earth was tiny cells like these.
- e.g. A green slimy film on a wet wall or in a rain puddle is made of tiny water-living cells.
- e.g. Stromatolites at Shark Bay in Australia are still being built by mats of tiny cells today.
- e.g. Your body is made of cells too, a huge number of them, each one mostly water.
### The RNA idea: how the first cell may have started · *idea*
Every cell today keeps instructions in long chains called DNA and RNA. RNA can carry instructions, and some RNA can also help join chemicals together. So many scientists think the first life may have used RNA alone. A chain of RNA that could help make copies of itself would make more and more chains. If a bubble of fat closed around such chains, that bubble could have become the first cell. This is an idea, not a finding. Nobody has yet made a living cell from chemicals in a lab.
- e.g. In labs, scientists have made RNA chains that can join other small chains together.
- e.g. Bubbles of fat form by themselves when some oily chemicals are shaken in water.
- e.g. Every cell alive today still uses RNA to turn instructions into working parts.
- **Many scientists:** RNA came first. It can carry instructions and help make copies, so it could have done both jobs before DNA and the other parts of cells existed.
- **Other scientists:** Cycles of chemical reactions came first, using energy from hot springs. Chains like RNA came later, once those reactions were running.
### One became many, very slowly · *defined*
**Definition:** Every living being comes from parents, and they came from theirs, back to the first cells. Over a very long time, small changes added up, and a few kinds became many kinds.

Children are a little different from their parents. Over thousands and thousands of generations, these small differences add up. That is how new kinds of living beings come about. It is very slow. For most of life's story, all life was tiny cells in water. The first animals big enough to see appeared in the sea only about 575 million years ago. Plants grew onto land about 470 million years ago. Animals with four legs walked out of the water about 375 million years ago. Humans like us appeared only about 300,000 years ago, in Africa. The crow at your window, the coconut tree, the ant on the floor and you all come from those first cells. You are all very distant cousins.
- e.g. The first animals big enough to see were soft, flat and lived in the sea.
- e.g. Birds come from dinosaurs, so the crow at your window is a living dinosaur.
- e.g. Humans and monkeys are cousins: they come from the same ancestors, millions of years back.

## Mostly agreed
- Life began in water. *How we know:* The oldest signs of life are in rocks that formed in water. Every living being needs water inside their cells, where the chemicals of life meet and react.
- The first life was tiny single cells, and for most of life's story almost all life was tiny cells. *How we know:* The oldest fossils are stromatolites and tiny cells, about 3.5 billion years old. Fossils of animals big enough to see appear only about 575 million years ago.
- The Earth is about 4.5 billion years old. *How we know:* Radioactive rocks act like clocks. In 1956, Clair Patterson measured lead in meteorites and found the Earth is about 4.5 billion years old.
- Every living being today comes from those first cells. *How we know:* All living cells use the same kind of instructions, DNA and RNA, and build themselves from the same kinds of chemicals. Fossils in older and newer rocks show kinds changing over time.

## How we found out
**Lord Kelvin, Henri Becquerel, Marie Curie, Arthur Holmes and Clair Patterson, 1862 to 1956.** For a long time, many people thought the Earth was only a few thousand years old. In 1862, the famous scientist Lord Kelvin worked out how long a hot young Earth would take to cool down. He said it was between 20 and 400 million years old. Later he said only about 20 to 40 million. Charles Darwin was worried. He thought the many kinds of life needed far longer than that to come about. Then, in 1896, Henri Becquerel found that uranium gives off rays. Marie Curie named this radioactivity. Radioactive rock slowly turns into other materials, at a steady speed, like a clock. It also makes heat inside the Earth, which Kelvin did not know about. So his cooling sum was wrong. In 1911, a young British geologist, Arthur Holmes, used this rock clock and found rocks about 1.6 billion years old. In 1956, the American scientist Clair Patterson measured lead in meteorites, rocks that fell from space. He found that the Earth is about 4.5 billion years old. Scientists still use that age today.  
*What people believed before:* That the Earth was young: first a few thousand years, then Kelvin's 20 to 40 million years. Kelvin did not know that radioactive rock heats the Earth. Rock clocks then showed rocks billions of years old, and Patterson's meteorites gave 4.5 billion years.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Life began in the deep sea.** (Many scientists who study the sea; *debated*)  
  For: Hot springs on the deep sea floor pour out warm water full of the chemicals life needs, and their rocks have tiny holes where chemicals can gather.  
  Against: Other scientists point out that long chains like RNA fall apart easily in lots of water. They think pools on land that dry and fill again suit life better.
- **Life came to Earth from space.** (A few scientists, starting with the idea called panspermia; *minority*)  
  For: Some meteorites carry chemicals that living things use. A meteorite that fell in Murchison, Australia, in 1969 held amino acids, which are building blocks of life.  
  Against: No living being has ever been found in a meteorite. And the idea only moves the question: how did life begin wherever it came from?
- **Humans came from monkeys.** (A common mix-up; *rejected*)  
  For: Humans and monkeys have many things in common, such as hands that grip, eyes at the front and a similar skeleton.  
  Against: Monkeys did not turn into humans. Humans and monkeys are cousins. They both come from the same ancestors, who lived millions of years ago and were neither of them.
- **The Earth and its life are only a few thousand years old.** (An old idea, held by many people in the past; *rejected*)  
  For: Long ago, people worked out the age of the world from family lists and old stories.  
  Against: Rock clocks made by radioactivity show rocks billions of years old. Layers of rock and fossils took a very long time to build up.

## Roots
We found that dry powders change only when water lets them meet, and we squeezed the story of life into one year. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your dry soda and citric acid sat together and did nothing. When you added a few drops of water, they fizzed at once. Life's chemicals also need water to meet and change. That is why life began in water, and why every cell in you is still mostly water.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of water as something we drink, and of the sea as a faraway place. But water is where the chemistry of every living being happens. When plants and animals moved onto land, they took water with them, inside every cell.  
*Evidence:* In the dry mix test, the dry bowl and the oil bowl did not fizz. The water and sea water bowls fizzed at once, and the oil bowl fizzed as soon as water reached the powder. The oldest signs of life, stromatolites about 3.5 billion years old, formed in shallow sea water.

- *who am i:* You are mostly water. Each of your cells keeps water inside, where your body's chemicals meet and react.
- *what is life:* Life began as tiny cells in water. Everything alive today, from a crow to a coconut tree to you, comes from those first cells.
- *what is this world:* The Earth was here a very long time before anything big enough to see lived on it. For most of the story, its life was tiny cells.

Before life, the young Earth had rock, water and chemicals, and no living being anywhere. In the water, chemicals met and changed, again and again. Then came the first living cells, and from those few came every kind of being, first in the sea and later on land. Everything is conscious, the smallest cells too: tiny cells today sense food and swim towards it, and the first cells sensed their world as well. One beginning in water became all the life there is now.

- **Rig Veda 10.129.3 (the Nasadiya Sukta, the hymn of creation):** In the beginning, darkness was hidden by darkness. All this was water, with nothing to tell one thing from another. (The old hymn pictures the beginning as water. Science also finds that life began in water. The same hymn later asks who really knows how it all began. - **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1:** From space came air. From air came fire, from fire water, and from water the earth. From the earth came plants, from plants food, and from food the person. (This is a story of beginnings in order, like the Year of Life calendar. The order science finds is different in places: the rocky Earth came before the oceans. But both put a person at the end of a long line that runs through water and plants.)
- **Thales of Miletus, as told by Aristotle in Metaphysics, Book 1, chapter 3:** Thales, a Greek thinker, said that water is where all things come from. Aristotle guessed Thales thought this because food and seeds are always moist. (Thales was wrong that everything is made of water. But he noticed something true: living things need water, and life did begin in it.)

- Science (open): Exactly where in the water life began, and how the first cell formed, is still unknown.
- Other view, Many scientists: They say life began from chemistry and chance alone. In their view, the first cells and the chemicals before them had no awareness. Awareness came much later, with brains.

## Story: The Packet That Waited
*Kavya, Arun and Paati are made up. Fruit salt is a fizzy powder sold at chemists, and it is mostly baking soda and citric acid. Chennai gets heavy rain from October to December.*

It was raining hard in Chennai. Kavya, who was eight, and her little brother Arun, who was six, could not go down to play. Their grandmother, Paati, was in the kitchen, tidying the spice shelf.

Arun found a small packet at the back of the shelf. "What is this, Paati?"

"Fruit salt," said Paati. "Grown-ups stir it into water when their stomach feels sour. That packet has been sitting there since Deepavali."

Arun shook the packet. The powder inside made a soft rattle. "It's just white dust," he said.

"Watch this," said Kavya. She tipped a pinch of the powder into a steel tumbler of water. At once the water hissed and fizzed. Tiny bubbles jumped up to the top. Arun leaned in so close that the bubbles tickled his nose.

"So why didn't it fizz in the packet?" asked Arun. "It just sat there for months."

Kavya opened her mouth to answer. Then she shut it again. She did not know.

Paati smiled. "Let's find out. Fruit salt is mostly two powders mixed together, baking soda and citric acid. I have both in the kitchen." She put four steel katoris on the counter.

Kavya measured half a spoon of baking soda and half a spoon of citric acid into each katori. Arun stirred them with a dry spoon. Nothing happened. He put his ear right down by a katori and listened. Nothing.

"Now each bowl gets something different," said Paati. "Oil in this one, water in this one, sea water in this one. The last one stays dry."

"We don't have any sea water," said Arun.

Kavya stirred a spoon of salt into half a tumbler of water. She let Arun taste one drop from her fingertip. He screwed up his face. "That is about how salty the sea is," she told him.

"First, guess," said Paati. Arun was sure the oil would fizz most, because oil is thick and strong. Kavya thought only the plain water would fizz.

Kavya poured two spoons of oil into the first katori. The oil lay on the powder like a yellow blanket. Not one bubble came. She poured two spoons of water into the next one. It fizzed at once, loud and white. Then the sea water. It fizzed just as hard. The dry katori sat there, still and quiet.

"So the sea is still water," said Kavya. "The salt doesn't stop it."

Then Paati handed her the tumbler of plain water. "Try the oil bowl again." Kavya dripped water into the oil. The drops sank slowly through the yellow oil. The moment they touched the powder, bubbles came climbing up through the oil like a string of tiny beads.

"The powder in the oil was ready all along," said Kavya slowly. "It only needed water."

Paati nodded. "Dry powder is made of grains. In a grain, the tiny pieces stay stuck where they are. Water dissolves them. Then the tiny pieces can move about and meet. When baking soda meets citric acid, they react, and they make the gas in the bubbles."

Arun looked at the fizzing sea water bowl. "Did the sea fizz like this, long ago?"

Paati laughed. "Not like this. But something a bit like it happened, very slowly. Chemicals floated in the water. They met and changed, again and again. After a very long time, some of them became the first living cells. Each one was far too small to see. That is why life began in water."

She took last year's calendar down from the wall and turned it to January. "Let's pretend the whole story of the Earth is one year. On the first of January, the Earth forms, hot and rocky." She turned to March. "At the end of March, we find the first signs of tiny cells in the sea."

Arun turned the pages. April. May. June. July. "Where are the animals?"

"Keep turning," said Paati. August. September. October. Still tiny cells. At last, in the middle of November, came the first animals big enough to see, soft and flat, on the sea floor. Then everything rushed into December: plants and animals on land, dinosaurs, and flowers.

"And us?" asked Arun.

Paati pointed to the last box, the thirty-first of December. "Humans like us come about half an hour before midnight, on the very last night of the year."

Arun was quiet for a moment. "So the tiny cells had the Earth nearly to themselves, almost all year?"

"Most of it," said Paati. "And they are still here, everywhere. In the sea, in the soil, even on your skin."

Kavya looked down at her arm. "Paati, if life began in water, where is my water?"

"Inside you," said Paati. "Every cell in your body is mostly water. That is where your own chemicals meet and react, all day and all night."

Kavya thought about the oil bowl. The powder had waited under the oil, ready, until the water reached it. She thought about the first cells in the old sea, and about the water inside her own hands.

Arun picked up the packet of fruit salt and held it tight in both hands. "I'll keep you dry," he told the packet, "until it's your turn."

**For the parent:** Kavya sees that the powders were ready all along, but could meet and change only in water. Life's chemicals also met in water, long ago, and her own cells still keep that water inside.

- Ask: What happened when Kavya dripped water into the oil bowl, and what did that show?
- Ask: On Paati's calendar, in which month did the first animals big enough to see appear?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Dry Mix: will the powders fizz without water? (25 min)
- **question:** Baking soda and citric acid sit together in a bowl. Do they change on their own, or only when we add a liquid? Which liquids make them change?
- **predict:** We will mix the two dry powders in four bowls. One stays dry. One gets oil. One gets water. One gets salty water, as salty as the sea. Which bowls do you think will fizz? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every bowl gets the same powders: half a spoon of baking soda and half a spoon of citric acid. Every liquid is 2 spoons. The only thing we change is what we add. We watch each bowl for the same time, one minute, so no bowl gets longer to fizz.
- **safety:** Citric acid stings if it gets in the eyes or a cut. Keep hands away from eyes, and if any powder gets in, rinse with plenty of water. Don't eat the powders by the spoonful.
- **record:** Fill in the results table for all four bowls. Draw each bowl: draw bubbles where you saw them. Then draw the oil bowl again after you added water.
- **results table:** Bowl | What we added | My guess: fizz or no fizz? | What happened | My drawing

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Why did the fruit salt in the packet not fizz for months?  
   Options: It was dry, so the powders could not meet / It was too old / The packet was too small  
   **Answer:** It was dry, so the powders could not meet. *Why:* The powders react only when water dissolves them. Picking 'too old' may mean thinking the powder went bad, but a pinch in water still fizzes.
2. (mcq) Kavya added cooking oil to the dry mix. What happened?  
   Options: Nothing, until she added water / It fizzed more than water / It turned yellow and bubbly at once  
   **Answer:** Nothing, until she added water. *Why:* Oil does not dissolve the powders, so they could not meet. When water reached them, they fizzed.
3. (mcq) Where did life on Earth begin?  
   Options: In water / On dry rock / In the air  
   **Answer:** In water. *Why:* The chemicals of life meet and react in water, and the oldest signs of life come from rocks made in sea water.
4. (mcq) What is most of every cell in your body made of?  
   Options: Water / Salt / Sand  
   **Answer:** Water. *Why:* Every cell is mostly water inside. That is where the body's chemicals meet and react.
5. (mcq) What were the first living beings like?  
   Options: Tiny cells, far too small to see / Big fish / Small dinosaurs  
   **Answer:** Tiny cells, far too small to see. *Why:* The first life was single tiny cells. Fish and dinosaurs came billions of years later.
6. (mcq) What is a stromatolite?  
   Options: A rock built in layers by mats of tiny cells / A dinosaur egg / A kind of fish  
   **Answer:** A rock built in layers by mats of tiny cells. *Why:* Mats of tiny cells in shallow water trap sand, then grow up through it, again and again. That builds a layered rock.
7. (mcq) Which of these came first in the story of life: tiny cells, fish or dinosaurs?  
   Options: Tiny cells / Fish / Dinosaurs  
   **Answer:** Tiny cells. *Why:* Tiny cells came about 3.5 billion years ago. Fish came about 500 million years ago, and dinosaurs about 230 million.
8. (mcq) Which of these came last: humans, flowers or dinosaurs?  
   Options: Humans / Flowers / Dinosaurs  
   **Answer:** Humans. *Why:* Humans like us came only about 300,000 years ago. Flowers came about 130 million years ago, and dinosaurs before that.
9. (mcq) If the story of the Earth were one year, when would humans like us appear?  
   Options: About half an hour before midnight on 31 December / On 1 January / In June  
   **Answer:** About half an hour before midnight on 31 December. *Why:* On the Year of Life, one day is about 12 million years. 300,000 years is only about half an hour.
10. (mcq) On the Year of Life calendar, what made up almost all life from April to October?  
   Options: Tiny cells / Dinosaurs / Trees and flowers  
   **Answer:** Tiny cells. *Why:* For most of the story, almost all life was tiny cells in water. Big animals and plants came in November and December.
11. (mcq) Where do all the living beings alive today come from?  
   Options: From the first cells, through a very long line of parents / Each kind appeared suddenly, all at once / From rocks  
   **Answer:** From the first cells, through a very long line of parents. *Why:* Every living being comes from parents, and they from theirs, all the way back to the first cells.
12. (mcq) How did a few kinds of tiny cells become so many kinds of living beings?  
   Options: Small changes added up over a very, very long time / They all changed in one day / People made the new kinds  
   **Answer:** Small changes added up over a very, very long time. *Why:* Children are a little different from their parents. Over thousands and thousands of generations, these small differences add up into new kinds.

## Applications
- Fizzy powders like fruit salt are packed in dry, sealed sachets, so they wait until they meet water.
- Cooks add fruit salt or soda to wet batters, such as dhokla, so the batter fills with bubbles and rises.
- Scientists who look for life on Mars and other worlds look first for signs of water.
- Geologists use radioactive rock clocks to work out how old rocks and fossils are.
- Many medicines are kept in sealed strips, because damp air can make them change before you need them.

## Spotted around us
- A pinch of fruit salt fizzing in a tumbler of water, at home or at a relative's house.
- A fish market in Kochi, Kolkata, Visakhapatnam or Mumbai, with prawns, crabs, squid and many kinds of fish. They are a few of the many kinds that still live in the sea.
- Tree trunks that turned to stone millions of years ago, at the National Fossil Wood Park in Tiruvakkarai, near Puducherry.
- A green slimy film in a rain puddle or on a wet wall in the monsoon. It is made of tiny water-living cells.
- Old stromatolites in the rocks at Jhamarkotra, near Udaipur in Rajasthan.

## Open questions
- **How exactly did the first living cell begin?** Scientists have made some of life's chemicals in labs, and even RNA chains that help join other chains. Nobody has yet made a living cell from chemicals.
- **Where did life begin: at hot springs in the deep sea, in warm pools on land, or somewhere else?** Each idea has some evidence. The rocks from that time are very old and much changed, so they hold few clues.
- **Does life also begin in water on other worlds, such as the salty oceans under the ice of the moon Europa?** Nobody has looked there yet. Space missions are now being sent to find out more about these oceans.

## Connected maths
- On the Year of Life calendar, the first signs of tiny cells come at the end of March. The first animals big enough to see come in the middle of November. Name the months in between, from April to October. How many months is that? **April, May, June, July, August, September and October: 7 months. So for more than 7 months of the year, almost all life was tiny cells.**. Count the months on your fingers, starting with April: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. The ends of March and the start of November add a little more. (queues `math.time.calendar.L2`)
- In the splitting game, Lakshmi starts with 1 cell. Every time she rolls an even number, every cell splits in two. How many cells does she have after 1 split, 2 splits, 3 splits, 4 splits and 5 splits? What pattern do you see? **2, 4, 8, 16 and 32. Each time, the number doubles.**. 1 and 1 more is 2. 2 and 2 is 4. 4 and 4 is 8. 8 and 8 is 16. 16 and 16 is 32. Each number is the last one added to itself. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L2`)
- Nandini, a girl in Bengaluru, did the dry mix test in 3 bowls with water and 3 bowls with oil. Her tally of bowls that fizzed: water |||, oil none. Then she dripped water into the 3 oil bowls, and all of them fizzed. How many bowls fizzed in the end? What does that tell her? **6 bowls fizzed in the end: 3 and 3. It tells her the powder under the oil was ready all along. It only needed water.**. 3 water bowls fizzed first. Then 3 more fizzed when water reached them. 3 + 3 = 6. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L2`)
- In the sea water sprouts test, Bidisha, a girl in Kolkata, put 10 moong in plain water and 10 in sea-salty water. 9 sprouted in plain water and 1 sprouted in sea-salty water. How many more sprouted in plain water? **8 more sprouted in plain water.**. 9 take away 1 is 8. The difference is big, so the salt clearly made a difference, not chance. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
