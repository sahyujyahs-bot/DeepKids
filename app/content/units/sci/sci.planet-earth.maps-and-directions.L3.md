# Maps and directions: how to draw your home so someone else can find their way
`sci.planet-earth.maps-and-directions.L3`

**Summary.** A map is a drawing of a place as if you were looking down on it from above. It does not show colours, smells or sounds, yet it can lead a stranger straight to a hidden coin. It works because it keeps how far apart things are and which way they lie. North, south, east and west are directions fixed to the Earth, so they mean the same for everyone, whichever way they face. You can find east from the morning Sun, and draw a map of your room by counting floor tiles.

**Intro.** *Put a coin on the floor of a room. Can you draw a map so good that someone who did not see you can walk in and put a finger on the coin?* In this unit you draw maps of your room, your home and your street. You find out which way north, south, east and west are in your own flat. You test two maps against each other to see which one finds a hidden coin. And you find out how a desert ant finds her way home by counting her steps.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the four directions, north, south, east and west, and find east and north in my home from the morning Sun or a compass.
1. I can explain why north, south, east and west work for anyone, while left and right change when a person turns.
2. I can draw a room from above, with a key of symbols and an arrow that shows north.
3. I can make a map that keeps distances right by counting tiles or steps, and say why that helps someone find a place.
4. I can turn a map so that it lines up with the room or street I am standing in.
5. I can read a simple map and give directions from one place to another.
6. I can tell how people found out that California is not an island, and how a desert ant finds her way home.

## Terms
- **Map**: A drawing of a place as seen from above, made smaller, that shows where things are. *e.g.* A map of your flat shows each room as a box, with the doors as gaps in the walls.
- **Bird's-eye view**: How a place looks from straight above, the way a bird flying over it would see it. *e.g.* From above, a round steel tumbler looks like a circle, and a bed looks like a rectangle.
- **Direction**: The way something lies or moves from where you are. *e.g.* The kitchen is in the direction of the window.
- **North, south, east and west**: The four main directions. They are fixed to the Earth, so they stay the same however you turn. They are also called the cardinal directions. *e.g.* In Chennai, the sea is to the east. In Kochi, the sea is to the west.
- **East**: The direction where the Sun rises, roughly. *e.g.* The window that gets the first sunlight in the morning faces roughly east.
- **Compass**: A tool with a small magnet needle that turns to point north and south. *e.g.* Many phones also have a compass app.
- **Compass rose**: The drawing on a map that shows which way north, south, east and west are. *e.g.* Most maps have a compass rose or a simple arrow marked N in one corner.
- **Symbol**: A small, simple mark on a map that stands for something real. *e.g.* A small square with a cross can stand for a window.
- **Key**: The box on a map that says what each symbol means. It is also called a legend. *e.g.* The key says that a blue circle means a water tank.
- **Scale**: How much smaller the map is than the real place. It says how far on the ground one square or one centimetre on the map stands for. *e.g.* On a tile map, one square on the paper stands for one floor tile.
- **Landmark**: Something easy to see and remember that helps you find your way. *e.g.* The milk booth at the gate and the big neem tree are landmarks in an apartment complex.
- **Route**: The way you go to get from one place to another. *e.g.* The route from the lift to your front door is: out of the lift, turn towards the window, third door.

## Explanations
### What a map is · *defined*
**Definition:** A map is a drawing of a place from above, made smaller, that shows where things are and how far apart they are.

A photo shows a room from the side, the way your eyes see it. A map shows it from straight above. From above, a bed is a rectangle, a round table is a circle, and a door is a gap in a wall. A map also leaves things out. It does not show the colour of the curtains or the smell from the kitchen. It keeps only what helps someone find their way: what is where, how far apart things are, and which way they lie. That is why a map can be far simpler than a photo and still be more useful for finding a place.
- e.g. A map of a school shows each classroom as a box with its number.
- e.g. A bus route map in Bengaluru shows the stops as dots on a line, and leaves out the houses.
- e.g. The map on a phone shows roads as lines and the place you are as a blue dot.
### North, south, east and west · *defined*
**Definition:** North, south, east and west are four directions fixed to the Earth. North points towards the North Pole, south towards the South Pole. East is where the Sun rises, roughly, and west is where it sets.

The Earth spins like a top. The two ends of the line it spins round are the North Pole and the South Pole. North is the direction towards the North Pole, from wherever you are. South is the opposite. The Earth spins towards the east, so every morning the Sun comes up roughly in the east, and every evening it goes down roughly in the west. In India, the place where the Sun rises shifts a little through the year. It rises a little north of east in June, and a little south of east in December. Between the four main directions are four more: north-east, south-east, south-west and north-west.
- e.g. Kanyakumari is at the southern tip of mainland India.
- e.g. Delhi is north of Chennai.
- e.g. Bengaluru is west of Chennai.
### Finding the directions in your own home · *defined*
**Definition:** In the morning, the Sun is roughly in the east. Stand with your right hand towards it, and you are facing north.

You do not need to go outside. Look at which window gets the Sun early in the morning, between about 7 and 9 o'clock. That side of your flat faces roughly east. Stand with your right arm pointing to that window. Now you are facing north. East is on your right, west is on your left, and south is behind you. A compass gives the same answer at any time of day. Its needle is a small magnet that turns to point north and south. Many phones have a compass app too. Never look straight at the Sun to find it. Look at where its light falls.
- e.g. In a Chennai flat, the balcony that gets the morning Sun faces east, towards the Bay of Bengal.
- e.g. The window that gets the red evening Sun faces roughly west.
- e.g. A compass on a table points north whichever way you turn the table.
### Left and right, or north and south? · *defined*
**Definition:** Left, right, forward and back belong to your body, so they change whenever you turn. North, south, east and west belong to the Earth, so they stay the same whichever way you face.

Say a friend is told, "Walk three tiles forward, then turn right." Where she ends up depends on which way she was facing when she began. If she starts facing a different wall, she ends up in a different place. Now say she is told, "Walk three tiles towards the east wall, then two tiles towards the south wall." She ends up in the same place whichever way she started. That is why maps use north, south, east and west. A map is read by many people, each standing a different way. Left and right are still useful when you walk with someone, because you both face the same way.
- e.g. A sailor at sea says the ship is heading north-east, not left.
- e.g. In the Guugu Yimithirr language of north-east Australia, people say "the cup is north of the plate" instead of left or right.
- e.g. Bus drivers in a city still say "take the second left", because they know which way you face.
### Symbols, a key and a north arrow · *defined*
**Definition:** A map uses symbols for real things, a key to say what each symbol means, and an arrow or compass rose to show which way is north.

Drawing every leaf of a tree would take hours, so a map uses a symbol, such as a small circle. The key in one corner lists every symbol and its meaning, so any reader can understand them. The north arrow tells the reader how to turn the map. Hold the map so its north arrow points to the real north. Now everything on the map lies the same way as the real things around you. This is called setting, or turning, the map. Most maps put north at the top, but that is only a custom. Al-Idrisi, a mapmaker in Sicily, drew a famous map of the world in 1154 with south at the top.
- e.g. On Indian railway maps, a small train symbol often marks a station.
- e.g. On a map of a temple, a small drawn tower can mark each gopuram.
- e.g. In a phone map, the blue dot is a symbol for you.
### Scale: keeping distances right · *defined*
**Definition:** Scale is how much smaller a map is than the real place. On a good map, every distance is shrunk by the same amount.

Many Indian flats have square floor tiles. If you draw one square on squared paper for each tile, the room on paper is smaller, but every distance shrinks by the same amount. A bed four tiles long becomes four squares long. A gap of two tiles becomes two squares. So the shape of the room stays right. If a map shrinks some things more than others, the shape goes wrong, and a reader looking for a spot will miss it. With no tiles, you can count your own steps, heel to toe.
- e.g. A tile map: one square on paper stands for one floor tile.
- e.g. A map of India in an atlas may say 1 centimetre stands for 100 kilometres.
- e.g. Nain Singh Rawat mapped the road to Lhasa by counting his steps on a string of 100 beads.
### How a desert ant finds her way home · *defined*
**Definition:** A desert ant finds her way home by keeping track of her direction, using the Sun, and of how far she has walked, by counting her steps.

Cataglyphis ants live in the Sahara desert, where the ground can be hotter than 50 degrees. A worker ant must go far from her nest alone to find dead insects to eat, and get back fast before the heat kills her. Smell trails do not help much on hot, windy sand. So the ant keeps track of two things. She uses the Sun to know which way she is going. And she counts her steps to know how far she has gone. Then she runs straight home. In 2006, scientists in Germany and Switzerland gave some ants tiny stilts. Those ants walked past their nest, because their longer steps carried them further than they had counted.
- e.g. An ant with stilts overshot her nest.
- e.g. An ant with shortened legs stopped before she reached her nest.
- e.g. Both results show the ant was counting steps, not just looking for her nest.

## Mostly agreed
- North, south, east and west are fixed to the Earth. North points towards the North Pole. *How we know:* A compass needle points the same way whichever way you turn its box, and sailors and pilots everywhere use the same directions.
- The Sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west, because the Earth spins towards the east. *How we know:* Anyone can watch which window gets the morning Sun and which gets the evening Sun, every day of the year.
- A map that keeps all distances shrunk by the same amount lets a reader find places on the real ground. *How we know:* A tile map, with one square for each floor tile, leads a reader to the right tile. Surveyors map whole countries the same way, by measuring.
- Maps can be wrong, and the only way to check a map is to compare it with the real place. *How we know:* For about a hundred years, European maps showed California as an island, until travellers walked round the top of the gulf.
- Many animals find their way without maps, using the Sun, smells, landmarks and counting steps. *How we know:* Desert ants on stilts walked past their nest, which shows they measure distance by counting steps.

## How we found out
**Francisco de Ulloa, a Spanish sailor, and Eusebio Kino, a priest born in Italy who worked in Mexico, 1539 (Ulloa's voyage), 1622 (an early island map), 1701 to 1705 (Kino's journey and map), 1747 (the Spanish king's order).** In 1539, a Spanish sailor named Francisco de Ulloa sailed up the long gulf west of Mexico. At its top, he found that the water ended in land. So California was joined to the rest of North America. But in the early 1600s, some mapmakers in Europe began to draw California as a huge island. A map printed in Amsterdam in 1622 showed it this way. Other mapmakers copied it, often without checking, and the island spread from map to map for about a hundred years. Then Eusebio Kino, a priest born in Italy, travelled for years by horse and on foot across what is now northern Mexico and Arizona. In 1701, from near the place where the Gila River joins the Colorado River, he saw the land carry on, unbroken, to the west. There was no sea there. His map showing California joined to the mainland was printed in Paris in 1705. Even so, some maps kept the island. In 1747, King Ferdinand VI of Spain declared that California is not an island.  
*What people believed before:* Mapmakers in Europe drew California as an island for about a hundred years, copying one another. Kino went there over land and saw that the land was joined to the mainland. A map is only as good as the checking behind it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **North is up, towards the sky.** (A common idea among children, because maps on a wall have north at the top; *rejected*)  
  For: On most maps, north is at the top, and wall maps hang with the top up.  
  Against: North is a direction along the ground, towards the North Pole. Lay a map flat on the floor, turn it to match the room, and north points to a wall, not the ceiling.
- **The Sun rises exactly in the east every day.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun always rises on the eastern side, so it is a fine rule for finding east.  
  Against: In India, the Sun rises a little north of east in June and a little south of east in December. It rises exactly east only around 20 March and 23 September.
- **A good map shows everything in a place.** (A common first idea when children draw maps; *rejected*)  
  For: A map with more things on it seems to tell you more.  
  Against: A map with everything on it would be crowded and hard to read. A good map shows what the reader needs, and a key explains it.
- **A compass needle points exactly to the North Pole.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: A compass does point roughly north everywhere in India.  
  Against: The needle points to the magnetic north pole, which is some distance from the North Pole and slowly moves. In India the difference is small, so a compass is good enough for a home map.

## Roots
We have drawn maps of a room, a home and a street, and tested which map finds a hidden coin. Now we ask what a map tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your tile map had no colours, no cushions and no smells. Yet it led someone straight to the coin. It kept only how far apart things are, and which way they lie. So a map can be true about a place while looking nothing like it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a true picture must look like the thing it shows, like a photo. But a map is true in a different way. It keeps the distances and directions between things, and drops almost everything else.  
*Evidence:* In the coin test, the plain tile map led the finder to the right tile, while the memory map, with its guessed distances, was tiles off. In the child-and-grown-up test, two maps of different sizes had the same shape, and both were right.

- *who am i:* You carry a map of your home inside you. That is how you find the bathroom in the dark.
- *what is life:* A desert ant keeps her own map by counting her steps and watching the Sun. That is how she gets home alive across hot sand.
- *what is this world:* The ground has no lines for borders or street names. People draw those, but the hills and rivers were there first.

One room can have many true maps: your tile map, your grandmother's map in her head, an ant's count of steps, a plumber's plan of the pipes. They look nothing alike. Each one keeps a different part of how the room is arranged, and each one works for the one who uses it. The room is one, and the maps are many.

- **Alfred Korzybski, Science and Sanity (1933), page 58:** A map is not the land it shows. But if the map is correct, it has the same arrangement as the land, and that is why it is useful. (This is what the coin test showed. The tile map was not the room, but every distance on it matched the room's arrangement. That is why it found the coin.)
- **Jorge Luis Borges, 'On Exactitude in Science' (1946):** In this very short story, mapmakers in an empire made a map as big as the empire itself, matching it point for point. The next people found it useless and left it to rot in the desert. (A map helps because it is smaller than the place and leaves things out. Your tile map fitted on one page, so you could see the whole room at once.)
- **Majjhima Nikaya 22, the Alagaddupama Sutta (the parable of the raft):** The Buddha said his teaching is like a raft for crossing a river. When you reach the other bank, you do not carry the raft on your head. You leave it and walk on. (A map is the same kind of tool. It is for getting somewhere, such as from the door to the coin. Once Thatha knew the way home, he hardly needed the map.)
- Science (settled): In 1971, John O'Keefe found 'place cells' in rats' brains, which fire when the rat is in one particular spot. In 2005, May-Britt Moser and Edvard Moser found 'grid cells', which fire in a grid pattern as the rat moves. The three shared the Nobel Prize in 2014.
- Science (settled): Desert ants measure distance by counting steps. Ants given stilts walked past their nest.
- Science (open): How signals from these cells become the feeling of knowing where you are is not known.
- Other view, Many mapmakers and geographers: No map is simply true. Every map leaves things out, and the mapmaker chooses what. A city map for cars can leave out the footpaths that walkers need. So instead of asking whether a map is true, we should ask what it is for and whom it leaves out.
- Practice, Walk the map with your eyes closed (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Picture your bed. Now picture walking from your bed to the kitchen. Count the steps in your head. Which way do you turn at each door? Open your eyes. Now walk it for real and count. Was the map in your head right?
- Ask: Your tile map looked nothing like a photo of the room, yet it worked. What makes a map true?
- Ask: You found the bathroom in the dark without a paper map. Where is the map you used?
- Ask: If an ant, a bird and you each map the same building, whose map is the real one?

## Story: Thatha and the Six Towers
*The story is made up. Everything about the map is true: a map works when it is turned to match the ground, and when its distances are counted, not guessed.*

Meenakshi's Thatha came to stay in the summer holidays. He had lived all his life in a village near Tirunelveli, where every lane had a name he knew and every neighbour knew him. Now he was in Chennai, in a flat on the fifth floor of Tower D.

There were six towers in the complex, A to F. They were all the same height and the same cream colour, with the same blue railings on every balcony.

On the first morning, Thatha went down to buy milk from the booth at the gate. He did not come back for an hour. When he finally rang the bell, his forehead was wet and he was holding the milk packet very tightly.

"I went into Tower B," he said. "Then Tower E. Every tower looks the same. Every lift looks the same. A watchman had to bring me here."

Meenakshi was eight, and she was good at drawing. "I will make you a map, Thatha," she said.

She sat at the dining table and drew six boxes for the six towers. She drew the gate and the milk booth. She drew the round water tank and the small Pillaiyar temple near the gate. She wrote D in big letters on their tower and drew a star on it.

The next morning, Thatha went down with the map in his shirt pocket. This time he was gone for forty minutes.

"Your map is beautiful, kanna," he said, sitting down heavily. "But when I came out of the milk booth, the temple was on my left. On your map it was on the right. So I walked the wrong way."

Meenakshi looked at the map. She had drawn the gate at the bottom of the page. But Thatha had come back in from the gate, facing the towers. When he held the map with the gate at the bottom, he was fine. When he turned round, the map did not turn with him.

"Left and right change when you turn," she said slowly. "I need something on the map that does not change."

Her mother was cutting vegetables in the kitchen. "What about the Sun?" she said. "In the morning, our kitchen window is full of sunlight. That side faces east."

So Meenakshi stood at the kitchen window. The Sun was pouring in over the towers, so she looked at the patch of light on the floor, not at the Sun. Then she went back to her map. She worked out where their kitchen was on Tower D, and which side of the complex was the sunny side. She drew a big arrow on the map and wrote E at its tip. Then she drew a cross with N, S, E and W.

"Thatha," she said, "tomorrow, when you come out of the milk booth, look for the Sun. Turn the map until the E arrow points at the sunny side. Then everything on the map will be on the same side as the real thing."

The next morning, Thatha was back in twenty minutes. "The arrow worked," he said. "I turned the map, and the temple was on the same side as on the paper. But then I walked too far. On your map, Tower D is right next to the water tank. In real life it is much further."

Meenakshi frowned. She had drawn the water tank as big as a tower, and squeezed the towers close together so they would fit on the page. She had guessed every distance.

That evening, she and her mother walked from the gate to Tower D. Meenakshi counted her steps. From the gate to the temple was 20 steps. From the temple to the water tank was 60 steps. From the water tank to Tower D was 90 steps. She wrote every number in her notebook.

At home, she drew a new map on squared paper from her maths notebook. One square was ten of her steps. The temple was 2 squares from the gate. The water tank was 6 squares further. Tower D was 9 squares beyond that. Now the map looked strange. The towers were small, and there was a lot of empty paper between them. But every gap was the right size compared with every other gap.

"It looks emptier," said her mother.

"It is more true," said Meenakshi.

On the fourth morning, Thatha came out of the milk booth. He found the Sun, turned the map until the E arrow pointed at it, and set off. Past the temple. A long walk to the water tank, just as long as on the map. Then a longer walk to the tower with the star. He rang the bell after nine minutes, and he was smiling.

"I did not need the watchman today," he said. He took a pencil from his pocket and drew a small circle on the map, on the path between the water tank and Tower D.

"What is that?" asked Meenakshi.

"The neem tree with the bench in the shade," said Thatha. "Tomorrow, I am going to sit there and read the newspaper. Now I know how to get back."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Meenakshi's first map failed twice for two different reasons. First, it used left and right, which change when the reader turns. An east arrow fixed that, because east is the same for everyone, and the reader can turn the map to match the ground. Second, the distances were guessed, so they were out of proportion. Counting steps fixed that, because every distance was shrunk by the same amount. A map that is turned the right way and has true distances can guide someone who has never been there.

- Ask: Why did Thatha walk the wrong way the second morning, even with the map?
- Ask: How did the E arrow help him, and how did Meenakshi find which way east was?
- Ask: Why did the counted map look emptier than the first one?
- Ask: Thatha added a neem tree to the map. What would you add to a map of your building?

## Experiments
### e1 · Memory Map or Tile Map: which one finds the hidden coin? (35 min)
- **question:** Does a map drawn by counting floor tiles lead someone closer to a hidden coin than a map drawn from memory?
- **predict:** You will draw two maps of the same room: one from memory, and one by counting tiles. Which one will lead a family member closer to a hidden coin? Or will both be just as good? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same room, the same finder and the same things drawn on both maps. Hide the coin in open floor, at least two tiles away from any furniture, so the finder cannot just look for 'next to the bed'. Use each map twice, taking turns, so that practice does not help one map more than the other. Only the way the map was made changes.
- **safety:** Clear toys and loose things off the floor first, so no one trips while counting tiles.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then add up the tiles off for each map. The smaller total is the better map.
- **results table:** Round | Which map | Where the coin really was (tiles from two walls) | Where the finder put the chit | Tiles off
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sit outside the room, with the door closed. 2. Draw the room from above on the first sheet, from memory. 3. Draw the walls, the door, the window and the big furniture. 4. Do not count anything.  
  *Parent says:* First, draw this room from memory, as a bird would see it from the ceiling. Don't go in and look. Just draw what you remember.  
  *Child mode:* Sit outside the room. Draw it from above, from memory. No counting!
  - Note: Most children draw the furniture too big and the empty floor too small. Do not correct it. That is part of the test.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go into the room. 2. Count the tiles along one long wall and one short wall. 3. On the second sheet, draw the walls with one square for each tile. 4. Count tiles from the walls to each piece of furniture, and draw it the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now we draw a second map of the same room. This time, one tile on the floor is one square on the paper. Count every tile.  
  *Child mode:* Count the tiles along the wall. One tile is one square. Draw each thing where you count it.
  - Note: Half tiles at the wall edges can be drawn as half squares. Small rooms often have 20 to 40 tiles along a wall if the tiles are small. If your tiles are big, the room may be only 5 or 6 tiles long. Both work.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Which map will lead the finder closer to the coin? Or will they be the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which map will work better? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that map will work better? (good: Because I counted, so the places are right.; Because the memory map has the bed too big.; Because both show the bed and the door, so both will work.)
  - Note: Any reason is fine. Do not say what you expect.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The finder waits outside. 2. Your child puts the coin on a tile in open floor, away from furniture. 3. They mark an X on the memory map and secretly write the true spot, as tiles from two walls. 4. They pick up the coin. 5. The finder comes in with the memory map and puts the chit where the X shows. 6. Put the coin back on its true tile and count the tiles between the chit and the coin. 7. Do the same with a new spot and the tile map. 8. Do one more round with each map.  
  *Parent says:* Now the finder comes in with only the map. No hints and no pointing. When the chit is down, we count how many tiles off it is.  
  *Child mode:* Hide the coin. Mark an X. Pick the coin up. Let the finder use the map. How many tiles off?
  - Note: Usually the tile map puts the chit on the right tile or the next one. The memory map is often 2 to 4 tiles off, because some things were drawn too big. If both maps land close, choose a spot further from all furniture and try again.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: What I think: the tile map is better for finding things. What I saw: the tile map was 0 and 1 tiles off, and the memory map was 3 and 2 tiles off. Why: on the tile map, every distance was shrunk by the same amount, so the X was in the right place compared with the walls.
  - Check: Why did the tile map lead the finder to the right spot? → **Every distance was shrunk by the same amount, so the X sat in the right place**
### e2 · Left-Right or East-West: which directions still work when you face another way? (20 min)
- **question:** If someone starts facing a different way, do 'forward and turn right' directions still lead to the coin? Do 'east and south' directions?
- **predict:** You will follow two sets of directions to a coin, starting each time facing a different wall. Which set will find the coin every time? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Start on the same tile each time, and walk the same number of tiles. Use tiles, not steps, because people's steps are different sizes. Only the way you face at the start changes.
- **safety:** Clear the floor first. Walk, don't run, and keep away from sharp corners.
- **record:** Fill in the table with a tick or a cross. Count the ticks for each set.
- **results table:** Start facing | Left-right directions: found the coin? | East-west directions: found the coin?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the cushion on a tile. 2. Stand on it facing east, and put the coin 3 tiles forward and 2 tiles to the right. 3. Write chit A: 'Walk 3 tiles forward. Turn right. Walk 2 tiles.' 4. Write chit B: 'Walk 3 tiles towards the east wall. Then walk 2 tiles towards the south wall.'  
  *Parent says:* We will write two sets of directions to the same coin. One uses forward and right. The other uses east and south.  
  *Child mode:* Two chits. Both lead to the coin. One says right. One says east and south.
  - Note: Check the coin's place. Facing east, your right hand points south. So '2 tiles right' and '2 tiles towards the south wall' are the same tiles.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will start facing a different wall each time. Which chit will find the coin every time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which chit will always work? Why do you think so?
  - Note: Many children think both will work, because both were right the first time.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick a folded 'face' chit. 2. Stand on the cushion facing that wall. 3. Follow chit A and put a finger on the tile where you stop. 4. Go back to the cushion, face the same wall, and follow chit B. 5. Fill in the table. 6. Do all four 'face' chits.  
  *Parent says:* Pick a chit to see which wall to face. Follow chit A. Did you land on the coin? Now chit B.  
  *Child mode:* Pick a chit. Face that wall. Follow A. Then follow B. Coin?
  - Note: Chit B finds the coin all four times. Chit A finds it only when you start facing east, the way the writer faced.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the results.  
  *Parent says:* Chit A worked only once. Chit B worked every time. Why do you think that is?  
  *Child mode:* Chit A worked once. Chit B always worked. Why?
  - Check: Why did chit B find the coin from every start? → **East and south stay the same whichever way you face**
### e3 · Child Steps, Grown-up Steps: do two maps of one room come out the same shape? (20 min)
- **question:** If a child and a grown-up each map the same room using their own steps, will the two maps have the same shape?
- **predict:** A grown-up's foot is longer than yours. Will a grown-up's map of the room be the same shape as yours, or a different shape? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both people walk heel to toe, with the heel touching the toe each time, along the same two walls. Each person counts twice and takes the closer count. Each map uses one square for one step.
- **safety:** Walk slowly heel to toe, and hold the wall if you wobble. A grown-up handles the scissors with a younger child.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Cut out both rectangles and stick them in your notebook, one on top of the other, sharing one corner.
- **results table:** Who | Long wall (steps) | Short wall (steps) | Map size (squares)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will both measure this room in our own footsteps. My feet are bigger. Will our maps be the same shape? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Will the two maps be the same shape? Why do you think so?
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The child walks heel to toe along the long wall and counts. 2. Then along the short wall. 3. The grown-up does the same. 4. Write all four numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Heel touching toe, every step. Count out loud. Then it's my turn.  
  *Child mode:* Walk heel to toe along the wall. Count every step.
  - Note: Example: a child counts 18 steps by 14, and a grown-up 13 by 10. The numbers are different, but each long wall is about 1 and a third times its short wall.
- **s3 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Each person draws their room as a rectangle on squared paper, one square for each step. 2. Cut out both rectangles. 3. Put the small one on the big one, sharing the bottom-left corner. 4. Lay the ruler from that corner through the far corner of the big rectangle.  
  *Parent says:* Let's cut out both maps and put one on the other. Does the ruler pass through the corner of the small map too?  
  *Child mode:* Cut out both. Put the small one on the big one. Lay the ruler corner to corner.
  - Note: If the two shapes are the same, the ruler line passes through the far corner of the small rectangle as well. It will be close, not perfect, because steps are never exactly equal.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the result.  
  *Parent says:* Our numbers were different. Is one of our maps wrong?  
  *Child mode:* Your numbers were different. Is one map wrong?
  - Check: A child's map of a room is bigger on paper than a grown-up's. Both used one square per step. Why? → **The child's steps are shorter, so the room took more of them**

## Activities
### a1 · The View From Above: what do things look like to a bird? (10 min)
- **safety:** If your child stands on a chair to look down, a grown-up holds the chair steady.
- **record:** Draw each thing twice: from the side and from above. Circle the drawing that would go on a map.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the five things on a low table. 2. Ask your child to sit at table height and draw them as they look from the side.  
  *Parent says:* Draw these five things the way you see them now, from the side.  
  *Child mode:* Sit low. Draw the five things as you see them.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to stand and look straight down at the table. 2. Draw the five things again, from above.  
  *Parent says:* Now stand up and look straight down, like a crow on the ceiling. Draw them again. What changed?  
  *Child mode:* Stand up. Look straight down. Draw them again.
  - Note: From above, the tumbler is a circle, the book a rectangle, the plate a circle, and the banana a curved strip. The tumbler's height disappears.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the two drawings.  
  *Parent says:* Which drawing shows how far apart the things are? Which one would help someone find the banana on the table?  
  *Child mode:* Which drawing shows where things are? Why?
  - Check: From straight above, what shape is a steel tumbler? → **A circle**
  - Think: What would your whole flat look like from straight above?
### a2 · Find the Four Directions in Your Flat (15 min)
- **safety:** Never look straight at the Sun, even for a moment. Look only at where its light falls on the floor or the wall.
- **record:** Draw a box for the room, mark which wall is N, S, E and W, and draw the window that gets the morning Sun.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the morning, between about 7 and 9 o'clock, find the window or balcony where sunlight comes in. 2. If it is later in the day, use a compass, or ask the family which side gets the morning Sun.  
  *Parent says:* The Sun rises in the east. Where is the morning sunlight coming in? Don't look at the Sun. Look at the patch of light.  
  *Child mode:* Find the sunny window. Don't look at the Sun. Look at its light.
  - Note: In India the Sun rises a little north or south of east, depending on the month, so this gives you rough east. That is fine for a home map. On a cloudy day, use a compass or a phone compass app.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand in the middle of the room with your right arm pointing to the sunny side. 2. You are now facing north. 3. Stick E on the sunny wall and N on the wall in front. Stick W on your left and S behind you.  
  *Parent says:* Point your right arm at the sunny side. That is east. Now you face north. Where are south and west?  
  *Child mode:* Right arm to the sun side. That is east. You face north.
  - Note: If the walls do not line up exactly with the directions, stick each label on the wall that is closest.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check with a compass if you have one. 2. Go round the flat. Which way does the front door face? Which way does the balcony face?  
  *Parent says:* Let's check with the compass. Does the needle agree with our labels? Now, which way does our front door face?  
  *Child mode:* Check with a compass. Which way does the front door face?
  - Check: You stand with your right arm pointing east. Which way are you facing? → **North**
### a3 · Map of Our Home, with a Key (25 min)
- **safety:** No special safety needs.
- **record:** Keep the map. You will use it in the Map for a Visitor project.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Walk through the home together. 2. In each room, ask your child which wall is next to which room.  
  *Parent says:* Let's walk through our home. Which room is next to the kitchen? Which door leads to the balcony?  
  *Child mode:* Walk through your home. What room is next to what?
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw each room as a box, in the right place next to the others. 2. Show doors as gaps. 3. Draw small symbols for a few things: the fridge, beds, taps, the pooja shelf, the TV.  
  *Parent says:* Draw each room as a box. Make the big rooms bigger. Leave a gap where each door is.  
  *Child mode:* Draw each room as a box. Doors are gaps.
  - Note: Help your child keep the rooms in the right order. Exact sizes do not matter here. The tile map in the experiment is for exact sizes.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make a key box in one corner. 2. Draw each symbol in the key and write what it means. 3. Draw a north arrow, using the labels from the Find the Four Directions activity.  
  *Parent says:* Now the key. Draw every symbol again in this box, and write what it means. Then add the north arrow.  
  *Child mode:* Make a key. What does each symbol mean? Add a north arrow.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the map on someone.  
  *Parent says:* Give the map to Amma. Can she find the pooja shelf using only the map and the key?  
  *Child mode:* Can someone find a thing using only your map?
  - Check: What is a key on a map for? → **To say what each symbol means**
### a4 · Directions in Our Languages (10 min)
- **safety:** No special safety needs.
- **record:** Write a table: English, and the words in one or two other languages. Add one place in India in each direction from your city.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the words below together. 2. Ask grandparents or neighbours for the words in your family's languages. Tamil: vadakku (north), therku (south), kizhakku (east), merku (west). Malayalam: vadakku, thekku, kizhakku, padinjaru. Hindi: uttar, dakshin, purab, paschim.  
  *Parent says:* In Tamil, east is kizhakku. In Hindi it is purab. What do we call east at home?  
  *Child mode:* Learn the four directions in another language.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at a map of India together, on paper or a phone. 2. Find your city. 3. Name one place to the north, south, east and west of it.  
  *Parent says:* Find our city on the map. What is to the south of us? What is to the east?  
  *Child mode:* Find your city. What is north of it? What is south?
  - Check: Kanyakumari is at the southern tip of mainland India. Which direction is Kanyakumari from Chennai? → **South**
### a5 · The Street from the Balcony (20 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up stays on the balcony. The child stays well away from the railing edge and never climbs on anything.
- **record:** Draw the street from above, with the road, buildings, trees and shops. Add a north arrow and a key.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go to a balcony or a window that looks over the street. 2. Ask what your child can see: the road, other buildings, trees, a shop, a temple, parked vehicles.  
  *Parent says:* Look down at the street. What can you see? Which things will still be there tomorrow?  
  *Child mode:* Look down. What do you see? What stays there every day?
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the road as two long lines. 2. Draw the buildings as boxes and trees as circles. 3. Leave out things that move, such as autos, cows and people.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the road first. Then the buildings as boxes and the trees as circles. Do we draw the autos?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the road. Draw buildings as boxes. Trees as circles.
  - Note: Things that move are left off maps, because they will not be there tomorrow. Landmarks that stay, such as a temple or a big tree, matter most.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add a north arrow. 2. Add a key.  
  *Parent says:* Which way does the street run? From north to south, or from east to west?  
  *Child mode:* Add a north arrow and a key.
  - Check: Why do we leave the parked autos off a street map? → **They move, so they will not be there tomorrow**
### a6 · Turn the Map: make the map match the room (10 min)
- **safety:** No special safety needs.
- **record:** Write one sentence: what happens to the rooms on the map when its arrow points the real north?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand in the hall holding the map with the writing the right way up. 2. Point to the kitchen on the map, then to the real kitchen. 3. Now turn the map until its N arrow points to the N wall. 4. Point again.  
  *Parent says:* Point to the kitchen on the map. Now point to the real kitchen. Same way? Now turn the map until its arrow points north.  
  *Child mode:* Point to the kitchen on the map. Now to the real one.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Go to another room and do it again.  
  *Parent says:* Now we are in the bedroom. Turn the map until the arrow points north. Where is the balcony?  
  *Child mode:* In another room, turn the map. Where is the balcony?
  - Check: Meera holds a map. The map's north arrow points to her left, but real north is in front of her. What should she do? → **Turn the map until its arrow points in front of her**

## Projects
### p1 · A Map for a Visitor: from the gate to our front door (60 min, 3 days)
- **safety:** A grown-up walks with the child the whole time outside the flat. Stay on footpaths and away from moving vehicles in the parking area.
- **record:** Keep the counting notes and the map. After the test, write what the visitor found hard, and one change you made.
- **schedule:** day 1: Walk from the main gate to your front door with a grown-up. Count steps between landmarks and note the turns. (20 min); day 2: Draw the route as a map, with one square for every 10 steps, a key and a north arrow. (25 min); day 3: Give the map to someone who has not walked the route, and walk behind them as they follow it. (15 min)
- **s1 (20 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start at the main gate of your building or complex. 2. Walk to your front door. 3. Count steps between landmarks: the gate, the watchman's cabin, a tree, the lift, the stairs. 4. Write each count and each turn.  
  *Parent says:* We will walk from the gate to our door. Count your steps out loud between each landmark. I will write them down.  
  *Child mode:* Walk from the gate. Count steps between landmarks.
- **s2 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the route on squared paper, with one square for every 10 steps. 2. Draw each landmark with a symbol. 3. Add a key, a north arrow and the floor number.  
  *Parent says:* Now we draw it. Ten steps is one square. Where does the path turn? Which way is north?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the route. Ten steps is one square. Add a key and a north arrow.
  - Note: Round the counts to the nearest 10. For example, 47 steps is about 5 squares. If there is a lift, write the floor number in a box at the lift.
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give the map to someone who has not walked the route, such as a cousin, an aunt or a neighbour. 2. Walk behind them without helping. 3. Note where they stop or go wrong.  
  *Parent says:* Let's see if your map works. I will not help. Watch where she gets stuck.  
  *Child mode:* Give your map to a visitor. Don't help! Where did she get stuck?
  - Check: Your visitor got lost at the lift, because two lifts were side by side. What is the best fix for your map? → **Add a symbol for each lift and mark which one to take**

## Challenges
### c1 · A Map with No Words (25 min)
- **problem:** Make a map of your home that leads a person to the fridge, the balcony and the bathroom, without a single written word. Someone who cannot read your language must be able to use it.
- **your turn:** Now design your own wordless map of a place you know well, such as a temple, a park or your grandparents' home, and test it on someone.
- **constraints:** No letters or words anywhere on the map, not even in the key.; The map must still show which way north is.; Test it on someone and change it if they get stuck.
- **general:** Use pictures as symbols: a small drawing of a fridge, a plant pot for the balcony, a tap for the bathroom.; Show north with a picture of a rising Sun on the east side instead of the letter N.
- **creative:** Use colours as a code: blue for water places, green for plants, yellow for light.; Draw footprints along the route, and a big dot where the walk begins.; Draw each door as the shape of the real door handle.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem and talk about how to show things without words.  
  *Parent says:* No words at all. How can we show the fridge? How can we show which way is east?  
  *Child mode:* No words! How will you show the fridge? And east?
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the rooms. 2. Draw a picture symbol for each place. 3. Show the east side with a rising Sun. 4. Draw the route from the front door.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the rooms and the picture symbols. How will the reader know which way to turn the map?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the rooms. Draw picture symbols. Show the east side.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Test the map on someone. 2. Read the usual and creative ideas. 3. Improve the map.  
  *Parent says:* Let's test it. Then look at the other ideas. Would any make your map better?  
  *Child mode:* Test it. Then try another idea.
  - Check: Why does a wordless map still need something that shows a direction, like a rising Sun on the east side? → **So the reader can turn the map to match the real place**
### c2 · Steer the Toy Car: directions on a tile grid (25 min)
- **problem:** Write the shortest set of direction instructions that moves a toy car from one corner of a tiled room to a coin, going round two obstacles. Use only directions and numbers, such as '3 tiles east'.
- **your turn:** Now design your own obstacle course and challenge your family to write the shortest route.
- **constraints:** Only north, south, east and west, and numbers of tiles. No left or right.; The car moves one tile at a time and cannot pass through an obstacle.; Someone else must follow your instructions without your help.
- **general:** Draw the room as a grid first, with the car, the obstacles and the coin, then plan the route on paper.; Count each straight stretch and write it as one instruction.
- **creative:** Find two different routes and see which has fewer instructions.; Add a 'fuel' rule: each instruction costs one coin, so the cheapest route wins.; Swap roles: let your parent write the route and you follow it.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the car on a corner tile, the coin on another tile, and two obstacles between them. 2. Draw the room as a grid on paper.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the car, here is the coin. Draw a grid of the floor with everything on it.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the floor as a grid. Mark the car, the coin and the cushions.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Plan the route on the grid. 2. Write it as instructions, such as '4 tiles north, 2 tiles east'. 3. Count the instructions.  
  *Parent says:* Plan the route on paper. How few instructions can you use?  
  *Child mode:* Plan a route. Write it as tiles and directions.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Give the instructions to someone else. 2. They move the car without your help. 3. Did it reach the coin? Read the other ideas and improve the route.  
  *Parent says:* Hand over your instructions. No helping! Did the car reach the coin?  
  *Child mode:* Give your route to someone. No helping. Did it work?
  - Check: A route says '2 tiles north, 3 tiles east, 2 tiles south'. Which shorter route reaches the same tile? → **3 tiles east**

## Worksheets
### Map Maker (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (fill) The Sun rises roughly in the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['east']. *Why:* The Earth spins towards the east, so the Sun comes up on the eastern side every morning.
2. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Map → a drawing of a place from above; Key → says what each symbol means; Symbol → a small mark that stands for a real thing; Compass → a tool whose needle points north; Landmark → something easy to see that helps you find your way. *Why:* These five words come up all through the unit.
3. (mcq) You face north. Which direction is on your right?  
   Options: West / East / South  
   **Answer:** East. *Why:* Facing north, east is on your right and west on your left. Stand up and try it with the wall labels.
4. (draw) Draw a steel plate, a pencil and a cup on a table, as a bird would see them from straight above.  
   **Answer:** A circle for the plate, a long thin rectangle for the pencil, and a smaller circle for the cup.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows only the shapes you see from above, with no sides or legs.
5. (truefalse) On a map, things that move, such as autos and cows, are usually left out.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A map should still be right tomorrow, so it shows things that stay, such as roads, buildings and big trees.
6. (sort) Sort these. Would each one go on a map of your street, or be left off?  
   **Answer:** the temple → On the map; a parked auto → Left off; the big neem tree → On the map; a crow on a wire → Left off; the bus stop → On the map; a vegetable cart passing by → Left off. *Why:* A map keeps things that stay in one place. A strong sorter asks: will it still be there tomorrow?
7. (write) Kavya's right arm points to the window where the morning Sun comes in. Which wall is she facing? Explain how you know.  
   **Answer:** She faces the north wall. The morning Sun is in the east, so her right arm points east. When east is on your right, you face north.. *Why:* A strong answer starts from the Sun being in the east and works out the rest.
8. (mcq) Why should you never look straight at the Sun to find east?  
   Options: It can hurt your eyes / The Sun moves away / It is not polite  
   **Answer:** It can hurt your eyes. *Why:* Sunlight is strong enough to damage your eyes. Look at where its light falls on the floor or wall instead.
9. (order) Put these in order, going clockwise round a compass, starting at north.  
   **Answer:** ['north', 'east', 'south', 'west']. *Why:* Clockwise from north: north, east, south, west. Some children remember it as 'never eat soggy wheat'.
10. (write) In the coin test, which map led the finder closer to the coin: the memory map or the tile map? Use your own numbers to explain.  
   **Answer:** The tile map. For example: the tile map was 0 and 1 tiles off, and the memory map was 3 and 2 tiles off. On the tile map I counted, so every distance was right compared with the others.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the result, the numbers as evidence, and the reason: counting kept the distances in proportion.
11. (fill) On a tile map, one floor tile is drawn as one ____ on the paper.  
   **Answer:** ['square']. *Why:* One tile, one square. That keeps every distance shrunk by the same amount.
12. (truefalse) 'Turn left at the door' means the same thing for everyone, whichever way they face.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Left depends on which way you face. Someone coming in from the other side turns the other way.
13. (label) Here is a cross for a compass. Write N, S, E and W at its four ends. North is at the top.  
   **Answer:** N at the top, S at the bottom, E on the right, W on the left.. *Why:* With north at the top, east is on the right. A strong answer checks it by facing north and pointing right.

   Options: A key / A north arrow / A photo of the Sun  
   **Answer:** A north arrow. *Why:* A north arrow, or a compass rose, shows which way north is. The key explains the symbols.
15. (write) Your aunt has never been to your flat. Write directions from the lift to your front door, using at least one direction word such as east or north.  
   **Answer:** Any clear directions with landmarks and counts. For example: Come out of the lift and walk east along the corridor, towards the window. Ours is the third door on the north side, with a kolam outside.. *Why:* A strong answer uses landmarks, counts and at least one direction word, so it works whichever way the aunt faces.

### Which Way? (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (write) In the coin test, why is it fair to hide the coin away from all furniture?  
   **Answer:** If the coin is next to the bed, the finder can just look for 'next to the bed' on any map. In open floor, only a map with the right distances shows where it is.. *Why:* A strong answer says how a landmark next to the coin would let a bad map look good.
2. (mcq) A memory map shows the cupboard as big as the bed. Really, the cupboard is half as long. What will a finder using this map probably do?  
   Options: Go to exactly the right spot / Miss the spot, because the gaps around the cupboard are wrong / Find the cupboard is missing  
   **Answer:** Miss the spot, because the gaps around the cupboard are wrong. *Why:* If one thing is drawn too big, the gaps next to it are squeezed or stretched, so an X near it is in the wrong place.
3. (order) Put the steps of making a tile map in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Count the tiles along each wall', 'Draw the walls on squared paper, one square per tile', 'Count tiles from the walls to each piece of furniture', 'Draw each piece of furniture where you counted it', 'Add a north arrow and a key']. *Why:* The walls come first, because everything else is counted from them.
4. (truefalse) A grown-up and a child each map a room in their own steps. The child's map has more squares, so it must be wrong.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The child's steps are shorter, so there are more of them. Both maps shrink every wall by the same amount, so both have the same shape and both are right.
5. (write) In the left-right test, chit A said 'forward, then right'. It found the coin only when you started facing east. Why that wall?  
   **Answer:** Chit A was written by someone facing east. It works only for someone standing the same way, because forward and right turn with your body.. *Why:* A strong answer links the one start that worked with the way the writer was facing.
6. (mcq) You face south. Which way is your right hand pointing?  
   Options: East / West / North  
   **Answer:** West. *Why:* Facing south, west is on your right and east is on your left. It is the mirror of facing north. Stand up and try it.
7. (draw) Draw a map of your bedroom from above. Include a key with at least four symbols and a north arrow.  
   **Answer:** A box for the room with gaps for the door and window, symbols for the bed, cupboard and other things, a key that explains every symbol, and an N arrow.. *Why:* A strong map shows things from above, explains every symbol, and shows north so the reader can turn it.
8. (fill) Holding a map so that its north arrow points to the real north is called ____ the map.  
   **Answer:** ['setting', 'turning']. *Why:* When a map is set, every place on it lies the same way as the real place.
9. (write) The Sun rises a little north of east in June and a little south of east in December. Is the morning Sun still good enough for finding east for a home map? Why?  
   **Answer:** Yes. It is only a little off, so it shows which wall is roughly east. For a home map, the closest wall is all we need. For a ship at sea, you would need a compass.. *Why:* A strong answer says how big the error is compared with what the map needs.
10. (sort) Sort these directions. Do they depend on which way you face, or are they fixed to the Earth?  
   **Answer:** left → Depends on you; north → Fixed to the Earth; forward → Depends on you; south-west → Fixed to the Earth; behind you → Depends on you; east → Fixed to the Earth. *Why:* Body directions turn when you turn. Earth directions stay put.
11. (mcq) On a map, 1 square stands for 10 steps. The shop is 4 squares from your gate. About how many steps is that?  
   Options: 4 steps / 14 steps / 40 steps  
   **Answer:** 40 steps. *Why:* Each square is 10 steps, so 4 squares is 4 times 10, which is 40 steps.
12. (write) A desert ant lives in the hot Sahara. She must find food far from her nest and get home before the heat kills her. How does counting her steps help her?  
   **Answer:** Smell trails do not work well on hot, windy sand. By counting her steps, and using the Sun for direction, she knows how far and which way her nest is. So she can run straight home instead of searching.. *Why:* A strong answer says her problem first, the heat and far trips, and then how counting steps solves it.
13. (truefalse) On every map in history, north has been at the top.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. North at the top is a custom. Al-Idrisi's famous world map of 1154 had south at the top.
14. (mcq) In the story Thatha and the Six Towers, Meenakshi drew a map for her grandfather. Why did he walk the wrong way, even though her first map showed every tower?  
   Options: The towers had moved / When he turned round, the map did not turn with him, so left and right swapped / He could not read  
   **Answer:** When he turned round, the map did not turn with him, so left and right swapped. *Why:* Her first map had no direction arrow, so he did not know how to turn it. After she added an east arrow, he could turn the map to match the ground.
15. (perspective) A family is deciding how to get to a new friend's house in Mysuru.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one way with a reason and fairly gives another view. For example, the phone map is exact, but a phone can run out of charge or lose signal. Landmarks are easy to remember. A drawn map needs the distances to be roughly right.. *Why:* Each way has real strengths and weaknesses. A strong answer weighs them.
### True Maps (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Your tile map had no colours, no cushions and no smells, yet it found the coin. What did it keep that made it work?  
   **Answer:** It kept where things are, how far apart they are, and which way they lie. Those are what a finder needs. Colours and smells do not help you find a spot on the floor.. *Why:* A strong answer names distance and direction, and says why the other things do not matter for finding a place.
2. (perspective) In the 1700s, mapmakers in Europe argued about California.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sides with Kino, because he checked the real place, while the maps were copies of each other. It also says the mapmaker's view made sense, because many maps agreeing looks like good evidence when you cannot go there yourself.. *Why:* The heart of the story: copies do not count as extra evidence. Only checking the real place does.
3. (mcq) You walk north and keep going, over land and ice, until you reach the North Pole. Which way is everything around you now?  
   Options: North / South / East  
   **Answer:** South. *Why:* At the North Pole, every direction leads away from the pole, so every direction is south. There is no further north to go.
4. (write) Nain Singh Rawat mapped the road to Lhasa in the 1860s by counting his steps on a string of 100 beads. Why do you think he used beads instead of just counting in his head?  
   **Answer:** Counting thousands of steps in your head is easy to lose. Moving one bead for each 100 steps keeps the count safe, even if you are tired or talking to someone.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that a long count needs a record that does not depend on memory.
5. (draw) Draw a map of a room where 'forward 2 tiles, then right 3 tiles' and '2 tiles north, then 3 tiles east' lead to the same tile. Mark which way the walker must face at the start.  
   **Answer:** A grid with the start, the coin 2 tiles north and 3 east, and an arrow showing the walker faces north at the start.. *Why:* The two sets agree only if the walker starts facing north, because then forward is north and right is east.
6. (mcq) Arun's map says 1 square is 10 steps. His sister draws the same street with 1 square for 5 steps. Whose map of the street is bigger on paper?  
   Options: Arun's / His sister's / They are the same size  
   **Answer:** His sister's. *Why:* Her squares stand for fewer steps, so she needs twice as many squares for the same street. Her map is twice as long and twice as wide, but the same shape.
7. (write) Some people think more detail always makes a better map. Do you agree? Give an example from your own maps.  
   **Answer:** Not always. My street map would be too crowded if I drew every auto and every flowerpot. A good map shows what the reader needs, such as roads and landmarks, and leaves the rest out.. *Why:* A strong answer tests the popular belief with an example and says what a map should keep.
8. (truefalse) Two people can draw two different maps of the same place, and both can be correct.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A map for a visitor shows the route and landmarks. A map for a plumber shows the pipes. Both are right about what they show.
9. (order) Put these in order of how big a piece of the world the map shows, from smallest to biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['a map of your room', 'a map of your flat', 'a map of your street', 'a map of Tamil Nadu', 'a map of India']. *Why:* Each map takes in a bigger area than the one before it, so each one has to shrink the real place more.
10. (mcq) A compass needle is a small magnet. Why does it point north and south?  
   Options: The Earth acts like a giant magnet and turns the needle / Wind blows it north / The Sun pulls it  
   **Answer:** The Earth acts like a giant magnet and turns the needle. *Why:* The Earth is magnetic, so a free magnet turns to line up north and south. You will learn more about this in the unit on compasses.
11. (write) A desert ant on stilts walked past her nest. An ant with shortened legs stopped too soon. Why do both results point to the same answer?  
   **Answer:** Both ants took their usual number of steps. Longer steps carried one ant too far, and shorter steps left the other short. So both were counting steps, not looking for the nest.. *Why:* A strong answer explains that the two opposite results both fit one idea: counting steps.
12. (mcq) Chennai is on the east coast of India, and Kochi is on the west coast. You fly from Chennai to Kochi. Which way do you go?  
   Options: Roughly east / Roughly west / Roughly north  
   **Answer:** Roughly west. *Why:* Kochi is on the west coast and Chennai on the east, so you travel roughly west, across the country.
13. (write) Scientists found cells in a rat's brain that fire in a grid pattern as the rat moves around. Why might a brain need something like a grid?  
   **Answer:** A grid lets the brain keep track of how far and which way the rat has gone, like squares on a tile map. Then the rat can find the way back, even in the dark.. *Why:* A strong answer links the brain's grid to measuring distance and direction, as on a tile map.
14. (truefalse) The lines that mark borders between Indian states can be seen on the ground from an aeroplane.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Borders are drawn by people on maps. On the ground there is usually no line. Sometimes a river or hills run along a border.
15. (draw) Design a map symbol for each of these: a school, a hospital, a temple and a bus stop. Then draw a key for them.  
   **Answer:** Four simple symbols, each easy to draw small and different from the others, and a key naming each one.. *Why:* A strong symbol is simple, easy to draw small, and cannot be mixed up with another one.

## Applications
- Phone maps use GPS satellites to find where you are. Gladys West's careful maths on the exact shape of the Earth helped make GPS accurate.
- In the 1950s, Marie Tharp drew the first maps of the floor of the Atlantic Ocean from depth measurements taken by ships. She found a long valley running down the middle of an underwater mountain range.
- Auto, taxi and delivery drivers use maps to reach addresses they have never visited.
- Pilots and sailors steer by compass directions, because at sea or in the sky there is no left turn at the temple.
- Architects draw a plan of a flat from above before it is built, with every room to scale.
- Fire exit maps in malls and hospitals show the way out, with a red dot marking 'you are here'.
- Farmers and village offices keep land maps that show where each field begins and ends.
- The Survey of India, started in 1767, makes the official maps of the country.
- Rescue teams after floods and cyclones use maps to plan which roads are still open.

## Open questions
- **How exactly do birds such as Arctic terns find their way across whole oceans?** Scientists know birds use the Sun, the stars, smells and the Earth's magnetism. But how a bird senses magnetism, and how they put all these clues together, is still argued about.
- **How does the brain turn signals from grid cells into the feeling of knowing where you are?** Scientists can record grid cells and place cells firing. Nobody yet knows how these signals become the sense of 'I am in my kitchen'.
- **Why do some people find their way easily and others get lost often?** Practice, attention and the way someone learned to give directions all seem to matter. Scientists are still finding out how much each one counts.

## Connected maths
- Lakshmi made a tile map of her room. The room is 8 tiles long and 6 tiles wide. How many tiles cover the whole floor? **48 tiles.**. The floor has 6 rows, and each row has 8 tiles. 6 times 8 is 48. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- On Meenakshi's map, 1 square is 10 steps. From the gate to the temple is 2 squares, the temple to the water tank 6 squares, and the water tank to Tower D 9 squares. How many steps is it from the gate to Tower D? **170 steps.**. 2 + 6 + 9 = 17 squares. Each square is 10 steps, so 17 times 10 is 170 steps. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- A treasure is 3 squares north and 4 squares east of the door on a grid. Ravi says, 'Then it is 4 squares east and 3 squares north.' Is he right? **Yes.**. Going 3 north then 4 east, or 4 east then 3 north, ends on the same square. The order of the moves does not change where you end up, as long as nothing blocks the way. (queues `math.position.directions-and-grids.L3`)
- In the coin test, the memory map was 3, 2 and 4 tiles off in three rounds. The tile map was 0, 1 and 0 tiles off. How many tiles off was each map in total? How many times better was the tile map? **The memory map was 9 tiles off in total. The tile map was 1 tile off. So the tile map was 9 times closer.**. 3 + 2 + 4 = 9. 0 + 1 + 0 = 1. 9 divided by 1 is 9. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- A child measured a room as 18 steps long and 14 steps wide. A grown-up measured the same room as 13 steps long. About how wide do you think the grown-up found it: 5, 10 or 14 steps? Why? **About 10 steps.**. For the child, the width is a bit less than the length, about 4 steps less out of 18. The grown-up's steps are longer, so both numbers shrink. 10 is a bit less than 13 in the same way. 14 would make the room nearly square, and 5 would make it very thin. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L3`)
