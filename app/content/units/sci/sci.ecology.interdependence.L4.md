# A forest floor holds the rain: how living things build a place together
`sci.ecology.interdependence.L4`

**Summary.** Pour a cup of pretend rain on bare soil, and the same cup on soil covered with dead leaves. Which one keeps the water, and which one sends brown mud down the slope? Your child tests three plates with a tea strainer, then tests how a blanket of leaves keeps soil damp. They find out how leaves, roots, worms and fungi together make a forest floor that holds water, which no one of them could do alone. They read the signs of life on a trail, meet the Bishnoi and the women of Reni who stood up for trees, and play a shared-pond game to learn how much we can take so that it grows back.

**Intro.** *Walk along a forest trail in the monsoon and the ground is soft, dark and wet under a thick carpet of dead leaves. Walk across a swept, cleared field in the same rain and the water runs off in brown streams. The rain is the same. Why does the ground behave so differently?* Dead leaves look like rubbish, and a swept patch looks clean. But in a forest, leaves, roots, worms, termites and fungi all work on the ground at the same time. In this unit you build three small patches of ground on plates and pour pretend rain on them, to see for yourself what leaves do. Then you follow the same idea out along a trail. You read the signs that creatures leave behind, find out how people across India have protected groves and trees for hundreds of years, and ask how much we can take from nature so that it grows back.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that a place is a web of many kinds of links between living things, not only eating and being eaten.
1. I can name six kinds of links and give an example of each from a trail or a balcony.
2. I can run a fair test with a control, and use my measurements to explain how leaf litter and soil together hold rain.

4. I can read the signs on a trail, such as a nibbled leaf, a mud tube or a dropping full of seeds, and say which link each sign shows.

## Terms
- **Ecosystem**: All the living beings of one place, together with the soil, water, air and sunlight they use. A pond is an ecosystem, and so is a forest or a balcony garden. *e.g.* A forest trail in the Western Ghats, with its trees, birds, insects, soil, streams and rain.
- **Species**: One kind of living being, such as a peepal tree, a house crow or a honeybee. Members of the same species can have young together. *e.g.* The house crow and the jungle crow are two species of crow.
- **Leaf litter**: The layer of fallen leaves, twigs, flowers and fruits that lies on the ground in a forest or a garden. *e.g.* The brown carpet of dry leaves under a neem tree.
- **Runoff**: Rain water that does not soak into the ground but flows over the top of it, downhill. *e.g.* The brown stream that runs down a bare slope in the monsoon.
- **Infiltration**: Water soaking down into the ground through the tiny gaps in the soil. A forest soil has many gaps, made by roots and by animals that dig. *e.g.* A puddle on loose, crumbly soil disappears within minutes.
- **Erosion**: Soil being carried away by water or wind. Erosion makes hillsides thin and fills rivers and ponds with mud. *e.g.* Red mud washing into a village lane after a heavy rain on a bare slope.
- **Mulch**: A layer of leaves, straw or husk spread over soil to keep it damp, cool and covered. *e.g.* Coconut husk placed around a banana plant, or paddy straw spread over a vegetable bed.
- **Ecosystem engineer**: A living being who changes a place so much that it becomes a home for others, or stops being one. *e.g.* Termites build tunnels and mounds. Snakes and other animals later live in the old mounds.
- **Transpiration**: A plant letting water out as vapour through tiny holes in her leaves. This water goes into the air. *e.g.* A big tree can send hundreds of litres of water into the air on a hot day.
- **Sacred grove**: A patch of forest that a community protects because it is holy to them. In Kerala it is called a kavu. *e.g.* The Mawphlang forest near Shillong in Meghalaya, which the Khasi people have protected for centuries.
- **Sustainable**: Used in a way that leaves enough for the future. Taking fish, wood or water no faster than they come back is sustainable. *e.g.* Cutting only the oldest bamboo stems, so the clump keeps growing.
- **Commons**: Something that a whole community shares and nobody owns alone, such as a village pond, a grazing field or a forest. *e.g.* A village pond that every family uses for water and fish.

## Explanations
### Six kinds of links in a web of life · *defined*
**Definition:** A web of life is all the living beings of one place and every link between them.

Eating is the link we notice first. But on a trail there are at least six kinds. One is food, such as a bulbul eating a berry. Two is shelter, such as a hornbill nesting in a tree hole. Three is help in making new plants, such as a bee carrying pollen. Four is travel, such as a bird carrying seeds in her gut. Five is clean-up, such as termites and fungi turning dead wood back into soil. Six is changing the place itself, such as roots holding a slope. One being can have all six links at once. Some links also hurt. A caterpillar eats a leaf, and two trees fight for the same light. A web has the harmful links too.
- e.g. A peepal tree feeds birds with her figs, shelters squirrels in her branches, and is held up by roots that also hold the soil.
- e.g. A termite eats dead wood, shelters inside a mound, and digs tunnels that let rain water into the ground.
### Ecosystem engineers: beings who change the place for everyone else · *defined*
**Definition:** An ecosystem engineer is a living being who changes a place so much that it becomes a home for others, or stops being one.

Some beings do more than eat and be eaten. They build the place. Termites dig tunnels and build mounds. Rain soaks into the tunnels, and snakes, mongooses and lizards later use the old mounds as homes. Earthworms eat soil and leaf bits and push the soil up. Charles Darwin watched worms for about forty years. In 1881 he published a book showing that the worms in one field bring up many tonnes of soil a year. Tree roots grip soil and stop it sliding. In dry riverbeds, elephants dig for water with their feet and trunks, and other animals drink from the holes they leave.
- e.g. Termites dig tunnels that let rain water sink into the ground.
- e.g. Earthworms turn the soil over and open it up so that air and water can go in.
- e.g. Elephants dig waterholes that smaller animals use.
### The forest floor is a sponge made by many beings · *defined*
**Definition:** Leaf litter is the layer of fallen leaves, twigs and fruits on the ground. Together with soil, roots, animals and fungi, it soaks up rain and lets it go slowly.

Rain falls first on the leaves of the trees, which slow it down. Then it meets the leaf litter, which soaks up some water and breaks the fall of the drops. Under the litter, roots, worms and termites have made tunnels, so the water sinks in instead of running off. Thin threads of fungi and fine roots bind the soil crumbs together, so the soil does not wash away. The soil then holds water in its tiny gaps, like a sponge, and lets it out slowly into springs and streams over many weeks. On bare soil, raindrops break the crumbs. The mud fills the gaps and forms a crust, and the water runs off downhill, carrying the soil with it. This is erosion. Field measurements in many countries find that bare soil loses far more soil and water than covered soil.
- e.g. Streams in forested hills often keep flowing in the dry months, long after the rain has stopped.
- e.g. Dams below cleared hillsides fill with mud faster than dams below forests.
### A blanket on the soil: why a layer of leaves keeps soil damp · *defined*
**Definition:** Mulch is a layer of leaves, straw or husk spread over soil.

Sun and wind turn the water in soil into vapour. This is called evaporation. A layer of leaves shades the soil, keeps the wind off it and keeps it cooler, so less water escapes. The soil below stays damp for longer. Earthworms and many tiny animals come up into the damp soil, and they slowly pull the old leaves down and turn them into soil. Farmers in India use this idea. They spread paddy straw over vegetable beds, dry sugarcane leaves around young plants, and coconut husk around banana plants.
- e.g. Paddy straw spread on a vegetable bed in Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. Coconut husk placed around a banana plant in Kerala.
### Reading a trail: every sign is a link · *defined*
**Definition:** A sign is something a living being leaves behind, such as a track, a dropping, a feather, a nibbled leaf, a nest or a web.

On a nature trail you rarely see the animals. They hear you first. But they leave signs. A dropping full of seeds shows that an animal carried a plant's seeds. A notch in a leaf shows that something ate there. A thin mud tube up a tree shows that termites are cleaning up dead wood. A hole in a trunk shows a nest. Each sign shows one of the six links. To read a sign, ask three questions. Who might have made this? What were they doing? Who else gains from it? A beak and claws can also tell you about a bird: a thick short beak cracks seeds, and a long thin beak probes flowers or mud. Good trail rules protect both visitors and wildlife. Stay with your group. Walk quietly. Do not touch or pick plants and animals you do not know. Take your rubbish home.
- e.g. A purple dropping under a fig tree is full of fig seeds.
- e.g. A tiny heap of sawdust under a tree shows a beetle tunnelling in the wood.
### Plants send water to the sky · *debated*
A plant takes water from the soil with her roots and lets most of it go as vapour through tiny holes in her leaves. This is transpiration, and it is well measured. The vapour rises, joins the air and may fall again as rain somewhere downwind. Studies of the Amazon forest estimate that roughly a quarter to a third of the rain there was once water that plants gave off, though estimates differ. What is debated is how far forests also pull in wet air from the sea. In 2007 two Russian scientists, Anastassia Makarieva and Victor Gorshkov, proposed a 'biotic pump'. They said that a big forest with strong transpiration lowers the air pressure over it and draws in moist sea air. Many atmospheric scientists doubt that this effect is strong, and the question is still being tested.
- e.g. A mature tree sends many litres of water into the air every day.
- e.g. Clouds often gather over forested hills.
- **Most hydrologists:** Plants return a large share of the rain to the air, so cutting forests can reduce rain downwind. How large the effect is depends on the place.
- **Makarieva and Gorshkov (2007):** Forests do more than recycle water. Their transpiration also pulls moist sea air inland, like a pump.
- **Many atmospheric scientists:** Winds and temperature differences matter more than the forest's pull. The pump idea needs more evidence.
### Is a forest one thing, or many things? · *debated*
A forest holds water like a sponge, but no single member holds it. So is a forest like one body with many parts, or only many separate lives that happen to share a place? Scientists have argued about this for over a hundred years. The answer may be 'a little of both'.
- e.g. The forest sponge exists only when leaves, soil, roots and fungi are all there together.
- e.g. A single tree can live on her own in a field, but a forest soil cannot form without many beings.
- **Frederic Clements (1916):** A plant community is like one living body. It is born, grows up and ripens, and its parts work as a team.
- **Henry Gleason (1926):** Each plant lives by her own needs and her own luck. A community is a chance meeting of many lives, not a body.
- **James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis (1970s):** On the scale of the whole Earth, living and non-living parts together keep the air and the oceans fairly steady, a bit like a body. This 'Gaia' idea is still debated.
### Sacred groves: forests kept by a community · *debated*
**Definition:** A sacred grove is a patch of forest that a community protects because it is holy to them.

Across India there are thousands of sacred groves. In Kerala they are called kavu, in Karnataka devarakadu, and in Maharashtra devrai. The Mawphlang sacred forest near Shillong in Meghalaya has been looked after by the Khasi people for centuries. In many groves, people may not cut a tree, hunt, or even carry away dead leaves. Many groves hold old trees and plants that have become rare outside. Some feed springs and streams. In 1975 the Indian ecologist Madhav Gadgil and V. D. Vartak wrote a paper asking for these groves to be kept. Scientists still ask how much groves protect nature, and how long they will last.
- e.g. A kavu in Kannur, Kerala, with old trees standing in the middle of farmland.
- e.g. The Mawphlang forest near Shillong in Meghalaya.
- **The families who keep the grove:** The forest belongs to the gods or the ancestors. Taking anything from it is wrong, and this rule has protected it for generations.
- **Madhav Gadgil and other ecologists:** Groves work as small nature reserves. They often hold plants and animals that are rare outside, and they often protect water.
- **Some other scientists:** Many groves are small and shrinking, and belief in the old rules is fading in some places. A grove alone cannot save a whole landscape.
### People who stood up for trees: the Bishnoi and the women of Reni · *defined*
**Definition:** A movement to protect trees is an organised effort by ordinary people who refuse to let trees be cut.

The Bishnoi people of Rajasthan follow rules, set down in the 1400s, not to cut green trees or hurt wild animals. Tradition says that in 1730, at the village of Khejarli near Jodhpur, soldiers of the king came to cut khejri trees. A woman named Amrita Devi hugged a tree to stop them, and many villagers did the same. Tradition says about 363 people died. The king then stopped the cutting. In 1974 in the village of Reni, in the Garhwal Himalaya, men were away when contractors came to fell trees. Women led by Gaura Devi stood in front of the trees and would not move, and the contractors left. This became known as the Chipko movement, from the Hindi word for 'stick' or 'hug'. In 1980 the government banned the felling of green trees in the hills of Uttar Pradesh, where Reni was then.
- e.g. The khejri tree, which gives shade and food in the Thar desert, is still protected by Bishnoi villages.
- e.g. Women in the Himalaya continued to protect forests long after Reni.
### Sustainable: taking only what grows back · *defined*
**Definition:** Sustainable means used in a way that leaves enough for the future.

Everything alive can be used up if we take it faster than it comes back. A bamboo clump grows new stems every year. If you cut only the oldest stems, the clump keeps going. If you cut them all, she dies. A fish pond works in the same way. If you leave enough fish to breed, the pond refills. If you take the breeding fish, it empties. This is why Kerala stops trawling at sea for weeks in the monsoon, when fish are breeding. Water works in the same way. Johads, the earth dams of Rajasthan, and the temple tanks of Tamil Nadu slow the rain and let it sink into the ground, so wells refill. An old Indian hymn to the Earth, in the Atharvaveda, asks that whatever is dug from her may quickly grow back.
- e.g. Cutting only the oldest bamboo stems.
- e.g. A fishing ban in the breeding season.
- e.g. Johads and tanks that refill wells.
### Shared things: who keeps them safe? · *debated*
A village pond, a grazing field and a forest are commons. In 1968 the biologist Garrett Hardin argued that people with a shared field will always add one more cow until the grass is gone. He called this 'the tragedy of the commons'. Elinor Ostrom, a political scientist, looked at real villages. Many had shared a meadow, a forest or an irrigation canal for hundreds of years without ruining it. They had rules about who could take how much. People watched each other, and cheats were warned and then fined. In 2009 she won the Nobel Prize in Economics for this work, the first woman to do so. Both are right in part. Without rules, a shared thing is often used up. With good rules made by the people who use it, it can last.
- e.g. The village pond or the gochar, the grazing land that every family may use.
- e.g. An irrigation canal where families take turns.
- **Garrett Hardin (1968):** If everyone takes a little more for themselves, the shared thing is destroyed. It needs a private owner or a government to protect it.
- **Elinor Ostrom (1990):** Villagers who use a shared thing can make and keep their own rules. Many have done so for centuries.

## Mostly agreed
- A forest floor of leaf litter, roots and soil animals soaks up rain and lets it go slowly. *How we know:* Measurements on covered and bare plots in many countries find that bare soil loses much more water and soil in heavy rain.
- A layer of mulch keeps soil damp for longer than bare soil. *How we know:* Farmers and scientists have compared mulched and bare beds. Mulched soil loses less water to evaporation.
- Most places hold many kinds of links between living beings, not only food links. *How we know:* Studies of pollination, seed travel, nesting and soil show links of many kinds between the same species.
- Plants give off large amounts of water as vapour, and this water becomes part of the rain downwind. *How we know:* Measurements of rain, humidity and plant water use in the Amazon and other forests show a large recycled share.
- Earthworms and termites change the soil and the land. *How we know:* In 1881 Charles Darwin weighed the soil that worms bring up. Later measurements of termite tunnels show that rain sinks in faster there.
- Shared resources can last for centuries when the people who use them make and keep rules. *How we know:* Elinor Ostrom and others collected hundreds of cases from many countries, including meadows, forests and canals.

## How we found out
**Elinor Ostrom, an American political scientist (1933 to 2012), who tested the idea of Garrett Hardin, 1968 to 1990.** In 1968 an American biologist named Garrett Hardin wrote an essay called The Tragedy of the Commons. He pictured a field that every herder shares. Each herder gains by adding one more cow, he said, so all of them add cows until the grass is gone. Many people took this to mean that shared land and water are always ruined, unless someone owns them or a government rules them. Elinor Ostrom, a political scientist, wondered whether it was true. She and her students gathered real cases: mountain meadows in Switzerland, forests in Japan, irrigation canals in Spain and the Philippines. Many had been shared for hundreds of years and were still healthy. The villagers had made rules about who could take how much, watched each other, and warned and fined cheats. Her 1990 book, Governing the Commons, showed that people can look after a shared thing if they make the rules together. In 2009 she became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Economics.  
*What people believed before:* Many people believed that a shared field, forest or pond is always used up, because each person gains by taking a bit more. Ostrom showed that many villages had kept shared things safe for centuries, by making and keeping their own rules.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Dead leaves on the ground are rubbish, and a clean-swept ground is healthier.** (Many gardeners and some city rules.; *minority*)  
  For: A swept yard looks tidy and has fewer places for some pests to hide.  
  Against: Leaf litter soaks up rain, keeps soil damp and feeds worms and fungi. Bare soil washes away and dries out.
- **A forest is one living body.** (Frederic Clements, early in the 1900s, and some later ecologists.; *debated*)  
  For: Many parts depend on each other, and the whole does things, such as holding water, that no part does alone.  
  Against: Plants and animals live by their own needs and do not share a body or a goal. Communities change when the climate changes, and their members move separately.
- **Forests make rain by pulling in wet air from the sea (the biotic pump).** (Anastassia Makarieva and Victor Gorshkov.; *debated*)  
  For: Forests give off a lot of vapour, and rain does fall more over forests in some places.  
  Against: Many atmospheric scientists find that winds and temperature differences do most of the work.
- **Shared things are always ruined, unless someone owns them or a government rules them.** (Garrett Hardin, 1968.; *rejected*)  
  For: Overfished seas and overgrazed fields exist where nobody set rules.  
  Against: Elinor Ostrom found many shared meadows, forests and canals that lasted for hundreds of years under village rules.
- **Sacred groves protect all the nature around them.** (Some writers on nature and culture.; *debated*)  
  For: Many groves hold old trees and rare plants, and some feed springs.  
  Against: Many groves are small and shrinking, and the land around them is often cleared.

## Roots
We have poured rain on soil and leaves, read signs on a trail and met people who protect trees. Now we ask what a forest floor tells us about who does the work, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Soil with no leaves lost the water fast, and leaves with no soil lost it too. Soil with leaves on top held it. Holding water is the power of no single part. It exists only in how the parts are joined.  
*Habit it breaks:* We give the credit to one hero, such as the biggest tree, the tiger or the worm. We picture a place as a heap of separate things, each with its own job. But what a forest does for water belongs to the group, not to any member.  
*Evidence:* In the Forest Floor Test, the plate with only soil and the plate with only leaves let most of the rain run off. The plate with soil under leaves kept more of it and sent out clearer water. If your results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* You are not one single thing. Your body holds trillions of cells and microbes, and none of them alone is you.
- *what is life:* Life is not only single beings eating and growing. It is also the places that living beings make together, such as soil, a pond or a grove.
- *what is this world:* Rain, leaves, roots, worms and wells pass water to each other all the time. The water that fell on a leaf last week may be in a root, a worm or a spring today.

A forest looks like many separate trees, animals and fungi. The test shows that some of what a forest does belongs to the joining and not to the pieces. The rain that lands on one leaf ends up, days later, in a root, a worm, a spring and a well. Many sages saw the world as one thing doing many jobs. A forest floor is a small place where you can watch that happen: one rain, many beings, one holding.

- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** Imagine a great net with a bright jewel at every knot. Each jewel shows the reflections of all the other jewels, and each of those shows all the rest. Nothing shines alone. (The forest floor is like this. Leaves, roots, worms and fungi each depend on the others. Take one away and the whole holding changes.)
- **Isha Upanishad, verse 1:** The Isha Upanishad says that the whole world is held within one. So enjoy what is given, and do not grab what belongs to someone else. (In the Shared Pond game, grabbing more than the pond can give empties it for everyone. Taking only what is needed leaves enough for all.)
- **Atharvaveda 12.1.35, the hymn to the Earth:** The hymn asks the Earth that whatever is dug from her may quickly grow back, and that we may never hurt her heart. (This is the rule of sustainable living in one verse: take only what grows back.)
- Science (settled): Soil is held together by living beings. The tunnels, threads and crumbs that hold water are made by roots, worms, termites and fungi. Without them, soil is closer to dust.
- Science (debated): Whether a forest is best thought of as one self-steadying whole, or as many separate lives, is still argued. The Gaia idea of James Lovelock and Lynn Margulis says the whole Earth steadies itself.
- Other view, Henry Gleason, an American plant ecologist (1926), and many ecologists since: A forest is not one thing. Each plant and animal lives by her own needs. A web is a name we give to many separate lives that happen to overlap, and the sponge is only the sum of what each part does.
- Practice, Follow one drop (3 min): Sit near a plant, a tree or an open window. Breathe in slowly three times. Now picture one drop of rain landing on a leaf. See it slide to the soil. See it sink past the roots. See a root drink it. Where could it go next? Open your eyes and look at the plant. Her leaves are letting a little of that water go into the air right now.
- Ask: When leaves, roots and worms hold the water together, who is doing the holding?
- Ask: If a forest can do what no part of it can do, are you also more than your parts?
- Ask: Who should look after a place that nobody owns, and who should decide?

## Story: The Well That Never Dried
*A made-up story. Devika, Ammamma and Sreeja Chechi are not real people. Sacred groves called kavu are real, and there are many in north Kerala. Leaf litter and soil really do soak up and hold rain, and bare, swept ground really does shed it as muddy water. Whether one grove feeds one well depends on the land, so that part is imagined.*

Devika came to her grandmother's house in Kannur in April, when the whole of Kerala was waiting for rain. The paddy fields were cracked. The red road was the colour of old brick. Even the crows sat in the shade with their beaks open.

Behind Ammamma's house the land climbed to a small, dark hill of old trees. This was the kavu. Nobody cut a branch there. Nobody picked a flower, or took a twig, or even carried away a dead leaf. "Gods live in there," said Ammamma. "We do not take from their house."

On the second morning Devika took a steel bucket to the well. She expected to see the bottom. Instead the water was only an arm's length down, cool and clear. Next door, Sreeja Chechi's well had been dry for three weeks. She was carrying water from the school tap in two plastic pots.

"Why does ours have water?" Devika asked. "Because of the kavu," said Ammamma, as if it were obvious. "That is not an answer," said Devika. She had noticed that grown-ups sometimes say because when they mean I do not know. Ammamma only laughed. "Then find a better one."

So Devika walked round both pieces of land. Sreeja Chechi's compound was spotless. Every leaf had been swept and burned, and every little bush cut down. It looked like a courtyard. Then Devika climbed into the kavu. Here everything was a mess. Leaves lay ankle-deep, brown on top and black underneath. Mud tubes ran up the tree trunks. A line of ants vanished under a rotting log. The air smelled of wet earth, though it had not rained for two months.

Devika knelt and pushed her hand under the leaves. The ground was cool and damp, and laced with fine white threads. A worm slid away from her fingers. She scooped up a handful of the black leaf-mould and squeezed it. Water ran out between her fingers, as from a wet cloth. She looked at her hand. Leaves from a dozen kinds of tree. Roots finer than hair. White threads of fungus. A worm. A beetle the size of a grain of rice. None of these was a well.

That evening the first storm of the summer arrived. The sky went black, the air smelled of dust, and then the rain came so hard that it bounced. Devika stood on the verandah with Ammamma. Down the lane ran a river the colour of tea with too much milk. It was red, thick and fast, and it came from Sreeja Chechi's neat compound. It was carrying her good soil away.

Then Devika looked the other way. Where the kavu met the lane, a thin trickle ran out from under the leaves. It was slow, and almost clear. Most of the rain had simply gone into the hill.

"It isn't drinking the rain," Devika said slowly. "The hill is holding it. And letting it out a little at a time. That is how the well still has water in April."

Ammamma said nothing, but her eyes crinkled. "My mother told me never to take even a dead leaf from the kavu," she said. "She said the gods would be angry. I never asked her what the gods were made of." Devika thought about it. "Leaves," she said. "Roots. Worms. Fungus." Ammamma laughed. "That is a great many gods. No wonder they are strong."

The next morning Devika helped Sreeja Chechi carry her pots. She did not say I told you so. She said, "Chechi, what if you did not burn your leaves? What if you put them round the coconut palms, like a blanket?" Sreeja Chechi looked at the leaves, and at her bare red ground, and at her empty well. "A blanket," she said. "My mother did that." She sighed. "We were so proud of being clean."

By the end of the week, each coconut palm in the compound stood in a patch of untidy brown. It was not a kavu. It would take years to become one. But when the next rain came, Devika saw it walk across that compound slowly, instead of running.

**For the parent:** The hill never drank the rain. Leaves, roots, worms and fungi held it together, and let it out slowly.

- Ask: What job does each part of a forest floor do for the water, and what is lost if one part is missing?
- Ask: Why might a rule that nothing is ever taken from a place help that place, whatever the reason for the rule?
- Ask: How could you find out whether leaves under a tree keep the ground damp, without harming the tree?

### g1 · Sign Detective: who left this sign, and what link does it show? (20 min)
- **rules:** Cut out the 24 sign cards and the 6 link tokens. Lay the tokens face up. Shuffle the cards and stack them face down.; One player is the reader. ## Experiments
### e1 · Forest Floor Test: which plate keeps the rain? (50 min)
- **question:** When pretend rain falls on a slope, how much water runs off, and how muddy is it, if the ground is bare soil, soil with dry leaves on top, or dry leaves with no soil?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which plate will send off the most water, and which will send off the muddiest water? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every plate is the same size, is lifted to the same slope, and gets the same amount of rain from the same strainer. Plate A, bare soil, is the starting point. Plate B changes one thing from A: dry leaves on top. Plate C has the same leaves as B but no soil. Because each plate differs in only one way, a difference in the runoff comes from that one thing.
- **safety:** Do the test over a bucket, a sink or a bathroom floor, because it is messy. Wash your hands after touching soil. Do not put soil or leaves near your mouth. Keep the dry leaves away from any flame.
- **record:** Write the seconds until the first drop, the millilitres that ran off in each rain, and how muddy the water was. Then draw a bar chart of the runoff in rain 1.
- **results table:** Plate | What is on it | Seconds to first drop (rain 1) | Runoff in rain 1 (ml) | Runoff in rain 2 (ml) | How muddy (clear, a little, very)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set out three identical plates, a tea strainer, a tumbler of soil and a bowl of crushed dry leaves. Show your child what goes on each plate. Write down their guess and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will pour pretend rain on three plates. Plate A will have only soil. Plate B will have soil with dry leaves on top. Plate C will have dry leaves and no soil. Which plate will send off the most water? Which will send off the muddiest water? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Plate A has soil. Plate B has soil and leaves. Plate C has only leaves. Which sends off the muddiest water? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that plate will send off the most water? (good: Bare soil has nothing to stop the water.; Leaves will slow the water down.; Soil soaks up water like a sponge.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write the guesses at the top of the results table.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Crumble the soil and spread one tumbler of it on plate A, about as thick as a fingertip. Do the same on plate B. Spread one handful of crushed leaves loosely over plate B. Spread another handful, with no soil, on plate C.  
  *Parent says:* Plate A is soil only. Plate B is the same soil with a loose blanket of crushed leaves on top. Plate C is the same leaves with no soil underneath. Only one thing changes at a time.  
  *Child mode:* Spread soil on plates A and B. Crush the leaves into small bits. Put a handful on plate B. Put a handful on plate C.
  - Note: Crush the leaves to pieces about the size of a postage stamp, and keep them loose. Whole leaves laid flat can act like roof tiles and send the water off. If you have no dry leaves, use dried used tea leaves. Do not press the soil down hard.
  - Check: Why do we keep one plate with soil only? → **It is the starting point to compare with**
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lift one edge of plate A on the stack of books. Put a labelled glass under the other edge, with a paper chute. Hold the strainer 10 centimetres above the plate. Pour 200 millilitres slowly over 30 seconds, moving the strainer back and forth. Count the seconds until the first drop reaches the glass. Repeat for plates B and C, one at a time, each with its own glass. Measure each glass and write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* We pour 200 millilitres of pretend rain, slowly, over 30 seconds. Watch what the water does. Count the seconds before the first drop falls into the glass. Then wait two minutes for the dripping to stop, and measure.  
  *Child mode:* Pour 200 millilitres slowly through the strainer. Count the seconds until the first drop. Wait two minutes. Measure the water in the glass.
  - Ask: What do you see the water doing on each plate? (good: On plate A the water runs off fast and is brown.; On plate B the water seems to disappear into the leaves.; On plate C the water slides off the leaves.)
  - Note: Pour the same way each time, at the same height, with the plate at the same slope. Write what you see even if it surprises you. On plate A the water often arrives first and is brown. On plate B it often arrives later and is clearer.
  - Check: Why do we pour the same amount, from the same height, on each plate? → **So that any difference comes from what is on the plate**
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour away the first glasses, or set them aside. Use a clean glass for each plate. Pour a second rain of 200 millilitres on plate A, then B, then C, in the same way. Wait two minutes after each. Measure each glass and write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now it is raining a second time, on ground that is already wet. What do you think will change? Pour the same way. Measure what runs off.  
  *Child mode:* It is raining again. Pour another 200 millilitres on each plate. Measure what runs off.
  - Ask: Is the second rain different from the first? How? (good: More ran off plate A the second time.; The soil was already wet, so it soaked up less.; Plate B still held more than plate A.)
  - Note: Wet soil usually soaks up less, so more water often runs off in the second rain. Do not explain yet. Ask what they notice. If the numbers are very close for all plates, write them down. That is also a result.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Line up the glasses. Hold a sheet of white paper behind them. Look through each glass. Decide whether the water is clear, a little muddy or very muddy. Write it in the table. Leave the glasses for five minutes and look at the bottom.  
  *Parent says:* Which glass is the muddiest? Which is the clearest? Look again after five minutes. Where did the mud go?  
  *Child mode:* Hold white paper behind each glass. Is the water clear, a little muddy or very muddy? Write it down.
  - Note: Muddy water from plate A is the most common result. If all the glasses look the same, the soil may be too coarse. Try again with finer, drier soil.
  - Check: Brown water running off bare soil is carrying something away. What is it? → **Tiny bits of soil**
- **s6 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar chart. Put the three plates along the bottom and the runoff in rain 1 up the side. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then ask what would change if the leaves were taken off plate B.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each plate. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that plate B kept more water, or sent out clearer water, than plate A, and that plate C let the water run off. After they have said what they saw, explain: leaves slow the drops and the flow, soak up some water, and protect the soil from the beating of the drops. The soil below soaks up the rest. Each needs the other. In a forest, roots, worms, termites and fungi also open tunnels and bind the soil. If plate B did not do better than plate A, say so. That is also a result.
  - Think: Plate C had leaves too. Why didn't leaves alone hold the water?
### e2 · Blanket Test: do dead leaves keep soil damp? (44 min, 5 days)
- **question:** Which cup of soil stays damp for the longest, if it is bare, if it has a thin cover of leaves, or if it has a thick cover of leaves?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which cup will dry out first, and which will stay damp longest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every cup has the same soil, the same depth, the same amount of water and the same place in the sun. Cup A, bare soil, is the control. Cup B and cup C differ from A in one way only: a cover of leaves. B and C differ from each other only in how thick the cover is.
- **safety:** Keep the cups away from the edge of a sill or balcony so that they cannot fall. A white fuzz on the leaves is a harmless fungus. Wash your hands after touching soil or leaves.
- **record:** Each day, write the length of the pale, dry part of the stick in the table. On day 5, draw a line graph with one line for each cup.
- **results table:** Cup | What covers the soil | Day 1 dry part (cm) | Day 2 (cm) | Day 3 (cm) | Day 4 (cm) | Day 5 (cm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Set up the three cups. Do the first stick test. (17 min); day 2: Stick test on each cup. Write the pale part in the table. (4 min); day 3: Stick test on each cup. Look under the leaves. (4 min); day 4: Stick test on each cup. Guess the number for day 5. (4 min); day 5: Last stick test. Draw the graph. Say what you found and why. (15 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three cups. Say that A will stay bare, B will get a thin cover of leaves and C will get a thick cover. Write down their guess and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will keep three cups of damp soil in the sun for five days. One is bare. One has a thin blanket of leaves. One has a thick blanket. Which will dry out first? Which will stay damp longest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Cup A is bare. Cup B has a few leaves. Cup C has a thick layer of leaves. Which dries out first? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that cup will dry out first? (good: The sun can reach the bare soil.; Leaves keep the sun off.; A thick blanket will keep more water in.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write the guesses at the top of the results table.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill each cup with soil to 6 centimetres. Pour 4 tablespoons of water slowly into each. Wait 10 minutes. Leave cup A bare. Cover cup B with a layer of crushed leaves about 1 centimetre thick. Cover cup C with a layer about 3 centimetres thick. Put all three side by side in a sunny place. Do the first stick test.  
  *Parent says:* Same soil, same water, same sun. Only the covering is different. To test how dry the soil is, we push a stick to the bottom, pull it out and measure the pale part.  
  *Child mode:* Fill the cups with soil. Add water. Cover B a little and C a lot. Put them in the sun.
  - Note: To do a stick test, push a clean stick straight down to the bottom of the cup. Pull it out. Damp soil makes the stick dark and dry soil leaves it pale. Measure the pale part from the top end of the soil mark, in centimetres, and write it in the table. On day 1 it should be 0 for all the cups. Use a new stick for every reading, or wipe the stick dry.
  - Check: Why do all the cups get exactly the same water and the same sun? → **So that only the cover is different**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* At the same time as yesterday, do the stick test on each cup with a clean stick. Measure the pale part. Write it in the table for day 2. Look at the soil and the leaves.  
  *Parent says:* What does the stick look like today? How much of it is pale? Which cup is drying out most?  
  *Child mode:* Push a clean stick into each cup. Measure the pale part. Write it down.
  - Note: Do not move the cups. After two days, the top of the bare cup is usually pale. If nothing has changed in any cup, that is a result.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow at the same time.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the stick test on each cup. Measure the pale part. Write it in the table for day 3. Smell the leaves and look under the thick cover.  
  *Parent says:* Is the pale part longer than yesterday? Which cup has changed most? What do you see under the leaves?  
  *Child mode:* Push a clean stick into each cup. Measure the pale part. Look under the leaves.
  - Note: Under the thick leaves you may see white threads of fungus or a tiny insect. These are normal and harmless. Do not poke the soil.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow at the same time.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the stick test on each cup. Measure the pale part. Write it in the table for day 4.  
  *Parent says:* What is the pattern so far? If we do nothing, what will the table say on day 5?  
  *Child mode:* Push a clean stick into each cup. Measure the pale part. Guess tomorrow's number.
  - Note: This is a good moment to ask for a prediction. A pattern like 0, 1, 2, 3 suggests 4 for the next day.
  - Wait: Come back tomorrow at the same time.
- **s6 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the last stick test. Draw a line graph. Put the days along the bottom and the pale length up the side, with one line for each cup. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line for each cup. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the bare cup dried fastest, and that the thicker cover slowed the drying more. After they have said what they saw, explain: sun and wind turn the water in soil into vapour. Leaves shade the soil, keep the wind off and keep it cooler, so less water escapes. This is why farmers use mulch. If the bare cup did not dry fastest, say so, and look for what else was different between the cups.
  - Check: A farmer spreads straw around young vegetable plants in summer. Why? → **The straw slows the loss of water from the soil**
  - Think: If the thick cover kept the soil damp, would a plant grow better under it? How could you test that?

### a1 · Window Trail: a nature trail from one spot (15 min)

  *Child mode:* Sit near the window. Stay still and quiet. Touch nothing you do not know.

  - Check: A crow sits on a wire and eats a fruit from a tree. Which link is this? → **Food**
### a2 · One Tree, Many Lives: a census of a tree near you (25 min)
- **safety:** A grown-up comes with you. Do not climb the tree or shake her. Do not touch nests, hives or hairy caterpillars. Stay away from busy roads.

- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up comes along. Choose one tree, such as a neem, a mango, a gulmohar or a peepal. Make five boxes in the notebook. Write a title on each: bark, leaves, flowers and fruit, ground, and air.

  *Child mode:* Pick a tree. Draw five boxes. Name each one.
  - Note: A road tree in a city counts. Choose one you can reach without crossing a busy road.
- **s2 (15 min)**

  *Child mode:* Draw the tree. Draw an arrow to each visitor. If she were cut down, who loses?
  - Note: Good answers name more than one loser: birds that nest, insects that eat the leaves, ants on the bark, the soil that the roots hold. Some visitors give back to the tree, such as the bee who carries pollen and the bird who carries seeds.
  - Think: Does the tree need any of her visitors?

### a4 · The Shared Pond: how much can we take? (30 min)
- **record:** For each round, write the number of beans in the pond at the start, the number each player took, the number left and the number added. Add up each player's score at the end.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 20 beans in the bowl. The bowl is the pond and each bean is a fish. Keep 20 beans in the spare cup. Read the rules: each round, every player secretly writes a number from 0 to 5. ### The Forest Floor and the Web (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) What is leaf litter?  
   Options: The layer of fallen leaves, twigs and fruits on the ground / Leaves that are still on the tree / Rubbish left behind by visitors  
   **Answer:** The layer of fallen leaves, twigs and fruits on the ground. *Why:* Leaf litter is the natural carpet of dead leaves, twigs, flowers and fruits on the ground. It is not rubbish. It soaks up rain, keeps the soil damp and feeds worms and fungi.
2. (mcq) Rain water that flows over the top of the ground, instead of soaking in, is called what?  
   Options: Runoff / Infiltration / Evaporation  
   **Answer:** Runoff. *Why:* Runoff flows over the surface. Infiltration is water soaking down into the ground. Evaporation is water turning into vapour and rising into the air.
3. (mcq) Why does a layer of leaves keep soil damp for longer?  
   Options: It shades the soil and keeps the wind off, so less water escapes / It adds water to the soil / It stops the sun from ever reaching the soil  
   **Answer:** It shades the soil and keeps the wind off, so less water escapes. *Why:* Sun and wind turn the water in soil into vapour. Leaves shade the soil and keep the wind away, so less water leaves. The leaves do not add water, and some warmth still gets through.
4. (mcq) Which one shows the link called travel?  
   Options: A bird's dropping with seeds that sprout far from the tree / A bee drinking nectar / A termite eating dead wood  
   **Answer:** A bird's dropping with seeds that sprout far from the tree. *Why:* In travel, an animal carries a plant's seeds to a new place. A bee at a flower shows making new plants, and a termite eating dead wood shows clean-up.
5. (mcq) A friend says: 'The web of life is just who eats whom.' What would you say?  
   Options: There are other links too, such as shelter, travel, clean-up and changing the place / Yes, eating is the only link / Links only matter in a forest  
   **Answer:** There are other links too, such as shelter, travel, clean-up and changing the place. *Why:* Eating is one link. A bird nesting in a tree, a worm opening the soil and a bee carrying pollen are links too. Saying eating is the only one misses most of the web.
6. (truefalse) Bare soil on a slope loses more soil in heavy rain than soil covered with leaf litter.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Raindrops break the crumbs of bare soil and the water carries the mud away. Leaf litter breaks the fall of the drops and slows the water, so less soil is lost.
7. (truefalse) A termite tunnel can help rain water sink into the ground.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Termite tunnels are small channels that carry water down into the soil. This is why termites are called ecosystem engineers.
8. (fill) Spreading leaves, straw or husk over soil to keep it damp is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['mulch', 'mulching']. *Why:* Mulch is a layer that covers the soil. It shades the soil and keeps the wind off, so water stays in the soil longer.
9. (fill) When water carries soil away from a slope, this is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['erosion']. *Why:* Erosion is soil being carried away by water or wind. It makes hillsides thin and fills rivers and ponds with mud.
10. (match) Match each sign to the link it shows most clearly.  
   **Answer:** A dropping full of fig seeds → Travel; A thin mud tube up a tree trunk → Clean-up; A hole in a tree with a bird flying in and out → Shelter; Bees buzzing at a flowering tree → Making new plants. *Why:* A dropping with seeds carries seeds away, so it shows travel. Termites in a mud tube eat dead wood, which is clean-up. A nesting hole shows shelter. Bees carrying pollen help the tree make seeds, which is making new plants.
11. (sort) Sort each example by whether it keeps water in the ground or lets it run off.  
   **Answer:** leaf litter on a forest floor → Keeps water; bare, swept soil on a steep slope → Lets water run off; coconut husk round a banana plant → Keeps water; a paved yard → Lets water run off; a thick mat of grass roots → Keeps water; a cleared hillside with no plants → Lets water run off. *Why:* Litter, husk and roots slow the water and open the soil, so water sinks in. Pavement and bare steep slopes have nothing to slow it, so the water runs off.
12. (order) Put these in order for what happens when rain falls on a forest.  
   **Answer:** ['Rain lands on the leaves of the trees', 'The leaf litter soaks up some water and slows the rest', 'The water sinks down through root and worm tunnels', 'The soil holds the water in its tiny gaps', 'The water seeps out slowly into a spring']. *Why:* The rain meets the leaves first, then the litter, then the tunnels and the soil. The soil lets it go slowly into springs, which is why streams can keep flowing in the dry months.
13. (write) A friend says: 'Plate C had leaves, so it should hold the most water.' What would you tell your friend, using what you saw in the Forest Floor Test?  
   **Answer:** Leaves with no soil underneath let the water slide off the hard plate. The soil with leaves on top kept more of the water and let out clearer water. The holding comes from soil and leaves working together, not from leaves alone.. *Why:* A strong answer says that plate C let the water run off, and that the soil and the leaves together did better. It names both parts.
14. (write) Why do farmers spread straw around young vegetable plants in summer? Use what you saw in the Blanket Test.  
   **Answer:** The straw shades the soil and keeps the wind off it, so less water turns into vapour. The soil stays damp for longer, like the cups with leaves in the Blanket Test.. *Why:* A strong answer links the straw to less evaporation and damper soil, using the cups as evidence.
15. (draw) Draw a cross-section of a forest floor. Show leaf litter, soil, a root, a worm tunnel and rain sinking in.  
   **Answer:** A layer of leaves on top, soil below, a root going down, a tunnel for a worm, and arrows showing water going down through the litter and the tunnels.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows rain meeting the litter first, then going down through the tunnels. It shows that the water is held in the soil, not only on the surface.
16. (count) Plate A let 90 millilitres run off from 200 millilitres of pretend rain. How much of the rain stayed in the soil?  
   **Answer:** 110. *Why:* 200 take away 90 is 110. So 110 millilitres soaked in. A smaller runoff means more water was held.

1. (mcq) Which sentence best describes a web of life?  
   Options: All the living beings of a place and every link between them / A spider's web in a forest / A list of who eats whom  
   **Answer:** All the living beings of a place and every link between them. *Why:* A web of life is the whole set of links between the beings in one place. Eating is one link, but not the only one.
2. (mcq) Which of these is a link that is not about eating?  
   Options: A bird nesting in a tree hole / A tiger eating a deer / A cow eating grass  
   **Answer:** A bird nesting in a tree hole. *Why:* A nest is shelter. The other two are food links.
3. (mcq) In the Forest Floor Test, why is plate A, with bare soil, called the control?  
   Options: It is the starting point to compare the others with / It has the most soil / It is the first plate  
   **Answer:** It is the starting point to compare the others with. *Why:* The control is the plain version. Each other plate differs from it in one way, so we can see what that change does.
4. (mcq) Muddy brown water running off a slope means what?  
   Options: Soil is being carried away, which is erosion / The rain is dirty / The slope has too many leaves  
   **Answer:** Soil is being carried away, which is erosion. *Why:* The brown colour is tiny bits of soil. Water carrying soil away is erosion. Leaves on a slope reduce it.
5. (mcq) Which of these is an ecosystem engineer?  
   Options: Termites that dig tunnels and build mounds / A crow sitting on a wire / A rain cloud  
   **Answer:** Termites that dig tunnels and build mounds. *Why:* Termites change the soil and the land so much that others can use their tunnels and mounds. A crow on a wire does not change the place.
6. (mcq) Why do farmers use mulch?  
   Options: It keeps the soil damp and cool / It makes the soil as cold as ice / It adds sunlight  
   **Answer:** It keeps the soil damp and cool. *Why:* Mulch shades the soil and keeps the wind off it, so less water evaporates. It does not add sunlight.
7. (mcq) A dropping full of purple seeds lies under a fig tree. Which link does it show?  
   Options: Travel of seeds / Clean-up / Shelter  
   **Answer:** Travel of seeds. *Why:* The animal ate the figs and is carrying the seeds in her gut. The dropping puts them in a new place. That is travel.
8. (mcq) What did Elinor Ostrom find about shared meadows, forests and canals?  
   Options: Many were kept safe for centuries by rules the villagers made / They were always ruined / Only governments can save them  
   **Answer:** Many were kept safe for centuries by rules the villagers made. *Why:* She studied real villages and found many shared things that lasted for centuries. People made rules, watched each other and fined cheats.
9. (truefalse) A sacred grove protects only the trees in it, and has no link to water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Many groves also feed springs and streams. Scientists are still studying how much groves protect.
10. (mcq) A bamboo clump grows new stems every year. Which way of cutting is sustainable?  
   Options: Cutting only the oldest stems / Cutting every stem / Cutting only the youngest stems  
   **Answer:** Cutting only the oldest stems. *Why:* Old stems can be cut while the young ones keep the clump growing. Cutting every stem kills the clump, and cutting the youngest takes away its future.
11. (mcq) Which is the best reason to leave fallen leaves under a tree in a garden?  
   Options: They soak up rain, keep the soil damp and feed the worms / They look untidy and that is good / They stop the tree growing  
   **Answer:** They soak up rain, keep the soil damp and feed the worms. *Why:* Leaf litter protects the soil from the beating of the rain, keeps it damp and feeds the animals and fungi that make the soil.
12. (mcq) What do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Whether a forest is best seen as one whole or as many separate lives / Whether leaves can soak up water / Whether rain falls on forests  
   **Answer:** Whether a forest is best seen as one whole or as many separate lives. *Why:* Ecologists have argued about this for over a hundred years. The other two are settled by measurements.

## Applications
- Farmers spread paddy straw, sugarcane leaves or coconut husk around crops to keep the soil damp and cool.
- Hill farmers dig terraces and plant rows of vetiver grass along slopes, so rain sinks in and soil stays.
- Forest departments and villages plant trees and shrubs on bare hillsides to stop landslides and fill springs.
- Villages in Rajasthan build johads, small earth dams that hold monsoon rain so that it sinks into the ground and refills wells.
- Gardeners and city parks leave fallen leaves under trees, because the litter keeps the soil damp and feeds the worms.
- Fishing is banned for weeks every monsoon in Kerala and other states, so that fish can breed.
- Community forest groups in many Indian villages make their own rules about who may collect wood, grass and fruit, and how much.
- Builders and city planners leave open soil and trees around buildings, so that rain sinks in instead of flooding the street.

## Open questions
- **How much do sacred groves protect the plants, animals and water around them?** Only a small share of the thousands of groves have been studied, and each one is different, so no one can give a single answer yet.
- **Do big forests pull wet air in from the sea, as the biotic pump idea says?** The idea has been tested with models and rain records, but scientists disagree about how strong the effect is.
- **What do most of the living beings in a handful of forest soil actually do?** Most soil bacteria and fungi have never been grown in a laboratory. Scientists can read their DNA, but often cannot tell what they do.
- **How much of the web of life has nobody noticed yet?** About two million kinds of living beings have names. Scientists think there are millions more, mostly insects and small soil animals, and the guesses range widely.
- **Can a community always learn to share a common thing without being forced?** Ostrom found conditions that help, but not a recipe that works everywhere. Shared things that cross many countries, such as the air and the oceans, are much harder.
- **Is a forest more like one body, or like many separate lives?** The answer depends on what we mean by a body, and ecologists have not agreed for over a hundred years.

## Connected maths
- Meenakshi pours 200 millilitres of pretend rain on each of three plates. Plate A lets 120 millilitres run off, plate B lets 60 millilitres run off, and plate C lets 160 millilitres run off. How much water did plate B hold back? How many times more water ran off plate A than plate B? **Plate B held back 140 millilitres. Plate A let 2 times as much run off.**. 200 take away 60 is 140, so plate B held back 140 millilitres. 120 divided by 60 is 2, so plate A let twice as much run off as plate B. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- Hari measures runoff from two plates. Plate A ran off 80 millilitres in rain 1 and 130 millilitres in rain 2. Plate B ran off 30 millilitres in rain 1 and 60 millilitres in rain 2. By how much did the runoff grow on each plate? What does the bigger rise on plate A suggest about wet soil? **Plate A grew by 50 millilitres and plate B by 30 millilitres. Bare soil that is already wet sheds more of the next rain.**. 130 minus 80 is 50, and 60 minus 30 is 30. Plate A's runoff rose more. Wet bare soil is already full of water and its surface is sealed with mud, so more of the second rain runs off. The leaves on plate B slowed the water, so the rise was smaller. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- Plate B in the Forest Floor Test received 200 millilitres of rain, and 60 millilitres ran off. What fraction of the rain ran off, and what fraction was held back? Write each as a fraction in its simplest form. **3 tenths ran off and 7 tenths were held back.**. 60 out of 200 is 60 over 200. Dividing top and bottom by 20 gives 3 over 10. The rest, 10 tenths take away 3 tenths, is 7 tenths. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In the Blanket Test, the dry layer in the bare cup measured 0, 1, 2 and 3 centimetres on days 1 to 4. The dry layer in the cup with leaves measured 0, 0, 1 and 1 centimetres. If each cup keeps drying at the same pace, on which day does each cup first have a dry layer of 5 centimetres? **The bare cup reaches 5 centimetres on day 6. The leaf cup reaches it on about day 12, which is twice as long.**. The bare cup grows 1 centimetre a day, so day 4 is 3 centimetres, day 5 is 4 and day 6 is 5. The leaf cup grows about 1 centimetre every 2 days after day 2, so it reaches 5 centimetres at about day 12. This is a guess from the pattern on a line graph. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- A pond holds 24 fish. Each year, the fish left over make new fish equal to half of their number. Villagers take 8 fish each year. How many fish are there after the new fish arrive, in year 1 and in year 2? What if the villagers take 12 fish each year instead: in which year is the pond empty? **With 8 taken the pond has 24 fish after year 1 and 24 after year 2, so it can last for ever. With 12 taken it has 18 after year 1, 9 after year 2, and in year 3 there are not enough fish to take 12, so the pond is empty.**. With 8 taken: 24 minus 8 is 16, and half of 16 is 8, so 16 plus 8 makes 24 again. With 12 taken: 24 minus 12 is 12, plus half of 12 (6) is 18. Next, 18 minus 12 is 6, plus 3 is 9. Next, 9 minus 12 cannot be done, so the pond is empty in year 3. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
- A canal gives 120 minutes of water each morning, shared equally among 8 farming families. How many minutes does each family get? If 2 more families join, how many minutes does each get, and how many fewer is that for each of the first 8 families? **Each of the 8 families gets 15 minutes. With 10 families each gets 12 minutes, which is 3 minutes fewer.**. 120 divided by 8 is 15. 120 divided by 10 is 12. 15 take away 12 is 3. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A school roof is 8 metres long and 5 metres wide. One millimetre of rain falling on one square metre gives 1 litre of water. How many litres of water fall on the roof in a shower of 20 millimetres? **800 litres.**. The roof area is 8 times 5, which is 40 square metres. Each square metre gets 20 litres. 40 times 20 is 800 litres. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
- On a trail, Kavya finds 12 signs. Four show food, 3 show shelter, 2 show travel of seeds, 2 show clean-up and 1 shows a change to the place. Which link is the most common? What fraction of the signs show food? Draw a bar chart in your notebook. **Food is the most common, with 4 signs. 4 out of 12 is one third of the signs. The bar chart has bars of height 4, 3, 2, 2 and 1.**. The biggest number is 4, which is food. 4 out of 12 simplifies to 1 out of 3. A bar chart puts each link on the bottom and the number of signs up the side. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
