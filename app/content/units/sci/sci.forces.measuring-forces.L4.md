# Measuring forces with care: arrows, sagging lines and how sure a number can be
`sci.forces.measuring-forces.L4`

**Summary.** A bottle that weighs only 5 newtons hangs in the middle of a washing line. Pull the line as flat as you can, and each end of the line pulls with 13 newtons or more. How can the ends pull harder than the whole bottle weighs? You will test this with a rubber-band gauge that you calibrate yourself, then ask how sure any measurement can be. You will also measure the hold of a water surface, which is only a few thousandths of a newton, and meet forces that change when things move.

**Intro.** *Hang a heavy wet shirt in the middle of a washing line. Now try to pull the line so tight that the shirt does not dip at all. You can pull as hard as you like, and the line still sags. Why can you never get it perfectly straight?* This looks like a small puzzle, but it hides a big idea. A force has a direction as well as a size. The same weight can need a small pull or a huge pull, depending on the angle of the line that holds it. In this unit, you will draw forces as arrows and measure the sagging line with a gauge that you test yourself. Then you will ask a harder question. How much can we trust any number that a tool gives us?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can draw a force as an arrow, with the length showing the size and the point showing the direction.
1. I can find the net force when forces act along one line, and I can say what balanced forces mean.
2. I can explain why a line holding a weight must pull harder as it gets flatter, and I can test it.
3. I can calibrate a rubber-band gauge with a graph, and use the graph to read an unknown force.
4. I can repeat a measurement, show how much my readings spread and choose a typical value.
5. I can explain the difference between a reading error and a zero error, and between accurate and precise.
6. I can explain that a moving thing can pull harder or softer than its weight.
7. I can say how a digital scale measures force, and name one force far smaller than a newton and how it was measured.

## Terms
- **Force arrow**: A drawing of a force. The arrow points the way the force acts, and its length shows how big the force is. *e.g.* If 1 centimetre stands for 1 newton, a 5 newton pull is an arrow 5 centimetres long.
- **Direction**: The way something points or moves, such as up, down, left, right, forwards or towards a place. *e.g.* A pull to the left and a pull to the right can be the same size and still do opposite things.
- **Vector**: Any measurement that needs both a size and a direction. A force is a vector. A length or a mass is not, because it needs only a size. *e.g.* '5 newtons' is not enough to describe a pull. '5 newtons to the left' is.
- **Net force**: What is left when you add up all the forces on one thing, counting opposite forces as take-aways. It tells you which way the thing will start to move, and how strongly. *e.g.* A cart is pushed right with 8 newtons and pulled left with 3 newtons by friction. The net force is 5 newtons to the right.
- **Balanced forces**: Forces that cancel exactly, so the net force is zero. A thing with balanced forces keeps doing what it was already doing. *e.g.* A book on a table has its weight pulling down and the table pushing up with the same size, so the book stays still.
- **Tension**: The pull along a rope, string or wire that is held tight at both ends. *e.g.* A washing line holding a wet shirt is in tension. Every part of it pulls on the next part.
- **Span**: The distance between the two places where a line, a rope or a bridge is held up. *e.g.* A washing line tied to two window grills 1 metre apart has a span of 1 metre.
- **Sag**: How far the middle of a line dips below the straight line between its two ends. *e.g.* A line that dips 10 centimetres in the middle has a sag of 10 centimetres.
- **Reading error**: The small mistake that comes from the way we read a tool. We can read a ruler or a gauge only to about half of its smallest mark. *e.g.* On a ruler with marks every millimetre, you can say 'about 5.5 millimetres', but not 5.52.
- **Spread**: How far apart a set of repeated readings are, from the smallest to the largest. Scientists also call this the range. *e.g.* Ten readings between 4.7 and 5.3 newtons have a spread of 0.6 newtons.
- **Typical value**: The reading we choose to stand for all the repeats. The middle value, when the readings are lined up in order, is a good choice. *e.g.* The readings 4.8, 5.0, 5.0, 5.1 and 5.3 line up with 5.0 in the middle, so the typical value is 5.0.
- **Zero error**: A mistake that is the same in every reading, because the tool does not start at zero. Repeating the reading does not remove it. *e.g.* A kitchen scale that shows 3 grams with nothing on it makes every weighing 3 grams too big.
- **Accurate**: Close to the true value. *e.g.* A scale that reads 500 grams for a sealed 500 gram packet is accurate.
- **Precise**: Giving nearly the same reading every time, whether or not that reading is close to the truth. *e.g.* A scale that reads 508, 508 and 509 grams for a 500 gram packet is precise but not accurate.
- **Calibration graph**: A graph made by testing a gauge with loads you already know, so that you can read the size of an unknown load from it. *e.g.* You hang 5, 10 and 15 newtons on a rubber band, plot the lengths and draw a line through them.
- **Interpolate**: To find a value between two measured values by reading along the line you drew through them. *e.g.* A band 14 centimetres long lies halfway between the 12 centimetre and 16 centimetre marks, so its load is halfway between those loads.
- **Surface tension**: The pull along the surface of a liquid that makes the surface act like a thin stretched skin. *e.g.* A small steel needle laid carefully on water can float on this skin.
- **Strain gauge**: A thin metal pattern stuck onto a piece of metal. When the metal bends a tiny bit, the pattern stretches, and its electrical resistance changes. A chip reads that change as a force. *e.g.* A digital kitchen scale has strain gauges stuck inside it.
- **Weighbridge**: A huge scale set into the ground, big enough to weigh a whole lorry. *e.g.* A sugar mill weighs each lorry of cane on the way in and again on the way out.

## Explanations
### A force has a size and a direction · *defined*
**Definition:** To describe a force fully, you need two things: how big it is and which way it acts. We draw a force as an arrow. The arrow points the way the force acts, and its length shows the size.

Suppose you say, 'I pulled the door with 20 newtons.' That is not the whole story. Did you pull it open or push it shut? A pull towards you and a pull away from you are the same size and do opposite things. So scientists always give a direction with the size, for example '20 newtons towards me'. A measurement that needs both a size and a direction is called a vector. To draw a force, choose a scale, for example 1 centimetre for every 1 newton. A 5 newton pull to the right is then an arrow 5 centimetres long, pointing right. A bag hanging from your hand has two forces on it. Its weight pulls it down. Your hand pulls it up. You draw them as two arrows on the bag, one pointing down and one pointing up.
- e.g. A 100 gram apple on your palm: its weight is a 1 newton arrow pointing down, and your hand's push is a 1 newton arrow pointing up.
- e.g. Two people pulling a rope: each pull is an arrow, and the arrows point in opposite directions.
- e.g. On a map, a wind of 20 kilometres an hour from the south is also a vector, because both the speed and the direction matter.
### Forces on one line: add, take away or balance · *defined*
**Definition:** When forces act along one straight line, you add the ones that point the same way and take away the ones that point the opposite way. What is left is the net force.

In a tug of war, one team pulls the rope left with 400 newtons, and the other team pulls it right with 350 newtons. The rope moves left. The net force is 400 minus 350, which is 50 newtons to the left. If both teams pull with 400 newtons, the net force is zero. The forces are balanced, and the rope does not start moving. Balanced forces do not mean that nothing is happening. A book on a table has its weight pulling down and the table pushing up. Both arrows are the same size, and the book stays still. A bicycle at steady speed has the pedal push forwards and the air and road dragging backwards, also balanced. A thing with balanced forces keeps doing exactly what it was doing, whether that is staying still or moving steadily. A thing with unbalanced forces speeds up, slows down or changes direction. You can meet this idea again in the unit on changing motion.
- e.g. A cart is pushed right with 15 newtons and 25 newtons, and friction pulls left with 10 newtons. The net force is 15 plus 25 minus 10, which is 30 newtons to the right.
- e.g. A skydiver falling at a steady speed has her weight pulling down and the air pushing up with the same size.
- e.g. A bucket weighing 80 newtons is held still. Your hand pulls up with 80 newtons, so the forces are balanced.
### Why a line can never be pulled straight · *defined*
**Definition:** A line that holds a weight in its middle must pull on its ends more strongly the flatter it gets. The pull in the line is only equal to the weight when the line slopes at 30 degrees. Flatter than that, the pull is bigger than the weight.

Hang a 5 newton bottle in the middle of a line. The line bends into a V shape. Each side of the V pulls up and sideways on the bottle. Only the upward part of each pull helps to hold the bottle up. The two upward parts must add up to the bottle's weight, 5 newtons. If the line is steep, each side has a large upward part, so it need not pull hard. If the line is nearly flat, each side points mostly sideways, and its upward part is only a thin slice of its pull. To get 2.5 newtons of upward part from a nearly flat side, the side has to pull very hard. Measure the sag of a line 1 metre long. A sag of 25 centimetres makes each end pull about 6 newtons. A sag of 10 centimetres makes each end pull about 13 newtons. A sag of 1 centimetre needs about 125 newtons. A perfectly straight line would need an endless pull. That is why no washing line can be pulled perfectly straight with a weight on it. Engineers use this idea when they build suspension bridges. They let the main cables sag, often by about one tenth of the span, because a flatter cable would need to be stronger and would pull its towers harder.
- e.g. A wet saree on a washing line makes the line dip, however hard you pull the ends.
- e.g. A tightrope walker's rope sags where she stands, and the rope's ends are fixed to strong posts, because the pull on them is much more than her weight.
- e.g. A suspension footbridge across a river has cables that sag in a gentle curve, not a straight line.
### How sure is a number? Reading error and spread · *defined*
**Definition:** No measurement is exact. Every reading has a small reading error, and repeated readings spread out a little. To say how much we trust a number, we show the spread and choose a typical value.

Measure the same pull ten times and you will get slightly different numbers. Your eye sits a little higher or lower. The band is a little tired. The bottle swings a tiny bit. This is normal. It does not mean you are careless. The right thing to do is to measure several times and look at all the numbers. Line them up from smallest to largest. The smallest and largest show the spread, also called the range. The middle value is a good typical value. Suppose ten readings are 4.7, 4.8, 4.9, 5.0, 5.0, 5.0, 5.0, 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3 newtons. The typical value is 5.0 newtons and the spread is from 4.7 to 5.3, which is 0.6 newtons. We can write this as '5.0 newtons, give or take about 0.3'. It is more honest than one number alone. Also, do not write more digits than your tool can give. A gauge with marks every half newton cannot tell you 3.27 newtons.
- e.g. Ten children each read the same hanging bag on a gauge and get numbers within half a newton of each other.
- e.g. A cricketer's bat speed, a doctor's blood pressure and a shopkeeper's weighing all come with a small give and take.
- e.g. Scientists who measure the pull of gravity write numbers like 9.80 newtons for each kilogram, with a small 'give or take' printed after them.
### Accurate and precise are not the same · *defined*
**Definition:** Accurate means close to the true value. Precise means giving nearly the same reading each time. A tool can be precise without being accurate, if it has a zero error.

Picture a target with a bullseye. If your darts all land close together in the outer ring, you are precise but not accurate. If they land all around the bullseye, you are accurate on average but not precise. If they land close together on the bullseye, you are both. A kitchen scale shows the same idea. Put a sealed 500 gram packet on it. If it reads 508, 508, 509, the scale is precise. It gives nearly the same number every time. But it is not accurate, because the true value is 500. It may have a zero error, which means it starts a few grams too high. A zero error is the same in every reading. Repeating the reading does not remove it, but checking the zero and subtracting does. A reading error is different. It is random, a little up or a little down, and repeating readings and taking the middle value does help. So there are two kinds of mistake, and each needs its own cure. Check against something known to find zero errors, and repeat to tame reading errors.
- e.g. A bathroom scale that always shows 1 kilogram with nobody on it has a zero error of 1 kilogram.
- e.g. A cheap ruler printed a little too short makes every length too big, however carefully you read it.
- e.g. Two careful children reading a gauge from slightly different eye positions get slightly different readings. That is reading error.
### A calibration graph: reading between the points · *defined*
**Definition:** A calibration graph is made by testing a gauge with loads you already know. A line through the points then lets you read the size of an unknown load. Finding a value between two known points is called interpolating.

A rubber band is not marked in newtons. But you can make it a forcemeter. Hang a known load, such as one 500 gram packet, which pulls with about 5 newtons. Measure the band's length. Then hang two packets, which pull with about 10 newtons, and measure again. Do the same for three packets. Plot the points on a graph, with the load along the bottom and the length up the side, and draw a smooth line through the middle of them. Now hang something unknown on the band and measure its length. Find that length on the side of the graph, go across to the line, and then go down to read the load. If the length falls between two of your points, you have interpolated. Be careful beyond your last point. A rubber band stops following a neat pattern when it is stretched a lot, so a guess past your last load is not trustworthy. A tool is only reliable inside the range where it was calibrated. A graph also tells you how good the gauge is. If the points fall on a line, the band stretches in even steps. If they curve, the steps get bigger or smaller, and the graph can still be used, because it follows the curve.
- e.g. Band lengths of 8, 12, 16 and 20.5 centimetres for 0, 5, 10 and 15 newtons. A band of 14 centimetres is halfway between 12 and 16, so its load is about 7.5 newtons.
- e.g. A shopkeeper's spring balance has its marks printed from exactly this kind of test.
- e.g. Scientists calibrate a new instrument by measuring things they already know, before they trust it on something new.
### Forces change when things move · *defined*
**Definition:** The pull that a hanging thing puts on its support is equal to its weight only when the thing is still or moving steadily. A thing that is speeding up or turning can pull harder or softer than its weight.

Hang a bottle on a gauge. When it hangs still, the gauge shows its weight. Now lift the gauge quickly, so the bottle speeds up. For that moment, the gauge reads more than the weight, because the string must also give the bottle its speed. Lower it fast, and the gauge reads less. The same thing happens in a lift. When a lift starts to go up, the floor pushes you harder for a moment, and a bathroom scale under your feet would read more. When it starts to go down, it reads less. A swinging bottle shows it too. Let a 5 newton bottle swing down from the side, about 60 degrees from hanging, and at the bottom of the swing the string pulls with about 10 newtons. The bottle is not just hanging there. The string has to turn the bottle's path upwards, and that takes extra pull. A sudden jerk can be even stronger. Pull a cotton thread slowly and it holds. Jerk it, and it snaps, because for an instant the pull is far bigger than a steady pull would be. This is why engineers test bridges and ropes with moving loads, not only with still ones.
- e.g. A bathroom scale in a lift reads a little more as the lift starts going up and a little less as it starts going down.
- e.g. A child on a swing pulls hardest on the chains at the bottom of each swing.
- e.g. A bag dropped a short way and caught on a rope tugs much harder than the same bag hung gently.
### Inside a digital scale: how bending becomes a number · *defined*
**Definition:** A digital scale measures force with a strain gauge. This is a thin metal pattern stuck on a metal block. The weight bends the block a tiny bit. The pattern stretches, and a chip reads the change in its electrical resistance as a force.

A spring balance shows force by how far a spring stretches. A digital scale does the same job in a different way. Inside is a block of metal called a load cell. When you put something on the scale, the block bends, by less than the width of a hair. A thin zig-zag of metal foil is glued on the block. When the block bends, the foil stretches slightly. A stretched foil is a little longer and thinner, and electricity finds it a little harder to pass through. A chip measures this tiny change and turns it into a number on the screen. The first practical strain gauges of this kind were made in the late 1930s. The same idea weighs a lorry on a weighbridge. Under the platform are several strong load cells, and the chip adds their readings. A sugar mill weighs a lorry of cane on the way in and again on the way out. The difference is the cane. Your phone has a tiny sensor of another kind. It holds a very small block on silicon springs, and it senses how the block moves, so it knows when you turn or shake it.
- e.g. A kitchen scale, a bathroom scale and a vegetable shop scale all use load cells.
- e.g. A weighbridge at a sugar mill, a port or a mandi weighs lorries by measuring the bend of strong load cells.
- e.g. A phone knows which way is down because a tiny block inside it is pulled by gravity, and its springs show how far.
### A water surface pulls: surface tension and a button on a balance · *defined*
**Definition:** The surface of water acts like a thin skin that pulls along itself. This pull is called surface tension. For water it is about 0.07 newtons for every metre of edge, and soap makes it smaller.

A steel needle laid very gently on water can float, although steel is denser than water. The needle rests on the water's skin. The particles of water attract each other. Inside the water, each particle is pulled equally in all directions. At the surface, each particle has water only on one side, so it is pulled sideways along the surface and inwards. The surface behaves as if it were a stretched skin. We measure it as a force along a length. For water, the force is about 0.07 newtons for every metre of edge. A flat plastic disc 3 centimetres across has an edge of about 9 centimetres. A water surface can hold it with about a hundredth of a newton at most, which is the weight of about one gram. In 1891, a German woman named Agnes Pockels measured this kind of pull in her kitchen with a button hung from a simple balance. She added weights to the other side until the button was pulled off the water. Soap makes the pull much smaller, which is why a soapy surface cannot hold a needle. This is also why soap helps wash things. The soapy water spreads and gets into small gaps.
- e.g. A drop of water on a leaf forms a bead, because its skin pulls it into a ball.
- e.g. A water strider walks on the surface of a pond without sinking. She spreads her weight over the long edge of her legs.
- e.g. Dropping a little soap on a water surface with floating pepper makes the pepper rush to the edges, because the surface pull is weaker in the middle.
### Light pushes · *defined*
**Definition:** Light carries a push. When sunlight lands on a surface, it presses on it with a very small force called radiation pressure. A big, thin, shiny sheet in space can be pushed by sunlight alone.

It is easy to believe that light has no push, because it has no weight. But light does carry momentum, and when it is absorbed or reflected, it pushes. In 1619, the astronomer Johannes Kepler wrote that comet tails point away from the Sun, and he guessed that sunlight might be pushing them. In 1873, James Clerk Maxwell worked out from his theory of light that light must push. In 1900, Pyotr Lebedev in Russia measured it. In 1901, Ernest Nichols and Gordon Hull in the United States measured it too, with a twisting wire. The push is extremely small. Sunlight at the distance of the Earth pushes on a sheet of one square metre with only about five millionths of a newton, which is the weight of a grain of sand. But in space there is almost no air or friction. In 2010, Japan launched IKAROS, a spacecraft with a thin square sail, which used sunlight to push itself through space. In 2019, the Planetary Society's LightSail 2 used sunlight to raise its orbit around the Earth. The tails of comets, too, are blown by the Sun, mostly by a stream of particles from the Sun and by light.
- e.g. A solar sail is a very thin, very light sheet that catches the push of sunlight.
- e.g. Sunlight on a square sail 14 metres on each side would push with about one thousandth of a newton, enough to move a spacecraft slowly but steadily.
- e.g. The pressure of light inside a very big star helps to hold the star up against its own weight.
### A small magnet beats the whole Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** The electric force, which holds atoms together and makes magnets, is far stronger than gravity. A fridge magnet can hold a paper clip against the pull of the entire Earth.

The Earth has a mass of about six million million million million kilograms. Its gravity pulls the paper clip down. But a small magnet, held a centimetre above the clip, pulls the clip up and wins. That tells us something. Between two atoms, the electric and magnetic force is enormously stronger than the pull of gravity. Between two protons, the electric push is about a million million million million million million times bigger than their gravity. Gravity seems strong to us only because the Earth is so huge. All its atoms pull together, and there is no cancelling, because gravity only pulls. Electric forces come as both pushes and pulls, and in everyday things they cancel almost exactly. So what we feel as pushing and pulling, such as the stretch of a band, the grip of friction or the stiffness of a table, is the electric force at work between atoms. Scientists still do not know why gravity is so much weaker than the other forces. This is one of the open questions of physics.
- e.g. A magnet lifts a paper clip off a table, against the pull of the whole planet.
- e.g. Your hand does not sink through a table. The electric push between atoms is much stronger than your weight.
- e.g. A sheet of paper rubbed on hair can lift a tiny scrap of paper against the Earth's pull.
### Instruments made in India: Anna Mani and checking every copy · *defined*
**Definition:** A measuring instrument is only useful if every copy of it gives the same answer. So instruments must be built to a standard and checked against it.

Anna Mani was an Indian physicist, born in 1918 in Peermade in Kerala. She studied physics in Madras, and later studied the instruments used for measuring weather. After 1947, she worked at the India Meteorological Department in Pune. She is remembered for helping to make weather instruments in India, so that the country did not need to buy them all from abroad. Weather instruments measure forces and what forces do. A wind meter spins faster when the wind pushes harder. A rain gauge collects water to be weighed or measured. Instruments of this kind are placed in hundreds of stations, in places as far apart as hill towns and islands. For the numbers to be comparable, every copy of an instrument has to read the same when it meets the same wind or the same rain. So each one is checked against a standard before it is sent out, and checked again from time to time. This is the same lesson as the shopkeeper's scale and the kilogram standard. A number from one place can only be compared with a number from another place if both were checked against the same thing.
- e.g. Weather stations across India report wind, rain and sunshine, using instruments that have to agree with each other.
- e.g. A new thermometer is checked in ice water and in boiling water, two places where the true temperature is known.
- e.g. Legal Metrology officers check shop scales against standard weights.
### Is there one true value behind all the readings? · *idea*
When we take ten readings of a pull, we usually imagine that there is one true size of the pull, and our readings scatter around it. Most scientists work with this picture. They try to get closer to the true value with better tools and more readings. But at the size of single atoms, quantum physics says something stranger. Some pairs of things, such as a particle's exact position and its exact speed, cannot both have sharp values at the same time. The more sharply you pin down one, the less sharp the other becomes. This is not a flaw in the tools. It is how nature seems to be. In physics, an observer means anything that takes a measurement, such as a detector. It does not mean a person or a mind. How this should be understood is still argued about.
- e.g. Ten readings of a bottle's pull on a band scatter around 5 newtons.
- e.g. At the size of a single atom, the more exactly you fix where a particle is, the less exactly you can fix how fast it is moving.
- **Most working scientists:** There is a true value, and good measurements get closer to it. The spread tells you how close you are.
- **Quantum physicists:** For very small things, some measurable quantities do not have sharp values before they are measured. The spread is partly built into nature.
- **Philosophers of science:** We only ever meet our measurements, not the thing itself. Whether a 'true value' exists behind them is a question that physics alone does not settle.

## Mostly agreed
- A force has a direction as well as a size. *How we know:* Pulling a door open and pushing it shut use the same size of force and have opposite results. Scientists draw every force as an arrow for this reason.
- Forces along one line add up, with opposite forces taking away from each other. *How we know:* Hook two forcemeters together and pull. Each shows the same number. Add a second helper on one side and the net force is the difference of the pulls, which matches how the thing moves.
- A line that holds a weight in its middle pulls harder on its ends the flatter it is. *How we know:* The rule is worked out from the angles of the line. For a 5 newton bottle on a 1 metre line, each end pulls with about 6 newtons at a sag of 25 centimetres and about 13 newtons at a sag of 10 centimetres, and a gauge at the end shows the same trend. Engineers use the same rule when they design cables and give suspension bridges a sag.
- No measurement is exact, and repeated readings spread out a little. *How we know:* Measure one pull ten times and you get several slightly different numbers. Every field of science reports measurements with a small give or take.
- A tool can only be trusted inside the range where it was checked against known things. *How we know:* A rubber band follows a neat pattern for small loads and stops following it when stretched a lot. A forcemeter pulled past its range stays stretched and reads wrongly afterwards.
- The pull on a thing's support is the same as its weight only when the thing is still or moving steadily. *How we know:* A bathroom scale in a lift reads a little more when the lift starts upwards and a little less when it starts downwards. A swinging bottle stretches a band further at the bottom of the swing.
- Light pushes on what it hits, with a very small force. *How we know:* Lebedev in 1900 and Nichols and Hull in 1901 measured the push of light on a hanging vane. Solar sails in space, such as IKAROS in 2010, have been moved by sunlight.

## How we found out
**Agnes Pockels (1862 to 1935), a German scientist who taught herself physics, and Lord Rayleigh, who helped to publish her work, She wrote her letter in January 1891, and Nature printed it in 1891.** In the late 1800s, many scientists treated the pull of a water surface as one fixed number for clean water. Agnes Pockels was born in 1862 in Venice and grew up in Braunschweig in Germany. Girls were not allowed to go to a university, so she learned physics from her younger brother's textbooks. In her kitchen, while washing dishes, she noticed that grease changed how a water surface behaved. She hung a button from one arm of a simple balance and added weights to the other side, until the button was pulled off the water. This measured a tiny force without a laboratory. She found that a clean surface pulled steadily, and that a film of oil made the pull much smaller. In January 1891, she wrote to the English scientist Lord Rayleigh. He saw how good her work was, and he helped to get her letter printed in the journal Nature that year. Later, scientists used troughs like hers to study thin films.  
*What people believed before:* Many people treated the surface of water as a single fixed thing, with one pull that did not depend on what was on it. Pockels showed that a thin film of oil changes the pull a lot. She did it by measuring it, with a button, a balance and a basin of water.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A line holding a weight only has to pull as hard as the weight.** (Many people, who think that a support is as strong as the load it holds.; *rejected*)  
  For: A hook holding a hanging bucket needs to pull up with exactly the bucket's weight. That is true when the support pulls straight up.  
  Against: A line that slopes sideways pulls sideways too. Only the upward part holds the load. So a flat line has to pull much harder than the weight. In the Sagging Line test, a 5 newton bottle made the gauge read 13 newtons or more.
- **A tighter rope is a stronger rope.** (Many people who tie ropes, and Murugan Anna in the story, before he changed his mind. It is a made-up character.; *rejected*)  
  For: A slack rope sags, flaps and looks weak. A tight rope looks firm and strong.  
  Against: Tightening a rope with a load on it raises the pull in it a lot. It is the pull in the rope that breaks it. A rope with a good sag, or a prop in the middle, pulls less and lasts longer.
- **If you repeat a measurement carefully, you should get exactly the same number every time.** (Many students, and some textbooks that show only one clean result.; *rejected*)  
  For: Machines that do the same thing again give nearly the same answer. A careful person should do the same.  
  Against: Your eye position, a small wobble, a tired band and the way you hold the tool all change a little each time. Even the best instruments give a small spread. A set of readings with no spread at all usually means the tool is too crude to notice it.
- **A scale that shows more digits is more accurate.** (Many shoppers and buyers of gadgets.; *rejected*)  
  For: Seeing 502.37 grams feels more exact than 500 grams.  
  Against: The number of digits tells you how finely the scale shows its answer. It does not tell you how close the answer is to the truth. A scale with a zero error shows many wrong digits. Only a check against a known weight shows how accurate it is.
- **Your weight on a scale is always the same, wherever you stand and however you move.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: On a quiet day, standing still, the scale shows the same number every time.  
  Against: The scale really measures how hard you push on it. Bend your knees fast and it dips, then rises. In a lift that starts going up, it reads more. In a lift that starts going down, it reads less. On the Moon it would read much less.
- **Light cannot push anything, because it has no weight.** (Many people, until the late 1800s.; *rejected*)  
  For: A torch beam does not seem to move a feather. Light has no mass that we can weigh.  
  Against: Maxwell's theory of light, from 1873, said that light must push. Lebedev in 1900 and Nichols and Hull in 1901 measured the push. The push is tiny, which is why we do not feel it. In space, with no air, sunlight can move a thin sail.
- **There is one true size of every pull, and good measuring finds it.** (Most working scientists, as a useful way of working.; *debated*)  
  For: Better tools and more readings do tighten the spread. This lets us predict and build bridges that stand.  
  Against: At the scale of atoms, quantum physics says that some quantities do not have sharp values until they are measured. Philosophers of science also point out that we only ever meet our measurements. Whether a true value exists behind them is still argued about.

## Roots
We have drawn forces as arrows, hung a bottle on a sagging line and measured the hold of water on a coin. Now we ask what these measurements tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A 5-newton bottle on a nearly flat line made each end pull with about 13 newtons. A pull that holds something up can be far bigger than its weight. That bigger pull hides inside the line. So the size of a force is not set by the load alone.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a support only has to be as strong as the weight it carries, so we read the strength of a rope off its load. That leaves out the angle, which can change the pull by a large amount.  
*Evidence:* In the Sagging Line test, the band gauge showed a pull of about 13 newtons at each end when the sag was 10 centimetres, for a bottle of about 5 newtons. If your own numbers differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Your muscles often pull many times harder than the weight you hold, because of the angles of your bones. Your body is full of strong pulls that go unnoticed.
- *what is life:* Many living beings use curves to keep pulls small. A spider's web hangs in loose curves, and a branch bends in the wind instead of staying stiff.
- *what is this world:* A thing that looks calm can be full of strong pulls. Rest is often a balance of big forces, and the angles decide how big they are.

Every pull we measured has the same source in the end. The line, the band and the skin of water pull because their atoms hold on to each other with the electric force. That one force shows itself as a rope's tension, a band's stretch and a water surface's hold. The pull of the Earth is the odd one out, and nobody yet knows why gravity is so much weaker than the rest.

- **Sona Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 6.55:** The Buddha reminded a musician named Sona of his lute. If its strings are too tight, they snap and give no music. If they are too slack, they give no music either. When the strings are tuned evenly, the lute sings. (A line pulled too tight carries too much pull, and one with no sag cannot hold at all. A sag in the right place makes it last.)
- **Tao Te Ching 76:** What is stiff and unbending breaks. What is soft and yielding lasts. A tree that will not bend is easily broken. (The tight rope snapped, and the rope with a curve held through the monsoon.)
- Science (settled): The electric force between atoms explains tension, stretch and surface tension. Gravity is the one everyday force that is different.
- Science (open): Why gravity is so much weaker than the other forces is not known.
- Other view, Many engineers: Safe structures come from strong materials and large safety margins. If every part is strong enough, the angles do not matter much, and talk of balance is poetry.
- Practice, Feel the pull grow as the line goes flat (3 min): Hold a thread between your two hands. Hang a key in the middle. Start with your hands close together, so the thread sags deeply. Slowly move your hands apart and feel the pull grow in your fingers. Breathe in slowly three times. Then bring your hands back together and feel the pull ease.
- Ask: A rope that looks calm and still can be pulling very hard inside. Is a still thing doing nothing?
- Ask: If a force depends on the angle as well as the load, is the force a fact about the load, or about the whole arrangement?
- Ask: Which would you trust more, a rope pulled tight or a rope with a curve in it, and why?

## Story: The Rope That Would Not Be Straight
*A made-up story. The village, Thenmozhi, Mallika, Paati and Murugan Anna are imagined. The science is real. A line with a weight in its middle must pull harder on its ends the flatter it is, and a line with a good sag pulls less. Casuarina plantations really line the Tamil Nadu coast, and the northeast monsoon really brings rain from October to December.*

Thenmozhi lived in a fishing village on the coast south of Chennai, where the sand was grey and the casuarina trees all leaned the same way, away from the wind. When the boats came in, the nets came out, heavy with seawater and weed. Her mother, Mallika, and the other women hung them to dry on a long rope between two tall casuarina poles at the edge of the village.

That October, the northeast monsoon arrived early. The nets never dried properly, and they stayed heavy. Three times in two weeks, the rope snapped in the night. Three times, a whole row of nets fell into the wet sand. Amma spent two days picking them out, knot by knot, with her lips pressed tight.

Murugan Anna owned the biggest boat on the beach, and he settled most arguments there. He looked at the broken rope and said, 'The rope was slack. A slack rope is a lazy rope. Pull it tight, and it will not fail.'

So the next day, six men tied a new, thicker rope between the poles. They looped one end round a stout stump and heaved on a wooden lever until the rope was as tight as a veena string. When Murugan Anna plucked it, it hummed. 'There,' he said. 'A rope that sings is a rope that holds.'

Thenmozhi was ten. She watched from the sand with her chin on her knees, and she saw something that nobody else was looking at. Even the tight rope was not straight. With a few nets on it, the middle dipped. And at home, Amma's washing line did the same. However hard Amma pulled it, one wet saree bent it into a gentle smile.

'Why can't it ever be straight?' Thenmozhi asked her grandmother that evening. Paati was sorting dried chillies on a mat. 'Ask the saree, not me,' Paati said. But she said it in a way that meant, go and find out.

So Thenmozhi found out. She tied a thread across the window of their hut, from one iron grill to the other. In the middle, she hung a plastic bottle of water. Near one end, she tied in a wide rubber band, because a rubber band stretches more when it is pulled harder. To turn the band into a gauge, she hung Paati's one-kilo packets of rice on it, one after another. She marked its length on the wall with a pencil. One packet, two packets, three.

Then she hung the bottle on the thread and began to pull the thread flatter. The flatter she made it, the longer the band grew. When the thread was only two fingers from straight, the band was longer than it had been with three packets of rice hanging on it. Yet the bottle weighed much less than one packet. She tried it again, and again, to be sure. Every time the band said the same thing. The flatter the line, the harder it pulled.

Thenmozhi sat back on her heels. The bottle had not become heavier. Only the angle had changed. The thread was not just holding up a bottle. It was pulling against itself, and the flatter it got, the harder it pulled.

Next morning the nets hung on the rope again, and the rope was singing again, and the sky was the colour of wet slate. Thenmozhi walked up to Murugan Anna with her bottle, her thread and her band. 'Anna, may I show you something?' she asked. Some of the men laughed. A girl with a rubber band!

Paati stood at the back with her arms crossed. 'Let the child show,' she said in her sharpest voice. Nobody argued with Paati.

Thenmozhi tied her thread between two posts of the drying rack and hung the bottle in the middle. She handed one end to Murugan Anna. 'Pull it flat, Anna,' she said. 'As flat as you can.' He pulled. The band near his hand crept longer. He pulled more. The band grew long and thin, and he stopped and stared at it.

'The bottle is small,' said Thenmozhi. 'But your thread is pulling harder than three packets of rice. A rope with all the wet nets on it is doing the same, to the poles.' Murugan Anna was quiet for the length of three waves coming in. Then he looked up at the rope between the casuarinas, humming in the wind with the nets hanging from its middle.

'It is not the nets that break the rope,' he said slowly. 'It is me, pulling it tight.' He turned to the men. 'Untie it.'

That day they tied the rope again, four hands higher on each pole, with a deep, lazy curve in the middle. They set a forked pole in the centre to hold it up, so each half carried half of the nets. The women laughed that the rope now looked like a smile. 'A lazy rope,' said Murugan Anna. And then, to everyone's surprise, 'A wise rope.'

The monsoon went on for six more weeks. Every evening the nets hung on the smiling rope, heavy as ever. Not once did it break. Thenmozhi kept her band on the wall of the hut, with its pencil marks for one, two and three packets of rice. Sometimes, when the wind was strong and the rope dipped a little deeper, she looked at it and thought of the thread across the window. The flatter the line, the harder it pulls. And nobody, however strong, can pull it straight.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a line with a weight in its middle is pulled upwards and sideways. Only the upward part of each side's pull holds the weight up, so when the line is nearly flat, each side must pull many times the weight. Pulling the rope tighter made the pull bigger, not safer. A rope with a good sag, or with a prop in the middle, pulls the poles and itself far less. Thenmozhi also did something scientists do. She made a gauge from a rubber band and checked it against loads she already knew, the rice packets.

- Ask: Why does a line that holds a weight pull harder on its ends the flatter it gets?
- Ask: Why can nobody pull a washing line perfectly straight with a wet saree in the middle?
- Ask: Two ways of making a line hold a heavy load while pulling less on its ends are to let it sag more and to add a prop in the middle. Why does each one work?
- Ask: How can you turn a rubber band into a gauge for forces, and why does it need loads that you already know?
- Ask: A rope holds in a light wind, but breaks in a gale. Why might the same rope have a bigger pull in it when the wind blows?

### e1 · The Sagging Line: how hard does each end pull? (45 min)

  - Ask: What do you notice about the numbers of grains from try to try? (good: They are close but not the same.; One try was much lower, maybe because the coin tilted.; They are around the same number.)
  - Note: Expect somewhere between 20 and 100 grains. Numbers that are close but not equal are normal. If one try is far from the others, the coin probably tilted or touched the water unevenly. Repeat that try. Keep the middle value of the three.

  *Child mode:* Add a few drops of soap to the water. Stir gently. Test the coin three times.
  - Note: Soapy water is a bit slippery, so the surface pulls less and fewer grains are needed. Expect about half or fewer. If there is foam on top, skim it off with the spoon before the test.
  - Think: Why might soap change the hold of water on the coin? What does soap do when it is washing a dish?

  - Ask: How many of these water-holds would make the weight of an apple? (good: About a hundred or more, because the water holds with about a hundredth of a newton or less.; Well over a hundred, because the water holds with only a few thousandths of a newton.; Soapy water would need even more.)
  - Check: The coin needed 30 grains of rice to come off plain water. One grain weighs 0.02 grams. What is the pull in newtons, if 1 gram pulls with about 0.01 newtons? → **About 0.006 newtons**

## Worksheets
### Forces, arrows and sagging lines (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which one describes a force completely?  
   Options: 5 newtons to the left / 5 newtons / To the left  
   **Answer:** 5 newtons to the left. *Why:* A force needs both a size and a direction. '5 newtons' has no direction. 'To the left' has no size. Only '5 newtons to the left' has both.
2. (truefalse) In a tug of war, both teams pull with 400 N. This means that the rope must be standing still.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Balanced forces mean the rope does not change its motion. It may be still, or it may be moving at a steady speed. A steady speed also has a net force of zero.
3. (fill) A cart is pushed right with 9 N. Friction pulls it left with 4 N. The net force is ____ N to the right.  
   **Answer:** ['5']. *Why:* 9 take away 4 is 5. The bigger force wins, and the net force is the difference, pointing the way of the bigger force.
4. (match) Match each word to its meaning.  
   **Answer:** Span → the distance between the two places that hold a line; Sag → how far the middle of the line dips; Tension → the pull along a rope or string held tight; Net force → what is left after adding and taking away all the forces. *Why:* Span is the gap between the supports. Sag is the dip in the middle. Tension is the pull inside the line. The net force tells you which way a thing will start to move.
5. (mcq) A 5 N bottle hangs in the middle of a line. You pull the line flatter. What happens to the pull at each end?  
   Options: It gets bigger / It gets smaller / It stays at 2.5 N  
   **Answer:** It gets bigger. *Why:* A flatter line points more sideways. Only the upward part of the pull can hold the bottle, so the line must pull harder. A wrong pick of 2.5 N often comes from thinking that the ends just share the weight.
6. (sort) Are the forces on each thing balanced or not balanced?  
   **Answer:** a book resting on a table → Balanced; a car starting to move → Not balanced; a cyclist at a steady speed → Balanced; a skydiver just after jumping → Not balanced; a lamp hanging still → Balanced; a bucket speeding down a well → Not balanced. *Why:* A thing that is still, or moving at a steady speed, has balanced forces. A thing that speeds up, slows down or changes direction has a net force.
7. (write) Hari says: 'To stop my washing line sagging, I will pull it as tight as I can.' What would you tell Hari? Use the ideas of pull and angle.  
   **Answer:** A line with a wet cloth in the middle can never be pulled straight. The flatter you pull it, the harder each end pulls, so the line may break or pull the hooks out. It is better to let the line sag a little, or to tie the ends higher and add a prop in the middle.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a flatter line pulls harder on its ends, because only the upward part of the pull holds the cloth. It offers a fix, such as more sag or a prop.

   **Answer:** About 9 N. *Why:* 15 cm is three quarters of the way from 12 cm to 16 cm. Three quarters of the way from 5 N to 10 N is about 8.75 N, which is about 9 N. The answer 15 N mixes up centimetres with newtons.

   **Answer:** 2.6. *Why:* 13 divided by 5 is 2.6. The pull at each end is about 2.6 times the weight, although the bottle has not got any heavier.
11. (truefalse) A kitchen scale shows 3 g with nothing on it. This is called a zero error.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Every reading will be 3 g too big. This kind of mistake is the same each time. You can find it by checking with nothing on the scale, and you can fix it by taking 3 g off each reading.

   **Answer:** A book with an arrow 8 cm long pointing down, labelled 'weight 8 N', and an arrow 8 cm long pointing up, labelled 'push of the table 8 N'.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows two arrows of the same length, one pointing down and one pointing up, so the forces balance.

   Options: 0.6 N / 5.0 N / 10 N  
   **Answer:** 0.6 N. *Why:* The spread is the gap between the smallest and the largest reading. 5.3 take away 4.7 is 0.6. The answer 5.0 is the typical value, not the spread.
14. (count) A 5 N bottle is swung from the side by about 60 degrees. At the bottom of the swing, the string pulls with about double the weight. About how many newtons?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* Double 5 N is 10 N. The string must turn the bottle's path upwards at the bottom, which takes extra pull.

   **Answer:** No. Her weight has not changed. The lift is speeding up upwards, so the floor must push her up harder than her weight for a moment. The scale measures the push on it, so it shows a bigger number.. *Why:* A strong answer says the scale measures the push of the person on it, and that a thing which is speeding up needs more push than its weight.
16. (mcq) How does a digital kitchen scale measure a force?

   **Answer:** A thin metal pattern on a metal block stretches slightly when the block bends under the load. *Why:* A digital scale uses a strain gauge, a thin metal pattern that stretches when the block under the tray bends. A spring and a pointer are how a spring balance works. Nobody sits inside the scale.

   **Answer:** More than 10 N. *Why:* A flatter line points more sideways, so only a thin slice of its pull holds the bottle up. The pull has to be bigger. The answer 'exactly 10 N' is only true for the 30 degree line.

   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* The band stretches 12 cm for 15 N. That is 4 cm for every 5 N. So at 5 N it is 8 plus 4, which is 12 cm.

   Options: Swinging through the bottom of its path / Hanging perfectly still / Being lowered slowly at a steady speed  
   **Answer:** Swinging through the bottom of its path. *Why:* At the bottom of the swing, the string has to turn the bottle's path upwards, so it pulls more than the weight. When a bottle hangs still or moves at a steady speed, the pull equals the weight.
10. (write) Suspension bridges have cables that sag in a gentle curve instead of being pulled straight. Explain why, using the idea of pull and angle.  
   **Answer:** A straight cable would have to pull enormously hard to hold the weight of the bridge, because it would point mostly sideways. A cable that sags points more upwards, so it pulls less, and the towers and the cable need not be so strong.. *Why:* A strong answer links the flatness of the cable to a bigger pull, and says that a sag lowers the pull for the same load.
11. (perspective) Two children have ten readings of one pull and want to give one number.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer prefers the middle value, because one extreme reading can be a fluke. A test could be to repeat the ten readings again and see which method gives the same number more often.. *Why:* A typical value should not depend on one odd reading. The middle value uses all the readings and is not changed by one extreme. Testing both methods on a second set of ten readings shows which is steadier.
12. (count) A coin on water is held with about 0.006 N. An apple pulls down with 1 N. About how many times bigger is the apple's weight?  
   **Answer:** 170. *Why:* 1 divided by 0.006 is about 167, which is about 170. So the apple's weight is about 170 times bigger.
13. (mcq) Why can a small fridge magnet lift a paper clip against the pull of the whole Earth?

   **Answer:** Between atoms, the electric and magnetic force is far stronger than gravity. *Why:* Gravity looks strong to us only because the Earth is so big. Between two small things, the magnetic and electric force is vastly stronger. The Earth does pull on the clip, and the magnet wins anyway.
14. (truefalse) Light has no weight, so it cannot push anything.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Light carries a push, called radiation pressure. It is tiny, but in space sunlight can move a thin sail.

### Think like a measurer (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A rope holds a 100 N load in its middle. Each side slopes 10 degrees below the level. Roughly how hard does each side pull?  
   Options: About 290 N / About 100 N / About 50 N  
   **Answer:** About 290 N. *Why:* Only the upward part of each side's pull holds the load. At 10 degrees, that part is only about a sixth of the pull, so each side pulls about three times the load. The answer 50 N would be right for a rope hanging straight down, with two ropes sharing the load.
2. (count) A 1 metre line has a sag of 5 cm, and each end pulls about 25 times the load. The load is 4 N. What is the pull at each end, in newtons?  
   **Answer:** 100. *Why:* 25 times 4 is 100. The bottle weighs only 4 N, and the flat line makes each end pull with 100 N.
3. (write) Anjali's gauge reads 0.2 N with nothing hanging on it. She says it is small enough to ignore. A bag makes it read 3.1 N. What should she do, and why?  
   **Answer:** She should not ignore it. She should either reset the zero, or take 0.2 N off every reading, so the bag is 2.9 N. A zero error is the same each time, so leaving it makes every reading too big.. *Why:* A strong answer names the zero error and says it must be subtracted from all readings, or that the zero must be reset.
4. (mcq) Five readings of a pull are 4.9, 5.1, 5.0, 5.0 and 5.4 N. Which report is most sensible?  
   Options: About 5.0 N, with readings from 4.9 to 5.4 N / Exactly 5.08 N / 5.4 N, the largest, to be safe  
   **Answer:** About 5.0 N, with readings from 4.9 to 5.4 N. *Why:* The middle value is 5.0 N, and the readings spread from 4.9 to 5.4 N. Writing 5.08 N pretends the tool is finer than it is. Reporting only the largest ignores the other readings.
5. (truefalse) A scale that shows three decimal places must be more accurate than one that shows one decimal place.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The number of decimal places shows how finely a scale shows its answer, not how close the answer is to the truth. A scale with a zero error shows many wrong digits.
6. (sort) A 5 N bottle hangs from a string. Is the pull of the string bigger than, smaller than or equal to the weight at each moment?

It is bigger when the bottle speeds up upwards or turns upwards. It is smaller when the bottle starts to speed up downwards.

Both can be right in different ways.. *Why:* Priya's view helps us work. Sam's view reminds us that a measurement is all we ever have. Good thinkers can hold both.

## Open questions
- **Why is gravity so much weaker than the other forces?** Scientists can measure how much weaker gravity is, about a million million million million million million times weaker than the electric force between two protons. Nobody knows why.
- **Is there a true value behind a set of readings, or only the readings?** In everyday life, a true value is a useful idea. At the size of atoms, quantum physics suggests that some quantities have no sharp value before they are measured. How to understand this is still argued about.
- **How small a force can we ever measure?** Tools get better every few years, but quantum physics sets a limit on how gently any measurement can be made. Scientists are still looking for clever ways to get past it.
- **How exactly does a gecko's foot hold on to a smooth wall with tiny forces?** Scientists agree that the forces between very many tiny hairs and the wall add up. They are still working on how the grip is released so quickly.
- **Why is the surface pull of water so large compared with most liquids?** The pull comes from the way water particles hold on to each other. The exact details of water's behaviour at its surface are still being studied.
- **Can sunlight alone take a spacecraft to the stars?** Small solar sails have flown in space, but a sail big enough and light enough to travel far has not been built. Whether we can build one is an open engineering question.

- A 1 metre line has a sag of 2 cm, and each end pulls about 12.5 times the load. The load is 8 N. Would a thread that breaks at a pull of 60 N hold? **No. The pull is 100 N.**. 12.5 times 8 is 100. The thread breaks at 60 N, and the pull is 100 N, so it would snap. Letting the line sag more would lower the pull. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A coin needed 30 grains of rice to pull it off plain water, and 12 grains to pull it off soapy water. Each grain weighs 0.02 grams. What is the difference in the pull, in grams? How many times bigger is the plain-water pull? **The difference is 0.36 grams. The plain-water pull is 2.5 times bigger.**. 30 times 0.02 is 0.6 grams. 12 times 0.02 is 0.24 grams. 0.6 take away 0.24 is 0.36 grams. 30 divided by 12 is 2.5. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In a drawing, 1 centimetre stands for 10 N. Team A's arrow in a tug of war is 4.5 centimetres long. Team B's arrow is 3.8 centimetres long. How many newtons does each team pull with? Who wins, and by how many newtons? **Team A pulls with 45 N and Team B with 38 N. Team A wins by 7 N.**. 4.5 times 10 is 45. 3.8 times 10 is 38. 45 take away 38 is 7. The net force is 7 N towards Team A. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L4`)
- Ten readings of a pull, in newtons, are 4.8, 5.0, 5.2, 4.9, 5.0, 5.1, 5.0, 4.7, 5.0 and 5.3. What is the spread? Add them up and divide by 10 to find the mean. Is it close to the middle value? **The spread is 0.6 N. The mean is 5.0 N, the same as the middle value.**. The smallest is 4.7 and the largest is 5.3. 5.3 take away 4.7 is 0.6. The sum is 50.0, and 50.0 divided by 10 is 5.0. The middle value of the ordered list is also 5.0. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A kitchen scale shows 3 grams with nothing on it. You put a mango on it and it shows 287 grams. Then you put on a second mango and the scale shows 571 grams. How much does each mango weigh? What does the second weighing tell you? **The first mango weighs 284 grams and the two mangoes weigh 568 grams together. The second mango weighs 284 grams.**. 287 take away 3 is 284. 571 take away 3 is 568. 568 take away 284 is 284. The two mangoes weigh the same. The zero error of 3 grams is taken away once from every reading. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- A lorry full of sugar cane weighs 31.7 tonnes on the weighbridge on the way in. After the cane is unloaded, it weighs 12.4 tonnes on the way out. How many tonnes of cane were on the lorry? How many kilograms is that? About how many newtons does that cane weigh? **19.3 tonnes, which is 19,300 kilograms. It weighs about 193,000 newtons.**. 31.7 take away 12.4 is 19.3 tonnes. One tonne is 1000 kilograms, so 19.3 tonnes is 19,300 kilograms. Each kilogram weighs about 10 newtons, so 19,300 times 10 is 193,000 newtons. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What two things does a full description of a force need? → A size and a direction.
- A cart is pushed right with 8 N and pulled left with 3 N. What is the net force? → 5 N to the right.
- What does balanced forces mean? → The forces cancel, so the net force is zero and the motion does not change.
- When a line holding a weight gets flatter, does the pull in the line go up or down? → Up.
- What is the typical value of a set of readings? → The middle value, once they are lined up in order.
- What is a zero error? → A mistake that is the same in every reading, because the tool does not start at zero.
- What do you need to make a calibration graph? → Known loads, and the band length for each.
- Does a bottle swinging through the bottom pull harder or softer than its weight? → Harder.
- What holds a coin on the surface of water? → Surface tension, the pull along the water's surface.
