# Symmetry in nature and art: why so many shapes have matching parts
`sci.patterns-symmetry.symmetry-in-nature.L3`

**Summary.** Fold a hibiscus flower's picture down the middle, and the two halves match. Turn a snowflake one-sixth of the way round, and it looks the same again. Both are kinds of symmetry: a shape has symmetry when a part of it is repeated by flipping or turning. In this unit you blow a raft of soap bubbles and find that, in the middle, most of them press into six-sided shapes that nobody drew. At the edge, where the push is not the same on every side, the bubbles come out lopsided. The same idea helps explain snowflakes, flowers, honeycomb and the kolams and rangolis people draw. You also find out why a flower's even shape helps her bees, why animals that walk or swim have a left and a right side, and why no living body is perfectly even.

**Intro.** *Look at a five-petalled flower, such as a periwinkle or a hibiscus. Now look at a kolam at a doorstep, or a rangoli at Diwali. A person drew the kolam carefully, turning it as she went. But who drew the flower? Nobody did. So how did the flower come out so even?* People have asked this for a very long time. In this unit you look for two kinds of symmetry: shapes that match when folded, and shapes that match when turned. Then you make even shapes appear with soap bubbles, without drawing them. That gives you a real answer to how nature makes symmetry without a plan, and why the evenness sometimes breaks.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can find a line of symmetry by folding a shape, and say whether a shape has no line, one line or many lines of symmetry.
1. I can find rotational symmetry by turning a shape, and count how many times it looks the same in one full turn (its order).
2. I can explain why bubbles pressed together in a raft come out six-sided in the middle and lopsided at the edge, using what I saw.
3. I can explain why a snowflake has six matching arms: the way water molecules join in ice, and all six arms growing in the same air.
4. I can say how a flower's symmetry helps her bees find her and land on her, and why animals that move forward have a left side and a right side.
5. I can draw a kolam or rangoli that has line symmetry and rotational symmetry, and point out both.
6. I can spot where symmetry breaks, such as at the edge of the bubble raft or in a plant beside a window, and say what was different on one side.

## Terms
- **Symmetry**: A shape has symmetry when one part of it is repeated by flipping it or turning it, so the shape still looks the same. *e.g.* A steel thali looks the same however far you turn it. A butterfly's left wing matches the right wing.
- **Line of symmetry**: A line that cuts a shape into two halves that match exactly when you fold along it. It is also called a mirror line. *e.g.* Fold a paper heart down the middle, and the two halves sit exactly on top of each other. The fold is its line of symmetry.
- **Line symmetry**: A shape has line symmetry when it has at least one line of symmetry. Some shapes have many lines. *e.g.* The letter A has one line of symmetry, down the middle. A square has four.
- **Rotational symmetry**: A shape has rotational symmetry when you can turn it part of the way round its centre and it looks exactly the same as before. *e.g.* A ceiling fan with three blades looks the same after one-third of a turn.
- **Order of rotational symmetry**: The number of times a shape looks exactly the same during one full turn. A shape that only matches itself at the end of a full turn has order 1, which means no rotational symmetry. *e.g.* A five-petalled periwinkle flower has order 5. The letter S has order 2.
- **Centre**: The point a shape turns around. In rotational symmetry, every repeated part is the same distance from the centre. *e.g.* The centre of a kolam is the dot in the middle. The arms of a snowflake all grow out from its centre.
- **Hexagon**: A flat shape with six straight sides. *e.g.* Each cell of a honeycomb is a hexagon. The squashed bubbles in the middle of a bubble raft are mostly hexagons too.
- **Soap film**: The very thin skin of soapy water that makes the wall of a bubble. The film pulls itself as tight as it can, so its walls stay as short as possible. *e.g.* When you blow a bubble, the soap film pulls into a round ball, the shape with the least skin for the air inside.
- **Molecule**: The smallest bit of a substance that is still that substance. It is made of atoms joined together. *e.g.* A water molecule is made of one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms. In ice, water molecules join up in rings of six.
- **Pollinator**: An animal that carries pollen from one flower to another, so the plant can make seeds. Bees, butterflies, moths, some birds and bats are pollinators. *e.g.* A bee visits a sunflower for nectar, and yellow pollen sticks to her hairy body. She carries it to the next sunflower.
- **Bilateral symmetry**: Two-sided symmetry: a body or a flower with one line of symmetry, so it has a left half and a right half that match. *e.g.* Your body has bilateral symmetry. So does a tulsi flower, a pea flower and a butterfly.
- **Radial symmetry**: Symmetry all the way round a centre, like the spokes of a wheel. The shape matches itself when turned, and it has several lines of symmetry. *e.g.* A hibiscus flower and a jellyfish have radial symmetry.
- **Kolam**: A pattern drawn on the ground at the doorstep, usually with rice flour, in Tamil Nadu and other parts of South India. A rangoli is a coloured floor pattern made in many parts of India. Many kolams and rangolis are drawn with symmetry. *e.g.* A pulli kolam is drawn around a grid of dots. The line loops round the dots without touching them.

## Explanations
### Two kinds of symmetry: folding and turning · *defined*
**Definition:** A shape has line symmetry if you can fold it along a line so that the two halves match exactly. A shape has rotational symmetry if you can turn it part of the way round its centre and it looks exactly the same.

The fold test finds lines of symmetry. The turn test finds rotational symmetry. A shape can have one kind, both kinds, or neither. A butterfly has one line of symmetry but no rotational symmetry: turn it upside down and it looks different. The letter S has no line of symmetry, but it looks the same after half a turn. A five-petalled flower has both: five lines of symmetry and rotational symmetry. Your hand has neither.
- e.g. A butterfly: one line of symmetry, no rotational symmetry.
- e.g. The letter S or the letter Z: no line of symmetry, but order 2 rotational symmetry.
- e.g. A square kolam with four matching loops: four lines of symmetry and order 4.
- e.g. Your hand: neither kind.
### Counting rotational symmetry: the order · *defined*
**Definition:** The order of rotational symmetry is the number of times a shape looks exactly the same during one full turn.

To count it, put a small mark on one corner of the shape, such as a bindi sticker. Turn the shape slowly round its centre. Count each time it looks exactly as it did at the start. Stop when the mark comes back to where it began. That count is the order. Every shape looks the same after one full turn, so a shape with order 1 has no rotational symmetry. A circle is special: it looks the same however little you turn it.
- e.g. A three-bladed ceiling fan: order 3.
- e.g. A periwinkle flower with five petals: order 5.
- e.g. A snowflake: order 6.
- e.g. A star fruit cut across: order 5.
### Why bubbles in a raft become six-sided · *defined*
**Definition:** A soap film pulls itself as tight as it can. When bubbles of the same size crowd together, their walls meet three at a time, at equal angles. Each bubble in the middle is pressed equally by about six neighbours, so it becomes a six-sided cell.

A single bubble floating in the air is round. A ball is the shape that holds the air inside with the least skin. When many bubbles touch, each wall is shared by two bubbles, and the films pull the walls short and flat. Where walls meet, they always meet in threes, never in fours. A Belgian scientist, Joseph Plateau, wrote this rule down in 1873, after years of studying soap films. Three walls that pull equally settle at equal angles, a third of a full turn apart. When every bubble is the same size and is pressed equally on every side, the shape that fits these rules is a hexagon. At the edge of the raft, a bubble has neighbours on one side and open water or the plate on the other. The pushes are not even, so the bubble is lopsided. A bubble much smaller than its neighbours usually has fewer than six walls, and a bigger one usually has more.
- e.g. In the middle of your raft, most bubbles have six walls.
- e.g. At the edge, bubbles have a curved side and fewer walls.
- e.g. Foam on a cup of filter coffee or in a sink of washing-up has the same three-wall meeting points.
### Why a snowflake has six matching arms · *defined*
**Definition:** A snowflake is a crystal of ice. In ice, water molecules join up in rings of six, so ice crystals grow in six directions. The six arms match because they all grow at the same time in the same air.

A snowflake starts as a tiny six-sided piece of ice high in a cloud. Water vapour, which is water as a gas, freezes onto its corners, and six arms grow out. As the snowflake falls, the air around it changes: a little warmer, a little colder, a little wetter, a little drier. Each change reaches all six arms at the same moment, because the snowflake is tiny. So when one arm grows a side branch, the other five grow the same branch. That is why the arms match without being joined by any plan. Snowflakes fall through slightly different air, so big branching snowflakes almost never look exactly the same. Most real snowflakes are also a little uneven, because they bump or because one side meets slightly different air. The scientist Ukichiro Nakaya grew snowflakes in a cold laboratory in Japan in the 1930s. He showed that the temperature and the moisture of the air decide whether a snowflake grows as flat plates, long needles or branching stars.
- e.g. A snowflake has six arms, never eight. A paper snowflake folded into eight is wrong!
- e.g. Frost on a cold window can grow in feathery branches for the same reason.
- e.g. Snow falls in winter in Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh.
### Why many flowers are symmetrical · *idea*
A plant cannot walk to another plant to share her pollen. She needs a pollinator, such as a bee, to visit and carry the pollen for her. Her flowers have to be easy for a bee to find, and to land on the right way. Many flowers solve this with symmetry. A flower's parts grow in rings around the tip of the bud, so petals often come in fives, as in hibiscus and periwinkle, or in threes, as in lilies and the flowers of onions. These round flowers can be visited from any side. Other flowers, such as tulsi, peas and orchids, have one line of symmetry. They have a lip at the bottom where a bee lands, always the same way up. Then the pollen touches the same place on every bee's body, and is carried to the same place in the next flower. Experiments show that honeybees can learn to tell symmetrical patterns from lopsided ones, and that many bees prefer symmetrical ones. Scientists are still finding out how much this preference shaped flowers.
- e.g. Hibiscus and periwinkle: five petals in a ring.
- e.g. Tulsi and pea flowers: one line of symmetry, with a landing lip.
- e.g. A sunflower head is made of hundreds of tiny flowers packed in spirals around the centre.
- **Many botanists:** Symmetry mostly comes from how a bud grows: parts form in rings around the tip, so they come out evenly spaced. The bees then pick the plants whose flowers suit them.
- **Bee researchers:** In experiments, honeybees learned symmetrical patterns and often preferred them. Bees choosing symmetrical flowers may have helped make flowers more symmetrical over a very long time. How strong this effect is, is still being studied.
### Why animals that move have a left side and a right side · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals that move forward through the world, such as people, fish, birds, ants and earthworms, have bilateral symmetry: a left half and a right half that nearly match. Animals that stay in one place or drift, such as jellyfish and sea anemones, often have radial symmetry.

An animal that moves forward meets the world front first. The front is where her mouth and eyes are, because that is where food and danger come from. Her left side and right side meet the world in the same way, so they nearly match. A jellyfish drifts in the sea, and food and danger can come from any side. So her body is the same all the way round. A starfish is a surprise. Her young swim as tiny two-sided larvae, and only later grow into a five-armed shape.
- e.g. You: two eyes, two ears, two arms and two legs, one on each side.
- e.g. A fish: fins on the left and right, mouth at the front.
- e.g. A jellyfish: the same all the way round.
### Nothing in nature is perfectly even · *defined*
**Definition:** Real living bodies and natural shapes are nearly symmetrical, never exactly. Their symmetry breaks wherever one side meets something different.

Your heart sits a little to the left of your chest. Most people's two feet are slightly different sizes. A plant on a windowsill grows more leaves on the side facing the light, and bends towards it. In the bubble raft, the bubbles at the edge are lopsided because they are pressed on one side only. A snowflake that meets warmer air on one side grows uneven arms. Each break in symmetry is a clue: it tells you that one side met something the other side did not.
- e.g. A money plant on a windowsill leans towards the window.
- e.g. A tree at the edge of a road grows more branches on the open side.
- e.g. Your two hand spans are close, but measure them, and they are often a little different.
### Symmetry in kolams and rangolis · *defined*
**Definition:** People make symmetrical art by repeating one part: they draw it, then flip it or turn it and draw it again.

To draw a pulli kolam, you first put down a grid of dots. Then you loop a line around the dots. Many kolam artists draw one quarter, then turn and draw the same loops on the next quarter, until they have gone all the way round. That gives order 4 rotational symmetry. If the loops in each quarter are themselves mirror images, the kolam also has lines of symmetry. Rangolis, mandalas, temple floor tiles and the patterns on a Kanchipuram saree border use the same tricks of flipping and turning. Mathematicians, such as Gift Siromoney in Chennai in the 1970s, have studied the rules inside kolams.
- e.g. A pulli kolam drawn round a 3 by 3 grid of dots.
- e.g. A rangoli with eight matching petals at Diwali.
- e.g. A tiled floor where one tile, turned and repeated, makes the whole pattern.

## Mostly agreed
- In ice, water molecules join in rings of six. This is why snowflakes and other ice crystals grow with six-fold symmetry. *How we know:* Scientists have shone X-rays through ice and measured exactly where the molecules sit. Photographs of millions of snowflakes, starting with Wilson Bentley's in 1885, show six arms.
- Where soap films meet, they meet three at a time, at equal angles. *How we know:* Joseph Plateau set out these rules in 1873. Anyone can check them in a raft of bubbles, and they have been confirmed by many experiments and by mathematics.
- Most animals that move forward have bilateral symmetry, a left side and a right side. *How we know:* This is true of almost every animal group, from earthworms and ants to fish, birds and people.
- Honeycomb cells are hexagons, which cover a flat space with no gaps and use the least wall for the space they hold. *How we know:* That hexagons do this best was guessed long ago and finally proved by the mathematician Thomas Hales in 1999.
- No living body is perfectly symmetrical. *How we know:* Measure any body carefully and the two sides differ a little. In people, the heart sits to the left and most organs are not in matching pairs.

## How we found out
**Olaus Magnus, Johannes Kepler, Robert Hooke, Wilson Bentley and Ukichiro Nakaya, 1555 to the 1930s.** In 1555, a Swedish writer, Olaus Magnus, printed the first known pictures of snowflakes in Europe. Most of his snowflakes were odd shapes, such as a bell, a hand and an arrow. People did not yet look closely enough to see that snowflakes have six arms. In 1611, the astronomer Johannes Kepler noticed that every snowflake he saw had six corners. He wrote a little book about it as a New Year's gift for a friend, and asked why. He guessed that it had to do with how tiny pieces of matter pack together, but he could not prove it. In 1665, Robert Hooke looked at snowflakes through a microscope and drew their six arms in detail. In 1885, Wilson Bentley, a farmer in America, took the first photographs of single snowflakes. In the 1930s, Ukichiro Nakaya in Japan grew snowflakes in a cold room and showed how the air shapes their arms. Later, scientists shone X-rays through ice and found that water molecules join in rings of six. Kepler's guess about packing had been close.  
*What people believed before:* The first printed pictures showed snowflakes in many shapes, such as a bell, a hand and an arrow. Careful looking, first with the eye, then with a microscope and a camera, showed that snowflakes always grow six arms.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Symmetry in nature needs a designer who plans it, like a person drawing a kolam.** (A common idea, and the view of some older writers; *rejected*)  
  For: Symmetrical things look planned, and the symmetrical things people make are planned.  
  Against: Bubbles, snowflakes and crystals become symmetrical with no plan drawn, because the same rules act equally on every side. You can watch it happen in the bubble raft.
- **Every snowflake is perfectly symmetrical.** (A common idea, from neat pictures and paper cut-outs; *rejected*)  
  For: Photographs of snowflakes chosen for books show beautiful matching arms.  
  Against: Photographers pick the best ones. Most real snowflakes are a little uneven, broken, or stuck to others. Snow scientists such as Kenneth Libbrecht say this plainly.
- **No two snowflakes are alike.** (A popular saying; *debated*)  
  For: A snowflake's shape depends on the exact air it falls through, and each falls a slightly different way. So big branching snowflakes are almost certain to differ.  
  Against: Very small, simple snow crystals, such as plain six-sided plates, can look the same. Scientists have grown near-identical twin snowflakes side by side in the laboratory.
- **People find symmetrical faces more beautiful because symmetry is a sign of health.** (Some psychologists and biologists; *debated*)  
  For: In some studies, people rate more symmetrical faces as more attractive.  
  Against: Other studies find the link to health is weak or missing. People may simply find symmetrical shapes easier to see and remember.

## Roots
We have found symmetry in flowers, snowflakes, bubbles and kolams, and seen even shapes appear with no one drawing them. Now we ask what symmetry tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the middle of your raft, each bubble was pressed equally from every side. Most came out six-sided, like their neighbours. At the edge, bubbles were pressed on some sides only, and came out lopsided. Nobody drew the hexagons: an even push all round made an even shape.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a neat, even shape needs a maker who plans it, the way a person plans a kolam. The bubble raft shows an even shape can also come from even conditions all round, and that an uneven shape points to the side where something was different.  
*Evidence:* The middle bubbles and the edge bubbles were the same soap, the same size and under the same lid. Only the push around them differed. The middle ones came out six-sided, and the edge ones came out lopsided.

- *who am i:* You have a left side and a right side that nearly match, because you move forward and meet the world front first. Your heart sits a little to the left, so you are not perfectly even.
- *what is life:* A flower's even petals help her bees find her and land on her. Her shape is part of how she lives with the bees.
- *what is this world:* Bubbles, snowflakes and crystals make even shapes with no plan drawn, because the same rules act the same way on every side.

A snowflake begins as one tiny piece of ice, the same on every side. One rule, water molecules joining in rings of six, acts the same way in all six directions, and six matching arms grow out. A kolam grows the same way: one quarter, turned and repeated, becomes the whole. A symmetrical shape is one part showing up many times. Where the many parts differ, something outside them was different.

- **Avatamsaka Sutra, the image of Indra's net:** Indra's net has a jewel at every knot. Each jewel reflects every other jewel, so each one holds the whole net. (In a bubble raft, each bubble's shape is made by the bubbles pressing around it, and it presses on them in turn. No bubble is shaped on its own.)
- **Plato, Timaeus (the passage on the five regular solids):** Plato taught that fire, air, water and earth are made of tiny perfectly regular shapes, such as cubes and pyramids. (Plato's shapes were wrong. But he was right that the smallest parts of matter sit in regular patterns: in ice, water molecules really do join in rings of six.)
- Science (settled): Soap films meet three at a time at equal angles, which is why same-sized bubbles pressed together become hexagons. Joseph Plateau set out the rules in 1873.
- Science (settled): In 1918, the mathematician Emmy Noether proved that each symmetry in the laws of physics goes with something that is never used up. Because the laws are the same at every moment, energy is never made or lost.
- Other view, Many biologists: In living things, symmetry comes mostly from genes and from what helped their ancestors survive, not only from even conditions. A flower's five petals come out even in a lopsided wind too, because her bud grows that way.
- Practice, Even breath (3 min): Let's sit tall and put one hand on each side of our ribs. Breathe in slowly. Do both hands rise together? Breathe out slowly. Now move one hand a little left of the middle of your chest. Can you feel your heart beating there? Our two sides breathe together, but our heart sits on one side. Take three more slow breaths and feel both.
- Ask: The bubbles in the middle of your raft became six-sided, and nobody drew them. Where else might an even shape come with no one planning it?
- Ask: Your two hands nearly match, but not exactly. Which tells you more about you, the matching or the small differences?
- Ask: If everything were perfectly even everywhere, would anything have a shape at all?

## Story: The Kolam by the Shoe Rack
*Made-up story. Kolams drawn at doorsteps in the month of Margazhi, soap walls meeting three at a time, and plants leaning to the light are all real.*

Meenakshi lived on the fourth floor of a block of flats in Chennai. Every morning in the month of Margazhi, she drew a kolam outside the door of flat 4B. Her paati had taught her when she was five. Now she was nine, and she could draw a kolam of twenty-five dots without stopping.

Her cousin Arjun had come from Pune for the holidays. He was seven. He sat on the steps in his pyjamas and watched her put down the dots, five rows of five.

"Why do you turn round and round?" he asked.

"I draw one corner," said Meenakshi, "then I turn, and draw the same thing on the next corner. Four corners, four turns. That way all four sides match."

"So you make it even on purpose," said Arjun. "Nature doesn't do that. Nature is messy. Only people make things even."

Meenakshi stopped with the rice flour between her fingers. She looked at the pot of periwinkle by the lift. Every pink flower had five petals, spaced out evenly round a little dark eye.

"Who turned round and round to draw those?" she asked.

Arjun shrugged. "The plant, I suppose."

"The plant can't hold rice flour," said Meenakshi. "But she gets five even petals every time."

There was one problem with the kolam outside 4B. The neighbours' big shoe rack stood right beside the door. Every morning, the corner of the kolam next to the shoe rack came out squashed. There wasn't enough room for the loop. The other three corners were round and full. That one was flat and pinched.

"Your kolam isn't even," said Arjun, pointing.

"That's the shoe rack's fault," said Meenakshi.

That afternoon, it was Meenakshi's turn to wash the steel plates after lunch. The sink filled up with soapy water. A raft of little bubbles gathered in one big steel plate, all squeezed together. She bent down to look.

The bubbles were not round. They had straight walls. In the middle of the plate, nearly every bubble had six walls, like the cells of a honeycomb.

"Arjun! Come here!"

He came, dripping, from the bathroom. "What?"

"Who drew these?"

He looked. "Nobody. They're bubbles."

"But they're hexagons. Six sides. Count."

Arjun counted, poking each wall with a wet finger. "Six. Six. Six. Five. Six."

"Now look at the edge," said Meenakshi.

At the rim of the plate, the bubbles were different. Each one had bubbles on one side and the steel rim on the other. Their outer sides were curved, and they had only three or four walls. They were lopsided.

"The middle ones are even," said Arjun slowly. "The edge ones are squashed."

"Like my kolam by the shoe rack," said Meenakshi. "In the middle, every bubble has the same neighbours pushing on every side. So it comes out even. At the edge, something is in the way on one side."

"So nobody drew them," said Arjun. "They just got pushed the same way all round."

"Look where the walls meet," said Meenakshi. She pointed with a fork. "Three walls. Always three. Never four."

Arjun checked five places, then ten. Always three.

The next morning, they drew the kolam together. Arjun put down the dots. Meenakshi drew the first corner, and Arjun copied it on the next, turning round and round. When they reached the corner by the shoe rack, Arjun said, "Wait." He went inside and asked Paati if they could move the dots one step towards the stairs, away from the rack. Paati laughed and said yes.

This time, the four corners had the same room. The kolam came out even all the way round.

On the last day of his holiday, Arjun stood by the periwinkle pot. "Meenakshi," he said, "there's a plant on our balcony in Pune that leans towards the window. She has more leaves on that side."

"Then she gets more light on that side," said Meenakshi. "Something is different there, so she's not even. When everything is the same all round, things come out even. When one side is different, you can see it in the shape."

Arjun nodded. On the train home he drew a kolam in his notebook, one corner at a time, turning the book round and round.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the bubbles became six-sided because each one in the middle was pressed equally on every side by bubbles of the same size, and soap walls always meet three at a time at equal angles. Nobody drew the hexagons. At the edge, the push was not the same on every side, so the bubbles were lopsided, just as the kolam corner beside the shoe rack was squashed. A person makes a kolam even by turning and repeating. Bubbles, snowflakes and many growing things come out even when the same thing happens on every side. A flower's evenness also comes from how her bud grows, in rings round a centre. Where one side meets something different, like light from a window, the symmetry breaks, and the break tells you what was different.

- Ask: Arjun said only people make things even. What made him change his mind?
- Ask: Why was the kolam corner by the shoe rack squashed? What was the same about the bubbles at the edge of the plate?
- Ask: How did Meenakshi and Arjun fix the kolam?
- Ask: Why does the plant in Pune have more leaves on one side?
- Ask: Can you find something at home that is even in the middle but lopsided at its edge?

  - Check: A butterfly, a tulsi flower and your face all have order 1. What do they have in common? → **They each have one line of symmetry, a left side and a right side**
### g2 · Finish My Half: a dot-grid partner game (15 min)
- **rules:** Player 1 draws 5 straight lines joining dots on one side of the dotted line only.; Player 2 must draw the mirror image on the other side, so the dotted line becomes a line of symmetry.; Player 1 checks each line. Score 1 point for each correct line.; In round 2, use the four-quarter sheets. Player 1 draws in one quarter, and Player 2 turns the sheet to copy it into the other three quarters.; Swap roles every round.; Playing alone: draw your half, cover it, then try to draw the other half from memory. Uncover and check.
- **Printable board: Dot-grid sheets:** 6 squares of 8 by 8 dots, each with a dotted line of symmetry down the middle; 6 squares of 8 by 8 dots, each with a cross through the middle for a four-quarter pattern
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** A line for each round: 1 point for every line drawn in the right place, minus 1 for a wrong line
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play round 1 with the mirror-line sheets.  
  *Parent says:* I'll draw 5 lines on my side. You draw the matching lines on your side, so the dotted line becomes a line of symmetry.  
  *Child mode:* Your partner draws 5 lines on one side. Draw the matching lines on your side.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play round 2 with the four-quarter sheets.  
  *Parent says:* Now I'll draw in one quarter. You turn the sheet a quarter turn and copy it, until all four quarters match.  
  *Child mode:* Your partner draws in one quarter. Turn the paper. Copy it into each quarter.
  - Note: Many children copy without turning, which gives a mirror image instead of a turned copy. Both are symmetry, but different kinds. Point out the difference.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare a mirror pattern and a turned pattern.  
  *Parent says:* Which pattern has a fold line? Which looks the same when turned? Can one have both?  
  *Child mode:* Which pattern folds to match? Which turns to match?
  - Check: In round 2, you copied one quarter by turning the sheet a quarter turn each time. What kind of symmetry does the finished pattern always have? → **Rotational symmetry of order 4**

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bubble Raft: what shape do crowded bubbles take? (30 min)
- **question:** When many bubbles of the same size crowd together in one flat layer, what shape does each bubble take? Is it the same in the middle and at the edge?
- **predict:** Guess first. Each bubble starts round. When they are squashed together under a lid, what shape will a bubble in the middle become: round, square, triangle, six-sided, or all different? Will the edge bubbles be the same as the middle ones? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Blow every bubble the same way, so they are all about the same size. Count the walls of 5 bubbles in the middle and 5 bubbles at the edge, because one bubble on its own could be odd. Count the same way each time: go round the bubble and touch each wall once with a toothpick.
- **safety:** Blow out through the straw, never suck in. Soapy water stings the eyes, so wash hands after. Wipe up spills so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw 3 middle bubbles and 3 edge bubbles exactly as you see them. Mark one place where walls meet, and write how many walls meet there.
- **results table:** Bubble | Where (middle or edge) | How many walls? | Is it even all round? (yes or no)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour half a cup of water into a cup. 2. Add 2 teaspoons of dishwashing liquid and 1 teaspoon of sugar. 3. Stir slowly, without making foam. 4. Pour just enough onto the plate to cover its floor in a thin layer.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to make a raft of bubbles. Stir gently, because we don't want foam yet. We want to make the bubbles ourselves, one at a time.  
  *Child mode:* Mix water, dishwash liquid and sugar in a cup. Stir slowly. Pour a thin layer onto the plate.
  - Note: A thin layer, about as deep as a coin is thick, works best. Sugar helps the bubbles last longer, but the test works without it.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess and give a reason. Write the guess at the top of the record sheet.  
  *Parent says:* Each bubble starts round. When we squash lots of them together under a lid, what shape will a bubble in the middle become? What about one at the edge? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Each bubble starts round. What shape will it be when squashed? Will edge bubbles look the same? Write your guess and why.
  - Ask: What shape do you think the middle bubbles will be, and why? (good: Any guess with a reason. For example: squares, because they fit together like tiles. Or round, because bubbles are always round.)
  - Note: Don't give the answer. Any guess with a reason is a good start. The test will answer it.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the straw so its tip is just under the surface of the soapy layer. 2. Blow very gently, so bubbles come out one at a time, each about the size of a green pea. 3. Move the tip a little each time, so the bubbles crowd together in one layer. 4. Make a patch at least as big as your palm. 5. If a bubble climbs on top of others, pop it with a dry fingertip.  
  *Parent says:* Blow as gently as you can. Small, same-sized bubbles are what we want. Keep them in one flat layer, side by side.  
  *Child mode:* Put the straw tip just under the soapy water. Blow very gently. Make lots of small bubbles, all the same size. Keep them side by side in one layer.
  - Note: Blowing too hard makes big bubbles of mixed sizes. That still works, but the pattern is harder to see. If the bubbles keep popping, add another teaspoon of dishwashing liquid. Bubbles last longest away from a fan.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold the clear lid flat above the bubble patch. 2. Lower it gently until it rests on the bubbles. 3. Shine the torch from the side, low across the plate, or put the plate by a bright window. 4. Look down through the lid.  
  *Parent says:* Now we squash them gently. The lid presses every bubble down to the same height. Let's look from above. What shape are they now?  
  *Child mode:* Lay the clear lid gently on the bubbles. Shine the torch from the side. Look down through the lid. What shapes do you see?
  - Ask: What do you notice about the shapes? (good: The bubbles have straight walls now.; They fit together with no gaps.; The middle ones look six-sided.)
  - Note: The walls show up best with light coming low from one side. No clear lid? Look at the raft without one. Count how many bubbles touch each middle bubble. Most touch six.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose 5 bubbles in the middle. 2. For each one, touch each wall once with the toothpick, through or beside the lid, and count. 3. Choose 5 bubbles at the edge, and count their walls too. 4. Write each count in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count, not just look. Touch each wall once with the toothpick as you go round. Then do the same at the edge.  
  *Child mode:* Pick 5 middle bubbles. Count the walls of each one. Then pick 5 edge bubbles. Count again. Write the numbers down.
  - Note: Expect most middle bubbles to have 6 walls. A few may have 5 or 7, usually because they are a different size: smaller bubbles tend to have fewer walls, bigger ones more. Edge bubbles have a curved side facing open water or the plate, and fewer walls.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to find three places where walls meet, and count the walls at each one.  
  *Parent says:* Look where the walls join. How many walls meet at each joining point? Check three different places.  
  *Child mode:* Find a place where walls join. How many walls meet there? Check two more places.
  - Note: Three walls meeting at equal angles is the key fact. It is why the middle cells become six-sided.
  - Check: In the bubble raft, how many soap walls meet at each joining point? → **3**
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why. Write it under the table.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see that tells you? Why do you think the middle bubbles and the edge bubbles are different?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it.
  - Ask: Why were the middle bubbles six-sided, but the edge bubbles lopsided? (good: The middle bubbles were pressed equally from every side by their neighbours.; The edge bubbles had bubbles on one side and nothing on the other side.; The push was the same all round in the middle, but not at the edge.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses the edge bubbles as evidence: same soap, same lid, same size, but a different push around them, and a different shape.
  - Check: Nobody drew the hexagons in the bubble raft. What made them? → **Each middle bubble was pressed equally on every side by bubbles of the same size**
### e2 · Cut Across and Count: do fruits repeat their pattern? (30 min)
- **question:** When you cut a fruit across its middle, the inside often repeats around the centre. How many times does it repeat? Is that number the same in every fruit of one kind?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will two bhindis cut across show the same number of seed rows? Will two lemons show the same number of segments? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Cut every fruit across its widest middle, never along its length, so you compare the same view. Cut 2 of each kind, because one fruit could be unusual. Count each one twice.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the cutting. Lemon juice stings cuts and eyes. Use the cut fruit for cooking afterwards, so nothing is wasted.
- **record:** Draw each cut face from above, with the centre marked. Fill in the table.
- **results table:** Fruit | Fruit 1: how many parts around the centre? | Fruit 2: how many? | Same number? (yes or no) | Order of rotational symmetry (about)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the fruits. 2. Ask your child to guess, for each kind, how many parts will repeat around the centre. 3. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* When we cut these across, the inside will have a pattern around the middle. How many times will it repeat in a bhindi? In a lemon? Will two of the same kind match? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each fruit. Guess how many parts are inside, around the middle. Will two lemons match? Write your guesses and why.
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts each fruit straight across its widest middle. 2. Lay each piece cut side up on the board. 3. Wipe the cut faces with a cloth if they are too wet to see.  
  *Parent says:* I'll cut, and you watch. Look at the cut face. Can you find the centre?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to cut each fruit across the middle. Look at each cut face. Find the centre.
  - Note: Always cut across, like slicing a cucumber for salad, never along the length.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. For each cut face, count the parts arranged around the centre. In a bhindi, count seed rows. In a lemon, count segments. In a tomato, cucumber or capsicum, count seed rooms. 2. Count twice. 3. Write both fruits of each kind in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Count the parts around the middle. Touch each one as you count. Then count again to be sure.  
  *Child mode:* Count the parts around the middle. Touch each one. Count again. Write the number.
  - Note: Most bhindis show 5 seed rows, though some kinds have more. A cucumber usually shows 3 seed parts. An apple usually shows a five-pointed star of seed rooms. A banana shows a faint three-part pattern. Lemons often differ: one may have 8 segments and the other 10 or 11. Tomatoes differ a lot too, from 2 rooms to many. Whatever you find is the real answer.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the numbers. Which kinds matched, and which did not?  
  *Parent says:* Which fruits gave the same number both times? Which ones changed?  
  *Child mode:* Which fruits matched both times? Which ones did not?
  - Note: The parts inside a fruit grew from the flower's centre, one part for each section. Each section holds and feeds a row of seeds.
  - Check: Kavya, a girl in Class 3, cut two bhindis and two lemons across. Both bhindis had 5 seed rows. One lemon had 9 segments and the other had 11. What does this show? → **Some kinds of fruit keep the same number of parts, and others vary**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now about fruits and symmetry? What did you see? Why might a lemon vary but a bhindi not?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Ask: Why might every bhindi have 5 seed rows? (good: Because bhindi flowers grow 5 parts in their centre.; Because each row grew from one part of the flower.)

### Symmetry Puzzles and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which capital letter has both a line of symmetry and order 2 rotational symmetry?  
   Options: H / A / S / F  
   **Answer:** H. *Why:* H matches when folded across the middle or down the middle, and it looks the same after half a turn. A has only a fold line, S only a turn, and F neither.
2. (mcq) A shape has 4 lines of symmetry. What is the smallest order of rotational symmetry it can have?  
   Options: 1 / 2 / 4 / 8  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* A flat shape with 4 lines of symmetry, like a square, also matches itself 4 times in one turn. Two mirror lines at an angle always make a turn, so many mirror lines bring rotational symmetry with them.
3. (mcq) You fold a paper square into 8 layers and cut a snowflake. How many arms will it have?  
   Options: 6 / 4 or 8 / 12 / 3  
   **Answer:** 4 or 8. *Why:* Folding into 8 gives a four-fold or eight-fold pattern, not six. A real snowflake has six arms, so you fold into three after the first two folds.
4. (mcq) A snowflake falls past a warm chimney, so one side meets warmer air than the other. What will its arms most likely look like?  
   Options: All six still match perfectly / The arms on the warm side grow differently from the others / It will grow eight arms / It becomes a bubble  
   **Answer:** The arms on the warm side grow differently from the others. *Why:* The arms match only when they all meet the same air. If one side meets different air, those arms grow differently and the symmetry breaks. The break shows where the air was different.
5. (mcq) Kavya puts a bubble raft right next to a ruler lying in the plate. What will the bubbles touching the ruler look like?  
   Options: Hexagons like the middle ones / Flat on the side touching the ruler / Perfect circles / Triangles  
   **Answer:** Flat on the side touching the ruler. *Why:* The ruler presses on one side only, so the walls touching it are straight and the bubbles are lopsided, like the edge bubbles at the rim of the plate. Wherever the push changes, the shape changes.
6. (sort) Sort these living things by their symmetry.

7. (count) A snowflake has six arms. Each arm grows 4 side branches on each side. How many side branches does the whole snowflake have?

8. (truefalse) Shreya says, 'If a shape has rotational symmetry, it must also have a line of symmetry.' Is she right?

11. (write) Kepler, in 1611, wondered why every snowflake has six corners. If you could talk to him, what would you tell him, and how do scientists know?

## Applications
- Engineers make fan blades, propellers and wheels with rotational symmetry so they spin smoothly without wobbling.
- Builders use symmetry in temples, gopurams, bridges and buildings so the weight is spread evenly.
- Textile designers use mirror and turn symmetry in saree borders, block prints and carpets.
- Scientists who study crystals use symmetry to work out how atoms and molecules are arranged, and to design medicines.
- Honeycomb-shaped panels, made of hexagonal cells, are used in aircraft and packaging because they are light and strong.

## Open questions
- **How much did bees choosing symmetrical flowers shape the flowers we see today?** Bees learn and often prefer symmetrical patterns, but it is hard to measure how strongly this has shaped flowers over millions of years.
- **How exactly do bees build hexagonal cells?** Some scientists think warm wax pulls itself into hexagons. Others have filmed bees building hexagonal walls directly. The question is still argued.
- **Why are living bodies so nearly symmetrical, yet slightly uneven in such similar ways, like the heart on the left?** Scientists know some of the signals in a growing embryo that tell left from right, but not the whole story of how the first difference starts.
- **Why do people find symmetry beautiful?** Ideas include health, easier seeing and habit from nature. Studies disagree, and it may be a mix.
