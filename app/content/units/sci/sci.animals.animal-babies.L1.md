# Animal babies: how young animals grow into their parents' shape
`sci.animals.animal-babies.L1`

**Summary.** A pigeon chick hatches pink and yellow, with no grey feathers at all. Yet in four weeks the chick looks just like the parents. Every kind of animal has young, and humans are one kind of animal. Some young look like small copies of their parents, and some look very different. Your child measures how many 'heads tall' a grown-up, a child and a baby are, and finds that even a human baby is a different shape from a grown-up.

**Intro.** *Look at a photo of yourself as a tiny baby. Now look in the mirror. Is the baby just a small you, or a different shape?* Every animal starts small. A kitten looks like a small cat. A tadpole looks nothing like a frog. And a human baby? Babies look like their parents in some ways, and not in others. In this unit we look at baby animals and their parents. Then we measure our own bodies, with strips of paper, to find out how a baby's shape changes as they grow.

## Learning outcomes

2. By the end I can say which babies look like their parents and which look very different.
3. By the end I can say that a baby's head is big for their body, and that legs grow the most as a child grows up.
4. By the end I can say some ways animal parents look after their young, and that fathers do it too.
5. By the end I can make a guess, measure with a paper strip, and say what I found.

## Terms
- **Young**: The young of an animal are their babies. Every kind of animal has young. *e.g.* A kitten is the young of a cat. A tadpole is the young of a frog. You were the young of your parents.
- **Parent**: A parent is a mother or a father. Parents have young, and many parents look after them. *e.g.* A pigeon chick has two parents. Both of them feed the chick.
- **Hatch**: A baby hatches when they break out of their egg. *e.g.* Chicks, ducklings, baby lizards and baby crocodiles all hatch from eggs.
- **Grow up**: To grow up is to get bigger and change, until you become a grown-up. *e.g.* A puppy grows up into a dog. A caterpillar grows up into a butterfly.

## Explanations
### Every animal has young · *defined*
**Definition:** Every kind of animal makes young, and the young grow up into grown-ups who can have young of their own.

Cats have kittens. Cows have calves. Frogs have tadpoles. Humans have babies, because humans are animals too. Some young are born, like puppies, calves and human babies. Some young hatch from eggs, like chicks, baby lizards and baby fish. Either way, every animal comes from parents of their own kind. A cat's kittens always grow up into cats.
- e.g. A street dog in your lane may have 5 or 6 puppies at a time.
- e.g. A tiny gecko on your wall hatched from an egg about as big as a pea.
- e.g. Ants in the kitchen carry their white young from place to place.
### Some young look like their parents, and some do not · *defined*
**Definition:** Some young look like small copies of their parents. Others look very different at first, and change their whole body as they grow up.

A kitten looks like a small cat, and a calf looks like a small cow. A baby gecko looks just like a grown-up gecko. But a tadpole has a tail and no legs, and lives in water. Later the tadpole grows legs, loses the tail and becomes a frog. A caterpillar crawls and eats leaves. Later the caterpillar becomes a butterfly or a moth, with wings. A maggot is the young of a fly, with no legs and no wings. These young look so different that people once thought they were other kinds of animal.
- e.g. A pigeon chick hatches pink, with yellow fluff. Grey feathers grow over about four weeks.
- e.g. A silkworm is the young of a silk moth. Silk farmers in Karnataka and Assam raise silkworms.
- e.g. Flamingo chicks are grey and white. They slowly turn pink from the food they eat.
### A baby is shaped differently from a grown-up · *defined*
**Definition:** A newborn human baby is about 4 heads long. A grown-up is about 7 or 8 heads tall. After birth, the legs grow much more than the head.

A human baby has a big head, big eyes, a short body and short legs. As the child grows, the legs grow fastest. The head grows too, but much less than the legs. So a child of 6 is about 6 heads tall, and a grown-up about 7 or 8. Many other young animals have big heads and big eyes too, like kittens and puppies. But a calf is born with long legs. A calf can stand within about an hour, so they can follow their mother.
- e.g. When you measure in the Heads Tall experiment, a grown-up is about 7 heads tall, a child about 6, and a baby photo about 4.
- e.g. A kitten's head is big for their body. A grown-up cat's head looks small.
- e.g. A newborn calf stands up on long, wobbly legs soon after birth.
### How animal parents care for their young · *debated*
Many animal parents feed their young, keep them warm and keep them safe. Pigeon mothers and fathers both feed their chicks with a milk they make in their throat. An elephant calf is looked after by the mother, the aunts and the oldest female, who leads the herd. Some fathers do most of the work. A seahorse father carries the eggs in a pouch on his belly. Some parents leave. An olive ridley turtle lays her eggs on a beach in Odisha and goes back to the sea. The babies hatch and find the sea alone. Many animal parents know their own young. A penguin parent finds their own chick among thousands, just by the chick's call. Scientists agree on what parents do. They argue about what parents feel.
- e.g. Both pigeon parents sit on the eggs, and both feed the chicks.
- e.g. A mother cat carries her kitten gently by the loose skin on the back of the neck.
- e.g. A great hornbill mother seals herself into a tree hole with her eggs. The father brings food to a slit in the wall. Aparajita Datta, an Indian scientist, has studied hornbill families in Arunachal Pradesh for many years.
- **Scientists who study animal feelings:** Animal parents feel care and worry for their young, as human parents do. A mother elephant who loses her calf stays by the body and acts upset.
- **Other scientists:** Parents act this way because their bodies tell them to, with chemical messages inside. These scientists describe what parents do, and do not use words like love or worry for other animals.

## Mostly agreed
- Every kind of animal has young, and young come only from parents of their own kind. *How we know:* Nobody has ever seen a kitten born to a dog, or a calf born to a cat. Every young animal comes from parents of their own kind.
- Some young look like their parents, and some change their whole body as they grow. *How we know:* People can watch a tadpole become a frog, or a caterpillar become a butterfly, at home or in a school. It happens the same way every time.
- A human baby's head is big for their body, and the legs grow most after birth. *How we know:* Doctors measure babies and children at check-ups, all over the world. A newborn's head is about one quarter of their length. A grown-up's head is about one eighth.

## How we found out
**Giovanni Battista Grassi and Salvatore Calandruccio, Italian scientists (1896); Johannes Schmidt, a Danish scientist (1904 to 1922), Grassi and Calandruccio watched the change in 1896. Schmidt published where European eels breed in 1922..** For more than 2,000 years, nobody had ever seen a baby eel. Aristotle, in ancient Greece, wrote that eels grew out of mud. In the sea, fishermen sometimes caught a strange little fish. This fish was flat and clear as glass, shaped like a leaf. Scientists thought the leaf fish was a different kind of fish. They gave the leaf fish a name: Leptocephalus, which means 'thin head'. In 1896, two Italian scientists, Giovanni Battista Grassi and Salvatore Calandruccio, kept some of these glass leaves in tanks of sea water in Messina. They watched the leaf fish every day. Slowly, the flat leaves grew shorter and rounder. They turned into tiny see-through eels. The 'different fish' had been baby eels all along. They just looked nothing like their parents. Later, a Danish scientist, Johannes Schmidt, sailed the Atlantic Ocean for years looking for the smallest ones. They led him to the Sargasso Sea, where the eels of Europe are born.  
*What people believed before:* That eels have no babies and grow out of mud, and that the glassy leaf-shaped fish was a different kind of fish. Grassi and Calandruccio watched the leaf fish turn into young eels.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A baby is just a small copy of the grown-up.** (A very common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Kittens look like small cats, and human babies have the same parts as grown-ups: two eyes, two arms, two legs.  
  Against: A human baby is a different shape: about 4 heads long, where a grown-up is about 7 or 8. And a tadpole, a caterpillar or a maggot looks nothing like the parent at all.
- **Maggots and worms grow out of rotting food by themselves.** (What most people believed for about 2,000 years; *rejected*)  
  For: Maggots seemed to appear on old meat and rotting fruit from nowhere.  
  Against: In 1668, Francesco Redi in Italy covered jars of meat with thin cloth. Flies could not get in, and no maggots grew. Maggots come from eggs that flies lay.
- **Only mothers look after the young.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: In many animals, like cats, cows and dogs, the mother feeds the babies with her milk.  
  Against: Pigeon fathers feed their chicks too. Seahorse fathers carry the eggs. Pheasant-tailed jacana fathers, found on Indian lakes, sit on the eggs and raise the chicks alone.

## Roots
We have looked at baby animals and measured our own bodies with head strips. Now we ask what growing up tells us about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Our head strips showed a grown-up about 7 heads tall, a child about 6, and a baby about 4. After birth, the legs grow much more than the head does. So growing up changes the shape of a body, as well as the size.  
*Habit it breaks:* We often picture a baby as a grown-up made small. But a baby's body has their own shape, and each part grows at a different speed.  
*Evidence:* In the head strip test, each person was measured with a strip of their own head. The grown-up came out about 7 heads tall, the child about 6, and the newborn photo about 4.

- *who am i:* Your body has changed shape since you were born. You are still you, in a new shape.
- *what is life:* Every living being comes from parents, and keeps growing and changing all through life.
- *what is this world:* Kittens, pigeon chicks, tadpoles and human babies all start small and change as they grow. All around us, young animals are becoming grown-ups.

Every animal begins as one tiny cell, too small to see. That cell grows into a baby, and the baby into a grown-up. A tadpole becomes a frog, a yellow chick becomes a grey pigeon, and a baby becomes a child. One life takes many shapes, and each shape grows out of the one before.

- **Tao Te Ching 51:** The Tao gives birth to all things and raises them, but does not hold on to them as its own. (Pigeon parents feed their chicks for about four weeks. Then the young pigeons fly off and live their own lives.)
- **Isha Upanishad, opening verse (also Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.1.1):** That is whole, this is whole. From the whole, the whole comes out. Take the whole from the whole, and the whole remains. (A mother cat has four kittens and is still a whole cat. Each kitten is a whole new cat of their own.)
- Science (settled): Right after birth, a baby's head is about one quarter of their whole length. A grown-up's head is about one seventh or one eighth.
- Science (open): Each part of a growing body grows at its own speed. Exactly how each part knows when to grow, and when to stop, is still being studied.
- Other view, Many biologists: Many biologists say a body grows by following instructions. The instructions are inside every cell, and they are called genes. Chemical messages in the body tell each part when to grow.

Now kneel, then stand. Reach up as tall as you can. Feel how long your legs are now. You were once small and curled up like that.

## Story: The Yellow Chicks Behind the AC
*Nandini, Arul and Ammamma are made up. Pigeons really do nest behind AC boxes in Indian cities. Both parents really sit on the eggs and feed the chicks with crop milk, and the chicks really are pink and yellow at first.*

Nandini lived in a flat in Chennai, on the fourth floor. She was six. Her little brother Arul was four. Their grandmother, Ammamma, lived with them.

Outside the kitchen window there was a big grey AC box. One morning, Nandini heard a soft sound. Coo-roo, coo-roo. Two pigeons were walking behind the AC box. One of them had a twig in his beak.

'They are making a nest,' said Ammamma. 'We will watch through the glass. We will not open the window, and we will not touch.'

The nest was only a few twigs and a feather. But a few days later, there were two white eggs in it.

Every day, Nandini looked. A pigeon was always sitting on the eggs. 'Does that pigeon never get up?' she asked.

'Look closely,' said Ammamma. 'In the day, the father sits. In the evening, the mother comes, and he flies off. They take turns, to keep the eggs warm.'

Two weeks went by, and a few days more. Then one morning, Arul shouted from the kitchen. 'Akka! Come and see! Something is in the nest!'

Nandini ran. Next to a broken shell there were two tiny chicks. Their skin was pink. They had thin yellow fluff, like wet cotton. Their eyes were shut, and their beaks were big and dark.

'Those are not pigeons,' said Arul. 'Pigeons are grey. Those are some other bird.'

Nandini was not sure. The chicks did not look like their parents at all. Had another bird left them there?

'Let us find out,' said Ammamma. 'Watch who comes to feed them.'

Soon the father pigeon landed on the AC box. The chicks lifted their wobbly heads. One chick pushed their beak right inside the father's beak. The father pumped his neck up and down.

'He is feeding the chick!' said Nandini. 'But he has no worms in his beak.'

'Pigeon parents make a milk in their throat,' said Ammamma. 'Both the mother and the father make it. The chicks drink it from inside their beaks.'

'Birds make milk?' said Arul. He opened his mouth so wide that everyone laughed.

Every day, Nandini drew the chicks in her notebook. After a few days, their eyes were open. After two weeks, little grey feathers were poking out, like the tips of paintbrushes. After three weeks, the chicks were almost as big as their parents. They flapped their wings and walked along the AC box.

One evening, Amma came home with an old photo album. 'Look who this is,' she said. There was a baby with a round bald head, fat cheeks and tiny legs.

'Who is that?' asked Nandini.

'That is you,' said Amma. 'One day old.'

Nandini looked at the baby. Then she looked at Amma's face. 'I did not look like you at all,' she said. 'My head was so big, and my legs were so small.'

'And now?' said Ammamma. Nandini stood next to Amma by the mirror. Her legs were long now. Her face was more like Amma's face.

'The chicks are doing the same as me,' said Nandini slowly. 'They did not look like their parents at first. They are growing into their parents' shape.'

Ammamma smiled. 'That is right. Some babies look like their parents from the start. Some take time.'

Four weeks after the eggs hatched, Nandini came to the window and the nest was empty. Two young grey pigeons were sitting on the railing of the next building. They had grey feathers and pink feet, just like their parents.

'They flew!' said Arul. 'Will they come back?'

'Pigeons often use the same nest again,' said Ammamma. 'Maybe one day we will see new eggs.'

Nandini turned to the last page of her notebook. She drew a pink chick on the left, and a grey pigeon on the right. In between she drew an arrow. Under the arrow, she wrote, with a little help from Ammamma: GROWING UP.

**For the parent:** Nandini sees that the yellow chicks do not look like their parents at first, and then sees her own baby photo. She works out that babies can look very different from their parents, and grow into their parents' shape.

- Ask: How did Nandini find out that the chicks were pigeons?
- Ask: What did the pigeon parents feed their chicks?
- Ask: How was baby Nandini a different shape from her mother?

## Experiments
### e1 · Heads Tall: is a baby shaped like a small grown-up? (25 min)
- **question:** Is a baby shaped like a small grown-up? How many of their own heads tall are a grown-up, a child and a baby?
- **predict:** Guess first. How many heads tall is a grown-up? How many heads tall are you? And a baby? Will all three be the same number? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We measure every person the same way. The head strip goes from the chin to the top of the head. The height goes from the floor to the top of the head, with no chappals on, standing straight. Each person is measured with a strip of their own head. The baby is measured from a photo, so the answer is only close, not exact.
- **record:** Draw three people in a row: the baby, you and the grown-up. Under each one, write how many heads tall they are.
- **results table:** Who | My guess: how many heads tall | What we found: how many heads tall
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to guess how many heads tall a grown-up is, a child is, and a baby is. 2. Write the three guesses down for your child.  
  *Parent says:* Imagine we stack your head on top of itself, again and again, until we reach your feet. How many heads tall are you? And me? And a baby? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. How many heads tall is a grown-up? And you? And a baby? Why?
  - Ask: Will the baby, you and the grown-up be the same number of heads tall? (good: Yes, because a baby is just small.; No, because a baby has a big head.)
  - Note: Most children guess the same number for everyone, or guess the grown-up has more heads because they are bigger. Any guess is fine. Write it down and say nothing yet.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The grown-up sits down. 2. Lay the book flat on top of their head. 3. Your child holds a paper strip upright beside the face, with the end at the chin. 4. Mark the strip where it meets the book. 5. Cut the strip at the mark. 6. Write the grown-up's name on it.  
  *Parent says:* This strip is exactly as long as my head, from chin to top. Now we have a head ruler.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the strip by the face. Start at the chin. Mark the top of the head. Cut it.
  - Note: The book makes the top of the head easy to see. Keep the strip straight up and down, not along the curve of the face.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The grown-up takes off their chappals and stands straight against the door. 2. Put the book flat on their head, touching the door. 3. Mark the door paper at the bottom of the book. 4. Your child stands the strip on the floor against the door and marks the top end. 5. Move the strip up, end to end, and count each strip.  
  *Parent says:* Put the strip on the floor. Mark the top end. Move it up so the bottom is on the mark. Count with me. How many head strips tall am I?  
  *Child mode:* Start at the floor. Mark the top of the strip. Move it up. Count each one.
  - Ask: How many head strips tall is the grown-up? (good: About 7.; 7 and a bit.)
  - Note: Most grown-ups come out between 7 and 8 head strips tall. Half a strip left at the top counts as a half.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Swap. The grown-up makes a head strip for your child, the same way. 2. Your child stands against the door. 3. Mark the height and count the strips together.  
  *Parent says:* Now it's your turn. We'll use a strip of your own head. Before we count, what is your guess?  
  *Child mode:* Now you. Stand straight by the door. Count your own head strips.
  - Ask: How many of your own heads tall are you? (good: About 6.; Less than the grown-up.)
  - Note: A child of 6 usually comes out about 5 and a half to 6 head strips tall.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the baby photo on the phone, or hold the printed photo. 2. Hold a paper strip against the baby's head. Mark the chin and the top of the head, and cut the strip. 3. Step it from the top of the head to the heels, and count.  
  *Parent says:* This is a photo of a real newborn baby. Let's measure the baby the same way. How many heads long is the baby?  
  *Child mode:* Make a tiny strip for the baby's head. Step it along the baby. Count.
  - Ask: How many heads long is the baby? (good: About 4.; 4 heads.)
  - Ask: The grown-up was about 7 heads. The baby was about 4. What does that tell you? (good: The baby's head is big for the body.; The baby's legs are short.; A baby is a different shape from a grown-up.)
  - Note: Choose a photo where the baby lies fairly straight. A baby with curled legs measures shorter, so any answer from 3 to 5 is fine: it still shows fewer heads than the grown-up. The claim is: a baby is a different shape from a grown-up, with a bigger head and shorter legs.
  - Check: Who had the biggest head for their body? → **The baby**

### a1 · Guess the Grown-up: draw what each baby becomes (10 min)
- **record:** Keep the four drawings. Write the real grown-up's name under each one.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Describe each baby, one at a time, without saying the animal's name. Your child draws the grown-up they think the baby becomes. 1. 'This baby is a small wriggling swimmer with a tail and no legs.' 2. 'This baby crawls on leaves and eats them all day.' 3. 'This baby is fluffy and yellow, with webbed feet.' 4. 'This baby is white and wriggly, with no legs, and lives in old fruit.'  
  *Parent says:* Listen to this baby. What will the baby look like when they grow up? Draw the grown-up.  
  *Child mode:* Listen to the baby. Draw the grown-up you think they become.
  - Note: The four babies are a tadpole (frog), a caterpillar (butterfly or moth), a duckling (duck) and a maggot (fly). The maggot is the hardest. Any guess is fine.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Say what each baby really grows up into. Your child draws an arrow from the baby to the grown-up, and draws the baby if they like.  
  *Parent says:* The wriggling swimmer with a tail is a tadpole. A tadpole grows into a frog. Were you right? Which baby surprised you most?  
  *Child mode:* Hear the answers. Which one surprised you?
  - Ask: Which baby changes the most when they grow up? (good: The maggot, because a fly has wings.; The caterpillar, because a butterfly has wings.)
### a2 · Busy Beaks: feed the hungry chicks (12 min)
- **record:** Draw your nest of chicks. Write how many trips you made in two minutes.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Roll a dupatta into a ring on the floor for a nest. 2. Draw an open beak on each cup and put the cups in the nest. 3. Put the bowl of puffed rice across the room.  
  *Parent says:* These cups are your chicks. They can't fly yet, so they wait in the nest. You are the parent bird.  
  *Child mode:* These cups are your chicks. You are the parent bird. Get ready to feed them.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For two minutes, your child picks up one puffed rice with the peg. They walk to the nest and drop the puffed rice in a cup. Then back again. Count the trips aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Go! One piece at a time, with your beak. Give every chick a turn. I'll count your trips.  
  *Child mode:* Pick up one piece with the peg. Walk to the nest. Drop it in a cup. Go back.
  - Note: Most children make 10 to 20 trips in two minutes. Real chicks call out for food and open their mouths wide.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the trips. Then talk about real bird parents.  
  *Parent says:* You made all those trips in just two minutes. Some small bird parents bring food hundreds of times in one day. How would you feel after a whole day?  
  *Child mode:* How many trips did you make? Real bird parents make hundreds every day. Are you tired?
  - Ask: Why do bird parents need to make so many trips? (good: Chicks are always hungry.; Chicks grow fast and need lots of food.; Chicks can't fly to get food yet.)
  - Check: Why can't a young chick get their own food? → **The chick cannot fly yet**
### a3 · As Long as a Newborn: a string baby (10 min)
- **record:** Draw yourself lying down, with the string baby laid beside you, end to end.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure 50 centimetres of string with the ruler or the tape, and cut it.  
  *Parent says:* Most babies are about this long when they are born. You were about this long too.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the string. You were about this long when you were born.
  - Ask: How many of these string babies long are you now? Guess. (good: 2.; 3.; Any guess with a reason.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lies down straight on the floor. 2. Lay the string beside them, starting at the heels. 3. Move it up, end to end, and count.  
  *Parent says:* Lie down straight. I'll lay the string baby beside you. Count with me.  
  *Child mode:* Lie down straight. Count how many string babies fit beside you.
  - Note: A child of 6 is usually a little more than two string babies long, about 110 to 120 centimetres.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask whether your child's head grew as much as the rest of their body.  
  *Parent says:* You are now more than two newborns long. Did your head grow that much too? Think about the head strips.  
  *Child mode:* You are more than two string babies long now. Did your head grow that much too?
  - Ask: Did your head grow as much as the rest of you? (good: No. My legs grew more, like in the head strip test.; No. A baby's head is already big.)

## Projects
### p1 · The Banana Jar: watch fruit fly babies grow (25 min, 12 days)
- **record:** Draw the jar on day 4, day 7 and day 10. Then draw the four stages in a circle.
- **schedule:** day 1: Put the banana in the open jar on the kitchen counter. When you see a few small flies inside, cover the jar with cloth. (6 min); day 4: Look closely at the banana. Can you see tiny white wrigglers? (3 min); day 7: Look for small brown cases on the side of the jar. Draw what you see. (4 min); day 10: Look for new flies inside the jar. Count them. (4 min); day 12: Draw the whole story: fly, maggot, brown case, fly. A grown-up lets the flies go outside. (8 min)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the banana in the open jar. 2. Leave the jar on the kitchen counter for a few hours, or a day. 3. When you see 3 or 4 tiny flies inside, put the cloth over the top. 4. Fix the cloth with the rubber band.  
  *Parent says:* Fruit flies love ripe banana. When some come in, we'll close the jar with cloth. Air gets through the cloth, but the flies can't get out. What do you think will happen?  
  *Child mode:* Put the banana in the jar. Wait for tiny flies. Then cover the jar with cloth.
  - Note: Fruit flies are common in Indian kitchens for most of the year, especially near ripe fruit. In a cold month, they may take a few days to come. If none come in a week, try again when the weather is warmer.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 4, look closely at the banana for tiny white wrigglers. 2. On day 7, look for small brown cases stuck to the glass. 3. Draw what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Those tiny white wrigglers are maggots. Maggots are fruit fly babies. Do they look like their parents?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the banana. Can you see white wrigglers? They are fly babies.
  - Ask: Do the maggots look like flies? (good: No. They have no wings and no legs.; No, they are white and wriggly.)
  - Note: The maggots are very small, smaller than a grain of rice even when fully grown. A magnifying glass helps. The brown cases are pupae. Inside each one, a maggot is changing into a fly.
  - Wait: Come back on days 4 and 7.
- **s3 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On day 10, count the new flies. 2. On day 12, draw the four stages in a circle: fly, maggot, brown case, fly. 3. A grown-up takes the jar outside and lifts the cloth to let the flies go.  
  *Parent says:* Where did all these new flies come from? Nobody put them in the jar.  
  *Child mode:* Count the new flies. Where did they come from? Draw the whole story.
  - Ask: Where did the new flies come from? (good: From the maggots.; The first flies laid eggs, and the eggs became maggots, and the maggots became flies.)
  - Note: If it is cool and there are no new flies by day 12, wait a few more days before you decide. After you let the flies go, wash your hands and rinse the jar. Do not eat the banana from the jar.
  - Check: What is a maggot? → **A baby fly**
  - Wait: Come back on days 10 and 12.

## Challenges
### c1 · A Nest for Two Eggs (20 min)
- **problem:** Build a nest that holds two lemons, our pretend eggs. Carry the nest across the room without dropping either lemon.
- **your turn:** Now design your own nest. Draw it, build it, and test it. Did both eggs stay in?
- **constraints:** Use no bowl, box or cup.; Use only things from home: paper, cloth, string and twigs if you have them.; The nest must hold two lemons while you walk across the room.
- **general:** Twist newspaper into long ropes and coil them round and round into a bowl shape.; Weave strips of paper over and under each other, like a mat, and bend the edges up.; Tie a cloth into a small sling, and put the eggs in the middle.
- **creative:** Make a soft lining inside with cloth or cotton, as many birds line their nests with feathers.; Copy a weaver bird: hang a long nest from a string, with the eggs in a pocket at the bottom.; Pigeons use only a few twigs. Try a nest with as little as you can, and see if it still works.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the problem: two lemons, and no bowl.  
  *Parent says:* These two lemons are eggs. Birds don't have bowls. How could you build a nest to keep them safe?  
  *Child mode:* These lemons are eggs. Make a nest with no bowl. How will you do it?
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child picks an idea, or invents one, and builds the nest.  
  *Parent says:* Which idea will you try? Let's build it. Keep checking if the lemons sit still.  
  *Child mode:* Pick an idea. Build your nest. Put the lemons in to check.
  - Note: Help with twisting the paper if small hands find it hard. Let your child decide the shape.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test: your child carries the nest across the room. Then ask what they would change.  
  *Parent says:* Carry your nest across the room. Did both eggs stay in? What would you change next time?  
  *Child mode:* Carry the nest across the room. Did the eggs stay in? What will you change?
  - Ask: What made your nest hold the eggs? (good: The sides were high.; The middle was deep.; It was tied tightly.)

## Worksheets
### Babies and their Parents (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (match) Draw a line from each baby to their grown-up.  
   **Answer:** Kitten → Cat; Calf → Cow; Tadpole → Frog; Caterpillar → Butterfly. *Why:* A kitten grows into a cat and a calf into a cow. A tadpole grows into a frog, and a caterpillar into a butterfly.
2. (sort) Colour the babies who look like a small copy of their parent. Circle the babies who look very different.  
   **Answer:** kitten → Looks like the parent; puppy → Looks like the parent; baby gecko → Looks like the parent; tadpole → Looks very different; maggot → Looks very different; silkworm → Looks very different. *Why:* Kittens, puppies and baby geckos look like small grown-ups. Tadpoles, maggots and silkworms change their whole body as they grow up.
3. (draw) Draw yourself as a baby, and yourself now. Make the baby's head the right size for the baby's body.  
   **Answer:** A baby with a big head, about a quarter of the body, and short legs. A child now with a smaller head for the body, and longer legs.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the baby's head is big for the body, and that the legs grow the most.
4. (mcq) In the head strip test, who was the most head strips tall?  
   Options: The grown-up / The child / The baby photo  
   **Answer:** The grown-up. *Why:* The grown-up was about 7 head strips tall, the child about 6 and the baby about 4. As we grow up, our legs grow much more than our head.
5. (truefalse) Every kind of animal has young. Humans do too.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Every kind of animal has young, and humans are animals.
6. (label) Here are three people drawn the same height. One is a baby, one is a child and one is a grown-up. Write B, C or G under each one.  
   **Answer:** B under the one with the biggest head and shortest legs. G under the one with the smallest head and longest legs. C under the one in between.. *Why:* The baby is about 4 heads long, the child about 6, and the grown-up about 7 or 8. The bigger the head is for the body, the younger the person.
7. (order) Put the pigeon pictures in order, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['Two white eggs in a nest', 'A pink chick with yellow fluff', 'A chick with grey feathers poking out', 'A grey pigeon flying']. *Why:* First the eggs. Then the chick hatches, pink with yellow fluff. Grey feathers grow. After about four weeks, the young pigeon flies.
8. (odd) Which baby does NOT drink milk from their mother?  
   Options: Kitten / Calf / Hen's chick / Puppy  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A hen's chick does not drink milk. Kittens, calves and puppies drink their mother's milk. Pigeons are different again: both pigeon parents make a milk in their throat for their chicks.
9. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: a tadpole looks nothing like a frog. How can we know that a tadpole is a baby frog?  
   **Answer:** We can watch a tadpole grow. The tadpole grows legs, loses the tail and becomes a frog. Or we can see frog eggs hatch into tadpoles.. *Why:* A strong answer says we find out by watching: we see the tadpole change, or see where tadpoles come from.
10. (perspective) Sowmya and Ritu, two girls in Class 1, look at a photo of a newborn baby and a photo of a grown-up.  
   **Answer:** Ritu is right: a baby is about 4 heads long and a grown-up about 7. But Sowmya is right that a baby has the same parts: two eyes, two arms and two legs.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the head strips to measure, and is fair to both girls.
### How Babies Grow and Who Cares for Them (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) A calf can stand within about an hour of being born. A human baby takes about a year to walk. Why might a calf need to stand so soon?  
   Options: To follow the mother and the herd, and keep safe / Because calves don't like lying down / To play with other calves  
   **Answer:** To follow the mother and the herd, and keep safe. *Why:* A calf needs to walk with the mother and the herd, away from danger, from the first day. A human baby is carried by grown-ups, so they can take longer.
2. (match) Draw a line from each parent to how they carry their young.  
   **Answer:** Mother cat → In her mouth, by the neck; Mother monkey → Holding on to her belly; Mother scorpion → On her back; Seahorse father → In a pouch on his belly. *Why:* Each parent carries the young in their own way. The seahorse father carries the eggs in his pouch until they hatch.
3. (truefalse) Only mothers look after babies.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Pigeon fathers sit on the eggs and feed the chicks. Seahorse fathers carry the eggs. Many human fathers look after their babies.
4. (draw) Here is a silkworm, eating a mulberry leaf. Draw what the silkworm will grow into.  
   **Answer:** A moth with wings: the silk moth.. *Why:* A silkworm is the young of a silk moth. The silkworm spins a case of silk, changes inside it, and comes out as a moth with wings.
5. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down: a baby crow has just hatched from her egg. What will the crow parents do for the baby in the first week?  
   **Answer:** They feed the baby, keep the baby warm under them, and keep the baby safe from cats and other danger.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two or three jobs that parents do and says why the baby cannot do them alone yet.
6. (sort) Sort the babies: hatched from an egg, or born?  
   **Answer:** baby gecko → Hatched from an egg; chick → Hatched from an egg; tadpole → Hatched from an egg; puppy → Born; calf → Born; human baby → Born. *Why:* Geckos, hens and frogs lay eggs, and the babies hatch. Dogs, cows and humans grow their babies inside the mother's body, and the babies are born.
7. (count) A cat has 3 kittens. A hen has 5 chicks. How many babies are there in all?  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* 3 kittens and 5 chicks make 8 babies. Count on from 5: 6, 7, 8.
8. (perspective) Lakshmi and Tanvi watch a pair of crows on a tree in Mysuru. The crows are feeding a koel chick. Koel mothers lay their eggs in crows' nests.  
   **Answer:** Both are right in a way. The chick is a koel, from a koel's egg, and will grow up to look like a koel. But the crows are the ones who feed the chick and keep the chick safe, so they are the parents who raise the chick.. *Why:* A strong answer sees that a parent can be the one who laid the egg, or the one who looks after the baby, and gives a reason for one side.
9. (label) Here is the banana jar on day 8. Label the maggot, the brown case and the fly.  
   **Answer:** The white wriggler on the banana is the maggot. The small brown case on the glass is the case, called a pupa. The tiny insect with wings is the fly.. *Why:* A fruit fly starts as a maggot, makes a brown case, and comes out as a fly. All three are the same kind of animal at different ages.
10. (mcq) A great hornbill mother seals herself into a tree hole with her eggs. Who brings her food?  
   Options: The father hornbill / Nobody, she goes hungry / The chicks  
   **Answer:** The father hornbill. *Why:* The father hornbill brings fruit to a thin slit in the wall, and passes it to the mother and, later, the chicks.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these animals has young?  
   Options: Only cats and dogs / Every kind of animal, humans too / Only birds  
   **Answer:** Every kind of animal, humans too. *Why:* Every kind of animal has young. Humans are animals too.
2. (mcq) What is the young of a cow called?  
   Options: A kitten / A calf / A chick  
   **Answer:** A calf. *Why:* A cow's young is a calf. A kitten is a young cat, and a chick is a young bird.
3. (mcq) A tadpole grows up into what?  
   Options: A fish / A frog / A snake  
   **Answer:** A frog. *Why:* A tadpole grows legs, loses the tail and becomes a frog. Many people guess fish, because tadpoles swim.
4. (mcq) Which baby looks very different from the parent?  
   Options: A kitten / A puppy / A caterpillar  
   **Answer:** A caterpillar. *Why:* A caterpillar has no wings and crawls on leaves. The grown-up is a butterfly or a moth. Kittens and puppies look like small cats and dogs.
5. (mcq) Where do maggots come from?  
   Options: From eggs that flies lay / From rotting banana, by themselves / From the rain  
   **Answer:** From eggs that flies lay. *Why:* Maggots are fly babies. They hatch from eggs that flies lay on food. People once thought maggots grew out of rotting food by themselves.
6. (mcq) A newborn baby is about how many of their own heads long?  
   Options: About 4 / About 8 / About 20  
   **Answer:** About 4. *Why:* A newborn is about 4 heads long. A grown-up is about 7 or 8 heads tall, because the legs grow so much.
7. (mcq) As a child grows up, which part grows the most?  
   Options: The head / The legs / The ears  
   **Answer:** The legs. *Why:* The legs grow the most. That is why a grown-up is more heads tall than a baby.
8. (mcq) Who feeds a pigeon chick?  
   Options: Only the mother / Both the mother and the father / Nobody  
   **Answer:** Both the mother and the father. *Why:* Both pigeon parents make a milk in their throat and feed the chicks. They also take turns sitting on the eggs.
9. (mcq) Which father carries the eggs in a pouch on his belly?  
   Options: The seahorse father / The cat father / The crow father  
   **Answer:** The seahorse father. *Why:* A seahorse father carries the eggs in his pouch until the babies hatch and swim out.
10. (mcq) Before measuring with the head strips, what did a good scientist do first?  
   Options: Guessed, and said why / Measured only the grown-up / Asked a friend for the answer  
   **Answer:** Guessed, and said why. *Why:* Scientists guess first and say why. Then they test, and look at what really happened.

## Applications
- At every check-up, doctors and nurses measure a baby's length and the size of the head, to see that the baby is growing well.
- Silk farmers in Karnataka, Assam and other states raise silkworms, the young of silk moths, on mulberry and other leaves.
- Fish farmers raise baby fish in small ponds, then move them to big ponds when they are bigger.
- Along the Odisha coast, people guard olive ridley turtle nests, so the hatchlings can reach the sea safely.

## Open questions
- **Does a butterfly remember being a caterpillar?** In one study, moths remembered a smell they had learned to avoid as caterpillars. But nobody knows how much of the caterpillar's life the moth or butterfly remembers.
- **Why do human children stay young for so long?** A human takes many years to grow up, much longer than a cat or a dog. Scientists think it gives time to learn, but they still argue about why.
- **What does a chick feel when the parent comes back to the nest?** Chicks call and open their beaks wide when a parent lands. We can see what they do, but nobody knows what it feels like for the chick.

## Connected maths
- In the head strip test, a grown-up was 7 head strips tall. A child was 6. How many more head strips tall was the grown-up? **1 more head strip.**. 7 take away 6 leaves 1. The grown-up's legs and body have grown more than the head, so the grown-up is more heads tall. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L1`)
- Put these in order, from fewest heads tall to most: the grown-up (7), the baby (4), the child (6). **Baby 4, child 6, grown-up 7.**. 4 is less than 6, and 6 is less than 7. The older the person, the more heads tall, because the legs keep growing. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- A street dog had 6 puppies. An elephant had 1 calf. How many more babies did the dog have? Which mother can give each baby more time? **5 more babies. The elephant mother can give her one calf more time.**. 6 take away 1 is 5. With only one calf, the elephant mother, the aunts and the herd can care for that calf for years. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L1`)
- On day 10, a girl called Meena looked at her banana jar. She counted 3 maggots, 4 brown cases and 5 flies. Make a tally. Which was there most of? **Flies, with 5.**. Maggots: 3 marks.
