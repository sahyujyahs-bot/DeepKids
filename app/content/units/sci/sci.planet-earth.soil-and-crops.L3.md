# Soil: made from broken rock and dead plants, and mixed by worms
`sci.planet-earth.soil-and-crops.L3`

**Summary.** A handful of soil looks like one brown material, but it is a mix. Most of it is rock, broken into grains so small you can hardly see them. Some of it is humus, the dark remains of dead leaves, roots and animals. Air and water fill the gaps between the grains, and earthworms and countless tiny living things live there. Different rocks make different soils, so farmers in India grow rice in river soil, ragi in red soil and cotton in black soil.

**Intro.** *Put a spoon of soil from a flower pot into a glass of water and stir it. Some grains sink at once. The water turns cloudy and stays cloudy. Small dark bits float on top. Why does soil come apart like this in water?* Soil comes apart in water because it was never one thing. It is a mix of grains of different sizes, broken off rocks, and pieces of plants that were once alive. In this unit you shake soil in a jar and watch it sort itself into layers. You find out how rock becomes sand and clay, how dead leaves become humus, and how earthworms mix it all. You also find out why different soils in India grow different crops.

## Terms
- **Soil**: The loose top layer of the ground, where plants grow. It is a mix of rock grains, humus, air, water and living things. *e.g.* The brown earth in a flower pot is soil.
- **Rock**: The hard, solid material that the Earth's outer layer is made of. *e.g.* Granite, the speckled stone used for kitchen counters, is a rock.
- **Weathering**: The slow breaking up of rock into smaller and smaller pieces by heat, water, wind and living things. *e.g.* Over many years, a crack in a stone wall grows wider and bits of stone fall off.
- **Sand**: The biggest grains in soil. You can see each grain, and sand feels gritty when you rub it. *e.g.* The sand used to build houses, and the sand on Marina Beach in Chennai.
- **Silt**: Grains smaller than sand. Dry silt feels smooth, like flour. *e.g.* The fine mud left behind on the steps of a ghat when river water goes down.
- **Clay**: The smallest grains in soil, far too small to see one by one. Wet clay is sticky and can be shaped. *e.g.* Potters shape clay into pots and lamps, and fire them to make terracotta.
- **Humus**: The dark, soft part of soil made from dead leaves, roots and animals after they have rotted. *e.g.* The dark, crumbly layer under fallen leaves beneath a tree.
- **Decompose**: To rot and break down into simpler materials. Living things called decomposers do this work. *e.g.* A banana peel left on damp soil decomposes in a few weeks.
- **Nutrients**: Materials that a living thing needs to grow and stay healthy. Plants take some of theirs from the soil, dissolved in water. *e.g.* Farmers add manure to a field to give it more nutrients.
- **Earthworm**: A long, soft animal with no legs and no bones, who lives in soil and eats dead plants and soil. *e.g.* After heavy rain, earthworms are sometimes seen on a wet path.
- **Burrow**: A tunnel an animal digs in the ground to live or move in. To burrow means to dig such a tunnel. *e.g.* An earthworm burrows by pushing through the soil and eating the soil in their way.
- **Worm castings**: The small heaps of soil that come out of an earthworm's body after they have eaten. They are full of nutrients. *e.g.* Little curly heaps on a lawn after rain are worm castings.
- **Crop**: A plant that farmers grow in large amounts for food, cloth or other uses. *e.g.* Rice, ragi, sugarcane and cotton are crops.
- **Topsoil**: The top layer of soil, usually darker, with the most humus and the most living things. Most roots grow in it. *e.g.* When a pit is dug for a building, the top part of the wall of the pit is darker. That is topsoil.

## Explanations
### What soil is made of · *defined*
**Definition:** Soil is a mix of rock grains, humus, air and water, with many living things in it.

Most of the solid part of soil is rock, broken into grains. The grains come in three sizes: sand, silt and clay. Mixed in with them is humus, made from dead plants and animals. Between the grains are tiny gaps. Some gaps are full of air, and some are full of water. Plant roots need both. Roots take in water from the gaps, and they also need air to stay alive. In a good garden soil, roughly half is solid grains and humus. The other half is gaps, filled with air and water. Earthworms, ants, fungi and bacteria live in the gaps too. A teaspoon of healthy soil can hold hundreds of millions of bacteria.
- e.g. Soil from a flower pot, shaken in a jar of water, settles into layers of sand, silt and clay, with bits of humus floating on top.
- e.g. Squeeze a lump of damp soil and drops of water come out of the gaps.
- e.g. Drop a lump of dry soil into water and small bubbles rise. That was air in the gaps.
### How rock becomes soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Weathering is the slow breaking up of rock into smaller pieces by heat, water, wind, ice and living things.

All the grains in soil were once part of solid rock. Rock breaks up in many ways. In hot places, the Sun heats a rock by day and the night cools it. The outside of the rock grows and shrinks more than the inside, and thin pieces crack off. Rain gets into cracks. In cold mountains, the water freezes, grows bigger and pushes the crack wider. Some rain water slowly dissolves parts of the rock. Plant roots grow into cracks and widen them as they grow. Rivers roll stones against each other and wear them into pebbles and sand. Every one of these is slow. It can take hundreds of years, sometimes a thousand, to make one centimetre of new soil.
- e.g. A peepal plant growing out of a crack in an old temple wall slowly pushes the stones apart.
- e.g. River pebbles are smooth and round because they were knocked against other stones for a long time.
- e.g. The granite hills around Bengaluru and Krishnagiri have rounded boulders with thin flakes peeling off.
### Sand, silt and clay, and why they settle in layers · *defined*
**Definition:** Sand grains are the biggest, silt grains are smaller, and clay grains are the smallest. In still water, bigger and heavier grains sink faster.

You can see a single grain of sand. It feels gritty between your fingers. Silt grains are much smaller and feel smooth, like atta. Clay grains are so small that hundreds would fit side by side across one sand grain. Wet clay feels sticky and holds together. When you shake soil in water, all the grains are mixed up. Then you put the jar down. The heavy sand grains sink in a few seconds. The silt takes many minutes. The clay grains are so light that the water can keep them floating for a day or more. That is why the water stays cloudy. Bits of dead plants are often lighter than water, so they float on top.
- e.g. In the soil jar test, a sand layer forms at the bottom within a minute.
- e.g. A muddy river looks brown because of tiny silt and clay grains carried in the water.
- e.g. Water in a well or a tank becomes clear when it is left still, as the fine grains sink.
### Humus: how dead plants become part of soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Humus is the dark, soft part of soil made when dead plants and animals decompose.

Leaves, flowers and roots die and fall onto the soil. So do dead insects and animal droppings. Decomposers eat them. Decomposers are living things such as fungi, bacteria, earthworms and woodlice. They break the dead things into smaller and smaller pieces. What is left at the end is humus. Humus makes soil dark and crumbly. It soaks up water like a sponge and holds it for the roots. It also releases nutrients that plants need. This is why farmers spread manure and compost on their fields. A forest needs no farmer, because leaves keep falling and decomposing every year.
- e.g. Under a big tree, the top of the soil is dark with humus from fallen leaves.
- e.g. Farmers in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka spread farmyard manure on their fields before the rains.
- e.g. Compost made from kitchen peels is mostly humus.
### Earthworms: how their bodies fit life in soil · *defined*
**Definition:** An earthworm is a soft animal with a body made of many rings. They live in tunnels in damp soil and eat dead plants and soil.

An earthworm has a hard problem. They live in the dark, packed earth, and they must move through it, find food and breathe. Their body solves each part. They have no lungs. They take in air through their skin, and air can pass through skin only when it is damp. So an earthworm's body makes slime that keeps their skin wet. They have no legs either. Their body is made of rings, and each ring has tiny bristles that grip the tunnel walls. Muscles squeeze the front rings thin and long, to push forward. Then the back rings bunch up and are pulled along. Where the soil is too packed to push through, the worm eats the way through. Their food is the dead leaves and the humus mixed in the soil. They have no eyes, but their skin senses light, and they move away from bright light.
- e.g. Hold an earthworm gently on a wet palm and you can feel the bristles grip as they move.
- e.g. Earthworms come up on wet paths after heavy rain, when the soil is soaked.
- e.g. Worms kept for vermicompost are given damp, shaded bins, because dry skin and sunlight harm them.
### What earthworms do for soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Earthworms mix soil, make tunnels, and leave castings full of nutrients.

An earthworm swallows soil and bits of dead leaves as they move. Inside their gut, the food is ground up and partly digested. What comes out at the other end is a worm casting. Castings hold more nutrients that plants can use than the soil the worm ate. Earthworms also pull fallen leaves down into their burrows to eat. So they carry humus down and bring deeper soil up. Their tunnels let air and rain water into the soil. Roots often grow down along old worm tunnels, where the soil is easier to push through. Charles Darwin spent many years measuring this work, and found that worms slowly bury stones and old buildings under their castings.
- e.g. Vermicompost, sold in Indian nurseries, is mostly worm castings.
- e.g. In the worm tunnel test, water soaked into soil with tunnels much faster than into packed soil.
- e.g. Leaves pulled half into the ground, standing up like little flags, show where a worm is feeding.
### Soils of India and the crops grown in them · *defined*
**Definition:** A soil takes its colour and grain sizes from the rock it came from, and from the weather and living things where it formed.

India has several main kinds of soil. Red soil covers much of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka. It formed from very old rocks, and it is red because it holds iron, the same way rust is red. Ragi and groundnut grow well in it. Black soil covers much of the Deccan plateau, in Maharashtra and parts of Karnataka and Telangana. It formed from dark lava rock. It is full of clay, holds water for a long time, and cracks wide open in summer. Cotton grows well in it, so it is also called black cotton soil. Alluvial soil is silt and clay laid down by rivers. It covers the plains of the Ganga and the deltas of the Kaveri and the Godavari. Rice and wheat grow in it. Laterite soil is found in Kerala and along the west coast. It forms where heavy rain, year after year, washes most other minerals out and leaves iron behind. It can be cut into blocks for building, and cashew grows well in it.
- e.g. The Kaveri delta around Thanjavur grows so much rice that it is called the rice bowl of Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. Old houses in Kerala and Mangaluru are often built of red laterite blocks.
- e.g. In the dry months, fields of black soil in the Deccan crack into deep gaps you could put your hand into.
### Why crops need soil, and why it must be kept · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants make their own food from light, air and water. They take water and nutrients from the soil through their roots, and soil holds them up.

A rice plant makes their food in their leaves, using sunlight. But they need water and nutrients, and these come from the soil, through the roots. The roots also anchor the plant, so wind and rain cannot knock them over. Every crop taken from a field carries away some of the soil's nutrients. So farmers put nutrients back with manure, compost and fertilisers. Topsoil can also be lost. Heavy rain washes bare soil away, and strong wind blows dry soil away. This is called soil erosion. Because new soil forms so slowly, soil that is lost is gone for hundreds of years. Farmers protect it with plant roots, trees at the edges of fields, and walls or terraces on hill slopes.
- e.g. On the hills of the Nilgiris, tea is planted in rows across the slope, which slows the rain running down.
- e.g. After the monsoon, rivers run brown with soil washed off bare land.
- e.g. The Soil Health Card scheme, started by the Government of India in 2015, tests farmers' soil and tells them which nutrients it needs.
### Roots and soil life sense and choose · *debated*
Roots sense the soil around them. They grow towards water and towards patches rich in nutrients, and away from salt and from hard ground. Roots and fungi in the soil trade with each other. The fungi bring water and nutrients to the root, and the plant gives the fungi sugar made in the leaves. Bacteria in the soil sense chemicals and swim towards food. Earthworms sense light, touch, damp and shaking ground, and decide where to go. All of these beings feel and choose. Soil is full of minds at work.
- e.g. A root grows sideways towards a damp patch in a pot, rather than straight down into dry soil.
- e.g. Earthworms move away from a bright torch held over their soil.
- e.g. Most kinds of soil bacteria have never been grown or named in a laboratory.
- **Many plant scientists:** Roots sense and respond to water, nutrients and touch, and they make choices about where to grow. Some scientists call this plant intelligence, a growing field of study.
- **Other scientists:** These are automatic responses, built in by evolution, like a thermostat switching on. They say the word choice should be kept for animals with brains.

## Mostly agreed
- The mineral grains in soil come from rock that was broken up by weathering. *How we know:* Under a soil, digging down usually reaches broken rock and then solid rock. The grains in the soil are made of the same minerals as the rock below or nearby.
- Humus forms when decomposers break down dead plants and animals. *How we know:* Leaves left on damp soil slowly turn dark and crumbly. Scientists have watched and measured this in fields and in laboratories for over a hundred years.
- Earthworms mix the soil and leave castings that are richer in plant nutrients than the soil around them. *How we know:* Charles Darwin measured castings for many years and published his results in 1881. Many later studies measured the nutrients in castings.
- New soil forms very slowly, and soil can be lost quickly by erosion. *How we know:* The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations reports that one centimetre of soil can take up to a thousand years to form. One heavy storm on bare land can wash away more than that.
- Different rocks and climates make different soils. *How we know:* Soil scientists since Vasily Dokuchaev, in the 1880s, have mapped soils and found that they follow the rock below, the climate, the plants and the shape of the land.

## How we found out
**Vasily Dokuchaev, a Russian geologist, working for the Free Economic Society in St Petersburg, 1877 to 1883 (fieldwork across the black earth, and his book Russian Chernozem).** In the 1800s, most scientists thought soil was simply the top layer of rock, crumbled into dust. Some thought the rich black soil of southern Russia, called chernozem, meaning black earth, had been left behind by an ancient sea or by swamps. In 1877 a young geologist, Vasily Dokuchaev, began a study of the black earth for the Free Economic Society in St Petersburg. For several summers he travelled across the black earth lands. He dug pits and studied the soil layer by layer, from the dark top down to the rock below. He found that similar rocks could lie under very different soils, and that the soils changed with the climate and the plants growing there. The black earth was darkest where grasses had grown, died and decomposed for thousands of years, adding humus to the top layers. In 1883 he wrote that a soil forms from its rock, its climate, its living things, the shape of the land and the time it has had. Rock alone could not explain a soil. His work began the science of soils.  
*What people believed before:* People thought soil was just crumbled rock, and some thought the black earth was left by a sea or by swamps. Dokuchaev showed that soil forms from rock together with climate, living things, the shape of the land and time, and that the black earth was dark with humus from grasses.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Soil is just dirt, the same everywhere.** (A very common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Most soil looks like plain brown earth at a glance.  
  Against: A jar test shows different soils have very different amounts of sand, silt, clay and humus. India alone has red, black, alluvial and laterite soils, and they grow different crops.
- **If you cut an earthworm in half, you get two worms.** (A common belief among children; *rejected*)  
  For: A cut worm's two halves can both wriggle for a while.  
  Against: Usually only the front part, with the mouth, can survive and grow a new tail. The back part dies. Some kinds of worm cannot regrow at all.
- **Earthworms come up after rain because they would drown in wet soil.** (A common explanation, in many books; *debated*)  
  For: Worms do come up when the soil is soaked, and water in the soil holds less air than the gaps did.  
  Against: Earthworms can survive for days in water that has air in it. Some scientists think worms come up because moving over wet ground is easy and safe from drying out, so it is a good time to travel to new places. Others think the drumming of rain makes them come up.
- **Earthworms are always good for the land.** (Most gardeners and farmers; *accepted*)  
  For: In fields and gardens, worms mix soil, make tunnels and leave castings that help crops.  
  Against: In some northern forests of North America, there were no earthworms for thousands of years after the ice ages. Worms brought there from Europe and Asia eat the thick layer of fallen leaves that forest plants and animals depend on.
- **Plants eat soil to grow.** (An old idea, and a common guess today; *rejected*)  
  For: Plants grow in soil, and a field can wear out if crops are grown again and again.  
  Against: Most of a plant's weight comes from air and water, joined together using sunlight. From the soil, a plant takes water and small amounts of nutrients. A big tree grown in a tub hardly lowers the amount of soil in it.

## Roots
We have shaken soil in a jar, made worm tunnels and looked at where soil comes from. Now we ask what soil tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In your jar, the soil split into layers. Sand sank first, fine clay stayed cloudy for a day, and dead plant bits floated. The grains are rock broken very small, and the floating bits were once alive. So the ground under every crop is old rock and old life mixed together.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call soil dirt, as if it were one plain, worthless material. The jar shows it is two very old things mixed: mountains broken down over centuries, and plants and animals who lived and died there.  
*Evidence:* In the Soil Jar, sand settled within a minute, silt within half an hour and clay by the next day, while dark bits of dead roots and leaves floated on top. In Rock to Grains, knocking chalk made rounded pieces and powder like silt.

- *who am i:* Your body is built from rice, dal and vegetables that grew in soil, so you are partly made of what the rocks and old leaves gave them.
- *what is life:* Living things take from the soil while they live, and give their bodies back to it when they die, as humus for the next plants.
- *what is this world:* The ground under a city is mountains worn down over millions of years and mixed with the remains of countless living beings.

A rock on a hill and a leaf on a tree look like two separate things. Over hundreds of years, the rock is broken into grains and the leaf decomposes into humus. Earthworms swallow both and mix them in their gut. A rice plant then grows from the mix, and a family eats the rice. The same few materials keep going round, taking a new shape each time.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 2.1:** From space came air, from air fire, from fire water, from water earth. From earth came plants, from plants food, and from food the person. (The unit follows the last part of this chain. Rice grows from soil, and food from the rice builds the child's own body.)
- **Atharva Veda 12.1.12, the Bhumi Sukta:** The Earth is my mother, and I am her child. (Soil feeds every crop that feeds us, the way a mother feeds a child. The hymn asks us to treat the ground with the same care.)

- Science (settled): The atoms in our bodies were once in soil, water and air, and they will go back there. Atoms are not made or lost, only moved.
- Science (open): Most of the living things in soil, especially bacteria and fungi, have never been named or studied.
- Other view, Many soil scientists: Soil is a mix of minerals, water, air and living things. Only the living things in it are alive. The sand and clay are minerals, and calling soil a mother, or alive as a whole, is a picture, not a finding.

## Story: The Tired Pot
*The story is made up. The soil facts in it are true: soil in an old pot gets packed hard and loses its humus, water runs off packed soil, and compost and dead leaves bring humus back.*

Lakshmi lived on the third floor of a block of flats in Mysuru, with her little brother Arjun, their mother, and their grandmother, Ajji. On the balcony stood a row of pots. The biggest held a curry leaf plant that Ajji had planted when Lakshmi was a baby.

That summer, the curry leaf plant looked tired. Their leaves were small and pale. Some had turned yellow and dropped off. Every morning Arjun poured a mug of water into the pot, and every morning most of it ran over the top and down the side.

"The soil won't drink," said Arjun. He was six. He poked the soil with his finger. It was hard and grey, like a dry road.

Ajji came out with her tea. She bent down and pressed the soil with her thumb. "This soil is tired," she said. "It has fed this plant for nine years, and nobody has fed the soil back."

"Soil eats?" said Arjun, and laughed.

"Wait," said Lakshmi. She went inside and came back with an empty jam jar. She had done this in a science lesson the month before. She dug out two spoons of soil from the pot and put them in the jar. Then she filled it with water, closed the lid tight and gave it to Arjun. "Shake it as hard as you can. Count to a hundred."

Arjun shook it until his arms hurt. Then Lakshmi put the jar on the balcony wall, and they watched. Grains rained down to the bottom in a few seconds. Above them, a finer layer settled slowly. The water stayed cloudy brown.

"The bottom grains are sand," said Lakshmi. "The smooth layer is silt. The cloudy part is clay. All of it is rock, broken very small." She pointed to the top of the water. "Now look here. In my lesson, the jar had lots of dark bits floating on top. Those were bits of dead leaves. That is humus."

Arjun looked. There were only three tiny bits floating.

"So our soil is nearly all rock," said Lakshmi slowly. "The humus is almost gone."

Ajji nodded. "In my field near Mandya, nobody waters the soil with a mug. The rice straw is ploughed back in. The cows leave dung. The leaves of the coconut trees fall and rot. The earthworms pull it all down and mix it in. The soil is fed every year. In a pot, the plant takes and takes, and nothing comes back."

"And the water runs off because it's hard," said Lakshmi. "There are no worm tunnels. No gaps."

That weekend, Ajji took them to the nursery near the market. The woman at the nursery sold them a small bag of compost and a small bag of vermicompost. "Vermi means worm," she told Arjun. "This is what comes out of earthworms. Plants love it."

Arjun held the bag a long way from his nose. Then he sniffed it. It smelled like the ground after rain.

At home, they lifted the curry leaf plant out of the pot. Ajji held the stem, and Lakshmi gently shook the old soil off the roots. The roots had grown round and round the inside of the pot, looking for room. Lakshmi mixed a third of the old soil with the compost and the vermicompost. The new mix was dark and soft and crumbly. They put the plant back and pressed the soil down lightly with their fingers, not hard.

Arjun poured his mug of water. This time it soaked straight in, with a small fizz of bubbles.

"Bubbles!" he said.

"That's air coming out of the gaps," said Lakshmi. "The water is going in to take its place."

Lakshmi shook up a new jar with the new soil. This time, a thick dark layer of humus floated on top of the water.

Two weeks later, small new leaves opened at the tips of the curry leaf plant. They were bright green. Ajji picked a handful for the rasam, the first in a long time.

The next morning, Lakshmi found Arjun on the balcony with a pile of fallen curry leaves and a few dry flowers from the jasmine pot. He was not putting them in the bin. He was laying them on top of the soil in the big pot.

"What are you doing?" asked Lakshmi.

"Feeding it back," said Arjun. "So it doesn't get tired again."

**For the parent:** For the parent: soil in a pot gets packed down by years of watering and has no worms to loosen it, so water runs off instead of soaking in. The plant's roots also use up the nutrients, and the humus decomposes away with nothing new added. In a field or forest, fallen leaves, crop waste and dung keep adding humus, and worms keep mixing it in. Lakshmi's jar showed the difference: the old soil had almost no floating humus, and the new mix had a thick layer.

- Ask: Why did the water run off the top of the old pot?
- Ask: What did Lakshmi's jar show about the old soil?
- Ask: Where does the soil in Ajji's field get new humus from every year?
- Ask: Why were there bubbles when Arjun watered the new soil?
- Ask: Arjun put fallen leaves on top of the pot. What will happen to them over the next few weeks?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Soil Jar: what is soil made of? (30 min, 2 days)
- **question:** If we shake soil in water and leave it still, what will it separate into?
- **predict:** Look at the soil and feel it. What do you think will happen when we shake it in water and put the jar down? Will it all sink, or all float, or something else? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use a clear jar with straight sides, so the layers are easy to see and measure. Fill it one third with soil and nearly to the top with water. Shake it for a full two minutes, so every lump breaks up. Then do not move the jar at all, because a bump stirs the layers again. If you test two soils, use two jars of the same size with the same amounts.
- **safety:** Wash hands with soap after touching soil. Soil holds many bacteria, and a few can make a cut sore. Cover any cut on the hands before you start. A grown-up handles a glass jar.
- **record:** Draw the jar after 1 minute, after 30 minutes and the next day. Label each layer: sand, silt, clay, water and floating bits. Write the height of each layer.
- **results table:** When | What I see at the bottom | What I see in the middle | What I see in the water | What floats on top | Height of the settled layers (cm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Look at the soil, guess, shake the jar, and watch the first layers settle. Mark them. (25 min); day 2: Look at the jar again. Mark the new layers and measure them. (5 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread one spoon of soil on a white paper. 2. Look closely, with a magnifying glass if you have one. 3. Rub a pinch between finger and thumb.  
  *Parent says:* Before we test it, let's look closely. What can you see in the soil? What does it feel like when you rub it?  
  *Child mode:* Spread a spoon of soil on white paper. Look closely. Rub a pinch. What do you see and feel?
  - Ask: What do you see in the soil? (good: Tiny grains of different colours.; Little bits of root or leaf.; Some bits are bigger than others.)
  - Note: Most pot soil has grains you can see, finer powder, and small dark bits of plants. Gritty means sand. Smooth means silt. Sticky when wet means clay.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what will happen in the jar, and why. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will shake this soil in water, then leave the jar still. What do you think will happen? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will shake soil in water. Then the jar stays still. What will happen? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think the jar will look like after an hour? (good: The soil will sink to the bottom.; Some bits will float.; The water will stay muddy.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the jar one third with soil. 2. Add water until it is nearly full. 3. Add a few drops of dish-washing liquid. 4. Close the lid tightly. 5. Shake hard for two minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Fill it one third with soil, then water nearly to the top. Close the lid tight. Now shake it hard for two whole minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Soil to one third. Water nearly to the top. Lid on tight. Shake for two minutes!
  - Note: A grown-up checks the lid is tight and holds a glass jar over a towel. Take turns shaking. Two minutes is longer than it sounds. Every lump must break up.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the jar down on a shelf where nobody will touch it. 2. Watch for one minute. 3. Mark the top of the bottom layer with a pen or tape.  
  *Parent says:* Put it down gently and watch. What is happening at the bottom? What is happening at the top?  
  *Child mode:* Put the jar down. Watch for one minute. Mark the bottom layer.
  - Ask: Which grains reached the bottom first? (good: The big gritty ones.; The heavy grains sank first.)
  - Note: Within a minute, the biggest grains, the sand, form a layer at the bottom. Dark bits of plants float on top. The water stays brown and cloudy. If your soil is mostly coconut fibre, called cocopeat, a lot will float. That is still a result: it means the soil is mostly plant material.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. After 30 minutes, look again. 2. Mark the top of the new, smoother layer. 3. Draw the jar.  
  *Parent says:* Half an hour later, look again. Is there a new layer on top of the sand? Is the water still cloudy?  
  *Child mode:* Look again after 30 minutes. Is there a new layer? Mark it and draw the jar.
  - Note: A finer layer of silt has settled on top of the sand. The water is still cloudy because the clay grains are so small they are still floating.
  - Check: The water in the jar is still cloudy after 30 minutes. Why? → **Very tiny clay grains are still floating in the water**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The next day, look at the jar without moving it. 2. Mark the clay layer. 3. Measure each layer with a ruler.  
  *Parent says:* It's the next day. Is the water clearer? Can you see a thin layer on top of the silt? Let's measure each layer.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the jar today. Is the water clearer? Measure each layer.
  - Note: The water is clearer and a thin, smooth clay layer sits on top of the silt. In very clayey soil the water may need two or three days to clear. The layers may be hard to tell apart in pot soil. Look for changes in colour and how fine the grains look.
  - Wait: Leave the jar still until tomorrow.
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think soil is made of? What did you see that shows it? Why did the layers form in that order?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What is soil made of? What did you see? Why that order?
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: soil is a mix of grains of different sizes and bits of dead plants. What I saw: sand sank in seconds, silt in half an hour, clay by the next day, and dark bits floated. Why: the biggest, heaviest grains sink fastest, and the lightest grains sink slowest. The grains are broken rock. The floating bits were once alive.
  - Check: What were the dark bits floating on top? → **Bits of dead plants, which are part of the humus**
### e2 · Worm Tunnels: do holes help rain soak into packed soil? (25 min)
- **question:** Earthworms make tunnels in soil. Does water soak into packed soil faster when it has tunnels?
- **predict:** We will pack soil hard into two cups. One cup gets pencil tunnels, like worm tunnels. Then we pour the same water on both. Which cup will soak up the water faster? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two cups of the same size, the same dry soil, and the same amount in each. Press both down equally hard. Pour the same amount of water, in the same way, onto both. Only the tunnels change. Do the test twice, because a tunnel can be blocked by chance.
- **safety:** Wash hands after touching soil. Put the soil back in the pot when you finish.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw both cups, with the water on top and the tunnels.
- **results table:** Cup | Tunnels? | Try 1: seconds until no water is left on top | Try 2: seconds | What I saw
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill both cups three quarters full with dry soil. 2. Press each one down hard with the bottom of a tumbler, ten times. 3. Make the tops level.  
  *Parent says:* Feet, rain and years of watering press soil down hard. Let's make two cups of packed soil. Press each one ten times.  
  *Child mode:* Fill both cups with soil. Press each one down hard, ten times.
  - Note: Press both cups the same way, with the same number of presses. This is packed soil, like the hard soil of a footpath or an old pot that has been watered for years.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push the pencil straight down into one cup, ten times, all over the top. 2. Pull it out gently each time. Leave the other cup alone.  
  *Parent says:* An earthworm leaves tunnels behind them as they move. Let's make ten worm tunnels in this cup with the pencil. The other cup gets none.  
  *Child mode:* Push the pencil down ten times in one cup. These are your worm tunnels.
  - Note: The tunnels should go most of the way down.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess which cup will soak up the water faster, and why.  
  *Parent says:* We will pour the same water on both. Which cup will drink it faster? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which cup will soak up water faster? Why?
  - Note: Write the guess and the reason before you pour.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure half a small tumbler of water. 2. Pour it gently onto the cup with no tunnels. Start the clock. 3. Stop when no water is left standing on top. 4. Do the same with the tunnel cup.  
  *Parent says:* Pour gently, all at once. Watch the top. Tell me when there is no water left on top.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the water. Start the clock. Stop when no water is on top.
  - Note: On packed soil, a puddle sits on top for a long time, and some may overflow. With tunnels, the water drains down the holes in a few seconds, often with bubbles as air comes out. If the packed cup takes very long, stop at three minutes and write 'more than three minutes'.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty both cups onto newspaper, mix the soil, and let it dry a little, or use fresh dry soil. 2. Repeat the test once.  
  *Parent says:* One test can be a fluke. Let's do it again with fresh soil. Do we get the same result?  
  *Child mode:* Do it again. Is it the same?
  - Note: Wet soil soaks water differently from dry soil, so use fresh dry soil for the second try.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think tunnels make a difference?  
  *Child mode:* What did you find? What did you see? Why?
  - Note: A good answer: tunnels help water soak in faster. The tunnel cup was dry on top in a few seconds, and the packed cup kept a puddle. Water runs down the tunnels, and the air in them bubbles out. Earthworm tunnels do the same in a field, so rain soaks in instead of running off with the soil.
  - Check: Why does rain soak into a field with many earthworms faster? → **Their tunnels let water run down into the soil**
### e3 · Rock to Grains: does knocking break rock into smaller pieces? (25 min)

- **record:** Draw one piece of chalk before and after. Write how many pieces there were, and which box made more powder.
- **results table:** Box | Pieces before | What the corners looked like before | Pieces after | What the corners look like after | How much powder
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Break both chalk sticks into pieces about as big as a fingernail. 2. Count six pieces into each box. 3. Look at their sharp corners. 4. Add water to one box.  
  *Parent says:* Here are pieces of chalk with sharp corners. Chalk is made from minerals dug out of rock. Six pieces go in each box. This box gets water too.  
  *Child mode:* Break the chalk into small pieces. Put six in each box. Add water to one.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess what will happen, and why.  
  *Parent says:* What will happen to the corners after five minutes of shaking? Which box will make more powder? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* What will the corners look like after shaking? Which box makes more powder? Why?
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Close both lids tightly. 2. Shake each box for five minutes. Take turns, so both get the same shaking.  
  *Parent says:* Shake hard, for five minutes each. Can you hear the pieces knocking?  
  *Child mode:* Shake each box for five minutes. Listen to the knocking.
  - Note: Take turns, and count the shakes, so both boxes get the same amount.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour the wet box through the strainer onto a dark plate. 2. Tip out the dry box beside it. 3. Count the pieces. Look at the corners and the powder.

- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see? How is this like a river and its stones?  
  *Child mode:* What happened? What did you see? How is it like a river?
  - Note: A good answer: knocking breaks rock into smaller pieces and powder, and rounds the corners. Real rock is much harder than chalk, so a river needs many years, not minutes. This is one way rock becomes the sand and silt in soil.
  - Check: River pebbles are smooth and round. Why? → **They were knocked against other stones for a long time, which wore off their corners**

## Open questions
- **How many different kinds of living things live in soil?** Most kinds of soil bacteria and fungi have never been grown or named in a laboratory. Scientists find new ones every year.
- **How fast does new soil form in different places?** It depends on the rock, the climate, the plants and much more. It is hard to measure because it is so slow, and estimates vary a lot.
- **Why exactly do earthworms come to the surface after rain?** Scientists have several ideas, such as escaping soil with less air, travelling while the ground is wet, or reacting to the drumming of rain. None is proved for every kind of worm.
- **How do roots and fungi decide how much to trade with each other?** Scientists know that plants and fungi trade sugar for nutrients, and that both sides seem to choose partners. How they balance the trade is still being studied.

- [Lalbagh Rock, a National Geological Monument](https://www.gsi.gov.in) · place · Bengaluru: A huge outcrop of very old rock called peninsular gneiss, about 3,000 million years old, in Lalbagh garden. You can see the rock that much of South India's red soil comes from.
- [Soil Health Card portal](https://soilhealth.dac.gov.in) · interactive: The Government of India's site for the Soil Health Card scheme, which tests farmers' soil. A parent can show what a soil test report looks like.
- [FAO Global Soil Partnership](https://www.fao.org/global-soil-partnership) · interactive: For the parent: facts, pictures and short films about soil from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations.
- [Visvesvaraya Industrial and Technological Museum](https://www.vismuseum.gov.in) · place · Bengaluru: A science museum with galleries on the Earth and its materials.

## Things to buy
- [A hand magnifying glass](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=magnifying+glass+for+kids) (tool): Helps a child see the grains, roots and tiny creatures in soil. Sold in stationery shops. The steps work without one. Used in a1. Used again in units on insects, plants and crystals.
- [A small pack of composting earthworms, from a nursery or a vermicompost seller](https://www.amazon.in/s?k=earthworms+for+vermicompost) (material): Only for the Worm Window activity, if no earthworms can be found after rain. Many city nurseries sell them with vermicompost. Afterwards they can live in a compost bin. Used in a2. Can start a family compost bin, which the Compost unit uses.

## Warm-up
- What are the three sizes of rock grains in soil? → Sand, silt and clay.
- Which grains are the smallest? → Clay.
- What is humus made from? → Dead plants and animals that have decomposed.
- How does an earthworm breathe? → Through their damp skin.
- What are worm castings? → Soil that comes out of a worm after they eat. It is rich in nutrients.
- Which crop gives black soil its other name? → Cotton.
- Why is red soil red? → It holds iron, like rust.
- What is soil erosion? → Soil being washed or blown away.
- Who began the science of soils by studying Russia's black earth? → Vasily Dokuchaev.
