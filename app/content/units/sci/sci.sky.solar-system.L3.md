# The solar system: eight planets, one Sun, and Earth is one of them
`sci.sky.solar-system.L3`

**Summary.** The Earth is a planet. It is one of eight planets that go round the Sun. In order from the Sun, they are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The first four are small and rocky. The last four are giants, made mostly of gas and ice. All eight are lit and warmed by the same Sun. Its light spreads out as it travels, so the further a planet is, the weaker its sunlight. Neptune gets about 900 times less sunlight than the Earth. The Earth is at a distance where water can stay liquid, and that is part of why life could begin here.

**Intro.** *Stand in a sunny patch by a window and hold out your hand. Feel the warmth. That warmth came from the Sun. The same Sun is shining on seven other planets right now. Do they all feel the same warmth as your hand?* In this unit you find out. You meet the eight planets and learn that the Earth is one of them. With a phone torch and a sheet of squared paper, you measure how light spreads out as it travels. That test shows why Mercury is scorching and Neptune is freezing and dark. You also hear how Galileo looked at Venus through a telescope and found that not everything goes round the Earth.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the eight planets in order from the Sun, and say that the Earth is the third planet.
1. I can say what makes something a planet, and why Pluto is now called a dwarf planet.
2. I can run a fair test that shows how light spreads out with distance, and use it to explain why far planets are cold and dim.
3. I can explain why the planets keep going round the Sun, and why a planet further out takes longer to go round once.
4. I can tell how Galileo's view of Venus in 1610 showed that not everything goes round the Earth.
5. I can sort the planets into rocky planets and giant planets, and say how the two kinds differ.

## Terms
- **Solar system**: The Sun and everything that goes round it: eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets. *e.g.* The Earth and the Moon are both part of the solar system.
- **Planet**: A big, round world that goes round a star and has cleared its path of other big things. A planet does not make its own light. It shines only because sunlight falls on it. *e.g.* The Earth, Mars and Jupiter are planets.
- **Star**: A huge ball of very hot gas that makes its own light and heat. The Sun is a star. *e.g.* The Sun is the nearest star to the Earth.
- **Orbit**: The path one thing takes as it goes round another in space. An orbit is also the word for going round. *e.g.* The Earth orbits the Sun once a year.
- **Gravity**: The force that pulls things towards each other. Big things like the Sun pull very strongly. *e.g.* Gravity makes a dropped spoon fall to the floor. The Sun's gravity holds the Earth in its orbit.
- **Year**: The time a planet takes to go round the Sun once. *e.g.* A year on the Earth is about 365 days. A year on Mars is 687 Earth days.
- **Rocky planet**: A planet with a hard surface of rock and metal that you could stand on. The four planets nearest the Sun are rocky. *e.g.* Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars are rocky planets.
- **Giant planet**: A very big planet made mostly of gas, or of gas and ice, with no hard surface to stand on. The four planets furthest from the Sun are giants. *e.g.* Jupiter is a giant planet. More than 1,000 Earths could fit inside it.
- **Dwarf planet**: A round world that goes round the Sun but shares its path with many other icy or rocky things. It has not cleared its path. *e.g.* Pluto has been called a dwarf planet since 2006.
- **Asteroid**: A lump of rock or metal that goes round the Sun. Asteroids are much smaller than planets, and most are not round. *e.g.* Most asteroids go round the Sun in a wide ring between Mars and Jupiter, called the asteroid belt.
- **Atmosphere**: The layer of gas, or air, that wraps round a planet. *e.g.* The Earth's atmosphere is the air we breathe. Venus has a very thick atmosphere that traps heat.
- **Telescope**: A tube with lenses or mirrors that makes faraway things look bigger and clearer. *e.g.* Galileo used a telescope in 1610 to look at Venus and Jupiter.
- **Phases**: The different shapes a lit ball seems to have, depending on how much of its lit half we can see. *e.g.* The Moon shows phases from crescent to full. Venus shows phases too, seen through a telescope.
- **Spacecraft**: A machine built to travel in space. A spacecraft with no people on board is often called a probe. *e.g.* India's Mangalyaan spacecraft went into orbit round Mars in 2014.

## Explanations
### The Earth is a planet · *defined*
**Definition:** A planet is a big, round world that goes round the Sun and has cleared its path of other big things. The Earth does all three, so the Earth is a planet.

From the ground, the Earth feels flat and still, and the planets look like bright dots. So for a long time, most people thought the Earth was a different kind of thing from the planets. It is not. The Earth goes round the Sun, just as Mars and Jupiter do. Like them, it shines only with sunlight that bounces off it. Seen from Mars, the Earth looks like a bright blue-white dot in the sky. The Earth is the third planet from the Sun. It is the biggest of the four rocky planets.
- e.g. Photos taken by spacecraft near Mars show the Earth as a small bright dot.
- e.g. In the evening sky, Venus looks like a very bright star. Someone on Venus would see the Earth as a bright dot too.
- e.g. The Earth takes about 365 days to go round the Sun once. That is one year.
### The eight planets in order · *defined*
**Definition:** In order from the Sun, the eight planets are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.

One way to remember the order is a sentence where each word starts with the planet's first letter: 'My Very Easy Method Just Speeds Up Naming.' The four inner planets are close together, near the Sun. Then there is a wide gap with the asteroid belt in it. The four outer planets are very far apart. Neptune is about 30 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is. If the Earth were 10 centimetres from the Sun, Neptune would be 3 metres away.
- e.g. Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun.
- e.g. Jupiter is the biggest planet. Saturn has the brightest rings.
- e.g. Uranus spins tipped over on its side, so it seems to roll round the Sun.
### Rocky planets and giant planets · *defined*
**Definition:** The four planets nearest the Sun are small and made of rock and metal. The four furthest planets are giants made mostly of gas, or of gas and ice.

Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars have hard ground you could stand on. Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen and helium gas, the same gases the Sun is made of. Uranus and Neptune have a lot of water, ammonia and methane as well, mostly as hot, thick fluids deep inside, under thick air. None of the four giants has a hard surface to land on. Going down into one, the gas just gets thicker and hotter. All four giants have rings, though only Saturn's are bright enough to see easily with a small telescope.
- e.g. Mars is red because its rocks and dust contain rusty iron.
- e.g. Jupiter has a giant storm, the Great Red Spot, that is wider than the whole Earth.
- e.g. Saturn is so light for its size that, on average, it is less heavy than the same amount of water.
### Light spreads out as it travels · *defined*
**Definition:** Light from a small source spreads out in all directions. Twice as far away, the same light covers four times the area, so each part of it gets a quarter as much light.

In the torch test, the light through a small square hole made a square patch on the paper. When the paper was twice as far away, the patch was twice as wide and twice as tall. So it covered four times as many squares, and it looked dimmer. The Sun's light spreads out in the same way. Jupiter is about 5 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is. 5 times 5 is 25, so sunlight at Jupiter is about 25 times weaker than here. Neptune is about 30 times as far. 30 times 30 is 900, so Neptune gets about 900 times less sunlight. On Neptune at noon, the Sun would look like a dazzling tiny star.
- e.g. Mercury gets about 7 times as much sunlight as the Earth.
- e.g. Mars gets a little less than half as much sunlight as the Earth.
- e.g. A spacecraft going to Jupiter needs much bigger solar panels than one near the Earth.
### Why the Earth has liquid water · *defined*
**Definition:** The Earth is at a distance from the Sun where water can stay liquid on its surface. Its air keeps it a little warmer, too.

Close to the Sun, sunlight is so strong that water boils away. Far from the Sun, sunlight is so weak that water freezes solid. The Earth is in between, where water can be liquid. Distance is not the whole story, though. A planet's air matters too. Venus is further from the Sun than Mercury, but Venus is the hottest planet. Its very thick air traps the heat, so its ground is about 460 degrees Celsius, hot enough to melt lead. The Earth's thinner air keeps us warm enough without cooking us. Every living thing we know of lives on the Earth.
- e.g. Mars once had rivers and lakes. Today its water is frozen at its poles and under its ground.
- e.g. Mercury has almost no air, so its night side gets colder than the coldest place on Earth.
- e.g. Some moons of Jupiter and Saturn, like Europa and Enceladus, probably have oceans of liquid water under thick ice, kept liquid by their own inner heat.
### Why the planets keep going round the Sun · *defined*
**Definition:** Each planet is moving forwards very fast. The Sun's gravity pulls it inwards all the time. Together, these bend the planet's path into an orbit round the Sun.

Something that is moving keeps going in a straight line unless a force changes its path. In the marble test, the rim of the plate pushed the marble inwards and kept it going round. At the gap, nothing pushed any more, and the marble rolled off in a straight line. The Sun's gravity does the job of the rim for the planets, though it pulls without touching. If the Sun's gravity suddenly stopped, every planet would fly off in a straight line. The Earth moves at about 30 kilometres every second as it goes round the Sun.
- e.g. Swing a sock tied to a string in a circle. Let go, and it flies off in a straight line.
- e.g. The Moon stays in orbit round the Earth because of the Earth's gravity.
- e.g. Spacecraft use the same idea. Rockets give them enough speed, and gravity bends their path.
### Planets further out take longer to go round · *defined*
**Definition:** A planet's year is the time it takes to go round the Sun once. The further a planet is from the Sun, the longer its year.

There are two reasons. A planet further out has a much longer path to travel. It also moves more slowly, because the Sun's gravity is weaker out there. Mercury, the closest planet, goes round in just 88 days. Neptune, the furthest, takes about 165 Earth years. Neptune was discovered in 1846, and it finished its first full orbit since then only in 2011. In 1619 Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer, found an exact rule linking a planet's distance to the length of its year.
- e.g. A year on Mars is 687 Earth days, almost two of our years.
- e.g. Jupiter takes about 12 Earth years to go round the Sun once.
- e.g. A child who is 8 on the Earth has not yet lived one Saturn year, which is about 29 and a half Earth years.
### How the solar system was born · *idea*
Most scientists think the solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a huge, slowly spinning cloud of gas and dust. Gravity pulled most of the cloud into the middle, where it became the Sun. The rest flattened into a spinning disc round the young Sun. In the disc, dust and ice stuck together into bigger and bigger lumps, and some lumps grew into planets. Near the Sun, it was too warm for ice to stay frozen, so only rock and metal could clump. That is why the inner planets are rocky. Further out, ice could freeze too, so planets grew bigger and pulled in lots of gas. This idea explains a lot. All the planets go round the Sun the same way, in nearly the same flat plane. Telescopes have also photographed discs like this round young stars. But many details are still being worked out, such as how fast Jupiter grew.
- e.g. The oldest rocks found in meteorites are about 4.6 billion years old.
- e.g. In 2014, a telescope in Chile photographed a young star called HL Tauri with a dusty disc and dark rings in it.
- e.g. All eight planets orbit the Sun in the same direction.
### Is Pluto a planet? · *debated*
Pluto was found in 1930 by Clyde Tombaugh, a young American astronomer, and was called the ninth planet for 76 years. Then astronomers found more and more icy worlds out near Pluto. In 2005 they found Eris, which is about as big as Pluto. Were all of these planets? In 2006 the International Astronomical Union, the world group of astronomers, voted on a definition. A planet must go round the Sun, be round, and have cleared its path of other big things. Pluto shares its path with many icy worlds, so it was called a dwarf planet. Not everyone agrees.
- e.g. Pluto is smaller than our Moon.
- e.g. The New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto in 2015 and found mountains of ice and a heart-shaped plain.
- e.g. Ceres, the biggest object in the asteroid belt, is also called a dwarf planet.
- **The International Astronomical Union, in 2006:** A planet must have cleared its path. Pluto has not, so it is a dwarf planet. This keeps the list of planets to eight clear, different kinds of world.
- **Alan Stern, the scientist who led the New Horizons mission, and others who study planets' surfaces:** A planet should be any world big enough for its own gravity to pull it round. By that rule, Pluto is a planet, and so are Eris and some big moons. Where a world goes round should not decide what it is.
### Is there a hidden ninth planet? · *debated*
Far beyond Neptune, a few small icy worlds have long, stretched orbits that seem to point roughly the same way. In 2016 two astronomers, Konstantin Batygin and Mike Brown, said this could be caused by the gravity of an unseen planet, perhaps several times as heavy as the Earth. Nobody has found it yet. Other astronomers think the pattern is only chance. We have spotted just a few of these far worlds, mostly in parts of the sky that are easier to search.
- e.g. Neptune itself was found in 1846 in a similar way. Urbain Le Verrier, a French mathematician, worked out where it must be from the way it tugged on Uranus.
- e.g. A very far, dim planet would reflect very little sunlight, which is why it would be so hard to see.
- e.g. A new telescope in Chile, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, is searching the sky and may settle the question.
- **Konstantin Batygin and Mike Brown, in 2016:** The far icy worlds line up too neatly to be chance. A big unseen planet is pulling them into line.
- **Many other astronomers:** We have found only a handful of these worlds, so the pattern may come from where we happened to look. More searching is needed before anyone says there is a planet.

## Mostly agreed
- The Earth and seven other planets go round the Sun. *How we know:* Planet positions worked out this way match what telescopes see, and spacecraft are flown to other planets using these orbits.
- The Sun holds about 998 parts out of every 1,000 of all the matter in the solar system. *How we know:* Astronomers weigh the Sun by how fast the planets go round it. Its gravity is far stronger than all the planets put together.
- Sunlight gets weaker with distance: twice as far, a quarter as strong. *How we know:* Spacecraft measure the sunlight falling on their panels as they travel out, and it drops exactly this way. The torch test shows the same at home.
- The Earth is the only place where life is known to exist. *How we know:* Spacecraft have searched Mars, Venus and the Moon closely. They have found no sign of life yet, though the search goes on.
- The Sun and the planets formed together about 4.6 billion years ago. *How we know:* The oldest meteorites, rocks that fell from space, are about this old when dated by their natural radioactivity.

## How we found out
**Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, building on the work of Nicolaus Copernicus, 1610 (Galileo sees the phases of Venus).** For about 1,500 years, most astronomers in Europe and the Middle East followed Ptolemy, who lived in Egypt about 150 CE. He taught that the Earth stands still in the centre, and the Sun and planets go round it. In his plan, Venus always stays between the Earth and the Sun. If that were true, we would only ever see Venus as a thin crescent or a dark disc, never fully lit. In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus said the planets, the Earth among them, go round the Sun, but nobody could yet show who was right. In 1610 Galileo Galilei, an Italian scientist, turned his new telescope on Venus. Over many months he watched her change. When Venus was on the far side of the Sun, she looked small and almost round, fully lit. As she came closer, she grew bigger and thinner, into a large crescent. Venus could only be fully lit if she went round to the far side of the Sun. So Venus goes round the Sun, not the Earth, and Ptolemy's plan was wrong. Later measurements showed that the Earth goes round the Sun too.  
*What people believed before:* The Earth is the still centre, and everything in the sky goes round it, with Venus always between the Earth and the Sun. Galileo saw Venus fully lit and small, which can only happen if Venus goes round the Sun.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The Earth is the centre, and the Sun and planets go round it.** (Most people in many lands for thousands of years, and the Greek-Egyptian astronomer Ptolemy, about 150 CE; *rejected*)  
  For: The Sun, the Moon and the stars all seem to travel across our sky every day. We do not feel the Earth moving.  
  Against: Galileo saw Venus change from a small full disc to a big thin crescent, which only fits if Venus goes round the Sun. Later measurements showed the Earth moves too.
- **Pluto is a planet.** (Astronomers until 2006, and some planet scientists today; *debated*)  
  For: Pluto is round, has its own moons, mountains and air, and goes round the Sun.  
  Against: Pluto shares its path with many other icy worlds, and Eris is about as big. The 2006 rule says a planet must clear its path.
- **The planets closest to the Sun are always the hottest.** (A very common guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Sunlight is strongest close to the Sun, as the torch test shows.  
  Against: Venus is hotter than Mercury, though it is further from the Sun, because its thick air traps the heat.
- **The planets are spread out evenly.** (A common idea, because pictures in books often show them evenly spaced; *rejected*)  
  For: Drawings in books fit the planets into one page, at equal gaps.  
  Against: The four inner planets are packed close to the Sun. Neptune is about 30 times as far out as the Earth. Real spacing would not fit on a book page.
- **There is a ninth planet beyond Neptune that we have not seen.** (Konstantin Batygin and Mike Brown, 2016; *debated*)  
  For: Some far icy worlds have orbits that seem to line up, as if something big were pulling them.  
  Against: Nobody has seen it, and the pattern may come from where astronomers happened to look.

## Roots
We have met the eight planets and measured how light spreads out. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the torch test, twice as far meant the same light spread over four times as many squares. The Sun's light spreads the same way, out to all eight planets. Neptune is 30 times further from the Sun than Earth, so sunlight there is about 900 times weaker. Earth's warmth, rain and plant food all run on the share of sunlight that reaches Earth's distance.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk about 'the Earth' and 'the planets' as if the Earth were a different kind of thing, and as if its warmth were its own. But the Earth stands in the same sunlight as the other seven planets. What makes it warm, wet and living is mostly the share of that light its distance gives it.  
*Evidence:* At 20 cm the torch light covered 16 squares and was bright. At 40 cm the same light covered 64 squares and was dim. Spacecraft measure sunlight weakening in exactly this way as they fly out from the Sun.

- *who am i:* You live on a planet. The warmth on your skin and the food on your plate began as sunlight that travelled to the Earth.
- *what is life:* Every living thing we know of lives on the Earth, at a distance from the Sun where water stays liquid.
- *what is this world:* The Earth is one of eight planets sharing one Sun. Each gets a different share of its light.

One Sun sends out its light in every direction. The same light reaches all eight planets, but at each distance it is spread thinner. That one difference, along with what each planet is made of and how much air it has, gives eight very different worlds: scorched Mercury, cloud-wrapped Venus, blue Earth, red Mars and the cold, dim giants. On the Earth, the share of sunlight we get drives the wind and rain and feeds every plant. So one source of light becomes many worlds and, on one of them, countless living things.

- **Rig Veda 1.115.1:** The Sun is the self of all that moves and all that stands still. (Almost everything that moves on the Earth, wind, rivers, plants and animals, runs on the Sun's light. The unit shows how that light reaches each planet.)
- **Nicolaus Copernicus, De revolutionibus, 1543, Book 1, chapter 10:** In the middle of all sits the Sun. From there it can light up the whole at once. (Copernicus put the Sun at the centre because it lights every planet. The torch test shows how that light thins out with distance.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding, 1988:** Look deeply into a sheet of paper and you will see sunshine in it. Without sunshine, the forest cannot grow, and without the forest there is no paper. (The paper you drew your planets on was a tree, and the tree grew on the share of sunlight that reaches the Earth.)

- Science (settled): The Sun and the planets formed together from one spinning cloud of gas and dust, about 4.6 billion years ago.
- Science (open): Whether life exists anywhere else in the solar system is not yet known.
- Other view, Many astronomers: The Earth's distance from the Sun is not special. There are billions of planets, and some will be at a good distance by chance. We find ourselves on one of those only because we could not live anywhere else.

## Story: One Lamp for Eight Worlds
*Lakshmi, Arjun, their grandmother and this evening are made up. The facts in the story are real: the order of the planets, how sunlight weakens with distance, Venus's thick hot air, and Venus as the bright 'evening star'.*

The power went off at seven o'clock, just as Lakshmi opened her library book. The fan slowed and stopped. The whole flat went dark, and so did every window in the building across the road in Thiruvananthapuram.

'Nobody move,' said Ammamma. Lakshmi heard her grandmother feel her way to the pooja shelf. A match scratched. A small flame grew on the wick of the brass oil lamp, and Ammamma set it on the floor in the middle of the hall.

Lakshmi was eight. Her little brother Arjun was six, and he did not like the dark. He crawled right up to the lamp and sat with his knees almost touching it.

'Not so close,' said Ammamma. 'You'll get too hot. Move back a bit.'

Arjun shuffled back one hand's width. 'It's still hot on my face,' he said. 'But I can see everything. I can see the lines on my hand.'

Lakshmi took her book to the far corner, by the balcony door. She could hardly see the page. The lamp looked like a tiny bright star from there. 'It's too dark here to read,' she said. 'And it's cold. The breeze is coming in.'

Ammamma laughed. She had taught physics at a girls' school for thirty years, and she could never let a good question go by. 'Well,' she said, 'here we have a little solar system. The lamp is the Sun. Arjun is Mercury, the planet nearest the Sun. He is roasting. And you, Lakshmi, by the door, are far-off Neptune. You are cold and in the dark.'

'But it's the same lamp,' said Arjun. 'Why is it hot for me and dark for her?'

'Good question,' said Ammamma. 'What do you think?'

Arjun frowned. 'Because she's far away?'

'Yes. But why should being far away make the light weaker? The lamp is the same lamp.'

Nobody answered. Ammamma took an old envelope and tore a small square hole in it. She held it upright near the lamp. Lakshmi held a page from her maths notebook, the one with little squares, behind it. 'Look at the light that comes through the hole. Close to the hole, it makes a small, bright patch. Now, Lakshmi, move the paper back slowly.'

The patch of light grew wider and wider. It also grew paler.

'It's the same light,' said Lakshmi slowly. 'But it's spreading out over more squares. So each square gets less.'

'Exactly,' said Ammamma. 'Light from the lamp goes out in every direction. The further it goes, the bigger the space it has to cover, so it gets thinner. The Sun's light does the same. Neptune is about thirty times as far from the Sun as we are. By the time sunlight gets there, it is spread out so thin that it is about nine hundred times weaker than here. Neptune is colder than any freezer.'

'Then which planet are we?' asked Arjun.

'You choose,' said Ammamma. 'Where would you sit to read your book without getting too hot?'

Arjun looked at Lakshmi. Lakshmi looked at Arjun. They both moved, and they met in the middle of the hall, about two arm's lengths from the lamp. It was warm, but not too hot. The page was bright enough to read.

'There,' said Ammamma. 'You are the Earth. Not too close, not too far. On the Earth, water does not boil away and it does not freeze solid. It runs in rivers and falls as rain. That is part of why there are fish and trees and children here.'

'Can I be Venus?' asked Arjun. 'Venus is next to us.'

Ammamma pulled the thick bedsheet off the sofa and wrapped it round Arjun, right up to his chin. 'Venus is a little closer to the Sun than we are,' she said, 'and Venus has very thick air all round her. The air traps the heat like this sheet.'

'I'm boiling!' said Arjun, and threw the sheet off.

'So is Venus,' said Ammamma. 'Her ground is hot enough to melt lead. She is even hotter than Mercury, though Mercury is closer to the Sun.'

Just then the fan whirred and the lights came back on. Lakshmi ran to the balcony. The Sun had set an hour ago, and the sky over the coconut trees was deep blue. Low in the west hung one dot, brighter than anything else in the sky.

'Ammamma, look! That bright star!'

'That's not a star,' said Ammamma, coming out with the lamp still in her hand. 'That's Venus. People call her the evening star, but she makes no light of her own. You are seeing sunlight bouncing off her clouds.'

Lakshmi stared at the bright dot for a long time. 'Ammamma,' she said at last, 'if someone stood on Venus and looked this way, what would they see?'

Ammamma smiled. 'Another bright dot. A blue one. Us.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: the lamp lit everyone in the room, but its light spread out over a bigger and bigger space as it went further, so each part got less. The Sun's light does the same, which is why Mercury is scorching and Neptune is dark and frozen. The Earth is at a distance where water stays liquid. Distance is not the only thing that matters: Venus's thick air traps heat, so Venus is the hottest planet. And the Earth is not apart from the planets. Seen from Venus, the Earth is one more bright dot.

- Ask: Why was Arjun hot and Lakshmi cold, when they were lit by the same lamp?
- Ask: What happened to the patch of light when Ammamma moved the paper further from the lamp?
- Ask: Why did Ammamma wrap Arjun in a bedsheet to show what Venus is like?
- Ask: Why does Venus shine so brightly if she makes no light of her own?
- Ask: If someone on Venus looked at the Earth, what would they see?

## Experiments
### e1 · Light Through a Window: how does light spread out with distance? (30 min)
- **question:** When light travels twice as far, how much bigger is the patch it covers?
- **predict:** Guess first. When the squared paper is twice as far from the torch, will the patch of light cover the same number of squares, twice as many, or four times as many? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same phone torch, the same hole and the same card every time. Keep the torch and the card still. Change only one thing: how far the squared paper is from the torch. Count the squares at each distance twice, because it is easy to miss one.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the hole in the card. Do not look straight into the phone torch from close up.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the three lit squares on squared paper, side by side, at their real sizes.
- **results table:** Paper's distance from the torch (cm) | Width of the lit square (cm) | Number of squares lit | How bright (3 bright, 2 medium, 1 dim)
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut a square hole 2 cm by 2 cm in the middle of the card. 2. Close the curtains and switch off the lights.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to see what happens to light as it travels further. First I'll cut a small window in this card.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts a small square hole in the card. Then make the room dark.
  - Note: A dark room makes the patch much easier to see. Evening is the easiest time. A square hole gives a square patch, which is easy to count.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the phone upright against a stack of books, with its torch facing along the table. 2. Switch the torch on. 3. Push the bottom edge of the card into a lump of dough, so it stands up. 4. Stand it 10 cm in front of the torch, with the hole level with the light.  
  *Parent says:* The torch is our Sun. The card lets through just a little square of its light. Let's keep both of them still from now on.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the phone up with its torch on. Stand the card 10 cm in front of it. Line up the hole with the light.
  - Note: Measure from the torch's light, on the back of the phone, to the card. The torch should shine straight through the middle of the hole.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess before you measure anything.  
  *Parent says:* Soon we'll catch the light on squared paper, first 20 cm from the torch and then 40 cm. 40 is twice as far as 20. At 40 cm, will the light cover the same number of squares, twice as many, or four times as many? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. At twice as far, will the light cover the same squares? Twice as many? Four times as many? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Light spreads out, so it will cover more.; Twice as far, so twice as big.)
  - Note: Most children and many adults guess twice as many. Don't correct the guess. Let the counting answer it.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the squared paper to a big book. 2. Stand the book 20 cm from the torch, facing it, so the light through the hole falls on the paper. 3. Measure how wide the lit square is. 4. Count the squares that are more than half lit. 5. Score the brightness 3, for bright.  
  *Parent says:* Here's the patch of light at 20 cm. How wide is it? How many squares does it cover? Let's count them together.  
  *Child mode:* Put the paper 20 cm from the torch. Measure the lit square. Count the lit squares. Write the numbers.
  - Note: The patch should be about 4 cm wide and cover about 16 squares. The edges are a little fuzzy, so count a square if more than half of it is lit. Your numbers may be a little different. That's fine.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Move the book back to 30 cm, then to 40 cm. 2. At each distance, measure the width of the lit square and count the squares. 3. Score how bright the patch looks: 2 for medium, 1 for dim.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's move the paper back. Look at the patch. Is it bigger? Is it brighter or dimmer?  
  *Child mode:* Move the paper to 30 cm. Measure and count. Then 40 cm. Measure and count. Is it dimmer?
  - Note: At 30 cm expect about 6 cm across and about 36 squares. At 40 cm, about 8 cm across and about 64 squares. If your counts are a bit off, look at the widths. They should go 4, 6, 8, growing in step with the distance.
  - Check: At 20 cm the light covered 16 squares. At 40 cm, twice as far, it covered about 64 squares. How many times as many squares is that? → **4 times as many**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what this means for each square.  
  *Parent says:* The same light came through the hole every time. At 40 cm, it was shared out over 4 times as many squares. So how much light did each square get?  
  *Child mode:* The same light was shared out over 4 times as many squares. Did each square get more light or less?
  - Ask: Why did the far patch look dimmer? (good: The same light was spread over more squares.; Each square got only a quarter of the light.)
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child how this fits the planets.  
  *Parent says:* The Sun's light spreads out in just the same way. Jupiter is about 5 times as far from the Sun as the Earth is. 5 times 5 is 25. So sunlight there is about 25 times weaker. Neptune is about 30 times as far. 30 times 30 is 900. So Neptune gets about 900 times less sunlight than us.  
  *Child mode:* Sunlight spreads out like the torch light. Neptune is 30 times as far as Earth. So Neptune gets 900 times less sunlight. It is very cold and dim there.
  - Check: Mars is about one and a half times as far from the Sun as the Earth. What is sunlight like on Mars? → **Weaker than on the Earth, a bit less than half as strong**
- **s8 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happens?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: light gets weaker the further it travels. What I saw: at 20 cm the light covered 16 squares and was bright. At 40 cm it covered 64 squares and was dim. Why: the same light spreads over a bigger area, so each square gets less. That is why far planets are cold and dark.
### e2 · The Marble and the Missing Rim: what keeps a planet going round? (20 min)
- **question:** When nothing pushes a rolling marble round any more, which way does it go?
- **predict:** Guess first. A marble rolls round inside a ring, and then reaches a gap. Will it keep curving round, roll straight out from the middle, or roll straight on the way it was going? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same marble and the same ring every time. Start it in the same place and roll it with about the same speed. Roll it 5 times, because one roll can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up does the cutting. Keep marbles away from children under 4, who could choke on them. Pick up the marble afterwards so nobody slips on it.
- **record:** Draw the ring from above, and draw the path the marble took after the gap. Write which guess was right.
- **results table:** Roll number | Which way did it go? (kept curving, straight out from the middle, straight on)
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut away about a quarter of the paper plate's rim, so the ring has a gap. 2. Lay the plate face up in the middle of the newspaper.  
  *Parent says:* This plate's rim will push a marble round in a circle. Then I'll cut a gap where the rim stops pushing.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts a gap in the rim of the plate. Put the plate on the newspaper.
  - Note: No paper plate? Bend a strip of thin card, 3 cm high, into three quarters of a circle about 25 cm across. Tape it down onto the newspaper.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child where the marble will leave the ring. 2. Ask for a guess, and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* The marble will roll round inside the rim. When it reaches the gap, which way will it go? Will it keep curving round, roll straight out from the middle, or roll straight on? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. At the gap, will the marble keep curving? Go straight out from the middle? Or go straight on? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: It was going round, so it keeps going round.; Nothing is holding it any more.)
  - Note: Many children, and many adults, guess that it keeps curving. That is a very natural idea. Let the marble answer.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the marble against the inside of the rim, far from the gap. 2. Flick it along the rim so it rolls fast all the way to the gap. 3. Watch where it goes. 4. Draw a pencil line on the newspaper along its path. 5. Do it 5 times.  
  *Parent says:* Let's roll it and watch closely. I'll draw its path each time.  
  *Child mode:* Flick the marble round the inside of the rim. Watch where it goes at the gap. Draw its path. Try 5 times.
  - Note: If the marble jumps over the rim, flick it a little more gently. It should roll off in a straight line along the way it was moving at the gap. It does not keep curving, and it does not roll straight out from the middle.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the 5 pencil lines together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at our lines. Did the marble keep curving, or go straight? So while it was going round, what was making it curve?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your lines. Did the marble curve or go straight? What made it curve before?
  - Check: While the marble went round, the rim pushed it inwards. What does the Sun do for the planets, like the rim did for the marble? → **The Sun's gravity pulls each planet inwards, so its path keeps curving**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a moving thing goes straight unless something turns it. What I saw: all 5 times, the marble rolled straight on at the gap. Why: the rim had been pushing it inwards. The Sun's gravity does that for the planets.

### c1 · Sunlight for a Spacecraft Going to Jupiter (25 min)
- **problem:** A spacecraft runs on sunlight. Near the Earth, a solar panel 1 square big gives it all the power it needs. Design solar panels that give the same power at Mars, at Jupiter and at Saturn.
- **your turn:** Now design your own folding panel for Saturn. Saturn is about 10 times as far as the Earth. How many squares do you need? How will it fold up to fit in the rocket?
- **constraints:** Use squared paper: 1 square is the panel the spacecraft needs near the Earth.; Use the torch-test rule: times the distance by itself to find how much weaker the sunlight is.; Your panels must fold up to fit inside a rocket, which is about 5 squares wide.
- **general:** Make the panels bigger, in step with how weak the sunlight is. Jupiter is about 5 times as far, so the panel must be about 25 squares, 5 by 5.; Fold the panels like a fan or a map, so they open out in space.; Some spacecraft going very far, like Voyager 1 and 2, do not use sunlight at all. They carry a nuclear power source instead.
- **creative:** Use curved mirrors to gather extra sunlight onto a smaller panel.; Make the panels out of long thin strips that unroll, like a mat.; Point the panels straight at the Sun, because light that hits a panel at a slant is spread over more of it.

  *Parent does:* 1. Remind your child of the torch test. 2. Ask how big a panel must be at Jupiter, 5 times as far as the Earth.  
  *Parent says:* Remember the torch test? Twice as far, four times the squares. Jupiter is 5 times as far. How many squares must the panel be?

- **s2 (12 min)**

  *Parent says:* Real spacecraft have this problem. Juno, which reached Jupiter in 2016, has three solar panels, each about 9 metres long.

  - Note: Mars is about one and a half times as far, and one and a half times one and a half is about 2. Saturn is about 9 and a half times as far, so about 90 squares.

   **Answer:** About 25 times weaker than on the Earth. *Why:* Light spreads out wider and taller at the same time. 5 times as far means 5 times 5, which is 25 times weaker. Picking '5 times weaker' is the usual first guess, but the torch test showed the patch grows both ways.

## Applications
- Planning spacecraft journeys, like India's Mangalyaan to Mars in 2014, uses the orbits of the planets.
- Solar panels on spacecraft are sized using how sunlight weakens with distance.
- Lighting engineers use the same rule: a lamp twice as high lights the floor a quarter as brightly.
- Calendars count the Earth's trips round the Sun as years.
- Searching for life beyond the Earth starts with places that may have liquid water.
- Weather satellites and space telescopes are placed in orbits worked out from gravity.

## Spotted around us
- Venus shining low in the west after sunset, or in the east before dawn, brighter than any star.
- Jupiter, a bright steady dot that shifts slowly against the stars over weeks.
- A street lamp: right under it, the road is bright. A few steps away, it is much dimmer.
- Planet names in Indian languages: Mangal for Mars, Budh for Mercury, Shukra for Venus, Guru or Brihaspati for Jupiter, Shani for Saturn.
- The days of the week in many Indian languages are named after the Sun, the Moon and five planets, like Mangalvar or Chevvai for Tuesday, after Mars.

## Open questions
- **Is there an unseen ninth planet far beyond Neptune?** Some far icy worlds have orbits that seem to line up, but nobody has found a planet, and the pattern may be chance.
- **Is there life anywhere else in the solar system?** Mars once had water, and moons like Europa and Enceladus have oceans under ice. No life has been found yet, and the search goes on.
- **How did Jupiter grow so big so fast?** Scientists think Jupiter formed early and quickly, but how its core gathered gas so fast is still argued about.
- **Why does Uranus spin tipped over on its side?** The best idea is a giant crash long ago, but nobody knows for sure.

## Questions to ask kids

## Connected maths
- In the torch test, the patch of light was 4 cm wide at 20 cm and 8 cm wide at 40 cm. How many 1 cm squares does each patch cover? How many times as many does the far one cover? **16 squares and 64 squares. The far one covers 4 times as many.**. A patch 4 cm wide and 4 cm tall covers 4 times 4, which is 16 squares. A patch 8 cm wide and 8 cm tall covers 8 times 8, which is 64 squares. 64 divided by 16 is 4. So the far patch covers 4 times as many squares. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L3`)
- Sunlight gets weaker by the distance times itself. Mars is about 1 and a half times as far from the Sun as the Earth. Jupiter is about 5 times as far. Neptune is about 30 times as far. Work out how many times weaker sunlight is at Jupiter and at Neptune. **About 25 times weaker at Jupiter, and about 900 times weaker at Neptune.**. 5 times 5 is 25. 30 times 30 is 900. Mars is harder: 1 and a half times 1 and a half is 2 and a quarter, so about 2 times weaker. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
- In the 3-metre solar system, the Earth is 10 cm from the Sun and Neptune is 300 cm. How many times as far from the Sun is Neptune? Jupiter is at 52 cm. About how many times as far as the Earth is that? **Neptune is 30 times as far. Jupiter is about 5 times as far.**. 300 divided by 10 is 30. 52 divided by 10 is a little over 5. So Jupiter is about 5 times as far as the Earth. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L4`)
- A year on Mercury is 88 days. A year on Venus is 225 days. Arjun, a boy who is 6, has lived about 2,190 days. About how many Mercury years is that? About how many Venus years? **About 25 Mercury years and about 10 Venus years.**. 2,190 divided by 88 is about 24.9, so about 25 Mercury years. 2,190 divided by 225 is about 9.7, so about 10 Venus years. A shorter year means more birthdays. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- The Earth is about 150 million kilometres from the Sun. Neptune is about 30 times as far. About how far from the Sun is Neptune? **About 4,500 million kilometres, which is 4.5 billion kilometres.**. 150 times 30 is 4,500. So Neptune is about 4,500 million kilometres from the Sun. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
