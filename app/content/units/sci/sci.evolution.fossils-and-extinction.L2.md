# Fossils: how rocks keep traces of dinosaurs and other old life
`sci.evolution.fossils-and-extinction.L2`

**Summary.** A shell can lie inside a rock on a hill, far from any sea. It is a fossil: a trace of a being who lived long ago, kept in rock. Fossils show that dinosaurs lived on Earth for a very long time, and that the big ones died out about 66 million years ago. After them, mammals and birds spread into new kinds, and much later humans came. Your child presses hard and soft things into dough to see which leave a clear print, and digs down through a pile of old newspapers to see why deeper means older.

**Intro.** *Nobody has ever seen a living dinosaur with a big tail and teeth. So how do we know they were real?* We know from fossils. A fossil is a bone, a tooth, a shell, a footprint or a leaf print from long ago, kept in rock. Fossils are found in India too: dinosaur eggs in Gujarat, sea shells around Ariyalur in Tamil Nadu, and a stone tree trunk at Sattanur. In this unit you find out which things leave a fossil, and how we tell which fossils are older.

## Terms
- **Fossil**: A fossil is a trace of a living being from long ago, kept in rock. It can be a bone, a tooth, a shell, a leaf print or a footprint. *e.g.* A shell turned to stone in a hill near Ariyalur is a fossil.
- **Extinct**: A kind of animal or plant is extinct when every last one of that kind has died. They never come back. *e.g.* Mammoths are extinct. The last ones died about 4,000 years ago.
- **Dinosaur**: Dinosaurs were a big family of reptiles who lived on land for about 165 million years. Some were huge, and some were as small as a hen. *e.g.* Rajasaurus was a meat-eating dinosaur whose bones were found in Gujarat.
- **Palaeontologist**: A palaeontologist is a scientist who studies fossils to find out about life long ago. Say it as pay-lee-on-tol-o-jist. *e.g.* A palaeontologist brushes dust off a dinosaur bone with a small paintbrush.
- **Layer**: A layer is one flat sheet lying on top of another. Rock often forms in layers, one on top of the next, over a very long time. *e.g.* The side of a hill can show stripes. Each stripe is a layer of rock.
- **Trace fossil**: A trace fossil is a mark a living being left behind, not their body. Footprints, nests, burrows and tooth marks are trace fossils. *e.g.* A line of dinosaur footprints in rock is a trace fossil.
- **Mammal**: A mammal is an animal with hair or fur who feeds their babies on milk. Cows, cats, elephants, whales and humans are mammals. *e.g.* A mother goat feeding her kid on milk is a mammal.
- **Asteroid**: An asteroid is a big rock that travels through space around the Sun. A very large one hit the Earth 66 million years ago. *e.g.* The asteroid that hit the Earth then was about 10 kilometres wide.

## Explanations
### What a fossil is · *defined*
**Definition:** A fossil is a trace of a living being from long ago, kept in rock.

When a living being dies, the body usually rots away or is eaten. Sometimes mud or sand covers it quickly. Over a very long time, the mud presses down and turns into rock. Water carries tiny bits of minerals into the bones or shell and turns them to stone, keeping their shape. Other fossils are only marks, like a footprint in mud that dried hard and was covered over. You made marks like these in your Print Test.
- e.g. A dinosaur bone that has turned to stone.
- e.g. The print of a fern leaf in a dark rock.
- e.g. A shell turned to stone in the limestone around Ariyalur, Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. An insect caught in amber, which is tree resin that went hard. Amber about 50 million years old has been found in Gujarat.
### Why hard parts make most fossils · *defined*
**Definition:** Hard parts of a body, like bones, teeth, shells and wood, become fossils far more often than soft parts.

Soft parts rot away quickly or get eaten, by animals and by tiny living beings called bacteria. Hard parts last longer, so mud has time to cover them. In your Print Test, the key and the coin left sharp prints, and the banana and the tomato left blurry dents. So most fossils are bones, teeth and shells. Worms and jellyfish have lived in the sea for hundreds of millions of years, but we have found very few of their fossils.
- e.g. A shark's skeleton is made of soft cartilage, but its teeth are hard. Fossil shark teeth are common, and whole fossil sharks are rare.
- e.g. An ammonite was a sea animal with a soft body and a coiled shell. We find the shells, and almost never the soft body.
- e.g. Very rarely, soft bodies are kept too, when they are buried fast in very fine mud. Feathers and skin have been found this way.
### Deeper layers are older · *defined*
**Definition:** When layers pile up one on top of another, the layer at the bottom was laid down first, so it is the oldest.

Mud and sand settle at the bottom of rivers, lakes and seas. Each new layer lies on top of the last one. Over millions of years the layers turn into rock. So the deeper you dig, the further back in time you go, and a deep fossil is usually older than one above it. Your newspaper pile worked the same way: the oldest paper was at the bottom. Sometimes the Earth folds or tips the layers, and then scientists look for other clues.
- e.g. A pile of old newspapers waiting for the raddi collector has the oldest paper at the bottom.
- e.g. The striped sides of a quarry show many layers, with the oldest at the bottom.
- e.g. The Grand Canyon in the United States shows layers of rock almost two kilometres deep.
### Dinosaurs · *defined*
**Definition:** Dinosaurs were a big family of reptiles who lived on land from about 230 million years ago.

There were many kinds of dinosaurs. Some ate plants, and some ate meat. Titanosaurs had very long necks and were among the biggest animals ever to walk on land. Some small dinosaurs had feathers, and all dinosaurs laid eggs. Their bones and eggs are found in Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu. The big dinosaurs died out about 66 million years ago. The first humans came only about 300,000 years ago, so people never met them.
- e.g. Rajasaurus, a meat-eater found in Gujarat. The name means king lizard.
- e.g. Dinosaur eggs at Raiyoli, near Balasinor in Gujarat, found by scientists in 1981.
- e.g. Sanajeh, a snake found coiled round a dinosaur egg in Gujarat. The fossil was found in 1984 and named in 2010.
### What made the big dinosaurs die out · *debated*
About 66 million years ago, the big dinosaurs, the ammonites and many other kinds of life died out. Scientists agree on that. They still argue about why. A huge asteroid hit the Earth in what is now Mexico. At about the same time, giant volcanoes in western India poured out lava for thousands of years. Their rock still covers much of Maharashtra. Both could have filled the air with dust and gases, made the world dark and cold, and killed plants. Without plants, the plant-eaters starved, and then the meat-eaters.
- e.g. In 1980, Luis Alvarez, Walter Alvarez, Frank Asaro and the chemist Helen Michel found a thin layer of a rare metal called iridium in rocks of that age. Asteroids have much more iridium than the Earth's crust.
- e.g. The Deccan Traps are the layers of old lava in western India. The Western Ghats near Mahabaleshwar are made of them.
- e.g. The asteroid left a crater about 180 kilometres wide, called Chicxulub, now buried under the ground in Mexico.
- **Most scientists:** The asteroid was the main cause. The iridium layer and the giant crater both date to the very time the dinosaurs vanish.
- **Gerta Keller, a scientist at Princeton University, and others:** The Deccan volcanoes in India were the main cause. They were already changing the air and the seas before the asteroid came.
- **Many other scientists:** Both mattered. The volcanoes made life harder, and the asteroid was the final blow.
### The animals that came after · *defined*
**Definition:** After the big dinosaurs died out, mammals and birds spread into many new kinds.

Mammals lived alongside the dinosaurs for millions of years. Most were small, about the size of a mouse or a rat. When the big dinosaurs were gone, there was room and food for others. Over millions of years, mammals became many new kinds: elephants, horses, bats, whales and, much later, humans. Some small dinosaurs lived on as birds, so the crow at your window is from the dinosaur family. Crocodiles, turtles, snakes, frogs and sharks also lived through the change.
- e.g. Indohyus was a small deer-like mammal whose fossils were found in Kashmir. Scientists think whales came from animals like them.
- e.g. Mammoths were cousins of today's elephants. They lived until about 4,000 years ago, long after the dinosaurs.
- e.g. Humans are mammals too. We feed babies on milk and have hair, like a cat or a cow.

## Mostly agreed
- Fossils are the remains and traces of real beings who lived long ago. *How we know:* Fossil bones have the same shapes as the bones of living animals, and fossil shells match living shells. We can see bone turning to stone, and footprints in rock are the same shape as footprints in mud.
- The big dinosaurs died out about 66 million years ago, and people came much later. *How we know:* Dinosaur fossils are found only in rock layers older than 66 million years. Human fossils are found only in layers less than about 300,000 years old. No human fossil has ever been found with a big dinosaur.
- Birds come from dinosaurs. *How we know:* Fossils from China show small dinosaurs with feathers. Birds and these dinosaurs share many bones, like a wishbone and hollow bones.

## How we found out
**Georges Cuvier, a French scientist in Paris (1796); later Mary Anning, a fossil hunter in Lyme Regis, England (early 1800s), Cuvier read his paper on living and fossil elephants in Paris in 1796. Mary Anning made her finds from about 1811 onwards..** Before about 1800, many people in Europe thought no kind of animal could ever die out. When huge bones were dug up, they said the animals must still be alive somewhere far away. Georges Cuvier was a young scientist at the natural history museum in Paris. In 1796, he compared the teeth and jaws of elephants from India and from Africa. He showed that they were two different kinds of elephant. Then he looked at mammoth bones dug up in Siberia, in the far north of Russia. Their teeth were different from both kinds of living elephant. A mammoth was a huge animal. Travellers could not have missed herds of them. So Cuvier said that the mammoth was a kind that had died out for ever. Later, fossil hunters like Mary Anning, a young woman from a poor family in England, found many more animals that no longer lived anywhere. Today we know the last mammoths lived on Wrangel Island, in the Arctic Ocean, until about 4,000 years ago.  
*What people believed before:* That no kind of animal could ever die out, so huge fossil bones must belong to animals still living somewhere unexplored. Cuvier showed the mammoth was a different kind from any living elephant, and far too big to stay hidden.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Dinosaurs were slow, cold, tail-dragging lizards.** (Many scientists, about 100 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: Dinosaurs are reptiles, and most lizards today are slow when they are cold.  
  Against: Most dinosaur trackways have no mark of a tail dragging behind. Many dinosaurs walked tall and fast, and some had feathers to keep warm.
- **The dinosaurs were already dying out slowly before the asteroid hit.** (Some scientists; *debated*)  
  For: In some places, fewer kinds of dinosaurs are found in the last few million years before the end.  
  Against: Other scientists say we have found fewer fossils from those years, not fewer dinosaurs. In other places the dinosaurs look healthy right up to the end.
- **A kind of animal can never die out completely.** (Many thinkers in Europe before about 1800; *rejected*)  
  For: They believed nature was complete and perfect, so no kind of being could be lost from it.  
  Against: Georges Cuvier showed in 1796 that mammoths were a different kind from any living elephant. No one had ever seen one alive. Many kinds are gone, like the dodo of Mauritius.

## Roots
We have pressed hard and soft things into dough, and dug down through layers. Now we ask what the rocks tell us about life, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** In your dough, the key and the spoon left sharp prints, but the banana left a blurry dent. Rocks keep hard parts much better than soft bodies. Most fossils are bones, teeth, shells and wood. So the story in the rocks leaves out most soft animals, like worms and jellyfish.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think the fossils show us everything that lived long ago, and that no fossil means no animal. The Print Test shows that rocks keep mostly hard parts, so many soft beings lived and left no trace at all.  
*Evidence:* In the Print Test, every hard thing left a clear print, and every soft thing squashed into a blurry mark. Worms and jellyfish have lived for hundreds of millions of years, yet their fossils are very rare.

- *who am i:* You are an animal and a mammal, in a family of life far older than the dinosaurs. Your thumb print in the dough is a trace of you.
- *what is life:* Life keeps changing shape. Kinds of beings appear, live for a long time, and some die out, while others carry on as new kinds.
- *what is this world:* The ground under your feet was once sea floor and forest. The rocks hold traces of beings who lived there long before us.

Every being in the rocks once lived, sensed the world and made choices, just as the crow at the window does today. When the big dinosaurs died out, small mammals and birds spread into the empty places. Over millions of years, they became many new kinds: elephants, whales, bats and, much later, us. One family of life keeps carrying on in new shapes, even when some of its kinds are lost.

- **Dhammapada 277:** The Buddha taught that everything that is made will change and pass away. Seeing this clearly helps us suffer less. (Kinds of animals pass too. Ammonites and the big dinosaurs lived for millions of years, and then they were gone.)
- **Udana 6.4, the blind men and the elephant:** Some men who cannot see each touch one part of an elephant. One feels the leg and says an elephant is like a pillar. One feels the ear and says it is like a winnowing basket. Each is sure he is right. (Scientists often find only a few bones of an animal. (Honest not-knowing is part of science too. Nobody knows for sure yet what ended the big dinosaurs.)

- Science (debated): What ended the big dinosaurs: a giant asteroid, the Deccan volcanoes of India, or both together.
- Other view, Some people in many faiths: Some people believe the Earth is only a few thousand years old, so fossils cannot be millions of years old. ## Story: The Shell on the Hill
*Kavya, Arjun, Meenakshi Athai and Selvi are made up. It is true that the limestone around Ariyalur in Tamil Nadu is full of fossil sea shells, including ammonites, and that the sea once covered that land. Ammonites died out at about the same time as the big dinosaurs.*

Kavya was seven. Every summer she and her little brother Arjun went to stay with their aunt in Ariyalur, a small town in Tamil Nadu. Their aunt was called Meenakshi, and they called her Athai. Athai was a geologist. A geologist is a scientist who studies rocks.

Around Ariyalur the ground is full of limestone, a pale rock. Big machines dig it out to make cement. One morning Athai took them to the edge of an old quarry, where the digging had stopped long ago. The sides of the quarry had stripes. There was a cream stripe, a grey stripe, and a yellow stripe on top.

"Stay with me, and stay away from the edge," said Athai. "Now look at the ground by your feet."

Kavya crouched down. Among the pebbles was a round stone, as big as her palm. On it was a curl, going round and round like a coil of rope.

"A snail!" said Arjun. "A stone snail!"

"It looks like a snail," said Athai. "But this is the shell of an ammonite. Ammonites were sea animals. They had a soft body inside a coiled shell. Scientists think they had arms, a little like an octopus. This shell turned to stone a very long time ago."

Kavya frowned. "A sea animal? But the sea is far away from here. We came in a bus for hours."

"The sea is far away now," said Athai. "Long, long ago, the sea came right up to here. Ammonites swam in it. When they died, their shells sank to the sea floor. Mud and tiny shells piled on top of them, layer after layer. Over millions of years, the mud pressed down and turned into this limestone."

Kavya turned the stone over in her hands. She could see every ridge of the shell. "Where is the soft body, Athai? Where are the arms?"

"That is a good question," said Athai. "The soft body rotted away, or a fish ate the soft parts. Only the hard shell stayed long enough to be covered by mud. That is why we find so many shells in this rock, and almost no soft bodies."

Arjun picked up another stone. "Did a dinosaur eat the ammonite?"

"Ammonites lived at the same time as the dinosaurs," said Athai. "Dinosaurs lived on the land near here, and some of their bones have been found in this part of Tamil Nadu. But they did not swim in the sea. Big sea reptiles and fish hunted the ammonites."

"Can we see a living ammonite?" asked Arjun.

Athai shook her head. "No one can. Ammonites are extinct. That means every last one has died. They died out at about the same time as the big dinosaurs, about 66 million years ago. We know them only from their shells."

On the way back, they stopped at a tea shop. The woman who ran it, Selvi Akka, had a row of stones on her shelf. One had a coiled shell. One had a flat shell like a fan. "My father found these when he dug the well," she said. "Everyone round here finds them."

That night, Kavya could not sleep. She thought about the sea that was gone, and the ammonites that were gone. She thought about the soft arms that had rotted away.

In the morning, she found Athai making chapati dough in the kitchen. "Athai, can I have a bit?" Kavya pressed the dough flat on a steel plate. She pressed a spoon into it, then a curry leaf from the pot on the window, then a piece of banana from her breakfast.

She lifted them off one by one. The spoon left a smooth, sharp hollow. The curry leaf left a print with tiny lines, like the veins. The banana left only a squashed blob.

"Arjun, look!" Kavya called. "The hard things leave a good print. The soft banana just squashes. That is why we find the shells and not the arms!"

Arjun looked at the dough. Then he looked at his own hand. "Will my bones be a fossil one day?"

Kavya thought about it. "Maybe," she said, "if you lie down in some mud and wait a million years."

Arjun said he would rather have breakfast. Athai laughed, and gave them each a dosa. Then she wrapped the ammonite in a cloth, so Kavya could take it home to Chennai.

Outside the window, a crow landed on the ledge and looked at them. "Athai," said Kavya, "are all the dinosaurs gone, like the ammonites?"

Athai smiled and pointed at the crow. "Not all of them," she said. "Some small dinosaurs had feathers. Over millions of years, their grandchildren's grandchildren became the birds. So the crow is a cousin of the dinosaurs."

**For the parent:** Kavya sees that the shell stayed and the soft body did not, because hard parts last long enough to be covered by mud. Her dough shows the same thing: hard things leave a clear print, and soft things squash. So the rocks keep mostly shells and bones.

- Ask: Why was there a sea shell in a rock so far from the sea?
- Ask: What happened to the ammonite's soft body?
- Ask: What did Kavya's spoon, curry leaf and banana show her?
- Ask: Athai said ammonites are extinct. What does extinct mean?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Print Test: which things leave a fossil print? (25 min)
- **question:** When we press hard things and soft things into dough in the same way, which ones leave a clear print?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which of the eight things will leave the clearest print? Which will leave the worst? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every thing is pressed into the same dough, rolled to the same thickness. Each one is covered with the same steel plate and pressed for a count of 10. Each is lifted straight up. Only the thing we press changes.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all eight things. Draw the clearest print and the blurriest print under it.
- **results table:** Thing pressed in | Hard or soft? | Print: clear, blurry or none
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the eight things. 2. Ask which will leave the clearest print, and why. 3. Write the guess at the top of the sheet.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to press each of these into dough. Which one will leave the clearest print? Which will leave the worst? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the eight things. Which will leave the best print? Why?
  - Ask: Which do you think will leave the clearest print? (good: The key, because it is hard.; The banana, because it is big.; The coin, because it is heavy.)
  - Note: Write down any guess and say nothing yet. Many children pick the biggest or heaviest thing. The test will show them what matters.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Roll the dough out on the counter until it is about as thick as your child's finger. 2. Cut or pull it into eight rounds, each about the size of your palm. 3. Lay them on a plate or a clean counter.  
  *Parent says:* Let us roll the dough, all the same thickness. Then we make eight rounds, one for each thing.  
  *Child mode:* Roll the dough flat. Make eight rounds, all the same size.
  - Note: If the dough sticks to things, dust the top with a little dry atta. Stiff dough gives the clearest prints, so use only a little water.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child squeezes each thing gently between finger and thumb. 2. Put the four hard things in one row and the four soft things in another.  
  *Parent says:* Squeeze each one gently. Does it squash, or does it stay the same shape? Put the hard ones here and the soft ones there.  
  *Child mode:* Squeeze each one gently. Hard ones in this row. Soft ones in that row.
  - Note: Let your child decide which row each thing goes in, and talk about it. A steel spoon is hard. Cotton wool feels soft.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one thing on each round. 2. Cover it with the steel plate. 3. Your child presses down with both hands and counts to 10. 4. Lift the plate, then lift the thing straight up.  
  *Parent says:* Put the plate on top. Press with both hands while we count to ten. Now lift it straight up, slowly.  
  *Child mode:* Put the plate on top. Press hard and count to ten. Lift it straight up.
  - Ask: What happened to the banana when you pressed it? (good: It squashed flat.; It went mushy and stuck to the dough.)
  - Note: Press each thing the same way, so the test is fair. Pick soft things off with a spoon if they stick.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at each print together. 2. For each one, write clear, blurry or none in the table. 3. Look at the key print closely for its small lines.  
  *Parent says:* Look at each print. Can you see the shape clearly? Can you see any tiny lines? Is it clear, blurry, or nothing at all?  
  *Child mode:* Look at each print. Is it clear, blurry or nothing? Fill in the table.
  - Check: Which row of prints was clearer? → **The hard things**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they think, what they saw, and why. 2. Then read the explanation card on why hard parts make most fossils.  
  *Parent says:* Was your guess right? What did you see? Why do you think the hard things left better prints?  
  *Child mode:* Was your guess right? Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Why do we find so many fossil bones and shells, and so few fossil worms? (good: Bones and shells are hard, so they leave a good print and last.; Worms are soft, so they squash and rot away.)
  - Note: Listen to the reason before you explain. A strong answer says that hard things keep their shape, while soft things squash and rot. Throw away the dough that touched the fruit and the cotton.
  - Think: Your bones are hard. Your skin is soft. Which part of you would the rocks keep?

## Activities
### a1 · The Newspaper Pile: dig down through time (15 min)
- **record:** A list of the dates from top to bottom, with a star next to each place you found a buried thing.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stack the newspapers with the oldest at the bottom. 2. Slip the leaf in near the bottom. 3. Put the ticket in the middle and the wrapper near the top, while your child is not looking. 4. Ask your child the question.  
  *Parent says:* This pile grew one paper a day. Which paper do you think is the oldest, the top one or the bottom one? Why?  
  *Child mode:* This pile grew one paper each day. Is the oldest one at the top or the bottom? Why?
  - Note: If your pile has been mixed up, sort the papers by date first, with the newest on top. The activity only works if the pile grew in order. Pretend that each paper came on a different day, and that the three small things fell into the pile on the days they were put in.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lifts off one paper at a time. 2. Read the date together, and your child writes it on the list. 3. When a buried thing turns up, your child draws a star next to the date of the paper just above it.  
  *Parent says:* Lift off the top paper. What date does it say? Write it down. Now the next one.  
  *Child mode:* Lift off one paper. Read the date. Write it down. Do the next one.
  - Ask: Are the dates getting older or newer as you dig down? (good: Older. Each paper down is from an earlier day.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the list and the stars together. 2. Ask which buried thing is the oldest.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the dates next to the stars. Which thing is under the oldest papers? That one fell into the pile first. How do you know?  
  *Child mode:* Which thing was buried first? How do you know?
  - Note: This is how scientists tell which fossils are older. Rock layers pile up like newspapers, with the oldest at the bottom.
  - Check: A scientist finds one fossil deep down and another near the top. Which is usually older? → **The deep one**
  - Think: Rock layers take thousands of years to pile up. Newspapers take one day each. What else in our home piles up in layers?
### a2 · Footprint Detective: what can footprints tell us? (15 min)
- **safety:** Walk, do not run. Tape the ends of the newspaper so it does not slide on a smooth floor. Dry your feet before stepping off the paper.
- **record:** Write how many hand spans apart the slow prints were, and how many the quick prints were.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the newspaper in two lines and tape the ends. 2. Put the plate of water and the towel at the start of the first line. 3. Ask the question.  
  *Parent says:* When you walk fast, will your footprints be closer together or farther apart than when you walk slowly? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Walk slow, then walk fast. Will the prints be closer or farther apart? Why?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child dips both feet in the water and walks slowly along the first line. 2. Then they dry their feet and stand at the start of the second line. 3. They dip again and walk with big quick steps, without running.  
  *Parent says:* Dip your feet and walk slowly along the paper. Now dry your feet. This time take big, quick steps, but do not run.  
  *Child mode:* Dip your feet. Walk slowly. Dry your feet. Now take big quick steps.
  - Note: The wet prints fade in a minute or two, so measure them straight away. Hold your child's hand if the paper feels slippery.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In each line, measure from the heel of one print to the heel of the next. 2. Use hand spans or a ruler. 3. Write both numbers down.  
  *Parent says:* How many hand spans between your slow steps? How many between your quick steps?  
  *Child mode:* Measure the gaps with your hand. Which gaps are bigger?
  - Check: Your quick steps were farther apart. A dinosaur's footprints in rock are very far apart. What does that tell us? → **The dinosaur was moving fast**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what else the footprints show.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the prints again. What else could a detective tell about you from them?  
  *Child mode:* What else do your footprints tell about you?
  - Ask: What else do your prints show? (good: How big my feet are.; That I have five toes.; That I walk on two legs.)
  - Note: Footprints are trace fossils. A trace fossil is a mark an animal left, not their body. Dinosaur trackways have been found in many places around the world.

## Challenges
### c1 · A Footprint That Lasts (20 min)
- **problem:** Make a print of your thumb or a toy animal's foot that still looks clear two days later, after you sprinkle five drops of water on it.
- **your turn:** Now design your own way to make a print last. Test it against one of the ideas above. Which one stayed clearer?
- **constraints:** Use only things you have at home.; It must be a print, pressed in, not a drawing.; Two days later you sprinkle five drops of water on it, and it must still be clear.
- **general:** Let the print dry hard on a sunny windowsill for two days before the water test.; Use salt dough, which goes harder than plain atta dough. Make it about half a finger thick so it dries faster.; Make the print deep, so a little water cannot smooth it out.
- **creative:** Let a salt dough print dry hard, then brush a thin coat of school glue over it. Glue dries hard and keeps water out.; Sprinkle a thin layer of dry atta over the dried print to cover it, the way new mud covers a real footprint. Brush it off gently before the water test.; Press the print into thick mud from a plant pot. Dry the mud in the sun. Mud dries hard, like the mud of a real footprint.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the problem aloud. 2. Your child picks two or three materials to try.  
  *Parent says:* Real footprints in mud lasted millions of years. Can you make a print that is still clear tomorrow, even after some water?  
  *Child mode:* Make a print that lasts until tomorrow. Even after drops of water.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child makes the same print in each material. 2. Put them all on a sunny windowsill to dry.  
  *Parent says:* Make the same print in each one, pressed in the same way. Then let them dry.  
  *Child mode:* Make the same print in each one. Let them dry.
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sprinkle five drops of water on each print. 2. Wait one minute and look. 3. Talk about which lasted best, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Five drops on each. Now look. Which print is still clear? Why do you think that one lasted?  
  *Child mode:* Put five drops on each. Which print is still clear? Why?
  - Check: Five drops of water fall on a print that dried hard and on a print left soft and wet. Which is more likely to stay clear? → **The print that dried hard**
  - Wait: Leave the prints for two days

## Worksheets
### Fossil Clues (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Which one of these is a fossil?  
   Options: The print of a shell in a rock / A plastic toy dinosaur / A new shell picked up on the beach today  
   **Answer:** The print of a shell in a rock. *Why:* A fossil is a trace of a being from long ago, kept in rock. A toy was never alive, and a new shell is not old or in rock.
2. (draw) Draw the clearest print and the blurriest print from your Print Test. Write under each one what made it.  
   **Answer:** Two drawings, a sharp print of a hard thing and a blurry print of a soft thing, each labelled.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the sharp edges and lines of the hard thing's print, and the smudged shape of the soft thing's print.
3. (sort) Sort these into two groups: Often becomes a fossil, and Hardly ever becomes a fossil.  
   **Answer:** a tooth → Often becomes a fossil; a jellyfish → Hardly ever becomes a fossil; a snail's shell → Often becomes a fossil; an earthworm → Hardly ever becomes a fossil; a bone → Often becomes a fossil; a slug → Hardly ever becomes a fossil. *Why:* Teeth, shells and bones are hard, so they last long enough to be buried. Jellyfish, earthworms and slugs are soft, so they rot away first.
4. (truefalse) People and the big dinosaurs lived on Earth at the same time.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The big dinosaurs died out about 66 million years ago. The first humans came about 300,000 years ago.
5. (match) Draw a line from each fossil to what it tells us.  
   **Answer:** A footprint in rock → How the animal walked; A sharp, curved tooth → The animal ate meat; A nest of eggs → How the babies began; A sea shell in a hill → The sea once covered this land. *Why:* Each fossil is a clue. Footprints show how an animal moved, teeth show what they ate, eggs show how babies began, and sea shells show where the sea was.
6. (write) Say it to a grown-up, who writes it down. In your Print Test, why did the banana slice not leave a sharp print?  
   **Answer:** The banana is soft. It squashed and spread out when I pressed it, so its edges were not sharp.. *Why:* A strong answer says the banana is soft, so it changed shape when pressed instead of pushing its shape into the dough.
7. (perspective) Nila, a girl in Class 2, and her brother Ravi are talking about dinosaurs.  
   **Answer:** Both are partly right. The big dinosaurs, like Rajasaurus and the titanosaurs, all died out. But some small feathered dinosaurs lived on, and birds come from them.. *Why:* A strong answer picks a side with a reason, and sees that it depends on what we mean by dinosaurs: the big ones are gone, and the birds are their family.
8. (label) Here is the side of a hill with three layers of rock. Write 1 on the oldest layer and 3 on the newest layer.  
   **Answer:** 1 on the bottom layer, 2 on the middle layer and 3 on the top layer.. *Why:* Layers pile up one on top of another, so the bottom one was laid down first and is the oldest. Your newspaper pile showed the same thing.
9. (fill) Soft bodies, like a jellyfish, hardly ever become fossils, because they ________ away before mud can cover them.  
   **Answer:** rot. *Why:* Soft bodies rot away or are eaten quickly. Hard parts last long enough to be buried.
10. (count) Asha, a girl in Class 2, makes a pile of 7 newspapers, one for each day of a week, with the oldest at the bottom. She hides a leaf under the 3rd paper from the top. How many papers are under the leaf?  
   **Answer:** 4 papers. *Why:* 7 papers in all, and 3 are above the leaf. 7 take away 3 is 4. So 4 papers are under the leaf, and they are older than the leaf's day.
### Dinosaurs and After (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (perspective) Scientists still argue about what made the big dinosaurs die out 66 million years ago.  
   **Answer:** Any side, with a reason. For the asteroid: a giant crater and a layer of iridium date to that very time. For the volcanoes: the Deccan lava poured out around the same time and changed the air. For both: they happened close together.. *Why:* A strong answer picks a side, gives a piece of evidence, and fairly says one point from another side. Nobody knows for sure yet.
2. (order) Put these in order, from longest ago to most recent.  
   **Answer:** ['The first dinosaurs', 'The big dinosaurs die out', 'The last mammoths', 'You are born']. *Why:* The first dinosaurs lived about 230 million years ago. The big ones died out about 66 million years ago. The last mammoths lived about 4,000 years ago. You were born only a few years ago.
3. (write) Kavya, a girl in Class 2, finds a fossil sea shell in a rock on a hill in Ariyalur, far from the sea. What does this tell her about that place? Say it, and a grown-up writes it down.  
   **Answer:** The sea once covered that land, long ago. The shell sank into the mud on the sea floor, and the mud turned to rock.. *Why:* A strong answer says the sea used to be there, and explains how a sea shell came to be inside rock.
4. (odd) Which one is the odd one out? Elephant, whale, Tyrannosaurus rex, human.  
   Options: Elephant / Whale / Tyrannosaurus rex / Human  
   **Answer:** Tyrannosaurus rex. *Why:* Elephants, whales and humans are mammals, who came after the big dinosaurs. Tyrannosaurus rex was a dinosaur.
5. (draw) Draw an animal who is alive today. Draw a ring round the parts that would most likely become a fossil.  
   **Answer:** A drawing of an animal with rings round the bones, teeth, shell, horns or claws.. *Why:* A strong answer rings the hard parts, like teeth, bones, horns, claws or a shell, because hard parts last long enough to become fossils.
6. (mcq) A scientist finds the print of a leaf in a rock in a very dry place. What does it tell us?  
   Options: Plants once grew here / The leaf fell from a tree today / No plants ever grew here  
   **Answer:** Plants once grew here. *Why:* A leaf print is a trace of a plant. The plant lived there long ago, even if the place is dry now.
7. (truefalse) Every animal who dies becomes a fossil.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most bodies rot or are eaten before mud can cover them. Only a tiny few become fossils, and those are mostly the hard parts.
8. (write) Anjali, a girl in Class 2, says: 'The dinosaurs died out because they were stupid and slow.' What would you tell her? Say it, and a grown-up writes it down.  
   **Answer:** The dinosaurs lived on Earth for about 165 million years, so they were very good at living. They died out because the world changed suddenly, and many could not find enough food.. *Why:* A strong answer says the dinosaurs did well for a very long time, and that a sudden change, not being slow or stupid, ended the big ones. Many fast and clever animals died too.
9. (match) Draw a line from each animal to what is true about them.  
   **Answer:** Crow → A bird, from the dinosaur family; Elephant → A mammal, who came after the dinosaurs; Crocodile → A reptile who lived with the dinosaurs and is still here; Ammonite → A sea animal with a shell, now extinct. *Why:* Birds come from dinosaurs. Elephants are mammals who spread after the dinosaurs. Crocodiles lived through the big change. Ammonites died out.
10. (sort) Sort these into two groups: Still alive today, and Extinct.  
   **Answer:** crocodile → Still alive today; ammonite → Extinct; Tyrannosaurus rex → Extinct; turtle → Still alive today; mammoth → Extinct; crow → Still alive today. *Why:* Crocodiles, turtles and crows are alive today. Ammonites, Tyrannosaurus rex and mammoths are extinct.

## Applications
- Geologists use fossils to tell the age of rock layers. Rock with the same fossils in two places was laid down at about the same time.
- Coal is made from plants that lived long ago and were buried. Petrol and gas come from tiny sea beings buried long ago. This is why they are called fossil fuels.
- Museums use fossils to show people what life was like long ago, like the dinosaur eggs at the Raiyoli fossil park in Gujarat.
- Scientists study past extinctions to help protect animals in danger today. Cheetahs died out in India in the last century, and in 2022 cheetahs from Namibia were brought to Kuno National Park in Madhya Pradesh.

## Spotted around us
- A crow or a pigeon at the window. Birds are the only dinosaurs alive today.
- Some limestone steps and floors in old buildings and temples have tiny fossil shells in them. Look closely at the stone.
- A conch shell or cowrie shells at home. A shell like this is the hard part that can become a fossil.
- Dinosaur models and real fossils at a science museum, like the Government Museum in Chennai or the Indroda park in Gandhinagar.

## Open questions
- **What colours were most dinosaurs?** Colour is almost never kept in fossils. For a few small feathered dinosaurs, scientists have found tiny traces in the feathers that show some colours. For most dinosaurs, nobody knows.
- **Was it the asteroid, the Deccan volcanoes, or both, that ended the big dinosaurs?** Both happened at about the same time. Scientists are still measuring the dates more exactly and arguing about which mattered more.
- **How many kinds of animals lived long ago that left no fossil at all?** Soft-bodied animals rarely leave fossils, so many kinds may have lived and vanished without a trace. Nobody can count what left nothing behind.

## Warm-up
- What is a fossil? → A trace of a living being from long ago, kept in rock.
- Which leave better fossils, hard parts or soft parts? → Hard parts, like bones, teeth and shells.
- In a pile of rock layers, which layer is usually oldest? → The bottom one.
- What does extinct mean? → Every last one of that kind has died.
- Did people ever meet the big dinosaurs? → No. The big dinosaurs died out about 66 million years before humans came.
- Which animals alive today come from dinosaurs? → Birds.
