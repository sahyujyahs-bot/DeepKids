# How plants are grouped: by the way they make new plants
`sci.plants.plant-variety.L5`

**Summary.** Ferns and mosses never make a flower or a seed, yet new ferns and mosses keep appearing on wet walls. They grow from spores, single cells far too small to see one by one. Scientists group plants by how they make new plants: mosses and ferns make spores, conifers make seeds on cones, and flowering plants make seeds inside fruits. Your child tests how far spores and seeds travel in moving air, builds a key and a family tree of plant groups, and meets what botanists still argue about, such as where flowers came from.

**Intro.** *Look under the leaf of a fern, if you can find one in a pot or on an old wall. You may see rows of brown dots. A fern never makes a flower, a fruit or a seed. So how does a new fern grow?* The brown dots are packets of spores. A spore is a single cell, much smaller than a grain of sugar. When the packets dry, they burst and throw the spores into the air. If one lands somewhere damp, a new fern can grow. Mosses do the same. Pine trees and deodars make seeds, but on cones, not in fruits. Most plants you know, from rice to the neem tree, make seeds inside fruits. In this unit you find out why these differences matter so much that scientists use them to group all plants.

## Terms
- **Flowering plant**: A plant that makes flowers. The flower makes seeds, and the seeds grow inside a fruit. Botanists call these plants angiosperms, which means 'seeds in a vessel'. *e.g.* Rice, tulsi, the coconut palm and the neem tree are all flowering plants. So is the grass on a lawn.
- **Non-flowering plant**: A plant that never makes flowers. Some non-flowering plants make seeds on cones. Others make spores instead of seeds. *e.g.* A deodar tree, a fern and a moss are all non-flowering plants.
- **Spore**: A spore is one single cell that can grow into a new plant. Spores are far too small to see one by one, and carry almost no food. Ferns and mosses make spores. *e.g.* The brown dust that falls from the dots under a fern leaf is millions of spores.
- **Seed**: A seed holds a tiny young plant and a store of food, wrapped in a tough coat. A seed is made of many cells, so seeds are much bigger and heavier than a spore. *e.g.* Split a soaked rajma bean, and you can see the tiny young plant lying between the two halves of food.
- **Conifer**: A tree or shrub that makes seeds on cones, not inside fruits. Most conifers have narrow, needle-shaped or scale-shaped leaves that stay green all year. *e.g.* Deodar and chir pine in the Himalayas and the Khasi pine around Shillong are conifers.
- **Cone**: A cone is a woody or soft part made of many scales. Female cones hold seeds on their scales. Smaller male cones make pollen. *e.g.* A brown pine cone on the ground in Kodaikanal or Shimla has held seeds under each of its scales.
- **Naked seed**: A seed that is not inside a fruit. Conifers and cycads have naked seeds. Botanists call these plants gymnosperms, which means 'naked seeds'. *e.g.* A chilgoza pine nut from a dry fruit shop is a naked seed, taken from the scale of a pine cone.
- **Fern**: A plant with veins and leaves, often feathery, that makes spores instead of seeds. A fern's leaf is called a frond. A young frond is rolled up like the head of a violin. *e.g.* Maidenhair fern grows on damp walls, and the ladder fern grows in cracks of old buildings in many Indian cities.
- **Sorus (plural: sori)**: A brown dot under a fern frond. Each sorus is a cluster of tiny spore cases. *e.g.* Turn over a fern frond in a nursery pot, and you may see sori in neat rows.
- **Moss**: A small, soft green plant that grows in a carpet or cushion in damp places. Mosses have no roots and no stiff veins. They make spores in small capsules on thin stalks. *e.g.* The green velvet that grows on walls and steps in Mumbai and Kerala in the monsoon is mostly moss.
- **Rhizoids**: Thin threads that hold a moss to a wall or the soil. They grip, but they do not carry water up the plant the way roots and veins do. *e.g.* Peel a small patch of moss off a wet brick, and you see brown threads underneath. Those are rhizoids.
- **Veins (vascular tissue)**: Long tubes inside a plant that carry water up from the roots and food around the plant. Their walls are stiffened by a tough material called lignin, so veins also hold a plant up. Ferns, conifers and flowering plants have veins. Mosses do not. *e.g.* Hold a leaf up to the light, and you can see its veins as fine lines.
- **Pollen**: Fine powder made by flowers and by male cones. Each grain carries the male cells a seed plant needs to make seeds. Pollen travels by wind or on animals, so it needs no water to move. *e.g.* Tap a ripe hibiscus flower over dark paper, and yellow powder falls out. A pine's small male cones in spring shed it too.
- **Classification**: Sorting living things into groups. Today botanists try to make each group a family of relatives that share one ancestor. *e.g.* Rice, wheat, bamboo and lawn grass are in one family, the grass family, because they share an ancestor.
- **Key**: A list of yes-or-no questions that leads you, step by step, to the name or group of a living thing. *e.g.* First question: does the plant have flowers? If no, does the plant have cones? If no, does the plant have veins?
- **Family tree of plants**: A drawing that shows which groups of plants share an ancestor. Groups on nearby branches are close relatives. Scientists call it a phylogenetic tree. *e.g.* On the tree, conifers and flowering plants sit on one branch, because both make seeds.

## Explanations
### The four big groups of land plants · *defined*
**Definition:** Land plants fall into four big groups by how they make new plants and whether they have veins. Mosses (and their relatives the liverworts and hornworts) have no stiff veins and make spores. Ferns have veins and make spores. Conifers and cycads have veins and make naked seeds on cones. Flowering plants have veins and make seeds inside fruits.

A key with three questions sorts almost any plant. Does the plant have flowers? Then the plant is a flowering plant. If not, does it make cones? Then the plant is probably a conifer or a cycad. If not, does it have veins? Then the plant is a fern. Small, soft and without veins? Then the plant is a moss or one of their relatives. The groups are not equal in size. About 9 in every 10 kinds of plant alive today are flowering plants. There are about 12,000 kinds of moss, about 10,500 kinds of fern and only about 600 kinds of conifer.
- e.g. Flowering: tulsi, rice, lotus, the banyan tree (their tiny flowers are hidden inside the figs)
- e.g. Conifers and cycads: deodar, chir pine, the Khasi pine, the 'Christmas tree' (Araucaria) in many building gardens, and the cycad palm-like plant in hotel gardens
- e.g. Ferns: maidenhair fern on wet walls, Azolla floating on rice fields, the dhekia fern eaten as a vegetable in Assam
- e.g. Mosses and relatives: the green velvet on monsoon walls, and the flat green liverwort Marchantia that grows on wet nursery pots
### Spores and seeds · *defined*
**Definition:** A spore is one cell with almost no stored food. A seed is made of many cells: a tiny young plant, a store of food and a protective coat.

Because a spore is so small and light, gentle wind can carry spores a long way, even across seas. A fern can release millions of spores in a year, and most land where they cannot grow. A seed is heavier, so wind carries most seeds only a short way unless they have wings or hairs. But a seed carries food, so the young plant can push out a root and a shoot before they can make their own food. A seed can also wait, dry and alive, for months or years until rain comes. That is why seed plants could spread into dry lands. Orchids are flowering plants with seeds as fine as dust and almost no food. They travel far like spores, and to sprout they need help from a fungus that feeds them.
- e.g. In the fan test, haldi powder, as fine as spores, drifted far. Mustard seeds fell close, and the rajma dropped almost straight down.
- e.g. A coconut is a huge seed with a big food store. Coconuts travel by floating on the sea, not by wind.
- e.g. Aak (madar) seeds are light and have silky hairs, so the wind carries them far even though they are seeds.
### Why mosses stay small and damp · *defined*
**Definition:** Mosses have no veins with stiff lignin walls, and their sperm cells must swim through a film of water to reach an egg. So mosses stay small and live where they are often wet.

In a tree, water climbs inside narrow tubes with stiff walls. A moss has no such tubes. Water moves over a moss's surface and between their tiny leaves, the way water creeps up a cloth. That works only over a few centimetres, so most mosses stay a few centimetres tall. To make a new moss, a male cell must swim to an egg. A male cell can only swim through water, such as rain or dew. Ferns have veins, so they can grow tall, but their sperm must swim too, on a tiny heart-shaped plant that grows from a spore. Seed plants solved this problem with pollen. Wind or animals carry pollen to the egg, so no water is needed. Mosses are not helpless in dry weather, though. Many mosses sense drying, shut down and wait. When rain comes, they turn green again within hours.
- e.g. In the Mumbai monsoon, walls turn green with moss. In April, the same moss looks brown and dry, and turns green again with the first rain.
- e.g. Ferns grow tall in the wet forests of the Western Ghats, where tree ferns can be several metres high.
- e.g. A deodar can grow 50 metres tall on a dry Himalayan slope, because its veins carry water up and its pollen travels by wind.
- **Many plant scientists:** A moss reviving after rain is a set of chemical responses in their cells. They prefer not to call it sensing or deciding.
- **Scientists who study plant behaviour:** A moss senses drying, prepares for dry days and recovers when water returns. That is sensing and responding, which is what a mind does in every living being.
### How scientists group plants · *defined*
**Definition:** Botanists group plants so that each group is a family of relatives, all coming from one shared ancestor. They use body features, such as veins, seeds and flowers, and, since the 1990s, the plant's DNA.

You could sort plants by colour, by height or by use, and every way would give different groups. But only one way finds relatives: features that were passed down from a shared ancestor. Seeds appeared once, in one group of plants, and all seed plants are their descendants. So all seed plants form one branch of the family tree. Inside that branch, flowers appeared once, and every flowering plant shares that ancestor. DNA tests mostly agree with these groups. A family tree drawn from features can be checked with a family tree drawn from DNA. When they disagree, scientists look again at both.
- e.g. Seeds group a deodar and a mango together, though one has needles and the other has broad leaves.
- e.g. A banana plant and rice look nothing alike, but DNA and flower features show both are in the same large group of flowering plants, the monocots.
- e.g. Azolla, a tiny floating plant, looks nothing like a tall fern, but her spores and DNA show she is a fern.
### Where did flowering plants come from? · *debated*
Flowering plants appear in fossils suddenly, about 130 million years ago, and within a few tens of millions of years they were everywhere. In 1879 Charles Darwin called this sudden rise 'an abominable mystery', meaning a hateful puzzle. Botanists still argue about it.
- e.g. The oldest flower fossils, such as Archaefructus from China, are about 125 million years old.
- e.g. Fossil pollen from flowering plants goes back about 135 million years.
- e.g. Amborella, a small shrub found only on the island of New Caledonia, sits on the oldest living branch of the flowering plants.
- **Many fossil experts:** Flowering plants began not long before their oldest fossils, about 140 million years ago, and spread fast because flowers and fruits work so well with insects and other animals.
- **Many DNA scientists:** By measuring how fast DNA changes, they estimate that flowering plants began much earlier, perhaps more than 200 million years ago, and stayed rare and small at first, so they left few fossils.
- **Some botanists:** Both may be partly right: flowering plants may have begun early but spread only when insects, climate and soils changed.
### Are mosses and ferns 'lower plants'? · *debated*
Older textbooks called mosses and ferns 'lower plants' and flowering plants 'higher plants', as if plants climbed a ladder. Most botanists today reject this ladder. Mosses and ferns alive today have been changing for as long as flowering plants have.
- e.g. Mosses live on every continent, including Antarctica, where few flowering plants can survive.
- e.g. Sphagnum moss holds about 20 times their own dry weight of water. Peat bogs, built mostly from dead sphagnum, store more carbon than all the trees in the world's forests.
- e.g. Ferns kept spreading long after flowering plants arrived. Many fern groups alive today began in the shade of flowering forests.
- **Older textbooks and some teachers:** Mosses and ferns came first, and seed plants and flowers were added later, so 'lower' and 'higher' simply show the order in which the groups appeared.
- **Most botanists today:** Every plant alive today has an equally long history. A moss is not an unfinished fern. Each group is very good at living in its own way, so 'lower' is a misleading word.

## Mostly agreed
- All land plants share one ancestor, a green alga that lived in fresh water more than 470 million years ago. *How we know:* Land plants share a set of features and DNA with one group of green algae, the charophytes. The oldest spore fossils from land plants are about 470 million years old.
- Mosses and ferns make spores, and seed plants make seeds. *How we know:* Anyone can see sori under a fern frond and capsules on a moss. Botanists have grown ferns and mosses from spores in the lab for over 150 years.
- Ferns, conifers and flowering plants have veins with lignin. Mosses do not. *How we know:* Thin slices looked at under a microscope show stiff water-carrying tubes in ferns and seed plants, and none of these in mosses.
- Seed plants first appeared about 365 million years ago, and flowering plants are much younger. *How we know:* The oldest true seeds are found in rocks about 365 million years old. Pollen of flowering plants appears in rocks only from about 135 million years ago.
- About 9 in every 10 kinds of plant alive today are flowering plants. *How we know:* Botanists have named about 295,000 flowering plants, compared with about 12,000 mosses, about 10,500 ferns and about 1,000 conifers, cycads and their relatives together.

## How we found out
**Carl Linnaeus, a Swedish botanist; Wilhelm Hofmeister, a German botanist who taught himself, 1753 (Linnaeus), the 1840s (fern spores watched as they grew) and 1851 (Hofmeister).** In 1753 the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus sorted plants by counting the parts of their flowers. Ferns, mosses, algae and fungi had no flowers to count, so he put them all into one group. He called it Cryptogamia, 'hidden marriage', because nobody knew how they made new plants. In Europe, many people still believed ferns had invisible seeds. In the 1840s, botanists watched fern spores grow into tiny heart-shaped plants. Then a young man in Leipzig, Wilhelm Hofmeister, studied mosses, ferns and conifers under his microscope. He had never been to university. He worked in his father's music shop and studied plants in his spare time. In 1851 he published a book showing that mosses, ferns and conifers all follow one pattern. Each life has a spore-making stage and an egg-and-sperm stage. In conifers the second stage is hidden inside the cone and the pollen. So the 'hidden' plants were not a separate world. They were relatives of the seed plants, with the same plan of life. The University of Rostock gave him an honorary doctorate, and in 1863 he became a professor.  
*What people believed before:* That ferns and mosses were a separate, mysterious kind of plant with hidden or invisible seeds. Hofmeister showed that they follow the same pattern of life as conifers, only without seeds.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Ferns make invisible seeds.** (A common belief in Europe for hundreds of years; *rejected*)  
  For: New ferns appeared everywhere, but nobody could find their flowers or seeds. Some people said fern seed could make a person invisible.  
  Against: In the 1800s, botanists watched spores fall from the sori, grow into tiny heart-shaped plants, and then into new ferns. There is no seed at all.
- **Mosses, liverworts and hornworts form one natural family of relatives.** (Many botanists, using DNA studies since about 2018; *debated*)  
  For: Large DNA studies now mostly place these three groups together on one branch, next to the branch with all the plants that have veins.  
  Against: For decades, other DNA studies placed them as separate steps, one after another, below the plants with veins. Some scientists still think the question is not fully settled.
- **Glossopteris fossils show that India, Africa, South America, Antarctica and Australia were once joined.** (Alfred Wegener (1915), and many geologists later; *accepted*)  
  For: Glossopteris was a seed plant with tongue-shaped leaves. Their fossils are found in India, Africa, South America, Antarctica and Australia. Their seeds were too heavy to cross oceans.  
  Against: For about 40 years, most geologists rejected the idea, because nobody knew how continents could move. In the 1960s, sea-floor studies showed how. Today this is accepted.
- **Selaginella bryopteris, a small spore plant from India, is the Sanjeevani herb of the Ramayana.** (Some scientists and many people in India; *debated*)  
  For: This plant curls up and looks dead when dry, and turns green again within hours of being watered. That fits a herb that brings life back.  
  Against: The old texts describe Sanjeevani in words that could fit many plants, and scholars disagree about which plant was meant. There is no way to check.
- **Seaweeds and other algae are plants.** (Many older textbooks; *debated*)  
  For: Green algae make food from sunlight like plants, and land plants came from green algae.  
  Against: Many botanists now call only land plants 'plants'. Red and brown seaweeds are on very different branches of the tree of life, so they are not close relatives of plants at all.

## Roots
We have learned how mosses, ferns, conifers and flowering plants make new plants, and how scientists group them. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the fan test, the haldi dust drifted far, but the mustard seeds dropped close to the fan. A fern's spore is one tiny cell, so the wind carries spores far. A seed holds a young plant and a food store, so seeds are heavier and land nearer. In moving air, the lighter a plant's young are, the farther they travel, and the less food they carry.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call mosses and ferns 'lower plants', as if a spore were a failed seed. But spores and seeds are two different bargains. Spores travel far and cost the parent little each. Seeds travel less far but start their life fed. Neither is the better way everywhere.  
*Evidence:* In the fan test, haldi as fine as spores spread across the paper track, while mustard fell within a short distance and rajma almost straight down. Orchid seeds, as fine as dust and with almost no food, also travel far, and they need a fungus to feed them before they can sprout.

- *who am i:* You started as one cell too, like a spore. But you were fed by your mother for nine months, more like a seed.
- *what is life:* Many living beings send their young out into the world. Plants do it in different ways: many light travellers, or a few well-fed ones.
- *what is this world:* The air around you carries spores from ferns, mosses and fungi, too small to see. Some of them came from far away.

All land plants came from one kind of green alga, living in fresh water more than 470 million years ago. From that one ancestor came mosses, then plants with veins, then seed plants, then flowering plants. Each new branch kept going alongside the old ones. So the one green ancestor became the many plants around you, from the moss on a wall to the rice on your plate. They are all one family, living in different ways.

- **Rig Veda 10.97.15, the hymn to plants (Oshadhi Sukta):** The hymn speaks of plants that bear fruit and plants that bear none, plants without flowers and plants with flowers. (More than 3,000 years ago, people in India already noticed that some plants flower and fruit and others never do. Botanists today still use flowers, fruits and seeds to group plants.)
- **Jain teaching of anekantavada, many-sidedness, and the story of the blind men and the elephant:** Each blind man touched one part of an elephant and described a different animal. Each was partly right. Only by putting the parts together did they find the whole elephant. (Sorting plants by colour, by height or by use each shows one side of them. Body features and DNA together give the fullest picture of who is related to whom.)
- Science (settled): All land plants share one ancestor among the green algae.
- Science (debated): DNA studies now mostly place mosses, liverworts and hornworts together on one branch.
- Science (open): Why flowering plants spread so fast, about 130 million years ago, is still not fully understood.
- Other view, Some philosophers of biology: The family tree of plants is real, because plants really do have ancestors. But where we cut that tree into named groups is our choice. Nature does not label her branches 'fern' or 'moss'. People do.

## Story: The Brown Dust
*Everything about the plants is true: fern spores, the heart-shaped plant that grows from a spore, pine cones closing in damp air, moss capsules and the old European belief in invisible fern seed. Nila, Arjun, Kamala Paati and their visit to Kodaikanal are made up.*

Every May, Nila and her cousin Arjun went to stay with Kamala Paati in Kodaikanal. Paati had taught botany at a college for thirty years. Now she grew ferns along the stone wall of her garden, and moss grew between the steps whether she wanted it or not. Nila was ten. Arjun was eight, and he had a plan.

"I want a fern for my room in Chennai," he said on the first morning. "Paati, where are the fern seeds? I'll plant them in a pot."

Paati smiled and went on pouring filter coffee. "Look for them yourself," she said. "Nila, help him."

The children went out to the wall. The ferns were tall and feathery, and the young leaves were curled up tight, like the head of a violin. Arjun looked at the top of every leaf. He looked at the stems. He even dug a little at the roots. "There are no flowers," he said. "So there can't be any fruits. So where are the seeds?"

Nila turned a leaf over. Underneath, in two neat rows, were dozens of brown dots. "Is the fern sick?" Arjun asked. "Maybe they have some disease."

"The fern doesn't look sick," Nila said. "The dots are in rows. A disease would be messy." She picked one old leaf, the one with the darkest dots, and carried it inside. She laid it on a sheet of white paper, dots facing down, and turned a steel bowl upside down over it. "Let's see what happens by tomorrow," she said.

That evening Paati told them a story. "In Europe, hundreds of years ago, people had the same question as Arjun," she said. "Ferns spread everywhere, but no one ever saw a fern seed. So people decided the seed must be invisible. Some said that catching fern seed on one special night would make you invisible too. Even Shakespeare wrote a joke about it in one of his plays."

"Did it work?" asked Arjun.

"Nobody ever caught any," said Paati. "Because there was nothing to catch."

The next morning Nila lifted the bowl. Under the leaf, on the white paper, lay a fine brown pattern. It was the exact shape of the rows of dots, drawn in brown dust. Arjun touched it with a finger, and the dust smeared like cocoa powder. "The leaf printed itself," he said.

Paati brought her old hand lens. Through the lens, the dust was still just dust, a little grainy. "Each dot under the leaf is a bunch of tiny cases," she said. "When the air is dry, the cases split open and fling out spores. A spore is one single cell. This little heap is probably thousands of them. Each one could grow into a fern."

"So the invisible seed was real!" said Arjun.

"Almost," said Nila slowly. "But this isn't a seed. A rajma has a baby plant and food packed inside. A spore is just one cell, light enough to blow away."

Paati looked pleased. "Good. A seed is like a full tiffin box with a small passenger inside. A spore is a passenger with no tiffin at all. So the spore must land somewhere damp and start making food almost at once."

That afternoon they walked through the pine forest near the town. In the morning there had been mist, and the cones lying on the ground were shut tight. Now the sun was out, and Arjun found a cone with every scale bent open. When he shook it, a small seed with a papery wing spun down to the path.

"Pines have seeds, then," he said. "But no flowers."

"No flowers and no fruits," said Paati. "The seed sits naked on the scale of the cone. The cone opens in dry air, so the winged seeds can fly. In wet air the scales close again, and the seeds stay in." Nila took two cones home, one to keep dry and one to soak in a bucket, to see whether Paati was right. By bedtime, the soaked cone had closed.

On the last evening, Nila sat on the garden steps and looked at the moss between the stones. Rising out of the green were hundreds of thin brown stalks, each with a tiny capsule at the top, like a matchstick head. "Spores again?" she asked.

"Spores again," said Paati. "But a moss is different from a fern. A moss has no veins, the tubes that carry water up a stem. So moss stays small, and lives where the ground is wet. And to make a new moss, the moss needs rain. A male cell has to swim through a film of water to reach the egg."

"And pines don't need rain for that, because of pollen," said Nila. "The wind carries it."

"Now you know why botanists sort plants the way they do," said Paati. "Not by colour, not by size. By how they make new plants."

Arjun carried the paper with the brown print all the way home to Chennai in a closed box. With Nila's help, he tipped the dust onto damp soil in a clay pot and covered the pot with a sheet of glass. For weeks nothing happened. Then a faint green film appeared. When Nila looked closely, she saw tiny flat green hearts, smaller than a fingernail. Paati said each heart was a little plant grown from a spore, and that underneath, a male cell swims through a drop of water to an egg. Some months later, out of one of the hearts, a real fern leaf began to uncurl.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the brown dots under a fern leaf are sori, clusters of spore cases. A spore is a single cell with almost no food, which is why spores are so light and why ferns needed no seed. A spore grows first into a tiny flat heart-shaped plant, the prothallus. On it, male cells swim through water to an egg, and the young fern grows from there. Pine seeds sit naked on cone scales, and the cones open in dry air and close in damp air. Mosses have no veins and need water for their male cells to swim, so they stay small and live in damp places.

- Ask: Paati said a seed is like a full tiffin box with a passenger, and a spore is a passenger with none. What did she mean?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Fan Test: do spores or seeds travel farther? (35 min)
- **question:** In moving air, which travels farther: fine dust like spores, or seeds? And does the size of the seed matter?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will drop haldi powder, mustard seeds and a rajma bean in front of a fan. Which will land farthest from where you dropped them? Which will land closest? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Drop everything from the same height, level with the middle of the fan, at the same spot just in front of it. Keep the fan at the same speed and in the same place. Only the thing you drop changes. Drop each one 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Use only a pinch of haldi, and the fan's lowest speed. Keep faces out of the powder cloud, and never blow powder at anyone. Haldi stains clothes and floors, so keep it on the paper.
- **record:** Fill in the table, then draw a bar chart with one bar for each thing you dropped. Write one sentence for your claim, one for your evidence and one for your reasoning.
- **results table:** What we dropped | Try 1: farthest it landed (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Average (cm)
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the fan on a table or stool at one end of a clear floor, blowing straight across the room. 2. Lay the sheets of paper end to end on the floor in a straight line, about 2.5 metres long. Start under the spot 30 cm in front of the fan. 3. With the pencil, mark the edge of the paper every 25 cm from the start. 4. Set the fan to its lowest speed.  
  *Parent says:* Today we test how far the wind can carry tiny things and bigger things. First, let's make a track to measure along.  
  *Child mode:* Put the fan on a table, blowing across the room. Lay the paper in a long line on the floor. Mark every 25 cm with a pencil. Set the fan to slow.
  - Note: The drop spot is about 30 cm in front of the fan, level with its middle. The air must flow past the falling things, so drop from that height, not from near the floor. Close windows, so the room's air stays still apart from the fan.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the haldi, the mustard and the rajma. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason for each. 3. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* Which of these will travel farthest in the wind from the fan? Which will land closest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the haldi, the mustard and the rajma. Which will go farthest? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The haldi is lighter, so the wind can push it more easily.; The rajma is heavy, so the bean falls fast before the wind can push them.)
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold one rajma bean level with the middle of the fan, at the drop spot. 2. Let go without throwing. 3. Measure where the bean first landed, before rolling, if you can see. 4. Do this 3 times and write each distance in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Hold your hand at the drop spot, level with the middle of the fan. Just open your fingers. Don't throw.  
  *Child mode:* Hold one rajma at the drop spot, level with the fan's middle. Let go. Where did it land? Do it 3 times.
  - Note: If a bean rolls, measure where the bean first hit the paper. Watching carefully is part of the test.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold 3 or 4 mustard seeds at the same height. 2. Let them fall. 3. Measure to the seed that landed farthest. 4. Do this 3 times and write each distance in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now the mustard seeds. Same height, same spot. Watch where they land.  
  *Child mode:* Drop a few mustard seeds from the same height. Find the one that went farthest. Measure. Do it 3 times.
- **s5 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take a small pinch of haldi between your finger and thumb. 2. Hold it at the same height and rub your fingers gently, so it falls as a cloud. 3. Wait until the cloud settles, about one minute. 4. Look along the paper for the farthest yellow you can see, and measure. 5. Do this 3 times.  
  *Parent says:* Haldi powder is about as fine as fern spores. Let's see where the wind takes it. Keep your face away from the cloud.  
  *Child mode:* Take a tiny pinch of haldi. Rub your fingers so it falls as a cloud. Keep your face away. Wait a minute. Find the farthest yellow. Measure.
  - Ask: What did the haldi do that the seeds did not? (good: It floated in the air for a while.; It spread out along the paper instead of landing in one spot.; Some of it went past the end of the track.)
  - Note: Some haldi may float past the end of the track. If so, write 'more than 250 cm'. The faint yellow is easier to see if you look along the paper from low down, near the floor.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to choose one more thing to test, such as til, jeera, sooji or a wisp of cotton. 2. Ask them to predict where it fits among the three results. 3. Test it 3 times in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now you design part of the test. Choose one more thing. Where do you think it will land, compared with the others? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Choose one more thing to test. Guess where it will land. Say why. Test it 3 times.
  - Note: This is your child's own investigation. Ask them which things must stay the same for it to be fair: the height, the spot and the fan speed.
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Work out the average for each thing: add the three distances and divide by 3. 2. Draw a bar chart. 3. Say a claim, the evidence and the reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What did we find? Tell me your claim, the evidence from our numbers, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Add your three numbers. Divide by 3. Draw a bar for each thing. Say what you found and why.
  - Ask: A fern spore is about as fine as haldi. What does our test tell us about how ferns spread? (good: The wind can carry fern spores far away.; That is how ferns can appear on a wall far from any other fern.)
  - Ask: A seed is heavier than a spore. What does a seed have that a spore does not? (good: A young plant and a store of food inside.; A tough coat.)
  - Check: In the Fan Test, why did the haldi travel much farther than the rajma? → **Each grain of haldi is so light that moving air can carry it before it falls**
### e2 · The Pine Cone Weather Test (if you have a pine cone) (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Does a pine cone close when wet and open when dry? How does that help the pine tree?
- **predict:** Guess first. What will happen to an open pine cone after an hour in water? What will happen when it dries in the sun again? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Measure the same cone at its widest point each time, with the same ruler. Compare it with a second cone that stays dry, so you know the change came from the water and not from time passing.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the wet cone and the dry cone side by side.
- **results table:** Cone | Width at the start (cm) | After 1 hour (cm) | After 1 day drying (cm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Measure both cones. Soak one for an hour. Measure both again. (15 min); day 2: Measure the soaked cone after a day in the sun. Count the spirals on the dry cone. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure each cone across its widest part. 2. Write both widths in the table. 3. Ask for a prediction and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* What will water do to this open cone? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Measure both cones. Write the numbers. Guess what water will do. Say why.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push one cone under the water and hold it down with a cup. 2. Leave the other cone dry. 3. After one hour, take the wet cone out and measure both cones again.  
  *Parent says:* Let's leave this one in the water for an hour. The other one stays dry, so we can compare.  
  *Child mode:* Sink one cone in water. Keep the other dry. Wait one hour. Measure both again.
  - Ask: What changed? Why might a pine tree's cones close in rain? (good: The wet cone closed up.; Wet seeds can't fly well, so the cone keeps them in until it is dry.; The scales protect the seeds in rain.)
  - Note: Most cones close within one or two hours. The scales are made of two layers that swell differently when wet, so the scales bend shut. The cone is no longer growing, and it works like a hinge made by the tree.
  - Wait: Leave it for 1 hour
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the wet cone in a sunny spot for a day. 2. Measure it again. 3. On the dry cone, count the spirals of scales going one way, then the other way.  
  *Parent says:* Has the cone opened again? Now let's count the spirals of scales. Count the ones curving left, then the ones curving right.  
  *Child mode:* Measure the cone after a day in the sun. Count the spirals one way. Then the other way.
  - Note: Many pine cones have 8 spirals one way and 13 the other, or 5 and 8. These are neighbouring numbers in the Fibonacci pattern, where each number is the sum of the two before it.
  - Check: A pine cone opened on a dry, sunny day. Why is that a good time for a pine tree's seeds to come out? → **Dry winged seeds fly farther in dry air**

  *Child mode:* Look at the brown dots up close. What do you think they are? Why?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay each frond on white paper with the underside down. 2. Cover each with an upside-down bowl, so moving air cannot blow the dust away. 3. Leave them overnight.  
  *Parent says:* The bowl keeps the air still. Tomorrow we'll see if anything fell out.  
  *Child mode:* Put each leaf face down on white paper. Cover it with a bowl. Leave it overnight.
  - Wait: Leave it overnight
- **s3 (10 min)**

  *Parent says:* What do you see? Is there a difference between the two papers?  
  *Child mode:* Lift the bowls. Lift the leaves. What is on the paper? Look with the lens.

  - Note: Ripe dots usually leave a fine brown dust in the shape of their rows. If nothing appears, the dots may be too young or already empty. Try a leaf with darker, fuzzier dots. The spores are single cells, so even through a lens they look like fine dust.
  - Check: A fern leaf left a brown dust print on white paper. What is the dust? → **Spores, each a single cell**

2. (mcq) What is inside a seed that is not inside a spore?  
   Options: A tiny young plant and a store of food / Water only / Pollen / Nothing: they are the same  
   **Answer:** A tiny young plant and a store of food. *Why:* A seed is many cells: a young plant, food and a coat. A spore is one cell with almost no food. That is why seeds are heavier.
3. (mcq) In the Fan Test, haldi travelled far and rajma dropped close. What does this tell us about fern spores?  
   Options: Being tiny and light, spores can be carried far by gentle wind / Spores are heavier than seeds / Spores travel only on water / Spores never leave the parent fern  
   **Answer:** Being tiny and light, spores can be carried far by gentle wind. *Why:* Haldi is about as fine as fern spores. Like the haldi, spores fall slowly, so even gentle wind carries them far.
4. (mcq) You are using a key. The first question is 'Does the plant have flowers?' The answer is no. The next is 'Does the plant make cones?' The answer is no. The plant has fronds with veins. What is it?  
   Options: A fern / A conifer / A flowering plant / A moss  
   **Answer:** A fern. *Why:* No flowers, no cones, but veins and fronds: that is a fern. A moss would have no veins.
5. (mcq) Why do most mosses grow only a few centimetres tall?  
   Options: They have no veins with stiff walls to carry water up and hold them up / They are always young / They grow only at night / Insects eat their tops  
   **Answer:** They have no veins with stiff walls to carry water up and hold them up. *Why:* Without veins, water moves only over the moss's surface, which works over a few centimetres. Ferns and trees have veins, so they can grow tall.
6. (mcq) Why do mosses and ferns need water to make new plants, but pines do not?  
   Options: Moss and fern male cells must swim to an egg, but pine pollen is carried by wind / Pines drink more water / Mosses and ferns are thirsty all the time / Pines make spores  
   **Answer:** Moss and fern male cells must swim to an egg, but pine pollen is carried by wind. *Why:* In mosses and ferns, a male cell swims through a film of water to reach an egg. Seed plants use pollen, which needs no water to travel.
7. (mcq) On a family tree of plants, why are a pine and a mango on the same branch?  
   Options: Both make seeds, which came from one shared ancestor / Both are tall / Both have broad leaves / Both grow in Kerala  
   **Answer:** Both make seeds, which came from one shared ancestor. *Why:* Seeds appeared once, and all seed plants came from that ancestor. Height and leaf shape do not show relatives.
8. (mcq) Since the 1990s, what else have botanists used, besides body features, to group plants?  
   Options: DNA / Colour / Price / Smell  
   **Answer:** DNA. *Why:* DNA carries the instructions passed from parent to young, so comparing DNA shows who is related to whom. It mostly agrees with groups made from features like seeds and veins.
9. (mcq) Why do most botanists today avoid calling mosses 'lower plants'?  
   Options: Today's mosses have been changing for as long as flowering plants, and are very good at their own way of life / Mosses are taller than trees / Mosses came after flowering plants / Mosses have flowers  
   **Answer:** Today's mosses have been changing for as long as flowering plants, and are very good at their own way of life. *Why:* Mosses' ancestors came earlier, but living mosses are not unfinished plants. 'Lower' suggests a ladder, and a family tree is a bush.
10. (mcq) Rekha, a girl in Class 5, wants to know if til travels farther than mustard in the Fan Test. Which test is fair?  
   Options: Drop both from the same height, at the same spot, with the same fan speed, 3 times each / Drop til from higher up because it is lighter / Use a fast fan for til and a slow one for mustard / Drop each only once  
   **Answer:** Drop both from the same height, at the same spot, with the same fan speed, 3 times each. *Why:* In a fair test, only one thing changes: what is dropped. Repeating 3 times guards against a fluke.

## Applications
- Farmers in Kerala, Tamil Nadu and many other states grow Azolla, a tiny water fern, as cattle feed and to add nitrogen to rice fields. A microbe living inside Azolla takes nitrogen from the air.
- Gardeners pack sphagnum moss around orchid roots and line hanging baskets with moss, because moss holds water for a long time.
- Pine timber is used for furniture and boxes, and resin from chir pine in Uttarakhand and Himachal is made into turpentine.
- Much of India's coal formed from ancient forests of seed plants without flowers, such as Glossopteris.
- The ladder fern Pteris vittata soaks up arsenic from soil. Scientists are testing such ferns to clean soils polluted with arsenic, a problem in parts of West Bengal and Bihar.
- Botanists use keys, and now DNA tests, to identify plants, for example to check which plants are really in a packet of herbal medicine.
- Seed banks store dry seeds for many years, which works because a seed can wait alive until water comes. Spores of many ferns can be stored too.

## Spotted around us
- Green velvet moss on walls, compound walls and steps in the Mumbai, Goa and Kerala monsoon.
- The ladder fern growing out of cracks in old buildings, bridges and around drains in many Indian cities.
- A tall Araucaria 'Christmas tree' with flat layers of branches in a housing society garden.
- Cycas plants that look like short palms in hotel gardens and traffic islands.
- A red-green carpet of Azolla floating on a village pond or a rice field.
- Chilgoza pine nuts in a dry fruit shop, and pine cones sold in Ooty, Kodaikanal and Shimla.
- Bunches of curled dhekia fern tops sold as a vegetable in markets in Assam.
- Flat, shiny green Marchantia on the soil of pots in plant nurseries.

## Open questions
- **Why did flowering plants spread so fast, about 130 million years ago?** Fossils and DNA give different dates for when they began, and nobody knows for sure what made them take over so quickly.
- **What did the very first land plant look like?** The oldest land plant fossils are only spores. The plants that made them have not been found.
- **Did the first land plants need fungi to survive on land?** Some of the oldest plant fossils, about 407 million years old, already have fungi living inside them. Whether plants could have come onto land without fungi is still argued about.
- **How many kinds of plants are there?** About 2,000 new plant species are named every year, and many places, such as the forests of Northeast India, are still not fully explored.
- **How does a dried-out moss revive within hours of rain?** Scientists know some of the chemicals involved, but not the whole story of how the cells protect themselves and switch back on.

## Connected maths
- In the Fan Test, the haldi travelled 180 cm, 210 cm and 240 cm in three tries. What is the average distance? **210 cm.**. Add the three tries: 180 + 210 + 240 = 630. Divide by the number of tries: 630 ÷ 3 = 210. So the average is 210 cm. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L5`)
- In the Fan Test, the haldi travelled 210 cm on average and the mustard 20 cm. About how many times as far did the haldi travel? **About 10 times as far.**. 210 ÷ 20 = 10.5. So the haldi travelled about 10 times as far as the mustard. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Botanists have named about 295,000 flowering plants, 12,000 mosses, 10,500 ferns and 1,000 conifers and their relatives. What percentage of these are flowering plants? **About 93%.**. Total: 295,000 + 12,000 + 10,500 + 1,000 = 318,500. Flowering plants: 295,000 ÷ 318,500 is about 0.93. So about 93 out of every 100 are flowering plants. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- You make a timeline strip of plants on land, 47 cm long, from 470 million years ago to today. How far from the 'today' end do you mark the first seeds, 365 million years ago, and the first flowers, 130 million years ago? **Seeds at 36.5 cm, flowers at 13 cm from the 'today' end.**. 47 cm stands for 470 million years, so 1 cm stands for 10 million years. 365 ÷ 10 = 36.5 cm. 130 ÷ 10 = 13 cm. (queues `math.measurement-basics.scale-models.L5`)
- On a pine cone, Bornali, a girl in Class 5, counted 8 spirals going one way and 13 going the other. In the pattern 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, each number is the sum of the two before it. What are the next two numbers? **21 and 34.**. 8 + 13 = 21. 13 + 21 = 34. Sunflower heads often have 21 and 34, or 34 and 55, spirals. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In the Moss Sponge test, 200 ml of water went in with the moss. 110 ml was left after the moss was lifted out. With the soil, 180 ml was left. How much more water did the moss hold than the soil? **70 ml more.**. The moss held 200 − 110 = 90 ml. The soil held 200 − 180 = 20 ml. 90 − 20 = 70 ml more. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L5`)
