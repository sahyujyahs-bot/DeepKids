# Testing materials: which one is best for the job?
`sci.materials.testing-materials.L2`

**Summary.** A rubber band and a strip of milk packet can both be stretched. Only one of them goes back to its old length when you let go. You cannot find this out by looking. You have to test. In this unit you hang a water bottle from strips of rubber, cloth and paper, and measure how much each one stretches. You also find out which materials keep water out, and which are strong enough to lift a bottle.

**Intro.** *Hold a rubber band in one hand and a strip of an old milk packet in the other. Pull both gently. Both get longer. Now let go. What happens to each one?* The rubber band snaps back. The milk packet strip stays long and thin. Both looked like soft, bendy things before you pulled them. A material can surprise you, so scientists test it. A test means doing something to a material on purpose and watching what happens. Before you read on, guess. Which material in your home stretches the most? You are going to find out.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can test which material stretches the most, by hanging the same weight from each one and measuring.
1. By the end I can say if a stretched material springs back or stays stretched.
2. By the end I can test which materials are waterproof and which are absorbent.
3. By the end I can test which material is strongest by seeing how much it can lift before it tears.
4. By the end I can guess first, give a reason, and then say what my test showed.
5. By the end I can choose a material for a job and say which test tells me it is right.

## Terms
- **Material**: What a thing is made of. Rubber, cotton, paper, plastic, steel and wood are materials. *e.g.* A hair band is made of rubber and cloth. A tumbler is made of steel.
- **Test**: Doing something to a material on purpose, and watching or measuring what happens. *e.g.* Hanging a water bottle from a strip, and measuring how long the strip gets, is a stretch test.
- **Stretchy**: Gets longer when you pull it, without breaking. *e.g.* A rubber band and the top of a sock are stretchy. A strip of newspaper is not.
- **Elastic**: Goes back to its old shape after you stop pulling or pressing. *e.g.* A rubber band is elastic. The skin on the back of your hand is elastic too.
- **Waterproof**: Does not let water through. *e.g.* A raincoat, a polythene sheet and a banana leaf are waterproof.
- **Absorbent**: Soaks up water. *e.g.* A towel, a paper napkin and a sponge are absorbent.
- **Strong**: Hard to break or tear. A strong material can hold a big weight. *e.g.* A strip of cotton cloth can lift a full water bottle. A strip of paper napkin tears.
- **Predict**: To say what you think will happen, before you test it, and why. *e.g.* 'I predict the rubber band will stretch the most, because it is bendy.'

## Explanations
### Why we test materials · *defined*
**Definition:** A test is doing something to a material on purpose and seeing what happens. It shows what a material does, which looking alone cannot show.

A thick cotton towel looks as if it would keep rain out, but water soaks into it. A thin sheet of polythene looks weak, but it keeps water out completely. So we cannot always tell by looking. We test. In a fair test every material gets the same bottle, the same water and the same time, so only the material is different.
- e.g. Stretch test: the same full bottle hangs from every strip.
- e.g. Waterproof test: the same spoon of water goes on every square.
- e.g. Strength test: every strip is the same size, and water is added to the bottle one cup at a time.
### Stretchy and elastic: why a rubber band springs back · *defined*
**Definition:** A stretchy material gets longer when it is pulled. An elastic material goes back to its old length when the pull stops.

Everything is made of tiny particles, far too small to see. In rubber, the particles are joined in very long chains, curled up like a heap of loose thread. Pulling straightens the chains, and letting go lets them curl up again, so the rubber goes back. In a milk packet, the plastic chains slide to new places when you pull, and they stay there. Pull rubber too far, and the chains break and the band snaps.
- e.g. A rubber band, a hair band, the top of a sock and the skin on your hand are elastic.
- e.g. A strip of milk packet and a roll of atta dough stretch, but stay stretched.
- e.g. A strip of newspaper hardly stretches at all. Pull hard, and it tears.
### Waterproof and absorbent · *defined*
**Definition:** A waterproof material does not let water through. An absorbent material soaks water up.

Paper and cotton are made of tiny fibres, like very thin threads, with tiny gaps between them. Water creeps into the gaps and spreads along the fibres, so it soaks in and goes through. Polythene and foil have no gaps, so water stays on top as a round drop. A banana leaf also has a thin layer of wax, and water slides off wax.
- e.g. Waterproof: a polythene sheet, foil, a steel plate, a banana leaf, an umbrella.
- e.g. Absorbent: a towel, a paper napkin, newspaper, a cotton hanky, a sponge.
- e.g. A raincoat must be waterproof. A towel must be absorbent. Each one is right for its own job.
### Strong and weak · *defined*
**Definition:** A strong material is hard to break or tear. It can hold a big weight. A weak material breaks or tears with a small weight.

In cotton cloth, the threads are twisted and woven tightly, so they hold on to each other well. A paper napkin is made of short fibres pressed loosely together, so a small pull drags them apart. Strong is not always best: a napkin is useful because it is soft and absorbent, not because it is strong.
- e.g. Strong: cotton cloth, rope, a thick carry bag, steel.
- e.g. Weak: a paper napkin, toilet paper, a dry leaf.
- e.g. Wet paper is weaker than dry paper, because water loosens its fibres.
### Is the best material always the strongest one? · *debated*
There is often no one best material. It depends on the job, and on what matters most to the person choosing.
- e.g. A cloth bag is strong and can be used for years. A paper bag is weaker but rots away quickly if it is thrown out.
- e.g. A steel tiffin box is strong. A banana leaf is waterproof, free in many places, and goes back into the soil.
- **A shopkeeper:** The best bag is the cheapest one that does not tear on the way home.
- **A person who cares most about the Earth:** The best bag is one you use again and again, like a cloth bag, even if it costs more at first.
- **An engineer:** First say exactly what the job needs: how much weight, how wet, how long. Then test, and pick the material that passes.

## Mostly agreed
- You often cannot tell what a material can do just by looking at it. You have to test it. *How we know:* A thick towel looks as if it could stop water, but water soaks into it. Thin polythene looks weak, but it keeps water out. Tests show both.
- Rubber is elastic because it is made of very long, curled-up chains of tiny particles. *How we know:* Scientists have studied the long chains with special tools. When rubber is stretched, the chains line up, and X-rays have shown this.
- Most paper is absorbent because it is made of tiny plant fibres with gaps between them. *How we know:* Under a microscope, paper looks like a flat mat of tiny threads. Water creeps along them and fills the gaps.

## How we found out
**The Olmec, Maya and Aztec rubber makers; Charles Goodyear (1839); Dorothy Hosler, with Sandra Burkett and Michael Tarkanian (1999), About 1600 BCE in Mexico; 1839; 1999.** For a long time, many people in Europe and America believed that useful rubber was invented in 1839. Raw rubber went sticky in summer and hard and cracked in winter. In 1839, an American named Charles Goodyear heated rubber with sulfur. It stayed stretchy in heat and cold. But people in Mexico and Central America had made bouncy, long-lasting rubber more than 3,000 years before that. The Olmec, Maya and Aztec peoples made rubber balls for a ball game. In the 1990s, Dorothy Hosler, an archaeologist at MIT in the USA, wanted to know how. With her students, she collected milky latex from the Castilla rubber tree. They mixed it with juice from a morning glory vine. The mixture became rubber that was much bouncier and stronger than dried latex alone. Later, Hosler and Michael Tarkanian found that different amounts of vine juice made different rubbers: some best for bouncing balls, some best for tough sandal soles. The old rubber makers had been testing materials to fit each job.  
*What people believed before:* That useful, lasting rubber was first made by Charles Goodyear in 1839. Hosler's team showed that the peoples of ancient Mexico had made it more than 3,000 years earlier, by mixing latex with morning glory juice.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A thicker cloth always keeps out more water.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Thick cloth has more material for the water to get through, so it can slow the water down.  
  Against: A thick towel soaks up water and lets it through. A thin polythene sheet or an umbrella cloth keeps it out. What matters most is whether the material has gaps, not how thick it is.
- **Natural materials are always weaker than materials made in factories.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Steel and nylon rope are very strong. Many natural things, like leaves and paper napkins, tear easily.  
  Against: Some natural materials are very strong. Coir rope made from coconut fibre holds boats. Weight for weight, the silk that spiders use for their safety line is stronger than steel.
- **If a material stretches, it will always spring back.** (A common first guess; *rejected*)  
  For: Rubber bands, hair bands and socks stretch and spring back, and these are the stretchy things we use most.  
  Against: A strip of milk packet and a roll of atta dough stretch and stay stretched. Even a rubber band stays a little longer if it is stretched too far for too long.

## Roots
We have tested which materials stretch, keep water out and hold weight. Now we ask what the stretch test tells us about materials, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Under the bottle, the rubber band stretched long, then went back to its old length. The milk packet strip we pulled stayed long and thin. Both stretched, yet only one came back. So to know a material, we must also watch what it does after the pull stops.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think 'stretchy' is one thing, and that a material we have stretched is the same afterwards. The test showed two stretchy materials doing opposite things once the pull stopped.  
*Evidence:* In the stretch test, the rubber band grew about 7 cm with the bottle and went back to its old length. The milk packet strip, pulled by hand, got thin and white and stayed long. Inside rubber, long curled chains straighten and curl back. In the plastic, the chains slide to new places and stay.

- *who am i:* Your skin is elastic. You bend your knee, and the skin stretches. You straighten it, and the skin goes back.
- *what is life:* A rubber tree makes latex, and latex seals a cut in the bark. Living things make the materials they need.
- *what is this world:* Every material does something different when it is pulled, wetted or loaded. We only find out by testing.

Rubber, cotton, paper and polythene are all made of tiny particles, joined in different ways. Long curled chains let rubber spring back. Short, loose fibres let a napkin tear. Everything is conscious, the rubber tree too. When the tree is cut, latex flows out and dries to seal the cut.

- **Tirukkural 423:** Whatever you hear, and whoever says it, wisdom is seeing what is really true in it. (A shopkeeper may say a bag is the strongest. The Tirukkural, from Tamil Nadu, asks us to find out what is true. A fair test is one way to do that.)
- **Tao Te Ching 78:** Nothing in the world is softer than water. Yet nothing is better at wearing away what is hard and strong. (In the spot test, soft water found its way through paper and cloth. Only materials with no gaps kept it out.)
- **Jain teaching of anekantavada, many-sidedness, and the story of the blind men and the elephant:** Each blind man touched one part of the elephant and thought he knew the whole animal. Each was partly right. (Each of our tests showed one side of a material. Milk packet plastic kept water out, but it stayed stretched and did not go back. To know a material, we need many tests.)
- Science (settled): Rubber is made of very long chains of tiny particles. Stretching straightens the chains, and they curl back when let go. This is why rubber is elastic.
- Science (open): Weight for weight, spider silk is one of the strongest materials known. Scientists are still trying to make a material as good in a factory.
- Other view, Many physicists: They explain a rubber band without any mind. The chains are always jiggling, and jiggling chains tend to settle in curled shapes, so a stretched band is pulled back. They do not use awareness to explain it.
- Practice, Stretch and come back (3 min): Stand up tall. Stretch your arms up high, as far as they go. Feel your body get longer. Hold it. Now let go, slowly. Feel your arms come back to your sides. Breathe in slowly, and your chest gets bigger. Breathe out, and it goes back. Your body stretches and comes back all day long.
- Ask: If something stretches and stays stretched, is it still the same material?
- Ask: Which parts of you stretch and come back?
- Ask: Is there one test that tells you everything about a material?

## Story: The Band That Came Back
*Rubber trees (Hevea brasiliensis) are grown widely in Kerala, especially around Kottayam. Tappers cut a thin slanting groove in the bark early in the morning, and white latex drips into a cup. Latex left on skin dries into a thin rubbery film. Meenu, Susheela and Joseph are made up.*

Meenu was seven. In the school holidays, she went to stay with her aunt Susheela in a village near Kottayam, in Kerala. Behind the house grew rows and rows of tall rubber trees.

Her cousin Joseph was there too. He was nine, and he liked to say he knew everything.

On the first morning, Meenu's hair band broke. It was an old one. When she pulled it, it did not spring back. It just stayed long and loose, and then it snapped.

"Your band is finished," said Joseph. "Rubber gets old. Throw it away."

Meenu held the two broken ends. "Why did it stop springing back?" she asked.

Aunt Susheela laughed. "Come with me early tomorrow," she said. "I will show you where rubber comes from."

The next morning it was still dark and cool. Aunt Susheela took a small curved knife and a torch. They walked between the rubber trees. Each tree had a slanting line cut in the bark, and a coconut-shell cup tied below it.

Aunt Susheela shaved a very thin strip of bark off the line. Slowly, white drops came out. They ran down the groove and dripped into the cup.

"That is latex," said Aunt Susheela. "The tree makes latex to seal a cut, the way your blood seals a cut on your knee. We take a little each morning."

Meenu touched a drop. It was cool and sticky. She rubbed it between her finger and thumb. After a while, the white had turned into a thin, clear skin. She pulled at it. It stretched. She let go. It sprang back.

"It is rubber already!" said Meenu.

"Of course," said Joseph. "Everything soft is stretchy. Look." He picked up a piece of the polythene sheet that covered the firewood. He pulled it. It got longer.

"But does it come back?" asked Meenu.

Joseph let go. The polythene stayed long. It had gone thin and white in the middle. It did not come back at all.

Joseph frowned. "That is the same thing," he said. "It stretched."

"No," said Meenu. "The rubber stretched and came back. Your plastic stretched and stayed. They are different. We have to test them properly."

Back at the house, Meenu found a ball of string, a ruler and an old water bottle. She filled the bottle with water. She tied a piece of new rubber band, a strip of polythene and a strip of cotton from an old towel to the window bars, one beside the other.

"Every one gets the same bottle," she said. "Then it is fair."

She hung the bottle on the rubber band. The band stretched long, nearly double. Joseph measured it with the ruler. She took the bottle off. The band went back to its old length.

Then the polythene strip. It stretched a little and stayed a little longer. Then the cotton strip. It hardly moved at all.

"So the cotton is strongest and not stretchy," said Joseph slowly. "The rubber band is stretchy and comes back. The polythene stretches and stays."

"Now you know three things," said Aunt Susheela. "And you did not guess them. You tested them."

Meenu looked at her old hair band. "So why did my band stop coming back?"

"Rubber that is very old, or left in the hot sun, changes," said Aunt Susheela. "Sunlight and air slowly break the long chains inside it. Then it cannot spring back, and it snaps."

Meenu thought about the trees, making their latex every morning, sealing their cuts. She thought about her band, slowly getting old.

That evening she wrote in her notebook: "Do not believe it. Test it." Joseph read it over her shoulder. For once, he did not say he knew already.

**For the parent:** Two materials can both stretch and still be very different. You only find out what a material does by testing it.

- Ask: What did the rubber do that the polythene did not?
- Ask: How did Meenu make her test fair?
- Ask: Why did Aunt Susheela say the old hair band snapped?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Stretch Test: which material stretches the most? (24 min)
- **question:** Which material stretches the most when the same bottle hangs from it? And when the bottle comes off, which ones go back to their old length?
- **predict:** Pull each strip gently with your fingers. Then put them in a line, from the one you think will stretch the most to the one that will stretch the least. Why did you put your first one first?
- **fair test:** Every strip gets the same full bottle, hung the same way. Each strip is 2 cm wide. We wait the same time, 10 seconds, before we measure. We use the same ruler, and measure from the broom handle to the paper clip every time. The rubber band is not 2 cm wide and starts shorter than the strips, so we compare how many centimetres longer each one gets.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the strips. Lower the bottle gently, and keep feet away from under it, because a strip may tear. Keep faces away from a stretched rubber band.
- **record:** Write your guess and your reason at the top. Fill in the results table. Draw each strip before and with the bottle. Circle the ones that came back.
- **results table:** Material | Length before (cm) | Length with the bottle (cm) | How much longer (cm) | Length after the bottle comes off (cm) | Did it come back?

  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the rubber band and the three strips. 2. Let your child pull each one gently with their fingers. 3. Ask the question below. 4. Write down their order and their reason, word for word.

  *Child mode:* Pull each one gently. Which will stretch the most? Put them in a line. Say why.

### e2 · The Spot Test: which materials keep water out? (20 min)
- **question:** Which of these materials are waterproof, and which let water through?
- **predict:** Feel each square. Which ones do you think will keep the water out? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every square is the same size. Every square gets one level teaspoon of water, poured in the middle, one after the other. We lift all of them after the same 2 minutes. The papers underneath are all the same, with the same kind of sketch pen circle.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Stick the six papers in your notebook, in two groups: dry and wet. Write the name of the material next to each.
- **results table:** Material | My guess: waterproof? | Did the water stay on top or soak in? | Did the circle under it run? | Waterproof?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Help your child draw a circle with a sketch pen on each of the six papers. 2. Lay the papers on a tray. 3. Put one square of material on each paper.  
  *Parent says:* If water gets through a square, it will wet the paper and make the sketch pen run. That is how we will know.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a circle on each paper. Put one square on each.
  - Note: Water-based sketch pens run when wet, so a wet paper is easy to see. Do not use a ball pen or a permanent marker. If you have no banana leaf, a cabbage leaf from the market works.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Let your child feel each square. 2. Ask the question below. 3. Write their guess for each one in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which squares will keep the water out? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Feel each square. Which will keep the water out? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The foil is shiny and smooth, so water will stay on top.; The hanky is cloth, and water goes into my clothes in the rain.; The napkin will soak it up, because we use napkins to wipe spills.)
  - Note: Don't correct the guesses. Many children think thick things keep water out. The test will show them.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour one level teaspoon of water slowly onto the middle of each square, one after the other. 2. Watch the water on each one. 3. Start the clock after you pour the last square.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the water. On which squares does the water sit on top as a round drop? On which does it soak in?  
  *Child mode:* Pour one spoon of water on each square. Watch. Does it soak in or stay on top?
  - Ask: What is the water doing on the foil? (good: It stays in a round drop.; It sits on top and rolls.)
  - Note: On the napkin and newspaper the water soaks in at once. On the hanky it soaks in more slowly. On foil, milk packet and leaf it sits as a drop.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Wait 2 minutes. 2. Lift each square carefully, without tipping its water onto the paper. 3. Look at the circle underneath. 4. Write what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Now lift each one carefully. Look at the circle. Did the water get through?  
  *Child mode:* Lift each square slowly. Look at the circle. Is it dry or has it run?
  - Ask: Which circles are still dry? (good: Under the foil, the milk packet and the leaf.; The circles under the newspaper, napkin and hanky have run.)
  - Note: Tip the water off the waterproof squares into the tray first, so it does not drip onto the paper as you lift. If a circle is only a little damp, count it as wet: water got through.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to sort the squares into two heaps: waterproof and not waterproof. 2. Ask what they saw and why. 3. Then read the card 'Waterproof and absorbent'.  
  *Parent says:* Sort them into two heaps. Which keep water out? Which let it through? Was your guess right? Why do you think the water got through the hanky?  
  *Child mode:* Make two heaps: waterproof and not waterproof. Was your guess right?
  - Ask: Why did water get through the hanky? (good: Because cloth has tiny gaps.; Because cotton soaks up water and passes it on.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses what your child saw: 'The circle under the foil stayed dry, so foil is waterproof.'
  - Check: Thara, a girl in Class 2, wants to keep her notebook dry on a rainy walk to school. What should she wrap it in? → **A piece of milk packet**

  - Ask: Why? (good: The cloth, because my clothes don't tear.; The napkin will tear first, because it is thin.)
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**

Keep adding one cup at a time, and count. 5. Stop when it tears or the bottle is full. 6. Do the same for each strip.

  - Note: The napkin usually tears after only a few cups, often at the tape or the clip. The newspaper, carry bag and cloth may all hold the full bottle. If they do, write 'held the full bottle'. 4. (draw) Draw the rubber band before the bottle, with the bottle, and after the bottle came off.  
   **Answer:** Three pictures: a short band, a long band with a bottle, and a short band again.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the band getting longer with the bottle and going back to the same length after. That is what elastic means.
5. (truefalse) You can always tell if a material is waterproof just by looking at it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A thick cotton towel may look as if it keeps water out, but it soaks water up. We have to test.

   **Answer:** It got longer and thinner, went white in the middle, and stayed long.. *Why:* A strong answer says both parts: it stretched, and it did not come back.

   **Answer:** ['umbrella: waterproof', 'towel: absorbent', 'raincoat: waterproof', 'sponge: absorbent']. *Why:* An umbrella and a raincoat keep water out. A towel and a sponge soak it up.

## Open questions
- **Why does rubber get old, cracked and stop springing back?** Scientists know sunlight, heat and air slowly break the long chains. But exactly how fast this happens in each kind of rubber is still being studied, so makers keep testing new ways to make rubber last.
- **How do spiders make silk that is so strong and so stretchy at once?** Scientists have studied spider silk for many years. They still cannot make a material in a factory that is as good as the silk spiders make.

## Connected maths
- In the stretch test, the rubber band was 6 cm long. With the bottle it was 13 cm. How many centimetres longer did it get? **7 cm longer.**. Count on from 6 to 13: 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13. That is 7 steps. So the band got 7 cm longer. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L2`)
- In the stretch test, the T-shirt strip was 10 cm long at the start. After the bottle came off it was 11 cm. How much longer than at the start was it? **1 cm longer.**. 11 is more than 10. 11 take away 10 is 1. So the strip stayed 1 cm longer than it was at the start. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.meaning.L2`)
- Bornali, a girl in Class 2, tested 8 squares in the spot test. 5 kept water out. How many let water through? Her friend Fathima found only 3 waterproof squares. Who found more waterproof squares, and how many more? **3 let water through. Bornali found 2 more waterproof squares than Fathima.**. 8 take away 5 is 3. 5 is more than 3, and 5 take away 3 is 2. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- In the bottle lift, the napkin tore after 3 cups. Every cup holds 50 ml. How much water was in the bottle when it tore? **150 ml.**. 3 cups of 50 ml: 50, 100, 150. So the napkin held 150 ml of water, as well as the bottle. (queues `math.measurement-basics.standard-units.L2`)
- Look at the chart from the bottle lift. Which strip held the most cups? Which held the fewest? **The cloth held the most, 10 cups, the full bottle. The napkin held the fewest, 3 cups.**. The tallest bar is the cloth, at 10. The shortest bar is the napkin, at 3. The newspaper and the carry bag are in between. (queues `math.data.pictographs.L2`)
