# Where does the log go? How dead things return to the air and the soil
`sci.ecology.cycles-and-decomposers.L4`

**Summary.** A coconut palm falls in a storm, and a few months later the trunk is soft, then crumbly, then gone. Where did it go? You test a jar of soil with limewater and find that the soil breathes out a gas. You chop carrots to see why small pieces rot first, and you grow methi in rotted stuff to see if it feeds a new plant. You learn that matter goes round and round, while energy flows through and leaves as heat. You also meet the vultures of India, and what happened when a painkiller took the cleanup crew away.

**Intro.** *A big coconut palm falls in a storm. Nobody moves it. Come back in two years, and the trunk is gone. There is no heap of wood and nobody carried it away. Where did all that tree go?* A palm trunk weighs more than a hundred kilograms. It cannot just vanish. Some of it went into the soil. Some of it went somewhere you cannot see. In this unit you catch the invisible part in a jar, you find out why small pieces rot faster than big ones, and you grow a plant in rotted leaves to see who gets the rest.

## Terms
- **Decomposer**: A living being, such as a fungus or a bacterium, who feeds on dead things by digesting them and soaking up the food. *e.g.* The white threads of a mould growing through a fallen branch are decomposers at work.
- **Detritivore**: An animal who eats bits of dead plants and animals and breaks them into smaller pieces. Detritus is the word for dead bits. *e.g.* An earthworm pulls a dead leaf into the soil and eats it. A millipede and a woodlouse do the same.
- **Scavenger**: An animal who finds and eats dead animals, or leftover food, that she did not kill herself. *e.g.* A vulture eats a dead cow. A crow picks at rice dropped on the road.
- **Enzyme**: A tiny tool made by a living being that cuts food into pieces small enough to soak up. *e.g.* A mould pours enzymes onto a piece of bread, and the bread softens as it is cut up.
- **Respiration**: The way every living being gets energy from food. The food is broken down using oxygen, and carbon dioxide and water come out. *e.g.* You breathe out carbon dioxide because your cells are doing respiration. Soil microbes do the same.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas with no colour or smell. It is made when living things get energy from food, and when things burn. Plants take it in to make food. *e.g.* Limewater turns milky when carbon dioxide mixes with it.
- **Nutrients**: The simple materials, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, that plants soak up from the soil with water and use to build their bodies. *e.g.* Rotted leaves release nutrients into the soil, and the next plant's roots drink them in.
- **Humus**: The dark, soft, crumbly part of the soil that is left when dead plants and animals have been partly broken down. *e.g.* The black crumbly soil under a heap of old leaves is rich in humus.
- **Leaf litter**: The layer of fallen leaves, twigs and other dead bits that covers the ground under trees. *e.g.* In a forest, the leaf litter is full of millipedes, beetles, worms and fungus threads.
- **Surface**: The outside of a thing, where it meets the air, the water or other living things. Cutting a thing up makes more surface. *e.g.* A grated carrot has far more surface than a whole carrot of the same weight.
- **Matter cycle**: The path that the same atoms follow round and round, from the air to a plant, to an animal or a decomposer, and back to the air or soil. *e.g.* A carbon atom in your breath may become part of a leaf, then a caterpillar, then the soil, then the air again.
- **Energy flow**: The one-way path of energy through living things. It comes from the Sun, passes along food chains, and ends as heat that spreads out and is not used again. *e.g.* The warmth of a compost heap is energy from sunlight, once stored in leaves, now leaving as heat.

## Explanations
### Three cleanup crews: scavengers, detritivores and decomposers · *defined*
**Definition:** Dead things are cleared by three crews. Scavengers eat whole dead animals and leftovers. Detritivores chew dead plants and animals into small bits. Decomposers, mainly fungi and bacteria, digest the bits with enzymes and soak up the food.

Each crew does a different job, and they work in order. A dead cow is found first by vultures and crows, who are scavengers. Flies lay eggs on what is left, and the grubs eat the soft parts. Then beetles and other small animals chew the rest into bits. A dead leaf follows the same order. A millipede or an earthworm is a detritivore. She chews the leaf, and the bits she leaves behind are small. Fungi and bacteria then cover those small bits, pour enzymes on them and soak up the food. The three crews need each other. Without detritivores, decomposers have only the outside of a big leaf to work on. Without decomposers, the bits of leaf would pile up. No crew is the boss. Together they turn a dead body back into soil and air.
- e.g. Vultures and crows clear a dead animal first, and are scavengers.
- e.g. Earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, termites and dung beetles chew dead things small, and are detritivores.
- e.g. Moulds, mushrooms and soil bacteria digest the small bits, and are decomposers.
### Rotting is a slow, cool kind of burning · *defined*
**Definition:** Decomposers get energy from dead food by respiration. They use oxygen to break the food down, and they give out carbon dioxide, water and a little heat. A fire does the same thing quickly and hot.

When wood burns, the carbon in the wood joins with oxygen from the air and becomes carbon dioxide. The energy comes out as heat and light. When a fungus eats wood, the same thing happens, only slowly. The fungus pours enzymes on the wood and soaks up the sugars. Inside her body, she joins the sugars with oxygen and gets energy. Carbon dioxide and water leave. So a rotting log is breathing out, all the time, into the air. This is why a jar of damp soil with a little peel turns limewater milky. The soil is breathing. A big pile of rotting leaves can even feel warm, because all that tiny breathing gives out heat. Burning and rotting turn the same dead plant into the same gases. One takes minutes. The other takes years.
- e.g. Limewater above damp soil turns milky, because soil microbes breathe out carbon dioxide.
- e.g. A heap of wet grass cuttings feels warm in the middle, from the heat of respiration.
- e.g. A candle, a fire and a rotting log all join carbon with oxygen to make carbon dioxide.
### Where does the log go? Mostly into the air · *defined*
**Definition:** When a dead plant is decomposed, a large share of its carbon is breathed out by the decomposers as carbon dioxide. The rest becomes the bodies of decomposers, humus in the soil, and nutrients taken up by new plants.

A tree builds its wood mostly from carbon dioxide taken from the air, as you learned in the photosynthesis unit. So when the wood rots, much of it goes back the way it came. Decomposers breathe out the carbon as carbon dioxide, and the rest of the matter leaves as water vapour. In many studies of fallen leaves in warm, wet places, a large share of the carbon is breathed out within a year or two. What stays behind is partly the bodies of the fungi and bacteria, and partly dark, crumbly humus. The minerals in the wood, such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, dissolve in rain and sink into the soil. A root can then drink them. So the tree does not disappear. Part goes up into the air, part goes down into the soil, and plants take both back again.
- e.g. A rotting palm trunk loses weight mostly as carbon dioxide and water vapour.
- e.g. A compost heap shrinks to a small fraction of the weight of the kitchen scraps that went in.
- e.g. Banana plants beside a rotting trunk grow with the minerals it releases.
### Matter goes round, energy goes through · *defined*
**Definition:** The atoms in living things are used again and again, so matter moves in cycles. Energy is not used again. It comes from the Sun, passes along food chains, and leaves as heat.

Earth gets very few new atoms. The carbon in your body has been in plants, animals and the air before. A decomposer does not make new carbon. She only moves it on. Energy is different. A leaf holds energy from sunlight. A caterpillar eats the leaf and uses the energy to move and grow, and some leaves as heat. A fungus eats the caterpillar's droppings and the rest of the energy leaves as heat. Heat spreads into the air and into space, and no living thing can use it again. So life on Earth stays alive because the Sun keeps sending new energy, while the atoms keep going round. Decomposers are where the loop closes: they send the atoms back so plants can start again.
- e.g. The same carbon atoms pass from air to leaf to caterpillar to soil and back to air.
- e.g. The Sun's energy passes along the food chain and ends as heat. It does not go back to the leaf.
- e.g. A compost heap stays warm for weeks because stored energy is leaving as heat.
### Why small pieces rot faster: decomposers work on surfaces · *defined*
**Definition:** Decomposers can only work on the outside of their food. The more surface a food has, the more room they have to work, so small pieces rot faster than big pieces of the same weight.

A fungus cannot go inside a solid lump. She pours enzymes on the outside and soaks up what dissolves. A whole carrot has a small outside. Cut it into small cubes, and every cut makes new surface. A cube 4 centimetres on each side has a surface of 96 square centimetres. Cut it into 64 cubes of 1 centimetre, and the surface becomes 384 square centimetres. It is four times as much, with the same amount of carrot. This is why earthworms, millipedes and termites matter. They chew leaves into bits, and every bit gives fungi and bacteria new surface to work on. It is also why we chop vegetables for compost, and why a pile of whole logs takes much longer to rot than a pile of sawdust.
- e.g. Grated carrot rots in a few days. A whole carrot lasts weeks.
- e.g. Sawdust rots much faster than a log of the same weight.
- e.g. Worms and millipedes speed up rotting by shredding leaves.
### How dead things feed the next plant · *defined*
**Definition:** Decomposers release the minerals locked in dead things into the soil water. Plant roots drink these nutrients and use them to build new leaves, stems and seeds.

A dead leaf contains nitrogen in its proteins. Soil bacteria and fungi break the proteins apart and release the nitrogen as ammonium. Other soil bacteria change ammonium into nitrate, which dissolves in water, and a root can soak it up. Phosphorus and potassium are released in a similar way. This is why a rotted heap makes a rich soil, and why a farm field needs nutrients put back when the crop is carried away and not left to rot. It is also why the nutrients in many tropical rainforests are mostly held in the living trees. The soil is often poor, but the leaf litter rots so fast in the warm, wet air that the nutrients go straight back into roots. If the forest is cleared, the nutrients are lost.
- e.g. Methi seeds grow greener in soil with rotted compost than in plain cocopeat.
- e.g. Farmers put back dung and crop leftovers so the soil keeps its nutrients.
- e.g. A rainforest recycles its nutrients so fast that the soil below can look poor.
### Fast and slow cycles: heat, water and air decide · *defined*
**Definition:** Decomposers work fastest when it is warm, wet and airy. They work slowly when it is cold, dry or flooded with no air. So the same leaf can rot in months in one place and last thousands of years in another.

In a warm, wet forest, such as the Western Ghats in the monsoon, a fallen leaf may be mostly gone within a few months. In a cold northern forest, it can take years. In a peat bog, the ground is full of water and has almost no air, so most decomposers cannot breathe. Dead plants pile up there. Over thousands of years they turn into peat, and over millions of years, when buried deep, peat can turn into coal. In a desert, there is too little water, so a dry dead branch can stay for decades. That is why a desert and a bog both keep old things safe. Rotting is not a clock that ticks the same everywhere. It is a living process, and it runs on warmth, water and air.
- e.g. A leaf in a warm, wet forest rots in months, and the same leaf in a cold forest can take years.
- e.g. A peat bog preserves plants for thousands of years because there is almost no air.
- e.g. A dry wooden door frame in a desert can last for decades.
### Termites and fungus farms: decomposers work in teams · *defined*
**Definition:** Many decomposers work in partnership. Some termites keep microbes in their guts that digest wood. Other termites grow gardens of fungus, feed it on chewed plants, and eat the fungus.

Wood is hard to digest, because it is made of tough fibres. No termite makes the right enzymes for all of it. Some kinds of termites carry tiny microbes in their guts that do the work. In India, the termites called Odontotermes build tall mounds and keep a fungus garden inside. The workers bring chewed plants, the fungus, called Termitomyces, grows on them, and the termites eat the fungus. In the rainy season, the fungus sends up mushrooms from the mound or the ground close by. People in many parts of India collect and eat these mushrooms. In both cases, the partnership digests what neither partner could digest alone.
- e.g. A termite is a detritivore with decomposers living in her gut.
- e.g. Odontotermes termites grow a fungus garden inside their mound.
- e.g. Termite mushrooms are collected and eaten in the rainy season in several states of India.
### When the cleanup crew is missing · *debated*
**Definition:** Dead bodies and dung get cleared by a crew of scavengers and detritivores that have been fitted to the job for millions of years. If the crew is removed, or if new things arrive that the crew cannot eat, the leftovers pile up.

By the 1960s, Australia had millions of cattle, and they produced huge amounts of dung. The local dung beetles were fitted to the dry pellets of kangaroos and could not use it, so the dung dried into hard pads that covered the grass and bred flies. The scientist George Bornemissza brought in dung beetles from Africa and Europe to bury it. In India, in the 1990s and 2000s, more than 95 of every 100 vultures of three kinds died after eating cattle treated with the painkiller diclofenac. Carcasses lay longer in the open. Scientists agree that the loss of vultures changed how dead cattle were cleared. They disagree about how much it raised the number of street dogs and the risk of rabies. And plastic is a third case: it is so new that almost no decomposer can eat it yet.
- e.g. Australian cattle dung piled up until African and European dung beetles were brought in.
- e.g. Vultures in India crashed by more than 95 percent in about ten years.
- e.g. Plastic bags stay for hundreds of years because few microbes can digest them.
- **Some studies of India:** With fewer vultures, more dead cattle were left for street dogs. Dog numbers went up, and researchers estimated more dog bites and more risk of rabies in people.
- **Other scientists:** Dog numbers also depend on food waste, vaccination and many other things. It is hard to show how much of any rise in rabies came from the loss of vultures alone.

## Mostly agreed
- Fungi and bacteria digest dead plants and animals with enzymes, and give out carbon dioxide, water and heat. *How we know:* Soil, leaf litter and compost heaps give off carbon dioxide that can be measured, and compost heaps warm up.
- Most of the carbon in a rotting log or leaf goes back to the air as carbon dioxide. *How we know:* Scientists measure the carbon dioxide leaving the soil and the weight lost by leaves in mesh bags left on forest floors.
- Matter cycles on Earth, while energy flows through living things and ends as heat. *How we know:* The atoms in a food chain can be tracked from plants to animals to soil, and the heat given off at each step can be measured.
- Warm, wet, airy places rot things fastest. Cold, dry or waterlogged places rot things slowly. *How we know:* Leaf bags buried in different climates lose weight at different speeds, and peat bogs preserve plants for thousands of years.
- Breaking a food into smaller pieces makes it rot faster. *How we know:* Chopped and ground plant material loses weight faster than whole pieces in the same conditions.
- Plastics decompose extremely slowly, because few microbes make enzymes that fit them. *How we know:* Plastic in soil and water is found decades later. A few microbes, such as Ideonella sakaiensis found in Japan in 2016, can digest one kind of plastic, PET, but slowly.

## How we found out
**Vibhu Prakash, Lindsay Oaks, Rhys Green and many other scientists in India, Pakistan and Britain, 1990s to 2006.** In the 1990s, bird watchers in India noticed that vultures were vanishing. Vultures had always cleared the bodies of dead cattle and wild animals. At Keoladeo National Park in Rajasthan, Vibhu Prakash of the Bombay Natural History Society counted nests each year, and the numbers kept falling. By the early 2000s, more than 95 of every 100 vultures of three kinds were gone. Many scientists first thought an infectious disease was spreading from bird to bird. Then Lindsay Oaks and his team tested dead vultures in Pakistan. The birds' kidneys had failed, and they had eaten cattle treated with a painkiller called diclofenac. Their paper in Nature in 2004 showed that the drug was the cause. India banned diclofenac for cattle in 2006.  
*What people believed before:* People first thought that an infectious disease was killing the vultures. The team showed that the birds were being poisoned by a painkiller in the dead cattle they ate, and that a vulture's kidneys fail within days of eating it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **When something rots, it simply goes away.** (Many people, and many young children.; *rejected*)  
  For: A heap of leaves does shrink, and a banana peel does become a thin brown smear.  
  Against: Nothing is destroyed. Most of the matter goes into the air as carbon dioxide and water vapour. The rest becomes humus and nutrients in the soil, and the bodies of the decomposers.
- **Lush forests must grow on rich soil.** (A common idea, from the look of the trees.; *rejected*)  
  For: A thick, green forest looks as if it must be well fed.  
  Against: Many tropical rainforests grow on poor soil. Their nutrients are held mostly in the living trees and the fast-rotting leaf litter. When the forest is cleared, the nutrients wash away and the soil turns poor.
- **Coal exists because fungi had not yet learned to digest wood.** (Floudas and colleagues, in the journal Science, 2012.; *debated*)  
  For: Coal came mostly from plants that lived about 300 million years ago. Their study found that fungi able to digest lignin, the toughest part of wood, appeared around that time, and coal formation fell afterwards.  
  Against: Other scientists point out that coal stopped forming for other reasons too, including changes in climate and the shape of the continents. The question is still being tested.
- **Bioplastics that are labelled biodegradable will rot like a banana peel anywhere.** (Some makers of bioplastic packaging.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some plastics are made from plants such as corn, and can be digested.  
  Against: Many need the steady heat of an industrial compost plant, about 58 degrees Celsius. In a garden, a river or the sea, they can last for years.
- **Vultures, hyenas and flies are dirty, and the world is better without them.** (A common feeling.; *rejected*)  
  For: They eat rotten meat and carry germs on their bodies.  
  Against: They are the first crew in the cleanup. When India lost most of its vultures, carcasses lay in the open for longer. Scientists link the loss to more feral dogs, though they disagree about how much.

## Roots
We have followed a dead log into the air and the soil. Now we ask what the cleanup crews tell us about waste, and about what we are made of.

**Deep truth.** The soil in the jar breathed out carbon dioxide, and the limewater above it went milky. Tiny decomposers eat dead leaf and turn it into gas and into food for plants. In a forest, every leftover is someone's meal. 'Waste' is only what nobody has learned to eat yet.  
*Habit it breaks:* We split the world into useful things and waste, as if some things were made to be thrown away. A dead leaf, a dropped peel and a fallen palm look like rubbish. They are food for the next being, and the split only appears where there is no one to eat what is left.  
*Evidence:* In the Breathing Soil test, the jar with soil and peel turned limewater milky, and the jar with only air did not. In Cut It Up, the finely grated carrot was eaten first. Plastic stays because few decomposers have the enzymes for it.

- *who am i:* The carbon in your body was in the air, in plants and in other animals before it was in you, and it will move on again.
- *what is life:* Life is made of eating and being eaten. A fungus is as much a part of life as a lion, and she is the one who sends the atoms back for the next plant.
- *what is this world:* The world is a loop of matter powered by the Sun. Every leaf has been in the soil, the air and a body many times already.

A leaf is air and water made into a body by sunlight. When the leaf dies, a fungus or a worm unmakes it, and the same atoms go back into the air and the soil. A new leaf then makes the atoms into a body again. No part of the loop is the real beginning, and nothing in it is waste. Each body is a place where the same atoms rest for a while. The thing that keeps the loop going is the Sun, whose energy is passed along and ends as heat.

- **Bhagavad Gita 3.14:** From food, living beings come into being. Food comes from rain, and rain comes from giving and sharing. (The verse names a loop of food, rain and living beings. The soil jar shows part of that loop, where dead food turns back into the gas and minerals that plants use.)
- **Tao Te Ching 16:** All things grow and flourish, and then each returns to its root. Returning to the root is called stillness. (A leaf grows from the soil and the air, and returns to them. The cycle is a return to the root.)
- **Genesis 3:19:** From dust you were made, and to dust you will return. (Many traditions say it in their own words. The science says it as a count of atoms: the carbon and the minerals in a body come from the soil and the air, and they go back.)
- Science (settled): Matter is not made or lost when things rot. The atoms of carbon, nitrogen and the other elements are only moved from body to body, to the soil and the air.
- Science (debated): Underground networks of fungus threads may carry sugars and signals between trees. Scientists still argue about how common this is and how much it matters for a forest.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India: A person is only matter in a certain arrangement, and when the arrangement falls apart nothing remains. The body goes back to the elements, and there is nothing more to say. A view rooted in oneness can sit with this: it agrees on the atoms, and asks whether anything else is going round besides the atoms.
- Practice, Where does this breath go? (3 min): Sit still and breathe in slowly. Feel the air going in. Now breathe out slowly, and think that the carbon dioxide in this breath was once part of your breakfast. Imagine a leaf somewhere taking it in. Do this three times. Then say quietly, where will this carbon be tomorrow?
- Ask: If the same atoms keep going round, what part of you is really yours?
- Ask: Is a dead leaf rubbish, or a meal that nobody has eaten yet?
- Ask: Why do you think we call some things waste, and who gets to decide?
- Ask: If a fungus covers nine square kilometres, is she one being or many?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Nothing in a forest is called waste, but a city makes a lot of it. What does a forest do with its leftovers that a city does not? (hint: Think about who is waiting to eat each leftover.)
- *big:* The smell of rot makes you hold your nose. To a carrion fly or a dung beetle, it is the smell of dinner. Is the smell in the rotting thing, or in the nose that smells it? Are we smelling differently, or smelling different things? (hint: Think about the fruit that a bee sees as ultraviolet and you see as red.)
- *deep:* The carbon in your last breath may once have been in a leaf, a log, or a dinosaur. In what way is it yours? (hint: Think about how long you hold on to the atoms of your body.)
- *big:* Energy goes through living things once and ends as heat. How can life keep going for billions of years if energy is not used again? (hint: Where does the next supply of energy come from?)
- *deep:* Plastic stays because nothing has learned to eat it yet. Will something learn? What would that mean? (hint: Think about how long leaves have been around, and how long plastic has.)
- *deep:* A mushroom is only the fruit of a fungus. The rest of the fungus is a net of threads in the soil, and in Oregon one such net covers nearly ten square kilometres. Where does one fungus end? (hint: Think about what you would count as one living being.)
- *deep:* A tall tree in a rainforest grows in poor soil. Where does her food come from? (hint: Think about what lands on the ground every day.)
- *big:* Every atom in your body was once part of something else. Does that make you new, or old? (hint: Think about how long the atoms have been around, and how long you have.)

## Story: The Palm That Stayed
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. A fallen trunk is eaten by termites, grubs and fungi. Most of its matter goes into the air as gas, and the rest goes into the soil, where other plants use it.*

The storm came on the first night of the monsoon. By morning, the yard in Alappuzha was full of torn leaves, and the tall coconut palm by the gate was lying across the path. Meenakshi's grandfather stood over it with his hands on his hips. He did not say anything for a long time.

He had planted that palm the day Meenakshi's mother was born. Thirty-six years she had given coconuts, shade and a place for the crows to quarrel. Now it was a long grey log with a crown of broken fronds.

"I will get Raju to cut it up," said Appooppan. "We can burn the pieces. A dead tree is only a dead tree."

Meenakshi was nine, and she had a notebook with a green cover. She looked at the trunk. It was as thick as her waist and very hard. "Can we wait?" she asked. "Just until Onam. I want to see what happens to it."

"Nothing happens to a log," said Appooppan. But Ammoomma, from the kitchen door, said, "Let the child look." So they made a bargain. The log would stay until Onam.

On the first day, Meenakshi drew the trunk and pushed a thin stick into the bark. It went in the width of her thumbnail. She wrote: "Hard. Ants on the cut end."

After two weeks of rain, the stick went in as far as her first finger joint. The bark had gone dark and damp. Under it she found a thin white web, like cotton thread. She also found a fat, cream-coloured grub, curled up like a comma. A line of mud tubes ran up the side of the trunk, where termites were walking inside, out of the light. She wrote: "The log is full of people."

"Of course it is," said Ammoomma, bringing her a glass of buttermilk. "Everything dead is somebody's dinner."

By the fifth week, the stick slid in as far as her wrist. The cut end of the trunk had turned soft and brown, and orange shelves, like little ears, grew out of the bark. The air near the log smelled of wet earth. A small toad had moved in beneath it. Meenakshi dug out a handful of the wood, and it crumbled in her fingers like a stale biscuit.

She weighed it on the kitchen scale. Then she dried it in the sun for a day and weighed it again. The wood was light. "It is hardly there," she said to herself. "But where did the heavy part go?"

She asked Ammoomma, and Ammoomma only smiled. "What do you think? You were the one who said you wanted to see."

That night, Meenakshi lay awake with the rain on the roof. She thought of the grub chewing, and the white threads, and the mushrooms. All of them were breathing. She remembered a jar from school, where a spoon of soil had turned clear water milky. Maybe the heavy part had gone into the air, a little at a time, through all those tiny breaths.

The week before Onam, Appooppan came out with a cup of tea and found her sitting beside the log. He was grumbling about his banana plants. "The soil is tired," he said. "Every year the bunches get smaller, and every year I buy more fertiliser."

Meenakshi looked at the banana clump next to the log. Then she looked at the other clump, near the well. The leaves beside the log were as wide as umbrellas and dark green. The leaves by the well were pale and thin.

"Appooppan," she said. "Come and look." She pushed the spade into the ground under the old trunk. The earth there was black and crumbly, and full of white threads and small worms. It smelled sweet. She took a handful to him.

He rubbed it between his fingers for a long time. "This is better soil than I have anywhere in the yard," he said.

"It is the palm," said Meenakshi. "It did not go away. Some of it went up into the air, a little at a time, because the grubs and the fungus were breathing it out. The rest went down into the soil. The banana roots found it."

Appooppan looked at the log, and at the banana leaves, and at his granddaughter. "So it is not gone," he said slowly. "It has moved."

"The air part will come back too," said Meenakshi. "The leaves take it in."

He laughed, which he had not done since the storm. He did not call Raju. When Onam came, the log stayed, and a year later, a small coconut seedling pushed up through the soft wood where the trunk had been. Appooppan watered the seedling every morning, and showed everyone who visited where the old palm had gone.

**For the parent:** A dead trunk is not lost. Most of it goes into the air as gas, a little at a time, and the rest goes into the soil, where other plants use it.

- Ask: Why did the stick go in further each week?
- Ask: Where did the heavy part of the log go?
- Ask: What would have happened to the banana plants if the log had been burned and carried away?

### e1 · The Breathing Soil: what comes out of a jar of soil? (40 min, 2 days)

  *Parent says:* Pot A has only cocopeat. Pot B has cocopeat and rotted compost. Pot C has garden soil. Which pot will grow the best methi? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three pots. Which will grow the best methi? Why?
  - Note: Cocopeat holds water but has almost no nutrients. Vermicompost is made by worms and microbes from dead plant matter. Do not correct the guess.
- **s2 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the pots. Plant 8 methi seeds about 1 centimetre deep in each. Water each pot with the same amount, about half a cup. Put all three on the same window ledge.

  *Child mode:* Fill the pots. Plant 8 seeds in each. Water all three the same.
  - Note: Methi seeds sprout in 2 to 4 days. Keep the soil damp, not soaked. The seeds hold their own food for the first week, so the differences between the pots only show clearly after about 10 days. Be patient.
  - Check: Why do all three pots get the same amount of seeds, water and light? → **So only what the seeds grow in is different**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On days 4 and 8, water the pots equally and measure the height of the plants in each. Write down the colour, too. Look again on day 14.

  - Note: Use the average of the three tallest plants. Expected by day 14: pot A is pale or yellowish and short, pot B is tall and deep green, and pot C is in between or similar to B. If all three look the same, the seeds' own food may still be carrying them, so wait a few more days.
- **s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 14, measure and draw a bar chart of the average heights. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a bar for each pot. Tell me what you thought, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each pot. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the compost holds nutrients from dead leaves and other waste, released by decomposers, and the plants drink them. After they have explained, say that a methi plant has just done what a banana did in the story of the palm.
  - Think: Where did the nutrients in the vermicompost come from, before they were in the compost?

## Challenges
### c1 · The Rot Race: make the fastest-rotting jar (35 min)
- **problem:** You have 100 grams of fresh vegetable peels, two tablespoons of soil and one jar. Set up the jar so the peels soften and darken as fast as you can in 5 days.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the peels. Open the jars outdoors and do not sniff them. Anyone with asthma or a mould allergy should not open a fuzzy jar. Wash hands after.
- **your turn:** Sketch your first jar and say why each choice should make the peels rot faster. Set up jar 1. Change one thing, such as the size of the pieces or the warmth, in jar 2. After 5 days, which jar rotted more? Why?
- **constraints:** Use only the peels, the soil, the jar and things from your kitchen: water, a knife, paper and a lid.; No chemicals, no heating, and no adding of ready-made compost.; Change only one thing from your first jar at a time, so you can tell what helped.; After 5 days, score each jar from 0 to 5 for how soft, dark and crumbly the peels are.
- **general:** Chop the peels small, because small pieces have more surface for decomposers.; Keep the peels damp, not soaked, because decomposers need water but also air.; Put the jar in a warm place, because decomposers work faster when it is warm.
- **creative:** Mix in shredded dry paper to let in air, and so the jar does not turn slimy.; Open the lid for a minute each day to let in fresh air.; If you have a garden, add a spoon of soil from under a heap of old leaves, so that more decomposers start the work.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. Look at the constraints. Ask your child to sketch a plan for the first jar and say why each choice would help.  
  *Parent says:* You want the peels to rot as fast as possible. What do decomposers like? How could you give them more of it? Sketch your plan first.  
  *Child mode:* What do decomposers like? Sketch your jar plan.
  - Ask: What would make the decomposers work faster? (good: Smaller pieces.; More warmth.; Some water.; Some air.)
  - Note: Let your child use what they learned from the Cut It Up and Breathing Soil tests. Do not give the answer.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Build jar 1. Chop the peels, mix in the soil, add a little water, and close the lid. Build jar 2 with one change. Put both jars on a warm shelf. Write down what you changed.  
  *Parent says:* Build your first jar. Then build a second jar with just one change. Write down what the change is, so we can tell what helped.  
  *Child mode:* Build jar 1. Build jar 2 with one change. Write the change.
  - Note: Open the jars outdoors, and do not put your nose inside. If a jar goes slimy and sour, add dry paper. Slimy jars have too little air, and sour smells show that different bacteria are at work.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* After 5 days, open the jars and score each from 0 to 5 for how soft, dark and crumbly the peels are. Compare the two jars. Ask which change helped most.  
  *Parent says:* Which jar rotted more? What was the one change? Why do you think it helped?  
  *Child mode:* Score both jars. Which rotted more? Which change helped?
  - Note: Common results: chopping the peels small and adding some dry paper help the most. Too much water makes the jar slimy. A strong answer names the change and the reason. This is a quick version of a compost heap.
  - Think: What would you change next to make the peels rot even faster?

### Cleanup Crews and Breathing Soil (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) An animal chews dead leaves into small bits. Which crew is she in?  
   Options: Detritivore / Decomposer / Scavenger  
   **Answer:** Detritivore. *Why:* A detritivore eats bits of dead plants and animals and breaks them smaller. A decomposer digests food outside her body. A scavenger finds dead animals to eat.
2. (fill) Fungi and bacteria that digest dead things with enzymes are called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['decomposers', 'decomposer']. *Why:* Decomposers pour enzymes on dead matter and soak up the food. Their work turns dead things back into gas and soil.
3. (truefalse) A fungus chews her food in a mouth, like an earthworm.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A fungus has no mouth. She pours enzymes on her food, and the enzymes cut it into pieces small enough to soak up.
4. (match) Match each living being to the crew she belongs to.  
   **Answer:** Vulture → Scavenger; Earthworm → Detritivore; Mould → Decomposer; Dung beetle → Detritivore. *Why:* A vulture finds and eats dead animals. An earthworm and a dung beetle chew dead matter small. A mould digests food outside her body.
5. (sort) Sort each carrot into whether it rots faster or slower, when the carrots weigh the same.  
   **Answer:** grated carrot → Rots faster; whole carrot → Rots slower; carrot cut into small cubes → Rots faster; carrot kept cold in the fridge → Rots slower; carrot in a warm, damp box → Rots faster; dry carrot slice on a hot window ledge → Rots slower. *Why:* More cut surface gives decomposers more room to work, and warmth and water help them. A whole, cold or dry carrot gives them less.
6. (fill) The gas that made the limewater go milky was ____ ____.  
   **Answer:** ['carbon dioxide']. *Why:* Limewater reacts with carbon dioxide to make tiny white bits of chalk. Soil microbes breathe it out as they eat dead plants.
7. (mcq) In the Breathing Soil test, jar C had only air and limewater. Why was it there?  
   Options: To show what limewater does with no soil / To make the test look bigger / To hold the soil  
   **Answer:** To show what limewater does with no soil. *Why:* A check jar shows what happens without the thing you are testing. Jar C stayed clear, so the gas in the other jar came from the soil.
8. (write) Jar B, in the fridge, turned the limewater less milky than jar A in the warm room. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The cold slowed down the decomposers. They breathe out less carbon dioxide when they are cold, so less limewater turned milky.. *Why:* A strong answer says that decomposers are living, and cold slows them down. They still eat, but slowly.
9. (order) Put these in order, for what happens to a dead leaf on the forest floor.  
   **Answer:** ['The leaf falls and lies on the ground', 'A millipede chews the leaf into small bits', 'Fungi and bacteria digest the bits', 'Nutrients sink into the soil and a root drinks them']. *Why:* Detritivores come before decomposers, because they make the surface that the decomposers work on. The nutrients reach a root last.
10. (truefalse) When a log rots, most of it goes back into the air as gas.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The carbon is breathed out as carbon dioxide by the decomposers, and much of the rest leaves as water vapour. Only a small part stays as humus.
11. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Earthworm, millipede, termite, mould.  
   **Answer:** Mould, because she is a decomposer who digests outside her body. The other three chew dead things small.. *Why:* Earthworms, millipedes and termites are detritivores. Mould is a decomposer, who works with enzymes.
12. (draw) Draw the path of a carbon atom from the air to a leaf, to the soil, and back to the air.  
   **Answer:** A circle of arrows: air to leaf, leaf to soil and decomposers, decomposers to air.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that the atom returns to the air as carbon dioxide breathed out by decomposers, so the path is a loop.
13. (count) Meenakshi's palm log weighs 100 kilograms. About half of a dry log is carbon. How many kilograms of carbon are in the log?  
   **Answer:** 50. *Why:* Half of 100 is 50. As the log rots, much of that carbon is breathed out by decomposers as carbon dioxide.
14. (mcq) Which sentence is true about energy in a food chain?  
   Options: It goes through once and ends as heat / It goes round and round like carbon / It is used up and disappears  
   **Answer:** It goes through once and ends as heat. *Why:* Energy passes along the chain and leaves as heat. It is not used again. It does not disappear, because the heat spreads out into the air.
15. (perspective) Two friends look at a rotting banana peel on the road.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side, says why, and uses a fact such as: the peel is a meal for decomposers, or it can carry germs, so wash hands.. *Why:* Both views are true in a way. The peel is a meal for the cleanup crew, and it can carry germs that make people ill. The idea of waste depends on who is looking.
### Matter, Energy and Missing Crews (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A 4 centimetre cube is cut into 64 cubes of 1 centimetre. What happens to its surface?  
   Options: It becomes 4 times bigger / It stays the same / It becomes 64 times bigger  
   **Answer:** It becomes 4 times bigger. *Why:* The big cube has a surface of 96 square centimetres. The 64 small cubes have 384 square centimetres in all. 384 divided by 96 is 4.
2. (fill) The word for tiny jobs done by tools made by a fungus, to cut food into small pieces, is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['enzymes', 'enzyme']. *Why:* Enzymes are tools made by a living being that cut food into small pieces. A fungus pours them onto her food.
3. (truefalse) Rotting and burning make the same gas, carbon dioxide.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Both join carbon from a plant with oxygen. Burning is fast and hot, and rotting is slow and cool.
4. (mcq) Why do earthworms and millipedes speed up rotting?  
   Options: They make small bits, so fungi have more surface to work on / They kill the fungi / They add water to the leaf  
   **Answer:** They make small bits, so fungi have more surface to work on. *Why:* Chewing makes many small bits, and each bit has surface that decomposers can use. A wrong pick often comes from thinking that the animals do all the work.
5. (match) Match each place to what happens to a dead leaf there.  
   **Answer:** Warm wet rainforest → Gone within months; Cold northern forest → Lasts for years; Peat bog with no air → Piles up for thousands of years; Hot dry desert → Dries out and lasts for decades. *Why:* Decomposers need warmth, water and air. When one is missing, they work slowly or not at all.
6. (sort) Sort each of these into whether it goes round in a cycle or flows through and ends.  
   **Answer:** carbon atoms → Goes round; nitrogen atoms → Goes round; the energy from the Sun → Flows through and ends as heat; water → Goes round; the energy in a goat's food → Flows through and ends as heat. *Why:* Atoms and water are used again and again. Energy is not used again after it has become heat.
7. (write) Many rainforests have poor soil but huge trees. Use the idea of fast nutrient cycling to explain how.  
   **Answer:** Fallen leaves rot very fast in the warm, wet air. The nutrients go straight back into the roots of the trees. Most of the nutrients are held in the living trees, so the soil looks poor.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the nutrients are in the trees and the leaf litter, not in the soil, and that decomposers return them quickly.
8. (mcq) In the 1960s Australia had a problem with cattle dung. What was the main reason?  
   Options: Local dung beetles could not use cattle dung / Cattle made too little dung / There was no rain  
   **Answer:** Local dung beetles could not use cattle dung. *Why:* The local beetles were fitted to the dry pellets of kangaroos. The dung dried into hard pads. Dung beetles from Africa and Europe were brought in to bury it.
9. (order) Put these in the order they happened, in the story of the Indian vultures.  
   **Answer:** ['Vultures cleared dead cattle in a few hours', 'Cattle were given the painkiller diclofenac', 'Vultures that ate the cattle died of kidney failure', 'India banned diclofenac for cattle in 2006']. *Why:* The vultures were not killed by a disease, but by the drug in the cattle they ate. The ban was the final step.
10. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Glass bottle, plastic bag, banana peel, steel spoon.  
   **Answer:** Banana peel, because decomposers can eat it. The other three have no cleanup crew.. *Why:* A banana peel is made by a plant, so decomposers have the enzymes to digest it. Glass, plastic and steel are not made by living things.
11. (count) A scientist counts 500 vultures in a park in the year 1995. In 2005 she counts 20. How many vultures are gone, and how many out of every 100 is that?  
   **Answer:** 480 gone, which is 96 out of 100. *Why:* 500 take away 20 is 480. 480 out of 500 is the same as 96 out of 100, because 480 divided by 5 is 96.
12. (draw) Draw two pictures of a rotting log. In the first, show which gas goes up into the air. In the second, show which materials go down into the soil.  
   **Answer:** Log with carbon dioxide arrows going up and water vapour. Log with minerals, humus and nutrients going down to a root.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows both ways out. The gas goes to the air, and the minerals go to the soil. A plant will take both back.
13. (label) Label this picture: a mushroom on a log. Point to the part that is the fruit and the part that is the fungus's main body.  
   **Answer:** The mushroom is the fruit above the log. The fungus's main body is the net of threads, called mycelium, inside the log.. *Why:* The mushroom is only the part that makes spores. Most of the fungus is the hidden net of threads in the wood.
14. (perspective) A town is deciding what to do about the dead cattle left on its edge.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side, gives a reason, and names something to find out, such as whether the cattle were treated with a drug that is harmful to vultures.. *Why:* Both plans have a point. Vultures clear carcasses fast, but only if the meat is safe for them. The diclofenac story shows why.
15. (mcq) A dry log in a desert lasts for decades, but the same log in a rainforest is gone in a few years. What does this show?  
   Options: Rotting depends on warmth, water and air / Desert wood is stronger / Rainforest wood is softer  
   **Answer:** Rotting depends on warmth, water and air. *Why:* The same kind of wood rots at different speeds depending on whether decomposers have water, warmth and air.
16. (write) A farmer cuts her wheat and carries every straw away for years. Her soil gets poorer. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The wheat took nutrients from the soil. When all the straw is carried away, the nutrients never return, because there is nothing left to rot into the soil.. *Why:* A strong answer names the nutrients, says they are taken up by plants, and points out that the loop is broken when the plants never rot in the field.

## Applications
- Composting kitchen waste and garden leaves at home, in a school or in a housing society.
- Vermicomposting, where earthworms speed up the rotting of waste into rich soil.
- Setting farm fields so that straw and dung go back to the soil, instead of being burned.
- Mulching: spreading dead leaves around plants to keep the soil damp and feed it slowly.
- Sewage treatment, where bacteria are used to break down waste in water before it returns to a river.
- Biogas plants, where microbes digest cow dung without air and make a gas that can be burned for cooking.
- Peat and coal, which are made from dead plants that could not fully rot.
- Forensic science, where the order in which insects arrive on a dead body helps tell how long it has been there.
- Scientists studying microbes that can digest plastic, such as the bacterium Ideonella sakaiensis found in Japan.
- Protecting vultures, for example by banning diclofenac and breeding vultures in safe places.

## Open questions
- **Which microbes could one day digest plastic, and could they do it fast enough to matter?** A few microbes with enzymes that cut one kind of plastic have been found. Nobody yet knows if they can be used on the scale of the world's waste.
- **How much do underground fungus networks really share between trees?** Some studies find sugars and signals moving between trees, and others find little. Scientists are still testing how common and how important it is.
- **Did the rise of wood-digesting fungi end the age of coal?** The dates fit, but changes in climate and the shape of the continents could also be causes. It has not been settled.
- **How many kinds of decomposers live in one spoon of soil?** Most soil microbes have never been grown in a lab. Scientists estimate there are thousands of kinds in a spoon, but the count is still changing.
- **How much did the loss of vultures raise rabies in India?** Some studies estimate a large effect, but dog numbers and rabies depend on many things. It is hard to separate the vultures' part.
- **Will more of the carbon in soil be breathed out as the world warms?** Warmer soil makes decomposers faster, which could release more carbon dioxide. Scientists are still measuring how large this effect will be.

## Connected maths
- A carrot cube is 4 centimetres on each side. It has 6 faces. Each face is 4 times 4 square centimetres. How many square centimetres is the surface of the whole cube? **96 square centimetres.**. Each face is 4 times 4, which is 16 square centimetres. The cube has 6 faces. 6 times 16 is 96. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L4`)
- The same cube is cut into 64 small cubes, each 1 centimetre on each side. Each small cube has a surface of 6 square centimetres. How many square centimetres is the surface of all 64 small cubes together? How many times bigger than the big cube's surface is that? **384 square centimetres, which is 4 times bigger.**. 64 times 6 is 384. The big cube had 96. 384 divided by 96 is 4. So the same carrot has 4 times as much surface when it is cut small. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In the Cut It Up test, the grated carrot scored 3 on day 3. The chopped carrot scored 3 on day 5. The whole carrot scored 3 on day 9. How many times longer did the whole carrot take than the grated one? How many days longer? **3 times longer, which is 6 days longer.**. 9 divided by 3 is 3, so 3 times as long. 9 take away 3 is 6, so 6 days longer. These are made-up numbers, and yours will differ. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A pile of leaf litter weighs 80 grams. Every month, half of what is left is eaten. How many grams are left after 3 months? After how many months is less than 10 grams left? **10 grams after 3 months. Less than 10 grams after 4 months.**. 80 halves to 40, then 20, then 10. After 3 months, 10 grams are left. Half of 10 is 5, so after 4 months less than 10 grams are left. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A scientist counts 1000 vultures in a district. Ten years later she counts 30. How many have gone? How many out of every 100 is that? These are made-up numbers. **970 have gone, which is 97 out of every 100.**. 1000 take away 30 is 970. 970 out of 1000 is the same as 97 out of 100, because 970 divided by 10 is 97. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- A dry log has a mass of 100 kilograms. About half of it is carbon. A fungus breathes out 3 kilograms of that carbon in the first year. How many kilograms of carbon are left in the log? What fraction of the carbon is that? **47 kilograms are left, and 3 out of 50 has gone.**. Half of 100 is 50 kilograms of carbon. 50 take away 3 is 47. 3 out of 50 is the fraction that has gone, which is 6 out of 100. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Three jars of limewater, A, B and C, are checked at the same time. Jar A turns milky in 1 day. Jar B turns milky in 3 days. Jar C never does. Put the jars in order, from the most gas to the least gas, and say what the order tells you. **A, then B, then C. Jar A has the busiest decomposers, and jar C has none.**. The quicker the limewater turns milky, the faster the gas is made. Jar A made it fastest, jar B slower, and jar C none. So jar A's soil is the busiest, and jar C is the check. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L4`)
- A methi plant in pot A is 4 centimetres tall after 14 days. The plants in pot B with compost are 11 centimetres tall. How many times taller are the plants in pot B? What does this suggest? These numbers are made up. **A bit less than 3 times taller (11 divided by 4 is about 2.75). It suggests that the compost fed the plants.**. 11 divided by 4 is 2 with 3 left over, so about 2.75. Three times would be 12, so it is a bit less. The extra growth is a hint that the compost nutrients helped, though one test is not proof. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
