# Shadows: what happens when something blocks the light
`sci.light.shadows.L2`

**Summary.** Hold your hand in front of a torch, and a dark patch with the shape of your hand appears on the wall. That patch is a shadow. It forms because light travels in straight lines and cannot go through your hand. In this unit your child finds out what a shadow needs, which side it falls on, and why the same toy can make a tiny shadow or a giant one. They also make shadow puppets and put on a shadow play, like the puppeteers of Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Odisha.

**Intro.** *Switch on a torch and hold your hand in front of it. A dark hand appears on the wall. Where did that dark hand come from?* Hold your hand still between the torch and the wall. Now take your hand away. The dark hand is gone. Put your hand back, and it comes back. The dark hand on the wall is called a shadow. In this unit we find out what makes a shadow, and how to make one grow, shrink, split in two and tell a story.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say that a shadow forms where something blocks light.
1. By the end I can name the three things a shadow needs: a light, something that blocks it, and a surface for the shadow to fall on.
2. By the end I can show that a shadow grows when the thing moves nearer the light, and shrinks when it moves nearer the wall.
3. By the end I can say which side a shadow falls on: the side away from the light.
4. By the end I can count the shadows that two lights make, and say why there are two.
5. By the end I can make a shadow puppet, use it to tell a story, and name a shadow-puppet play from India.

## Terms
- **Light source**: A light source is something that gives out its own light. *e.g.* The Sun, a torch, a tube light and a lit diya are light sources.
- **Shadow**: A shadow is the darker patch where light cannot reach, because something is in the way. *e.g.* At lunch, there is a shadow on the floor under the dining table.
- **Block**: To block light means to stop it from getting through. *e.g.* Your hand blocks the torch light, so a hand-shaped shadow appears on the wall.
- **Opaque**: An opaque thing lets no light through. Opaque things make dark shadows. *e.g.* A steel plate, a book and your hand are opaque.
- **Travels in straight lines**: Light goes from the torch in straight lines. It does not bend round a thing that is in its way. *e.g.* That is why your hand's shadow has the same outline as your hand.
- **Outline**: The outline is the line all round the edge of a shape. *e.g.* A shadow shows only the outline of a thing, not its colour or its face.
- **Sharp shadow**: A sharp shadow has a clear, crisp edge. *e.g.* Hold your hand close to the wall, and its shadow is sharp.
- **Fuzzy edge**: A fuzzy edge is a soft, blurred edge round a shadow. Scientists call this soft edge the penumbra. *e.g.* Hold your hand close to the torch, and the edge of its shadow goes fuzzy.
- **Shadow puppet**: A shadow puppet is a flat cut-out figure on a stick. You move it in front of a light, and people watch its shadow. *e.g.* A cardboard crow on an ice-cream stick makes a crow shadow on the wall.
- **Screen**: In a shadow play, the screen is a thin white cloth or paper. The light is behind it, and the people watching sit in front. *e.g.* In Kerala, tholpavakoothu puppeteers use a long white cloth as their screen.

## Explanations
### What a shadow is · *defined*
**Definition:** A shadow is the darker patch where light cannot reach, because something blocks it.

Light comes out of the torch and lands on the wall. When your hand is in the way, the light that hits your hand stops there. The part of the wall behind your hand gets no light from the torch. That part looks dark. Nothing dark comes out of your hand. The shadow is simply the place the light could not reach.
- e.g. A dark hand on the wall when a torch shines on your hand.
- e.g. The dark patch under a table at lunch.
- e.g. The cool shade under a tree or an umbrella on a sunny day.
### A shadow needs three things · *defined*
**Definition:** To make a shadow you need a light, something that blocks the light, and a surface for the shadow to fall on.

Switch the torch off, and the shadow is gone. Take your hand away, and the shadow is gone. Shine the torch at an open window at night, and there is no wall for the shadow to fall on, so you see none. All three are needed at once: a light, a thing in the way, and a surface behind it.
- e.g. No light: switch off the torch and the hand shadow vanishes.
- e.g. No blocker: take your hand away and the wall is all lit.
- e.g. No surface: shine the torch out of a window into the night sky, and you see no shadow.
### Light travels in straight lines · *defined*
**Definition:** Light travels in straight lines, so it cannot bend round a thing that is in its way.

Light from the torch goes out in straight lines in many directions. The lines that pass just by the edge of your hand go straight on to the wall. The lines that hit your hand stop. So the edge of the shadow follows the edge of your hand. That is why a shadow has the outline of the thing that made it, but no colour and no face.
- e.g. A shadow of a cup has the outline of the cup.
- e.g. A shadow of your hand shows the gaps between your fingers.
- e.g. A shadow of a fork shows each of its prongs.
### Nearer the light, bigger shadow · *defined*
**Definition:** When a thing moves nearer the light, its shadow gets bigger. When it moves nearer the wall, its shadow gets smaller, until it is about the same size as the thing.

Light spreads out from a torch, like a cone. Close to the torch, the cone is narrow, so a small toy blocks a big part of it. That blocked part spreads out all the way to the wall, so the shadow is big. Close to the wall, the light has already spread wide, and the toy blocks only a small part, so the shadow is small. The toy itself stays the same size the whole time.
- e.g. A toy about as tall as your finger made a shadow about as tall as a ruler when it was a ruler's length from the torch.
- e.g. In a shadow play, a puppeteer pulls a puppet back towards the lamp to make the shadow grow huge.
### Sharp shadows and fuzzy shadows · *defined*
**Definition:** A shadow is sharpest when the light is small and the thing is close to the surface. It gets a fuzzy edge when the light is big or the thing is close to the light.

A torch is not a single point. Light comes from all over its bright part. Near the edge of a shadow, your hand hides some of that bright part but not all of it. So that part of the wall gets a little light and looks grey, not dark. That grey ring is the fuzzy edge, the penumbra. A tiny phone torch makes sharper shadows than a big torch.
- e.g. Your hand pressed close to the wall makes a sharp shadow.
- e.g. Your hand close to the torch makes a big shadow with a fuzzy edge.
- e.g. On a cloudy day, the whole sky is the light, so shadows are soft or hard to see.
### Which side a shadow falls on · *defined*
**Definition:** A shadow always falls on the side of the thing that is away from the light.

Light comes from the torch and stops at the toy. The place that misses out is behind the toy, on the far side from the torch. Move the torch to the left, and the shadow swings to the right. Lift the torch high above the toy, and the shadow becomes short and sits close round the toy's feet. If you know where a shadow is, you can work out where the light is.
- e.g. The torch on the left: the shadow falls on the right.
- e.g. A light on the ceiling: the shadow of a chair is under the chair.
- e.g. The Sun in the east in the morning: shadows point to the west.
### Two lights, two shadows · *defined*
**Definition:** Each light makes its own shadow of a thing. Two lights make two shadows.

Each torch sends its own light, and the toy blocks some light from each one. So there is one shadow for each torch. Each shadow is pale, not dark, because the other torch still lights it. Where the two shadows overlap, no light from either torch gets in, so that part is darkest. Switch one torch off, and its shadow disappears.
- e.g. A chair in a room with a tube light and a lamp has two shadows.
- e.g. Players on a cricket ground at night often have four shadows, one from each tall tower of lights.
- e.g. In a corridor with many ceiling lights, you can see a shadow of yourself under each light.
### Is a shadow a thing? · *debated*
A shadow has a shape and a size. You can draw round it and measure it. Yet it is made of light that is missing. People see this in different ways.
- e.g. You can measure your toy's shadow with a ruler.
- e.g. Switch off the light, and the shadow is gone at once, with nothing left behind.
- **The Mohists, thinkers in China about 2,300 years ago:** A shadow is the place where light is blocked. It does not really travel along with you. Where the light comes back, the old shadow is gone, and a new one forms in the new place.
- **Scientists today:** A shadow is a place that gets less light than the places round it. It is not a thing made of something, but its edge and size follow exact rules, so it can be measured.
### Living beings that sense shadows · *defined*
**Definition:** Many living beings sense shadows and change what they do.

A plant in the shade of other leaves senses the shade. The light under leaves has less red light, and plants feel that change. The plant then grows a longer stem to reach the sunlight. Giant clams in the sea pull in quickly when a shadow passes over them. A shadow can mean a fish is coming close. In Africa, a bird called the black heron spreads her wings into an umbrella over the water while she fishes.
- e.g. A tulsi plant on a crowded windowsill grows taller and thinner than one with space round them.
- e.g. A pigeon on the balcony rail may fly off when your shadow falls over them.
- e.g. The black heron fishes in the shade of her own wings.
- **Scientists who study how plants behave:** A plant feels shade and chooses to grow taller. Plants sense the light round them and decide how to grow.
- **Some other scientists:** They call the plant's growth an automatic response, set off by light-sensing parts called phytochromes.
### Shadow puppets in India and the world · *defined*
**Definition:** A shadow play tells a story with the shadows of flat puppets, moved between a light and a screen.

India has many shadow plays. In Kerala, tholpavakoothu puppeteers in Palakkad work behind a long white cloth, with a row of oil lamps behind them, and tell the Ramayana over many nights. In Andhra Pradesh it is called tholu bommalata, in Karnataka togalu gombeyaata, and in Odisha ravanachhaya. The puppets are cut from leather. In 1926, a German artist called Lotte Reiniger made a whole film with shadow puppets cut from thin card, The Adventures of Prince Achmed. It is the oldest full-length animated film that still survives.
- e.g. Tholpavakoothu in Kerala.
- e.g. Tholu bommalata in Andhra Pradesh.
- e.g. Ravanachhaya in Odisha, with puppets of plain leather that make dark shadows.
- e.g. Wayang kulit in Indonesia.

## Mostly agreed
- Light travels in straight lines, so a thing in its way makes a shadow with the same outline. *How we know:* Every shadow you make has the outline of the thing in front of the light. Light passing through a small hole into a dark room makes a straight beam you can see in dusty air.
- A thing makes a bigger shadow when it is nearer the light, and a smaller one when it is nearer the surface. *How we know:* You can measure it with a toy, a torch and a ruler. Shadow puppeteers have used this for hundreds of years.
- Each light makes its own shadow, so two lights make two shadows. *How we know:* Switch on two torches and count the shadows. Switch one off, and one shadow goes.
- Eclipses are shadows. In a lunar eclipse the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, and in a solar eclipse the Moon's shadow falls on the Earth. *How we know:* Astronomers can work out exactly when the shadows will fall, years ahead, and the eclipses come on time.

## How we found out
**The Mohists, followers of the thinker Mozi in China, About 2,300 years ago (the 4th to 3rd centuries BCE).** Long ago, in many lands, people thought a shadow was a part of a person, like a dark copy that followed them. Some thought it was even a part of their soul. About 2,300 years ago in China, the followers of a thinker called Mozi watched shadows closely. They are called the Mohists. They wrote short notes about light, in a book now called the Mohist Canon. One note says that two lights make two shadows. Another says that the size of a shadow changes with how far the thing is from the light. A third says that a shadow does not really move. Where the light comes back, the old shadow is gone, and a new one forms in the new place. These notes fit a simpler idea than a dark copy. A shadow is the place where a thing blocks the light. A copy of you would not double, grow or vanish. You can check each of the Mohists' notes yourself, with a torch and a toy.  
*What people believed before:* That a shadow is a dark copy of a person, or even part of their soul, that follows them about. Watching how shadows double under two lights, and grow and shrink with distance, showed that a shadow is only where light is blocked.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A shadow is a dark copy of you that follows you around.** (An old idea in many lands, and a common first idea of young children; *rejected*)  
  For: Your shadow has your shape, and it moves when you move.  
  Against: Two lights give you two shadows. Your shadow grows and shrinks as you move nearer or further from the light. Switch the light off and it is gone at once. A copy of you would not double, grow or vanish like that.
- **A shadow is completely black, with no light in it at all.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Shadows look dark, especially next to bright light.  
  Against: You can still see the floor inside a shadow. Light bounces off walls, ceilings and the sky into the shadow. Outdoors, shadows look a little blue, because light from the blue sky fills them.
- **Clear glass makes no shadow at all.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Most light goes through clear glass, so you can see through it.  
  Against: A glass tumbler of water makes a pale shadow with bright lines in it. Glass sends a little light back and bends some of it, so the patch behind it is not quite as bright.
- **The black heron spreads her wings to make shade, so she can see the fish in the water.** (Bird scientists who watch black herons in Africa; *debated*)  
  For: Shade cuts out the bright glare on the water, so it is easier to see into it.  
  Against: Some scientists think the shade also tricks fish, because small fish swim into shady places to hide. It may do both jobs. Nobody is sure which matters more.

## Roots
We have made shadows grow, shrink, split in two and tell a story. Now we ask what the Near or Far test tells us about shadows, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Your toy stayed the same size, yet its shadow grew as you moved it nearer the torch. A shadow is the patch the toy keeps light from reaching. Near the torch, the toy blocks a wider spread of the light. So a shadow's size is set by where the toy stands, as much as by the toy.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a shadow as a dark copy that belongs to a thing, with the thing's own size. But the same toy made a small shadow and a huge one. The size came from the toy's place between the light and the wall.  
*Evidence:* In the Near or Far test, the toy's shadow was only a little taller than the toy near the wall, and about three times as tall near the torch. The toy did not change at all.

- *who am i:* You are something light cannot pass through. Wherever you stand in the light, you leave a patch of shade behind you.
- *what is life:* A plant feels the shade of a neighbour and grows taller to reach the light. Living beings sense shadows and act on them.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of light and of the shapes that block it. Night itself is your side of the Earth in the Earth's own shadow.

One torch and one toy made a small sharp shadow, a big fuzzy one, and, with a second torch, two pale shadows. The toy never changed. Each shadow was a different meeting of the same light with the same toy. A shadow is made by the light, the thing and the surface together, and by where each one is.

- **Katha Upanishad 1.3.1:** The Katha Upanishad speaks of two that live together in the body. It says the wise call them shadow and sunlight. (In your tests, a shadow was only ever there because of the light. Shadow and light come as a pair: switch off the torch, and the shadow goes too.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 2:** The Tao Te Ching, an old Chinese book, says that long and short show each other, and high and low lean on each other. (Your toy's shadow was not long or short by itself. It was long or short depending on where the toy stood between the torch and the wall.)
- **Plato, Republic, Book 7 (514a to 520a):** Plato, a Greek thinker, told of people who could see only shadows on a cave wall, and thought the shadows were the real things. (A shadow shows an outline, but not the colour or the true size. In the Near or Far test, a small toy made a giant shadow. You knew the toy, so the shadow could not fool you.)
- Science (settled): Light travels in straight lines through air, so a shadow has the outline of the thing that blocks the light.
- Science (settled): Plants sense the shade of other leaves with light-sensing proteins called phytochromes, and grow longer stems to reach the light.
- Other view, Some philosophers: Some philosophers say a shadow is only a place where light is missing, so it is not anything at all. They say its 'size' is only a way of talking about where the light is.
- Practice, Breathing Shadow (3 min): Sit near a wall with the torch on. Hold one hand up in the light, so its shadow is on the wall. Breathe in slowly, and move your hand towards the torch. Watch the shadow grow. Breathe out slowly, and move your hand back to the wall. Watch it shrink. Do this five times. Your hand stayed the same. What changed?
- Ask: If a shadow is only missing light, why can we still measure it?
- Ask: Your shadow is a different size under every light. Which one is really yours?
- Ask: When you stand in a grown-up's shadow, you make no shadow of your own. Where has your shadow gone?

## Story: Paati's Lamp and the Growing Elephant
*A made-up story. It is true that tholpavakoothu is a shadow-puppet play from the Palakkad area of Kerala, performed behind a white cloth lit by oil lamps, that it tells the Ramayana over many nights, and that a puppet's shadow grows as it moves towards the light.*

Nila lived on the fifth floor of a tall building in Chennai, with her Amma, her Paati and her little brother Arjun. Nila was seven. Arjun was five. Paati was their grandmother. She had grown up in a village near Palakkad, in Kerala.

One evening in May, the fans stopped. The lights went out. The fridge went quiet. "Power cut," said Amma, and she went to find the torch.

Paati did not wait for the torch. She took out a small brass oil lamp, filled it with oil and lit the wick. She set it on the dining table, far from the curtains. A warm yellow flame stood up, still and bright.

Arjun gave a shout and grabbed Nila's arm. "Look! A giant!" On the wall behind Paati there was a huge dark head, with a bun on top, as tall as the door.

Paati laughed and moved her head. The giant moved its head too. "That giant is only my shadow," she said. "My head is in the way of the lamp. The light cannot get to that part of the wall, so it looks dark."

Arjun was not sure. He held his own hand up. A dark hand appeared on the wall. He wiggled his fingers, and the dark fingers wiggled. He hid his hand behind his back, and the dark hand was gone.

"Where did it go?" he asked.

"Nowhere," said Nila slowly. "It did not go anywhere. When your hand moved away, the light just reached the wall again."

Paati nodded. "When I was a girl," she said, "we walked to the temple at night to watch the tholpavakoothu. The puppeteers sat behind a long white cloth. Behind them was a row of little oil lamps, just like this one. They held leather puppets up between the lamps and the cloth. On our side, we saw only the shadows. Rama, Sita, Hanuman and the great king Ravana. They argued and sang and fought, night after night."

"Can we make one?" asked Nila.

Paati found an empty biscuit box. She drew an elephant on the card, with a long trunk and big ears. Nila cut it out carefully with the round-ended scissors. Paati made a small hole with a pencil for the eye, and they taped the elephant onto an ice-cream stick.

Arjun held the elephant up near the wall. A small, sharp elephant appeared, about the size of his hand. A tiny dot of light shone through the eye.

"It is only a baby elephant," said Arjun, disappointed.

"Try moving it," said Paati. "But keep it away from the flame."

Arjun stepped back towards the table, holding the elephant out in front of him. He stopped a long arm's length from the flame. The shadow on the wall grew. Its ears spread wide. Its trunk got longer and longer, until the elephant was taller than Amma, who had come back with the torch.

"It is growing!" Arjun shouted. "Is the elephant getting bigger?"

Nila looked at the card elephant in his hand. It was the same small elephant. Then she looked at the lamp. Its light spread out from the little flame, wider and wider, all the way to the wall.

"The elephant is not growing," said Nila. "When it is close to the lamp, it blocks a lot of the light. That missing light spreads all the way to the wall. So the dark patch is huge. When it is close to the wall, it only blocks a little bit."

Paati smiled. "Shadow puppeteers use the same trick," she said. "When Ravana comes on, angry and proud, they pull him back towards the lamps, and he fills the whole cloth."

Amma switched on the torch and shone it at the elephant from the other side of the room. Now there were two elephants on the wall, one from the lamp and one from the torch. Each one was grey, not black. Where they crossed, there was one dark patch.

"Two lights, two shadows," said Amma.

Arjun counted. "One elephant. Two shadows." He thought for a moment. "So the shadow belongs to the lamp, too. Not only to the elephant."

Then the fans whirred, and the tube light flickered on. The whole wall went bright. The elephant shadows went pale and nearly vanished.

"Oh," said Arjun. "The big light filled them in."

Paati blew out the little lamp. "Tomorrow," she said, "we will make Ravana. With ten heads."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Arjun first thinks the shadow is a thing that goes away and comes back, and that the elephant grows. Nila sees that the shadow is only the place the lamp's light cannot reach, so its size depends on where the puppet is. The second light shows Arjun that a shadow comes from the light and the puppet together.

- Ask: Where did Arjun's hand shadow go when he hid his hand?
- Ask: Why did the elephant's shadow grow, when the card elephant stayed the same size?
- Ask: Why were there two elephant shadows when Amma switched on the torch?
- Ask: Why did the shadows nearly vanish when the tube light came on?

  - Note: A strong answer says that the caller was already blocking the light, so the child was standing in the dark and made no new shadow.

## Experiments
### e1 · Near or Far: when does a shadow grow? (20 min)
- **question:** When a toy moves from the wall towards the torch, what happens to the size of its shadow?
- **predict:** Guess first. Where will the toy's shadow be biggest: next to the wall, halfway, or next to the torch? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same toy, the same torch and the same wall each time. The torch stays in one place. Only the toy moves. We mark every shadow on the same paper, so we can compare them. We try each place twice, because one try can be a mistake.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Leave the three pencil marks on the paper, with a label next to each: wall, halfway, torch.
- **results table:** Where the toy stood | My guess: small, middle or big? | How tall the shadow was (centimetres or finger widths) | Sharp or fuzzy edge?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table on paper, with three rows: near the wall, halfway, near the torch. 2. Show your child the toy. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason for each place.  
  *Parent says:* We will put this toy in three places between the torch and the wall. Where will its shadow be biggest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Where will the shadow be biggest? Near the wall, halfway, or near the torch? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Near the wall, because the wall is where the shadow is.; Near the torch, because it blocks more light.; It will be the same, because the toy stays the same.)
  - Note: Any guess is fine if it has a reason. Many children guess the shadow stays the same size, because the toy does not change. Do not explain yet.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push the table against a plain wall, or work on the floor next to the wall. 2. Tape the two sheets of paper on the wall, one above the other, starting at the table top. 3. Lay the torch on its side on the table, about one metre from the wall, pointing at the paper. 4. Close the curtains and switch off the room lights.  
  *Parent says:* This is our test track. The torch must stay exactly here the whole time. Why do you think that matters?  
  *Child mode:* The torch stays in one place. Only the toy moves.
  - Note: One metre is about two big steps for a child of 7. If you use a phone, prop it against a box so the torch on the back faces the wall. Evening is easiest, with the curtains closed. Keep the torch as low as the table top, so the shadows start at the table.
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one paper strip half a ruler (about 15 centimetres) from the wall. 2. Put one halfway between the torch and the wall. 3. Put one a whole ruler (about 30 centimetres) from the torch. 4. All three go in a straight line between the torch and the wall.  
  *Parent says:* These strips are the three places for the toy. Let us put them in a straight line, so the shadow always lands on the paper.  
  *Child mode:* Put the three strips in a line. Near the wall, halfway, near the torch.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands the toy on the strip near the wall. 2. They look at the shadow on the paper. 3. They make a pencil mark at the top of the shadow. 4. Write 'wall' next to the mark.  
  *Parent says:* Stand the toy near the wall. Look at its shadow. Put a small mark at the very top of the shadow.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the toy near the wall. Mark the top of its shadow.
  - Note: Your child must keep their own hand and head out of the light while they mark, or they will add their own shadow. Marking from the side works best.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Move the toy to the halfway strip, and mark the top of the shadow. Write 'halfway'. 2. Move it to the strip near the torch. Mark the top and write 'torch'. 3. Try each place a second time to check the marks.  
  *Parent says:* Now move the toy to halfway. Is the shadow changing? Now move it near the torch. What do you see? Look at the edge too.  
  *Child mode:* Move the toy to halfway. Mark it. Then near the torch. Mark it. Look at the edge.
  - Ask: What happened to the edge of the shadow near the torch? (good: It went fuzzy.; It was blurry, not sharp.)
  - Note: Near the torch the shadow is much taller, and its edge is soft. Mark the middle of the soft edge. If the shadow goes above the top sheet, tape on a third sheet.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch the room lights back on. 2. Measure each mark from the table top with a ruler, or count finger widths. 3. Write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Let us measure each mark. Which one is tallest? How many times taller is it than the one near the wall?  
  *Child mode:* Measure each mark. Which is tallest? Write the numbers.
  - Note: With a toy about 8 centimetres tall and the torch one metre from the wall, expect about 9 or 10 centimetres near the wall, about 16 halfway and about 27 near the torch. Your numbers will be different, but the pattern will be the same.
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child what they think, what they saw, and why. Then read the card Nearer the light, bigger shadow.  
  *Parent says:* Was your guess right? The toy never changed size. So why did its shadow grow?  
  *Child mode:* Was your guess right? The toy stayed the same. Why did the shadow grow?
  - Ask: Why was the shadow biggest near the torch? (good: Near the torch, the toy blocks more of the light.; The light spreads out, so the blocked part gets bigger on the way to the wall.)
  - Note: A strong answer uses the marks: 'Near the wall the shadow was 10 centimetres, near the torch it was 30. The toy blocked more of the light near the torch.'
  - Check: Meenakshi, a girl of 7, wants a giant shadow of her small toy horse. Where should she put the horse? → **Close to the torch**
### e2 · Two Lights: how many shadows? (20 min)
- **question:** If two lights shine on one toy from different places, how many shadows will it make, and how dark will they be?
- **predict:** Guess first. With two lights on, will the toy have one shadow, two shadows, or none? Will they be darker or paler than with one light? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same toy in the same place the whole time. We keep each light in the same spot. We change only which lights are switched on, so we know what made each change.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw what the wall looked like with both lights on.
- **results table:** Which lights are on | My guess: how many shadows? | How many shadows I saw | Dark or pale?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table with three rows: light A only, both lights, light B only. 2. Ask your child to guess, with a reason.  
  *Parent says:* We have one toy and two lights. When both lights are on, how many shadows will the toy have? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* One toy. Two lights. How many shadows? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: One, because there is one toy.; Two, because each light makes a shadow.; None, because the second light will light up the shadow.)
  - Note: All three guesses are common. Do not say which is right.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the toy on a table, a hand-span from a plain wall. 2. Put light A on a pile of books, a big step from the toy, pointing at it. 3. Put light B a hand-span to one side of light A, also pointing at the toy. 4. Make the room dark.  
  *Parent says:* Let us set up both lights. They must both point at the toy, but from different places.  
  *Child mode:* Two lights. Both point at the toy, from different places.
  - Note: If you use phones, a grown-up handles them. A table lamp works as one of the lights if you take off the shade.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch on light A only. 2. Your child counts the shadows and says how dark the shadow is. 3. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Light A only. How many shadows? Is it dark or pale?  
  *Child mode:* Light A only. How many shadows? Dark or pale?
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch on light B as well. 2. Your child counts the shadows. 3. They look at where the shadows overlap. 4. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now both lights. How many shadows? Are they as dark as before? Look where they cross.  
  *Child mode:* Both lights on. Count the shadows. Look where they cross.
  - Note: You should see two shadows, each a paler grey than before. Where they overlap, it is dark. If the shadows are on top of each other, move one light a little further to the side. If they do not touch at all, move the lights closer together or the toy closer to the wall.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off light A, so only light B is on. 2. Your child says which shadow went away, and whether the other got darker. 3. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* I am switching off light A. Which shadow went away? What happened to the other one?  
  *Child mode:* Light A is off. Which shadow went? Did the other get darker?
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child what they think, what they saw, and why. Then read the card Two lights, two shadows.  
  *Parent says:* How many shadows did the toy have with two lights? Why was each one paler than with one light?  
  *Child mode:* How many shadows with two lights? Why were they paler?
  - Ask: Why were the two shadows pale grey and not dark? (good: The other light shone on each shadow.; Light A lit up light B's shadow.)
  - Note: A strong answer: 'Two shadows, one from each light. Each one was pale, because the other light still shone there. Where they crossed, no light got in, so it was dark.'
  - Check: Jonali in Guwahati has a tube light and a table lamp on in her room. How many shadows does her chair make on the floor? → **Two**

## Worksheets
### Shadows (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Which three things do you need to make a shadow?  
   Options: A light, something to block it, and a surface / A light, a mirror, and some water / Only a dark room  
   **Answer:** A light, something to block it, and a surface. *Why:* A strong answer names all three: a light, something to block the light, and a wall or floor for the shadow to fall on.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a toy's shadow falls on the side of the toy away from the torch.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The light stops at the toy, so the place behind it, away from the torch, gets no light.
3. (draw) Draw a torch, a ball and a wall. Then draw where the ball's shadow falls, and colour it dark.  
   **Answer:** A torch on one side, the ball in the middle, and a round dark shadow on the wall behind the ball.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the shadow on the wall on the far side of the ball from the torch, in a straight line with them.
4. (match) Draw a line from each thing to the shape of its shadow.  
   **Answer:** a steel plate, flat face to the torch → a circle; a book, front cover to the torch → a rectangle; a fork, flat face to the torch → a shape with spikes; an ice-cream cone, held side-on → a triangle. *Why:* A shadow has the outline of the thing, as seen from the torch.
5. (fill) A shadow is the dark patch where light is ___.  
   **Answer:** blocked. *Why:* Something is in the way, so the light cannot reach that patch.
6. (write) Remember the Near or Far test. Say it, and a grown-up writes it down: why did your toy's shadow grow when it was near the torch?  
   **Answer:** Near the torch, the toy blocked more of the light, so the dark patch on the wall was bigger.. *Why:* A strong answer says the toy stayed the same size, and that near the torch it blocked a bigger part of the spreading light.
7. (label) Label the picture: torch, light, toy, shadow, wall.  
   **Answer:** ['torch: where the light comes from', 'light: the yellow lines from the torch', 'toy: the thing in the way', 'shadow: the dark patch on the wall', 'wall: where the shadow falls']. *Why:* These are the parts of every shadow: a light, the light itself, a blocker, the shadow and a surface.
8. (sort) Sort these into: makes a dark shadow, or makes only a very pale shadow.  
   **Answer:** a steel tumbler → Dark shadow; a clean glass window → Very pale shadow; your hand → Dark shadow; a clear polythene sheet → Very pale shadow; a book → Dark shadow. *Why:* Opaque things like steel, books and hands block the light, so they make dark shadows. Clear glass and clear polythene let most light through.
9. (count) Look at the picture. Three toys stand in a row on a table, and one torch shines on all of them. How many shadows are on the wall?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Each toy blocks the light of the torch, so each toy has its own shadow. One torch and three toys make three shadows.
10. (order) Put the toy's shadows in order, from the smallest to the biggest.  
   **Answer:** ['the toy close to the wall', 'the toy halfway', 'the toy close to the torch']. *Why:* The nearer the toy is to the torch, the more light it blocks, so the bigger its shadow.
### Shadows (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (mcq) Lakshmi holds her puppet close to the wall. Its shadow is small and sharp. What happens if she moves it towards the lamp?  
   Options: The shadow gets bigger and fuzzier / The shadow gets smaller and sharper / The shadow disappears  
   **Answer:** The shadow gets bigger and fuzzier. *Why:* Moving towards the lamp, the puppet blocks more of the spreading light, so the shadow grows. Its edge also gets fuzzy.

   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses evidence. Kavya is right that the outline comes from the puppet. Farhan is right that each light makes its own shadow. 3. (write) Remember the Two Lights test. Why did one toy have two shadows?  
   **Answer:** Each light made its own shadow, because the toy blocked some light from each one.. *Why:* A strong answer says one shadow came from each light, and may add that switching one light off made its shadow go.
4. (draw) Draw a puppet with a hole for the eye. Next to it, draw its shadow on the wall. Show what the eye looks like in the shadow.  
   **Answer:** A dark puppet shadow with a bright dot where the eye hole is.. *Why:* The card blocks the light, but the hole lets light through, so the eye shows as a bright dot in the dark shadow.

   Options: A lit diya / The Sun / A tube light / The Moon  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A diya, the Sun and a tube light give out their own light, so they are light sources. The Moon gives out none. We see the Moon because sunlight bounces off it.
6. (truefalse) True or false: a shadow can fall on the ceiling.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A shadow falls wherever something blocks the light and a surface is there. Hold your hand above a torch that points up, and its shadow falls on the ceiling.
7. (fill) Light travels in ___ lines, so it cannot bend round the toy.  
   **Answer:** straight. *Why:* Because light goes in straight lines, the edge of the shadow follows the edge of the toy.
8. (mcq) Sai sees his shadow on the floor pointing towards the door. Where is the light?  
   Options: On the side away from the door / Next to the door / Under the floor  
   **Answer:** On the side away from the door. *Why:* A shadow points away from the light. So if the shadow points to the door, the light is on the other side, away from the door.
9. (write) Many people think a shadow is completely black. In your tests, could you still see the floor or wall inside a shadow? Why?  
   **Answer:** Yes. Some light bounces off the walls and ceiling into the shadow, so it is dark grey, not totally black.. *Why:* A strong answer uses what the child saw. Light bounces off the walls, the ceiling and other things into the shadow.
10. (label) Label the picture of a big shadow: the dark middle, and the fuzzy edge.  
   **Answer:** ['dark middle: where no light from the torch gets in', 'fuzzy edge: where a little light from part of the torch still gets in']. *Why:* The fuzzy edge is grey because part of the torch's bright face can still be seen from there.
### Shadows (challenge) (challenge, 10 questions)
1. (perspective) In Africa, a bird called the black heron spreads her wings like an umbrella over the water while she fishes. Three people give a reason.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. Dr Asha is right that shade makes it easier to see into water. Thenmozhi is right that many small fish hide in shade. Gauri may be right too. Scientists still argue about this.. *Why:* This is a real question that scientists are still studying, so more than one answer can be well argued.
2. (write) Hetal, a girl in Class 2, wants the shadow of her toy to be almost the same size as the toy. Where should she hold it? Why?  
   **Answer:** As close to the wall as she can, because there the toy blocks only a small part of the spreading light, so the shadow is about the same size as the toy.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the place and says the shadow grows as the toy moves towards the torch, so near the wall it stays small.
3. (draw) Draw the shadow of a steel tumbler standing up, when a torch shines at it from the side. Then draw its shadow when the tumbler lies on its side with the open end facing the torch.  
   **Answer:** Standing: a rectangle, a little wider at the top. Lying with the open end to the torch: a circle.. *Why:* A shadow shows the outline of a thing as seen from the light. A standing tumbler seen from the side looks like a rectangle. Seen from its open end, it looks round.
4. (mcq) In a long school corridor, there is a light on the ceiling every few steps. What do you see as you walk along?  
   Options: Several shadows of you, one from each nearby light / No shadows at all / Exactly one shadow, always in front  
   **Answer:** Several shadows of you, one from each nearby light. *Why:* Each light makes its own shadow, so you have several shadows, and they change as you walk past each light.
5. (write) A shadow is made of missing light. Yet in the Near or Far test you measured it with a ruler. How did you measure something made of missing light?  
   **Answer:** I marked where the dark patch ended at the top and measured from the table to the mark. The edge of the shadow is where the light starts again.. *Why:* A strong answer says you measure where the light stops and starts. The edge is real, even if the patch is only missing light.
6. (truefalse) True or false: a plant can sense the shade of other leaves, and grow taller to get out of it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Light under leaves has less red light. Plants sense this and grow longer stems to reach sunlight.
7. (match) Draw a line from each shadow-puppet play to the state it comes from.  
   **Answer:** tholpavakoothu → Kerala; tholu bommalata → Andhra Pradesh; togalu gombeyaata → Karnataka; ravanachhaya → Odisha. *Why:* India has shadow plays in many states, each with its own name, puppets and stories.
8. (sort) Sort these into: gives a sharper shadow, or gives a fuzzier shadow.  
   **Answer:** a tiny phone torch → Sharper shadow; a big window on a cloudy day → Fuzzier shadow; the toy close to the wall → Sharper shadow; the toy close to the torch → Fuzzier shadow. *Why:* Small lights and things close to the wall give sharp shadows. Big lights, like a whole cloudy sky, and things close to the light give fuzzy edges.
9. (order) Put these steps for a box theatre shadow play in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Cut a window in a box and cover it with white paper', 'Make puppets on sticks', 'Put the torch behind the box', 'Hold the puppets between the torch and the screen']. *Why:* First the screen, then the puppets, then the light behind, and last the puppets go between the light and the screen.
10. (mcq) Four children stand in a room with two lamps on. How many shadows of children are there?  
   Options: 8 / 4 / 2  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* Each child has one shadow from each lamp, so 2 each. Four children with 2 shadows each make 8.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What is a shadow?  
   Options: A dark patch where light is blocked / A dark copy that comes out of a thing / A kind of light  
   **Answer:** A dark patch where light is blocked. *Why:* A shadow is the place the light cannot reach, because something is in the way. Nothing dark comes out of the thing.
2. (mcq) Kavya wants to make a shadow on her bedroom wall. Which of these does she NOT need?  
   Options: A mirror / A light / Something to block the light  
   **Answer:** A mirror. *Why:* A shadow needs a light, something to block it, and a surface for the shadow to fall on. A mirror is not needed.
3. (mcq) In the Near or Far test, where was the toy's shadow biggest?  
   Options: When the toy was close to the torch / When the toy was close to the wall / It was the same size everywhere  
   **Answer:** When the toy was close to the torch. *Why:* Close to the torch, the toy blocks a big part of the spreading light, so the shadow on the wall is big.
4. (mcq) A torch shines on a doll from the right. Where is the doll's shadow?  
   Options: On the left of the doll / On the right of the doll / In front of the torch  
   **Answer:** On the left of the doll. *Why:* A shadow falls on the side away from the light. The light comes from the right, so the shadow is on the left.
5. (mcq) A lamp hangs straight above a dining table. Where is the table's shadow?  
   Options: Under the table / On the ceiling / On the wall behind the lamp  
   **Answer:** Under the table. *Why:* The light comes from above, so the blocked place is below the table, on the floor.
6. (mcq) With two torches on, why is each shadow pale grey, not dark?  
   Options: The other torch still shines into it / Shadows are always pale / The toy lets light through  
   **Answer:** The other torch still shines into it. *Why:* Each shadow is lit by the other torch. Only where the two shadows overlap does no light get in, so that part is dark.
7. (mcq) Your shadow puppet is a crow coloured bright blue. What colour is the crow's shadow?  
   Options: Dark, with no blue / Bright blue / Pale blue  
   **Answer:** Dark, with no blue. *Why:* The card blocks the light, so the shadow is dark. A shadow shows only the outline, not the colours painted on the card.
8. (mcq) Tholpavakoothu is a shadow-puppet play from which state?  
   Options: Kerala / Punjab / Assam  
   **Answer:** Kerala. *Why:* Tholpavakoothu comes from Kerala, mostly round Palakkad. Andhra Pradesh has tholu bommalata, Karnataka has togalu gombeyaata and Odisha has ravanachhaya.
9. (mcq) Why does a shadow have the same outline as the thing that makes it?  
   Options: Light travels in straight lines / Light bends round things / The wall copies the thing  
   **Answer:** Light travels in straight lines. *Why:* Light that just misses the edge goes straight on to the wall, and light that hits the thing stops. So the edge of the shadow follows the edge of the thing.
10. (mcq) Revathi holds her hand very close to the torch. What does the edge of the shadow look like?  
   Options: Soft and fuzzy / Sharp and crisp / Bright yellow  
   **Answer:** Soft and fuzzy. *Why:* Close to the torch, the edge of the shadow gets a little light from part of the torch, so it is grey and soft. Close to the wall, the edge is sharp.

## Applications
- Shadow-puppet plays in Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Odisha tell long stories with leather puppets, lamps and a white cloth.
- Umbrellas, sun hats, shade nets and trees block sunlight to keep people and other animals cool.
- Plant nurseries in India grow young plants under green shade nets, which block part of the strong sunlight.
- An X-ray picture is a kind of shadow picture. Bones block more X-rays than the soft parts do, so they show up pale.

## Spotted around us
- At lunch, look under the dining table. The ceiling light makes a dark shadow on the floor.
- In a tholpavakoothu play in Palakkad, the shadows of leather puppets move on a long white cloth lit by oil lamps.
- Walking at night past a street light, your shadow is short when you are close to the pole and gets longer as you walk away.
- On a hot afternoon, people wait for the bus in the shade of a banyan tree or a bus shelter.

## Open questions
- **Did shadow-puppet plays begin in India, in China, or somewhere else?** Shadow plays are very old in India, China and South-East Asia. Old written records are few, so historians still argue about where the first ones were.
- **How does a plant weigh up a neighbour's shade against everything else they sense?** Scientists know plants sense shade with light-sensing proteins. How a plant puts this together with water, touch and the seasons to decide how to grow is still being worked out.
- **What exactly did the Mohists write about shadows?** Their book was copied by hand for many centuries, and parts were damaged or muddled. Scholars still argue about how to read some of the notes.

## Connected maths
- In a Near or Far test, a toy's shadow was 10 centimetres tall near the wall and 30 centimetres tall near the torch. How many times taller was the shadow near the torch? **3 times taller.**. Count in tens: 10, 20, 30. That is three tens. So 30 centimetres is 3 times 10 centimetres. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- Three puppets stand in a room with two lamps on. Each puppet has one shadow from each lamp. How many shadows are there in all? **6 shadows.**. Each puppet has 2 shadows. Three puppets: 2, 4, 6. So there are 6 shadows. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L2`)
- Three shadows of the same toy were 14 centimetres, 9 centimetres and 31 centimetres tall. Put them in order from smallest to biggest. Which one was the toy close to the torch? **9, 14, 31. The 31 centimetre shadow was the toy close to the torch.**. 9 is the smallest, then 14, then 31. The nearer the toy is to the torch, the bigger its shadow, so the biggest one, 31, was nearest the torch. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L2`)
- Nila folds a card in half and cuts out half a butterfly along the fold. When she opens it, both wings match. Will the butterfly's shadow have matching wings too? Why? **Yes. The shadow has the same outline as the card, so its two halves match as well.**. The fold is a line of symmetry. A shadow keeps the outline of the card, so the shadow has the same line of symmetry. (queues `math.symmetry.line-symmetry.L2`)
- The family's shadow play started at 7 o'clock in the evening and lasted half an hour. At what time did it end? **Half past 7.**. Half an hour after 7 o'clock, the long hand has moved half way round the clock, to the 6. That is half past 7. (queues `math.time.telling-time.L2`)
