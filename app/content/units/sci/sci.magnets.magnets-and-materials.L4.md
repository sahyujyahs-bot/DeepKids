# Magnets and magnetic materials: the magnet pulls the nail, but does the nail pull back?
`sci.magnets.magnets-and-materials.L4`

**Summary.** A magnet picks up a nail, so it looks as if the magnet does all the pulling. Float the magnet on a tiny raft, hold a nail near it, and the magnet sails across the water to the nail. Your child tests this with two rafts, finds out why two nails hanging from one magnet spread apart like a V, and tries to keep a magnetism in nails, needles and fridge magnets. Along the way they meet soft iron, hard steel, keepers, and the oldest rule about pulls: they always come in pairs.

**Intro.** *A magnet can pick up a nail. Everyone says the magnet is pulling the nail. But what if the nail is pulling the magnet too? Put the magnet on a little boat on water and bring a nail close. What do you think the boat will do?* In Class 3 you found out that a magnet attracts iron, and that every iron atom is a tiny magnet. Now we go one step deeper. How does a plain nail, which is not a magnet, get pulled at all? Why do two nails hanging from one magnet spread apart? Why does a needle stay magnetic when a nail does not? And how can a fridge magnet grip your almirah on one side only? You find out by testing it yourself.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that when a magnet attracts a nail, the nail attracts the magnet just as strongly, and I can show it with floating rafts.
1. I can explain why the lighter of two things pulled together moves further, even though the two pulls are equal.
2. I can explain that a nail near a magnet becomes a temporary magnet itself, and use this to explain why two hanging nails spread apart.
3. I can use a repel test to tell a real magnet from a plain piece of iron.
4. I can explain the difference between soft magnetic materials, which lose their magnetism, and hard ones, which keep it, and say which jobs each is used for.
5. I can explain why magnets are stored with keepers, and what weakens a magnet.
6. I can say how a fridge magnet, a hard disk and a magnetic stripe use many tiny magnetised regions.
7. I can say what scientists know and what they still argue about when they say a magnetic field is real.

## Terms
- **Magnetic force**: The force between a magnet and a magnetic material, or between two magnets. It can attract or repel, and it acts across a gap. *e.g.* A magnet held just above a pin pulls the pin up before they touch.
- **Attract**: To pull towards. Two things attract each other when the force between them pulls them together. *e.g.* A magnet and a nail attract each other.
- **Repel**: To push away. Only a magnet can repel another magnet. A plain piece of iron is never pushed away. *e.g.* Turn one fridge magnet round and it pushes the other one away.
- **Magnetic material**: A material that a magnet attracts, such as iron, most steel, nickel and cobalt. It is not a magnet by itself. *e.g.* A steel nail is a magnetic material. It is not a magnet until a magnet is near.
- **Permanent magnet**: A magnet that keeps its magnetism for years without anything to help it. *e.g.* A fridge magnet and a bar magnet are permanent magnets.
- **Temporary magnet**: A piece of magnetic material that acts as a magnet only while a magnet is near it. *e.g.* A nail hanging from a magnet can hold a pin. Pull the magnet away and the pin drops.
- **Induced magnet**: A magnet that a nearby magnet has made in a piece of magnetic material. The word induced means brought about by something else. *e.g.* The nail that hangs from a magnet and holds up a paper clip is an induced magnet.
- **Soft magnetic material**: A magnetic material that is easy to magnetise and loses its magnetism quickly. Soft here does not mean squashy. It is only about magnetism. *e.g.* A plain iron nail is a soft magnetic material.
- **Hard magnetic material**: A magnetic material that is harder to magnetise but keeps its magnetism for a long time. Hard here is also only about magnetism. *e.g.* A steel sewing needle is a hard magnetic material.
- **Demagnetise**: To take the magnetism out of a magnet. Heat, hard knocks and a strong opposite magnet can all do it. *e.g.* Hammering a magnet hard many times makes it weaker.
- **Keeper**: A piece of soft iron laid across the ends of a magnet when it is stored, to help it keep its magnetism. *e.g.* A horseshoe magnet in a shop is often stored with a flat iron bar across its two ends.
- **Pole**: An end of a magnet where its pull is strongest. There is a north pole and a south pole. *e.g.* Paper clips cling to the two ends of a bar magnet.
- **Domain**: A tiny region inside iron or steel where the tiny magnets of billions of atoms point the same way. *e.g.* In a plain nail the domains point different ways, so the nail is not a magnet.
- **Saturation**: The point when all the domains in a piece of iron already point the same way, so no magnet can make it any more magnetic. *e.g.* A thin nail can be magnetised only up to a limit, however strong the magnet.
- **Mass**: How much stuff something is made of. A heavier thing has more mass. We measure mass in grams and kilograms. *e.g.* A steel spoon has more mass than a plastic spoon of the same size.
- **Third law of motion**: Isaac Newton's rule that when one thing pushes or pulls another, the other pushes or pulls back just as hard, in the opposite direction. *e.g.* When you push a wall, the wall pushes your hand.
- **Ferrite**: A hard dark ceramic made from iron oxide, which is iron joined with oxygen. Most fridge magnets and speaker magnets are made of ferrite. *e.g.* The black round magnet inside an old loudspeaker is ferrite.
- **Neodymium magnet**: A very strong small magnet made from neodymium, iron and boron. It is much stronger than a ferrite magnet of the same size. *e.g.* Tiny silver magnets inside earphones and phones are neodymium magnets.
- **Magnetic stripe**: A thin band on a card that holds information as many tiny regions magnetised one way or the other. *e.g.* The dark stripe on an old bank card or a hotel key card is a magnetic stripe.

## Explanations
### A pull has two ends: the nail pulls the magnet too · *defined*
**Definition:** When a magnet attracts a nail, the nail attracts the magnet with exactly the same strength, in the opposite direction. The two pulls always come as a pair.

We say 'the magnet pulls the nail' because the nail is the one we see move. But float the magnet on water, hold the nail still, and the magnet moves to the nail. The nail was pulling all along. Isaac Newton wrote this as a rule in his book of 1687, the Principia. It is called the third law of motion. When one thing pushes or pulls another, the other pushes or pulls back, equally hard. You feel it every day. Push a wall and you feel the wall push your hand. Stand on skates and push a wall, and you roll backwards. The wall did not move, but it pushed you.
- e.g. A magnet on a raft sails to a held nail, so the nail is pulling the magnet.
- e.g. A swimmer pushes the water back, and the water pushes the swimmer forward.
- e.g. A falling apple pulls the Earth upwards by exactly as much as the Earth pulls the apple down. The Earth is so heavy that it moves by a tiny amount that we can never see.
### Equal pulls do not mean equal moves · *defined*
**Definition:** Two things pulled together by equal forces move different distances if they have different mass. The one with less mass moves more.

Push a light shopping bag and a heavy school bag with the same push. The light one slides far, and the heavy one hardly moves. It is the same with a magnet and a nail. The two pulls are equal. If both float on rafts that have the same mass, they meet in the middle. If the nail raft is three times heavier, it moves a third as far, and the magnet raft does most of the travelling. This is why a fridge magnet seems to do all the work when it jumps onto an almirah. The almirah is pulled just as hard. It has so much mass, and so much friction with the floor, that it does not move.
- e.g. A tiny magnet jumps to a heavy iron pan, and the pan does not move. The pan is pulling the magnet with exactly the same force.
- e.g. Two rafts of equal mass, one with a magnet and one with a nail, meet in the middle of the tub.
- e.g. If you hold a magnet in your hand and a pin hangs from a thread, the pin swings to the magnet. The magnet pulls your hand too, but your hand is far too heavy to feel it.
### A nail near a magnet becomes a magnet itself · *defined*
**Definition:** A piece of iron near a magnet becomes an induced magnet. The magnet turns the domains inside the iron to point one way, and the iron becomes a magnet for as long as the magnet stays near.

Why does a magnet pick up a plain nail at all? A plain nail is not a magnet. Its domains point in all directions and cancel out. When a magnet comes near, its magnetic field turns the domains in the nail to point the same way. Now the nail is a magnet too, with its own north and south poles. The end of the nail closest to the magnet's north pole becomes a south pole, and a north pole and a south pole attract. So a magnet never attracts plain iron directly. It first makes the iron into a magnet, and then the two magnets attract each other. This is also why a chain of clips can hang from a magnet. Each clip becomes a magnet and holds the next one.
- e.g. A nail touching a magnet can pick up pins. Pull the magnet away and most pins fall.
- e.g. A compass needle is pulled by either end of a plain iron nail, because the nail makes itself a magnet facing the right way.
- e.g. A chain of paper clips hangs from one magnet, with every clip magnetised by the one above.
### Why two hanging nails spread apart like a V · *defined*
**Definition:** Two nails hanging from the same pole of a magnet spread apart because both become magnets with the same kind of pole at their free ends, and two poles of the same kind repel.

Touch the heads of two nails to the north pole of a magnet. Each nail becomes an induced magnet. The head, next to the magnet's north pole, becomes a south pole. The far end, the tip, must then be a north pole. Both tips are north poles, and two north poles repel. So the tips push each other apart, and the two nails hang in a V. If you swap the magnet's south pole in, the same thing happens, with both tips south poles. The longer the nails are, the further their tips are from the magnet and from each other, so they have more room to spread.
- e.g. Two pins hanging from one magnet spread at the tips.
- e.g. Three pins hang as a small bunch, with the tips pushing apart as far as they can.
- e.g. A chain of clips from one clip hangs in a line, because each clip has only one clip below it to push on.
### Only a magnet can repel: the repel test · *defined*
**Definition:** If two things attract each other, you cannot tell if one is a magnet or both are. If two things repel, both must be magnets. Repelling is the proof of a magnet.

A plain nail is attracted by either end of a magnet. A magnet is attracted by one end of another magnet and pushed away by the other end. So here is a test for a mystery piece of metal. Float a magnet on a raft, or hang it from a thread. Bring one end of the mystery piece near, then the other end. If either end pushes the magnet away, the mystery piece is a magnet. If both ends pull, it is plain iron, or a magnet that is too weak to push. Pulls fool us, but pushes tell the truth. That is why scientists treat a repulsion as stronger evidence than an attraction.
- e.g. A stroked needle pushes the floating magnet away with one end, so it really is a magnet.
- e.g. A plain nail pulls the floating magnet with both ends, so it is only magnetic.
- e.g. A fridge door pulls a fridge magnet, but it never pushes the magnet away, so the door is not a magnet.
### Soft and hard magnetic materials · *defined*
**Definition:** A soft magnetic material, such as plain iron, is easy to magnetise and loses its magnetism as soon as the magnet goes away. A hard magnetic material, such as hard steel, is harder to magnetise but keeps its magnetism for years.

Touch an iron nail and a steel sewing needle to a magnet, and both pick up pins. Take the magnet away. The nail drops most of its pins. The needle keeps its pins, and it can still lift a pin next week. Scientists call the nail soft and the needle hard. These words mean something different from 'soft' and 'hard' in a toy shop. They only describe how easily the domains turn and how well they stay turned. The two kinds are used for different jobs. A permanent magnet is made from a hard material, so it keeps its magnetism. The core of an electromagnet, a coil of wire with electricity running through it, is made from a soft material. When the electricity is switched off, the core stops being a magnet, and the crane drops its load.
- e.g. Fridge magnets, loudspeaker magnets and compass needles are made of hard magnetic materials.
- e.g. The iron core inside an electric bell or a scrapyard crane magnet is a soft magnetic material.
- e.g. A screwdriver can become a little magnetic after it is stroked with a magnet, because its steel is fairly hard.
### Why hard steel keeps its magnetism · *defined*
**Definition:** Hard steel keeps its magnetism because tiny flaws and bits of carbon in the steel hold its domains in place after they have turned.

In pure soft iron, the edge between one domain and the next can slide about easily, so the domains turn back by themselves once the magnet leaves. In hard steel, there are tiny bits of carbon and other flaws in the way. They act like pebbles in a stream, and the domain edges catch on them. Once the domains are turned, they stay turned. This is why adding carbon to iron to make steel changes its magnetism as well as its strength. It is also why hammering, heating or a strong opposite magnet can undo the magnetism of a needle. They shake the edges loose from the flaws. Scientists describe it in this way from studying domains under special microscopes.
- e.g. A sewing needle has a lot of carbon, so it stays magnetised.
- e.g. Alnico, a mixture of aluminium, nickel, cobalt and iron, is built so that its domains are held very firmly.
- e.g. A magnet that is hammered hard many times gets weaker.
### Hearing the domains click · *defined*
**Definition:** When a magnet slowly magnetises a piece of iron, the domains do not turn smoothly. They flip in sudden small jumps, and the jumps can be heard as a crackle. This is called the Barkhausen effect.

In 1919, the German physicist Heinrich Barkhausen wound a coil of wire around an iron rod. He joined the coil to a new kind of amplifier and a loudspeaker. Then he moved a magnet slowly towards the rod. The loudspeaker crackled like oil frying. Each click was a group of domains turning over all at once. So the iron does not turn into a magnet like a dimmer switch. It changes in thousands of tiny jumps, like a crowd standing up one row at a time. Engineers today use the same crackle to test steel parts, because a part with a hidden flaw crackles in a different way.
- e.g. Slowly bringing a magnet near an iron rod makes a loudspeaker crackle.
- e.g. Engineers listen to the crackle of a steel gear to find out if it was heated properly.
- e.g. Rain on a tin roof is a useful picture. Each click is a drop, and there are thousands.
### A piece of iron can only be magnetised so far · *defined*
**Definition:** When every domain in a piece of iron already points the same way, the iron is saturated. Even a stronger magnet cannot make it any more magnetic.

The more a magnet turns the domains in a nail, the stronger the nail's own magnetism gets. But there is a limit. A thin nail has only so many domains to turn. Once they all point the same way, that is the most magnetism the nail can ever have. A fat bolt has many more domains, so it can hold a stronger magnetism. This is why a thin sheet of tin, held on a fridge magnet, grips less than a thick plate of the same steel. It is also why engineers who make strong electromagnets choose a fat iron core. Past saturation, a bigger push from a stronger magnet changes almost nothing.
- e.g. A thick bolt can lift more pins than a thin nail, when both touch the same magnet.
- e.g. A magnet cannot make a thin steel wire any stronger a magnet than its saturation limit.
- e.g. A cup holds only so much water. After it is full, a stronger tap adds nothing.
### Keepers: why magnets are stored in pairs · *defined*
**Definition:** A keeper is a piece of soft iron laid across the poles of a magnet. It gives the magnet's field an easy path from one pole to the other, and helps the magnet to stay strong.

A bar magnet is slightly working against itself. Its own north pole sits not far from its own south pole, and the field from one pole tends to turn the domains near the other pole backwards. Over many years, this can make the magnet weaker. A keeper is a small bar of soft iron. When it lies across the poles, the field runs through the iron instead of around through the air, and the magnet's domains are not pushed backwards. Shops once kept horseshoe magnets with a flat iron bar across their ends. Two bar magnets stored side by side with opposite poles together, and a keeper at each end, hold in the field in the same way. The magnets that need this most are made from materials like alnico. Modern ferrite and neodymium magnets are far more stubborn and need less care.
- e.g. A horseshoe magnet with a flat iron bar across its ends.
- e.g. Two bar magnets stored in a box side by side, north next to south, with an iron bar across each end.
- e.g. Stack fridge magnets with their sticking faces together, and not loose in a drawer with metal tools.
### A fridge magnet that grips on one side only · *defined*
**Definition:** A bendy fridge magnet is rubber filled with ferrite powder, magnetised in many thin stripes of north and south side by side. This makes it grip strongly on one face and hardly at all on the other.

Try this with a flat bendy fridge magnet. Press its front on the steel door, and it holds well. Flip it over, and it barely sticks. The two faces are different. The maker magnetises the rubber sheet in thin stripes, each stripe a north pole next to a south pole next to a north pole again. The field loops between the neighbouring stripes. On the front face, the loops reach out far enough to grip steel. On the back, the loops cancel out and the grip is weak. The stripes are only about a millimetre or two wide, so your fingers do not notice them, but a special viewing film shows them. A magnet like this is an example of design: the same amount of magnet, used where it matters.
- e.g. A bendy fridge magnet sticks to the fridge on one side only.
- e.g. The rubber strip that seals a fridge door has a magnetic strip inside it, so the door pulls itself shut.
- e.g. Cut a bendy fridge magnet in two with scissors, and each piece is still a magnet, with its own strong side.
### Magnet against the whole Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** A small magnet can lift a nail up against the pull of the whole Earth. This shows that the magnetic force is much stronger than gravity between everyday things.

The whole Earth is pulling the nail down. It has an enormous mass. A small ceramic magnet, which you can hold in your fingers, pulls the nail up and wins. Scientists have compared the strengths of the forces of nature. Gravity is by far the weakest of them. Gravity only seems strong because the Earth is so huge, and gravity never pushes, it only pulls, so it all adds up. The magnetic force is a part of the electromagnetic force. It also holds atoms together, and it is much stronger than gravity between two small things. A fridge magnet beats the Earth in a tug of war, but only at a gap of a few millimetres. It loses its grip quickly as the gap grows.
- e.g. A small magnet holds a nail against the Earth's pull.
- e.g. A paper clip hangs from a magnet, and a whole planet cannot pull it off.
- e.g. To pick up a coin by gravity you need to drop it, and to pick it up by magnetism you only need a magnet.
### Magnetic pull and static pull are not the same · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnet pulls only iron, nickel, cobalt and a few other materials. A rubbed comb pulls scraps of paper. These are two different pulls, and a simple test tells them apart.

Rub a plastic comb on dry hair, and it picks up tiny bits of paper. Is the comb a magnet? Test it. A magnet does not pick up bits of paper, and a rubbed comb does not pick up a steel pin. The comb's pull is static electricity, which comes from electric charge. The magnet's pull is magnetism. Scientists have found that electricity and magnetism are two sides of one force, the electromagnetic force. James Clerk Maxwell wrote the rules for it in 1865. But in the kitchen, they behave differently, and the tests show which one you have. The tests are what a scientist does first: ask what picks up what.
- e.g. A rubbed comb picks up paper but not a pin.
- e.g. A magnet picks up a pin but not paper.
- e.g. A balloon rubbed on hair sticks to a wall, and a magnet does not stick to a plastered wall.
### Magnetic memory: how a hard disk and a card hold information · *defined*
**Definition:** A magnetic stripe, a cassette tape and a hard disk store information as millions of tiny regions of magnetic material, each magnetised one way or the other. Each region is like a domain that has been turned on purpose.

A hard disk has a thin coat of hard magnetic material on a spinning plate. A tiny coil called a write head passes over it and turns each tiny region of the coat either up or down. A pattern of ups and downs can stand for numbers, and the numbers can stand for letters, pictures and sound. To read it back, a second tiny sensor passes over the plate and feels which way each region points. This only works because the material is hard. The regions must stay turned for years, or the information would fade. Old cassette tapes and the dark stripe on an old bank card work in the same way. Strong magnets near them can scramble the pattern, which is why you are told to keep cards away from magnets.
- e.g. A hotel key card or an old bank card has a dark magnetic stripe.
- e.g. An old cassette tape is a thin ribbon coated with magnetic powder.
- e.g. A hard disk inside a computer stores a whole library on spinning magnetic plates.
### Is a magnetic field a real thing? · *debated*
A magnet moves a nail across a gap with nothing in between. How does the pull cross the gap? Scientists use the idea of a magnetic field, a map of the pull at every place around the magnet. They agree the field works as a description. They do not all agree what it is.
- e.g. Iron filings sprinkled on paper over a magnet line up along the field.
- e.g. A compass held in a magnetic field turns to point along it.
- e.g. Light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields moving through space, and it can cross empty space.
- **Isaac Newton, about 1693:** In a letter, Newton said it was absurd that one body could act on another across empty space with nothing between them. He did not offer an answer.
- **Michael Faraday, 1840s:** Faraday, a self-taught scientist who began work as a bookseller's boy, pictured lines of force filling the space around a magnet. He said the lines are real.
- **Many physicists today:** The field is real, because it carries energy and light is a wave of it. Fields cross empty space. The exact nature of the field, and how it connects to quantum physics, is still being worked out.
- **Some philosophers of science:** The field is a very good description, but we only ever measure the pulls. Asking whether the field is a thing is a question science cannot settle by measuring.

## Mostly agreed
- A magnet and a piece of iron pull each other with exactly equal force, in opposite directions. *How we know:* Newton's third law has been tested for over 300 years. Floating rafts show it: a magnet on one raft and a nail on the other both move.
- A piece of plain iron near a magnet becomes a magnet itself for as long as the magnet is near. *How we know:* The nail picks up pins while it touches the magnet, and drops them when the magnet goes. Special microscopes show the domains turning.
- Soft magnetic materials lose their magnetism quickly, and hard ones keep it. *How we know:* A nail drops its pins, and a needle keeps them. Engineers use the two kinds for different jobs.
- Only a magnet can repel another magnet. *How we know:* Plain iron is attracted by both ends of a magnet. A push away has only ever been seen between two magnets.
- Magnets can be weakened by heat, hard knocks and strong opposite magnets. *How we know:* People have measured the loss of strength in hammered and heated magnets for centuries.
- Magnetic stripes, tapes and disks store information as many tiny regions magnetised one way or the other. *How we know:* Engineers write and read the patterns every day, and magnetic viewers show them.

## How we found out
**William Gilbert, an English doctor, and Isaac Newton, an English mathematician, 1600 and 1687.** For centuries, many writers described a lodestone as the active one, which called or drew the iron, and the iron as the one that was drawn. In 1600, the English doctor William Gilbert wrote that the attraction goes both ways. He reported floating a lodestone on the water in a small vessel and bringing iron near it, and the lodestone moved towards the iron. In 1687, Isaac Newton wrote down a rule for every push and pull in nature. When one body pushes or pulls another, the other pushes or pulls back with equal force in the opposite direction. The rule has been tested for more than three hundred years, on planets, on rockets and on magnets on rafts. It has never failed once.  
*What people believed before:* Many people thought the lodestone was the only one that pulled, and the iron was merely drawn. Gilbert's floating lodestone showed that the iron draws the lodestone too, and Newton showed that this is true of every pull.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The magnet does all the pulling. The nail is just pulled.** (Most children, and many grown-ups.; *rejected*)  
  For: The nail is the one that moves, and the magnet stays in your hand.  
  Against: Float the magnet on a raft and hold the nail, and the magnet moves instead. The two pulls are always equal. Which one moves depends on which one is free to move and how heavy it is.
- **A magnet holds a nail the way glue or suction does.** (Many children.; *rejected*)  
  For: The nail sticks and does not fall.  
  Against: A magnet pulls through air and paper, with a gap, and it works in empty space. Glue needs touching, and suction needs air. A magnet needs neither.
- **If two things attract, one of them must be a magnet.** (A very common idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: A magnet and a nail attract, and a magnet is one of them.  
  Against: Two plain iron nails near a magnet attract each other, and neither was a magnet before. Even a single magnet's pull on a plain nail is two magnets pulling, because the nail is made into a magnet. Only a push is proof.
- **A magnet loses strength every time it lifts something, like a battery running down.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Old magnets are often weaker than new ones.  
  Against: Holding a nail for years takes nothing from a magnet. Old magnets weaken because of heat, knocks, nearby opposite magnets or very slow changes inside the material. A good magnet can hold a weight for decades.
- **A stronger magnet always lifts a heavier load.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: Strong magnets do lift heavy things.  
  Against: What the magnet lifts also matters. A thin sheet of steel saturates early and gives the magnet less to hold. A rough, rusty or curved surface leaves tiny gaps. A flat thick plate lets the same magnet hold much more.
- **Every magnet must have iron in it.** (A common idea.; *rejected*)  
  For: Most everyday magnets have iron, and the old magnet stone was made of iron oxide.  
  Against: A magnet made of just samarium and cobalt, called samarium-cobalt, needs no iron in its simplest form. What matters is whether the atoms line up in domains.
- **A magnetic field is only a drawing and not a real thing.** (Some philosophers of science.; *debated*)  
  For: We only ever measure the pulls on things.  
  Against: A field carries energy, and light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields travelling through empty space. Most physicists say this makes fields real.

## Roots
We have floated magnets and nails, hung nails in a V, and tested needles and nails for memory. Now we ask what a pull between two things tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A magnet and a nail pull each other exactly as hard. Put both on rafts and both move. A pull is never one thing acting alone. It is what two things do together.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say the magnet pulls the nail, as if one thing acts and the other is only acted on. That split between a doer and a done-to comes from watching which one moves, not from the forces themselves.  
*Evidence:* On two equal rafts, the magnet and the nail meet in the middle. On unequal rafts the pulls stay equal and the light raft travels further. The creeping of the heavy raft shows the pull on it.

- *who am i:* When you push a wall, the wall pushes you. You cannot push or pull anything without being pushed or pulled back.
- *what is life:* A root pushes into the soil, and the soil pushes back. A bird pushes air down, and the air lifts her up.
- *what is this world:* Every push and pull in the world comes as a pair. No force has ever been found alone.

A magnet and a nail look like two separate things, one doing and one being done to. But the pull between them is one thing with two ends. Take away either end and the pull is gone. Seen like this, the many things in the world are the ends of pulls and pushes that join them. The magnet and the nail are two places where one pull shows itself.

- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika, chapter 8:** Nagarjuna, an Indian Buddhist teacher of about the 2nd century CE, taught that a doer and a deed depend on each other. There is no doer without a deed, and no deed without a doer. (A magnet is a puller only because there is a nail to pull. The pull is what they do together.)
- **Tao Te Ching 2:** Being and non-being give birth to each other. Hard and easy complete each other. Long and short measure each other. Front and back follow each other. (North and south poles are born together. A pull has two ends, and each end needs the other.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment B51 (Diels-Kranz numbering), about 500 BCE:** Heraclitus said that the harmony of the world comes from tension that pulls in both directions, like the bow and the lyre. (A pulled bowstring pulls the bow back as hard as the bow pulls the string. Magnet and nail are held in the same kind of tension.)
- Science (settled): Newton's third law: when one body pulls on another, the second pulls on the first with an equal force in the opposite direction. It has never failed a test.
- Science (debated): What a magnetic field is, underneath, and whether it is a thing or only a very good description.
- Other view, Many physicists: Saying that forces come in pairs is a way of keeping accounts. It does not show that the world is joined together. In daily life, saying that a magnet pulls a nail is perfectly correct, and nothing needs to change in how we talk.
- Practice, Press your palms (3 min): Sit facing a partner. Hold up your palms and press them together, gently. Breathe in slowly. Can you push without being pushed back? Try pushing a little harder, and notice that your partner's hands push harder too. Now both ease off slowly, together. Breathe out. Which hand was pushing, and which was being pushed?
- Ask: When you push something, what pushes back? Can you find three examples around you?
- Ask: If the nail pulls the magnet as hard, why does it feel as if only one of them is pulling?
- Ask: Can you think of a time when two people or two creatures changed each other, even though only one seemed to do something?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The magnet pulls the nail, and the nail pulls the magnet. When we say 'the magnet pulls the nail', are we telling the story from the nail's side or from the magnet's? (hint: Is there a way to describe the pair without picking a side?)
- *deep:* A pull needs two things. Can anything act on the world without the world acting back on it? (hint: Think about walking, speaking, planting a seed.)
- *deep:* A nail becomes a magnet the moment a magnet is near and stops when it goes. Is the nail 'really' a magnet or 'really' not? (hint: Does a word like 'magnet' describe a thing or what is happening to it?)
- *deep:* A needle keeps its magnetism for years, and a nail forgets in seconds. What do you think it means for a material to 'remember'? (hint: What would you need to find out to say a material remembers?)
- *big:* The magnetic force beats the whole Earth's gravity, yet we say gravity is the force that holds the universe together. How can the weaker force be the one that shapes planets and stars? (hint: Think about what adds up over a huge amount of stuff, and what cancels.)
- *big:* The same domains that make a fridge magnet also store the songs on a hard disk. Does it change how you feel about the music that it is held by tiny magnetised regions? (hint: Are you still the same you if you know what your brain is made of?)
- *big:* Two people who work together to move a heavy thing both move it, but only one might get the thanks. Who does the 'pulling' in a team? (hint: Who is the nail, and who is the magnet?)
- *big:* Newton thought it was absurd that one thing could pull another across empty space. Faraday, who had no university education, found a way to picture it. Why do you think a new way of seeing sometimes comes from outside the usual path? (hint: Who was allowed to study science in the 1800s, and who was not?)
- *big:* You are sure the magnet is the one pulling because the nail moves. What other times in life do we think one side is the 'doer' because the other side is the one that moves? (hint: Think about a river and its bank, a teacher and a class, a mother and a baby.)
- *deep:* A repulsion is stronger proof of a magnet than an attraction. What other kinds of evidence are stronger when they say 'no' than when they say 'yes'? (hint: A fever test or a lock that does not open.)

## Story: The Train That Met in the Middle
*A made-up story. Channapatna in Karnataka really is known as the toy town, and its wooden toys are coloured with lac while the wood spins on a lathe. Revathi, her family and the toy buyer are made up. The science is real: when a magnet and a nail pull each other, the pulls are equal, and the lighter one moves more.*

In the town of Channapatna, where the streets smell of wood shavings and warm lac, ten-year-old Revathi lived in a house full of toys. There were wooden tops on the shelves, wooden rattles hanging from the beams, and wooden elephants lined up on the windowsill, all in shiny red and yellow and green.

Her Ajji, her grandmother, turned the wood on a lathe. The wood spun and spun, and Ajji pressed a stick of coloured lac against it. The lac softened in the heat of the spinning and spread over the wood like a ribbon of butter. Ajji never looked at her hands. She looked at the wood, and the wood told her what to do.

One morning, a woman came to the house in a red scooter. Her name was Mrs Nasreen Khan, and she owned a toy shop in Hyderabad. She put a small wooden train on the table. It had an engine and two carriages, joined by tiny metal hooks.

'The children love it,' she said. 'But the hooks catch little fingers, and the mothers complain. Can you make me a train whose carriages join without hooks? No pinching, no hooks. They should simply find each other.'

Revathi's mind lit up. She had a ceramic magnet from the back of an old loudspeaker. 'Ajji, I can do it with a magnet!'

She hid the magnet in the back of the engine, and a steel nail in the front of each carriage. She placed the engine on the table with a carriage a hand's width behind it. The carriage rolled forward, all on its own, and joined the engine with a click.

'Wonderful,' said Mrs Khan. But Revathi's uncle Mohan, who painted the faces on the elephants, frowned. 'It is not good enough yet. Look, the engine has the magnet. The magnet is the one doing the pulling. The nail is only being pulled, like a fish on a line. If we want a stronger join, we will use a bigger magnet.'

Revathi was not sure. She had seen something, and it had been too quick to catch. She put the engine and the carriage back on the table, and set them going again. This time she watched the engine, and not the carriage.

There it was. Just as the carriage shot forward, the engine twitched. It rolled a tiny way backwards, towards the carriage, and then they met.

'Did you see?' she said. 'The engine moved! It has the magnet, and it moved. So the carriage is pulling it too.'

'Nonsense,' said Uncle Mohan. 'A nail cannot pull. It has no power in it.'

Her little sister Sowmya, who was six, had been sitting under the table with a lump of clay. She popped her head up. 'Uncle, what if you put the nail in the front and the magnet in the back and held the nail with your hand?'

Nobody answered her, so she took the carriage in one hand and the magnet engine in the other, and set them far apart on the table. She let go of both at once. The engine slid towards the carriage. The carriage slid towards the engine. They met in the middle of the table, exactly half way.

Revathi's uncle stopped talking. Ajji, who had been turning a top on the lathe, switched it off and came to look. 'Do it again,' she said.

Sowmya did it again. And again. Then she put a big lump of clay in the carriage, so it was much heavier. This time the engine slid nearly all the way across the table, and the heavy carriage only crept a little way to meet it.

'It is the same pull,' said Revathi slowly. 'The nail pulls the magnet as hard as the magnet pulls the nail. But the light one moves further. That is why the engine only twitched in my first try. It was much heavier than the carriage, so the carriage did the running.'

'And that is why the carriage banged into the engine and chipped the red lacquer,' said Ajji. 'A light thing coming fast. If a bigger magnet pulled harder, the bang would be even worse.'

So they did the opposite of what Uncle Mohan said. They used a smaller magnet, so the pull was gentle, and they made the engine and the carriages weigh the same, with a pinch of clay in the lightest. Now whenever Revathi set them on the table, the engine and the carriage slid towards each other, side by side, and met softly in the middle with a little click, like two people shaking hands.

Mrs Khan ordered two hundred trains. Uncle Mohan painted the faces on every one, and he was quiet for a whole week. At the end of the week, he put a small magnet into his pocket. 'For Sowmya,' he said. 'For asking the question that nobody asked.'

**For the parent:** A magnet and a nail pull each other with exactly equal force. The engine twitched backwards because the carriage was pulling it. The lighter carriage moved further because the same pull moves a light thing faster than a heavy thing. A gentle meeting needs a smaller pull and balanced weights.

- Ask: Why did the engine twitch backwards when the carriage rushed forward?
- Ask: When Sowmya let go of the engine and the carriage together, where did they meet? Why there?
- Ask: Why did the carriage move further when it was light and the engine heavy?
- Ask: Uncle Mohan was sure the nail could not pull. What would you have said to him, using only what you can see?
- Ask: A six-year-old asked the question that nobody asked. Why do you think questions from the youngest are sometimes the best?

### e1 · Rafts on the Water: does the nail pull the magnet too? (45 min)
- **question:** A magnet picks up a nail. If we put the magnet and the nail on floating rafts, will only the nail move, only the magnet, or both? And does it change if one raft is heavier?

- **fair test:** Every trial uses the same tub, the same still water and the same starting gap of 8 centimetres. The two rafts are made the same size and, except in the last trial, the same mass. We wait for the water to go still before each trial and let go of both rafts at the same moment. Only one thing changes at a time: first a wooden control, then the nail raft held still, then both free, then a heavy nail raft. Each trial is done three times, because one try can be a fluke.

- **record:** For every trial write how far each raft moved, in centimetres. Do each trial three times and write down the middle value. Then draw a bar for the magnet raft and a bar for the nail raft in each trial.

Why: the pulls are a pair of equal forces, and the force moves light things further than heavy things.
### e2 · The Hanging V: why do two nails spread apart? (25 min)
- **question:** If two nails hang side by side from one magnet, will they hang straight, stick together, or spread apart? And do longer nails spread further?
- **predict:** Before you start, draw what you think two hanging nails will look like. Then guess. Will the two free ends hang straight down, move together or spread apart? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We use the same magnet and hold it still above the table. We touch the heads of the nails to the same flat face of the magnet, side by side. We measure the gap between the tips with the same ruler held in the same place. Only the nail length changes, so any difference in the gap comes from the length. Each pair is tested three times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Nails have sharp points. Hold them by the head and keep the points away from faces. A grown-up should stay beside a younger child. Keep magnets away from phones and bank cards.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Draw the magnet and the two nails in each try, and draw the V of the hanging tips to scale.
- **results table:** Nails | Gap try 1 (cm) | Gap try 2 (cm) | Gap try 3 (cm) | Middle value (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the magnet and the nails. Ask them to draw what two hanging nails will look like, then to guess and give a reason.  
  *Parent says:* I will hang two nails from the magnet, side by side. Draw what you think they will look like. Will they hang straight, or lean in, or lean out? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Two nails hang from a magnet. Draw them. Will they hang straight? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the nails will hang that way? (good: They are both heavy, so they hang straight down.; They are both pulled to the magnet, so they will lean together.; They push each other apart.)
  - Note: Do not correct the drawing. Most children draw two parallel nails hanging straight down. Keep the drawing to compare with the result.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the magnet still above the table. Touch the heads of the two short nails to its flat face, side by side. Let go gently. Look at the tips.  
  *Parent says:* Touch the heads of two nails to the flat face of the magnet, side by side. Let go gently. What do the tips do? Do they hang straight?  
  *Child mode:* Touch two nail heads to the magnet. Let go gently. What do the tips do?
  - Note: The tips should hang apart in a V, though the gap may be small, around half a centimetre to two centimetres. If the nails will not hang, use a stronger magnet or lighter, thinner nails. If the V is too small to see, hold a sheet of paper behind and look from the side.
  - Check: The two nail tips spread apart. What is the best explanation? → **Both tips became the same kind of pole, so they repel**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the ruler flat on the table under the tips, close to them. Read the gap between the two tips. Do it 3 times, each time hanging the nails again. Write the middle value.  
  *Parent says:* Measure the gap between the tips. Hang them again, and measure again. Three times. The middle one is the number we use.  
  *Child mode:* Measure the gap between the tips. Do it 3 times. Pick the middle number.
  - Ask: Why do we hang the nails again and measure three times? (good: One try can be a fluke.; The gap changes a little each time.; Every nail hangs a little differently each time.)
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same with the two long nails. Read the gap three times. Write the middle value.  
  *Parent says:* Now try the two long nails. Is the V wider or narrower? Why do you think that is?  
  *Child mode:* Hang the two long nails. Measure the gap 3 times. Is the V wider?
  - Note: Usually the long nails have a wider gap at the tips, because the tips are further from the magnet and each other's tips are more nearly free. If your result is the other way round, write it honestly. A weak magnet may hold a long nail with a different tilt, and that is worth noting.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened. Then add a third nail and look at the tips.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened? Then hang a third nail. What do the three tips do?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why. Then add a third nail.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the nails become magnets and their tips repel. What I saw: the tips spread into a V, wider for the long nails. Why: each nail became a magnet with the same pole at its tip, and two of the same pole repel.
### e3 · Soft or Hard? Which steel keeps its magnetism? (40 min, 2 days)
- **question:** If we stroke a nail, a needle, a pin and a paper clip with the same magnet, which one keeps its magnetism the longest?
- **predict:** Before you start, line up the four things from the one you think will keep its magnetism longest to the one that will lose it first. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We stroke each thing the same number of times, 40, with the same magnet, in the same direction. We test each one with the same heap of staples, the same way, at the same times. Only the thing changes, so any difference comes from the steel. Each test is done three times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A needle and a pin are sharp. A grown-up handles them and puts them away afterwards. Staples are small and sharp, so keep them away from children under five. Keep the magnet away from phones and cards.
- **record:** Fill in the table for every thing. Then draw a bar for each thing, as tall as the number of staples it held right after, and another bar, in a different colour, as tall as the number it held the next day.
- **results table:** Thing | Staples right after stroking | After 10 minutes | After 20 taps on the table | The next day
- **schedule:** day 1: Stroke each thing 40 times. Count the staples it lifts right after, after 10 minutes, and after 20 taps. (30 min); day 2: Count the staples each thing lifts the next day. Say what you think, what you saw and why. (10 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Line up the nail, needle, pin and paper clip. Ask your child to put them in order from keeps the magnetism longest to loses it first, and to give a reason. Write the order down.  
  *Parent says:* We will make each of these a magnet with the same magnet, in the same way. Which one will keep its magnetism longest? Which will lose it first? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Line them up. Which keeps the magnetism longest? Which loses it first? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will keep its magnetism longest? (good: It is the thickest.; It is the hardest.; It is made of the best metal.)
  - Note: Do not correct the order. Children often say the biggest, thickest thing will keep it longest. Write the order to compare later.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Test each thing on the heap of staples first, before any stroking. Then stroke the nail 40 times from head to tip along one end of the magnet. Lift the magnet high to come back. Do not stroke back and forth.  
  *Parent says:* First test every thing on the staples before stroking, to see if it was already a magnet. Then stroke the nail from head to tip, 40 times, always the same way. Lift the magnet high to come back.  
  *Child mode:* Test it on the staples first. Then stroke it 40 times, always one way.
  - Note: Stroke each thing in the same direction and use the same end of the same magnet. Count in tens. The needle and pin are sharp, so a grown-up strokes them, holding the eye end down on a table with a finger. Do the nail first.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Right after stroking the nail, dip its tip into the heap of staples and lift. Count how many cling. Write the number. Stroke and test the needle, the pin and the paper clip in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Dip the end into the staples and lift. Count how many cling. Do the same for every thing, as soon as you have stroked it.  
  *Child mode:* Dip the tip in the staples. Lift. Count how many cling. Do this for each one.
  - Note: Test the same end each time, the end that touched the magnet last. A nail might lift 5 to 10 staples. A needle might lift fewer at first, because it is thinner. Write whatever you see.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wait 10 minutes. Count the staples each thing lifts again. Then tap each one on the table 20 times and count again. Write all the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Wait ten minutes. Test again. Then tap each one on the table twenty times, and test again. What changes?  
  *Child mode:* Wait 10 minutes. Test again. Tap each one 20 times. Test again.
  - Check: After 10 minutes the nail lifts fewer staples than before, and the needle lifts almost the same. What does this show? → **The needle is a harder magnetic material than the nail**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The next day, test every thing again on the staples, and write the numbers. Then draw the bar chart.  
  *Parent says:* A day has passed. Test each thing on the staples again. Who still has some magnetism?  
  *Child mode:* A day later, test each one on the staples. Who still holds some?
  - Note: The needle and pin may still hold a few staples. The nail may hold none or one. Your numbers may differ, because the steel in nails, needles and clips differs from maker to maker. Write what you see.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: harder steel keeps its magnetism longer than soft iron. What I saw: the needle still held staples the next day, and the nail held none. Why: in hard steel the domains are held in place, and in soft iron they turn back.

### a1 · Hold Still, Magnet! A hanging magnet and a nail (12 min)
- **record:** Draw the hanging magnet before and after the nail comes near it, and write one sentence about what you saw.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the pencil across the table edge, so it sticks out. Tie the thread to the pencil and hang the magnet from it. Wait until it hangs still. Ask your child to guess what the magnet will do when a nail comes near.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a magnet hanging from a thread. I will hold a nail near it, but not touch it. What will the magnet do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A magnet hangs on a thread. What will it do when a nail comes near? Why?
  - Ask: Will the magnet move, or will it stay still? (good: It will stay still, because the nail is not a magnet.; It will lean towards the nail.; It will move a little.)
  - Note: Keep the table away from any steel. Do not correct the guess. Most children say the magnet will stay still.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the nail about 3 cm from the magnet, level with it, and not touching. Hold it still and watch. Move it a little closer if nothing happens.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the nail still, a few centimetres away. Watch the magnet. What does it do?  
  *Child mode:* Hold the nail still, a little way from the magnet. Watch the magnet.
  - Check: The hanging magnet leans towards the held nail. What does this show? → **The nail pulls the magnet**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now hang the nail from the thread instead, and hold the magnet near it. Watch the nail. Then say what you think the nail and the magnet do to each other.  
  *Parent says:* Now we swap. The nail hangs and I hold the magnet. What does the nail do? What does that tell us about the pull?  
  *Child mode:* Hang the nail. Hold the magnet near it. Which one moves now?
  - Note: The nail swings towards the magnet, and the hanging magnet swung towards the nail. Both are pulled. Whichever one is free to move is the one you see move.
  - Think: Last time the magnet moved. This time the nail moved. Who was doing the pulling each time?
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: the nail pulls the magnet too. What I saw: the hanging magnet leaned to the held nail. Why: the pull works both ways, and the thing that is free to move is the thing that moves.
### a2 · Magnet or Just Iron? The repel test (20 min)
- **record:** A table with five rows, A to E. For each, write what end 1 did and what end 2 did, and your verdict: magnet or plain iron.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up helper stroke one needle for 40 strokes and puts it with the others. The helper picks five pieces. Use a stroked needle, a plain nail, a second magnet, a steel bolt and a steel spoon. They are labelled A to E, so the tester does not know which is which.  
  *Parent says:* I have five pieces for you. Some are real magnets and some are only plain iron or steel. You can only use this hanging magnet. How could you tell?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up hides which pieces are magnets. You have one hanging magnet to test with. How could you tell?
  - Ask: If a piece pulls the hanging magnet, is it a magnet? (good: Not for sure, because plain iron pulls too.; It could be a magnet or plain iron.; A pull could come from a magnet or from plain iron, so a pull proves nothing.)
  - Note: The point is to use a push as proof. Let your child say what they think first. If they say that a pull proves a magnet, let the test show them otherwise.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take piece A. Bring one end near the hanging magnet, a few centimetres away. Does the magnet swing towards the piece or away from it? Write pull or push. Turn the piece round and test the other end. Do the same for B to E.  
  *Parent says:* Test both ends of each piece. Write pull or push for each end. If one end pushes the hanging magnet away, that piece is a real magnet.  
  *Child mode:* Test both ends of each piece. Pull or push? Write it down.
  - Note: Hold each piece at the same distance and keep the tester still. A weak magnet may only push a little. A plain nail pulls at both ends. If a piece moves the hanging magnet at both ends in the same way, call it plain iron.
  - Check: A piece pulls the hanging magnet at one end and pushes it away at the other end. What is it? → **A real magnet**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sort the pieces into two piles, real magnets and plain iron. Write your verdict for each. Then the helper tells you which were magnets. Count how many verdicts were right.  
  *Parent says:* Make two piles. Which pieces pushed? Those are your magnets. Which only pulled? Those are plain iron. Now let's reveal. How many did you get right?  
  *Child mode:* Make two piles. Magnets and plain iron. Now check. How many were right?
  - Note: If a verdict was wrong, look at the notes together. A very weak magnet can look like plain iron. That is why the safest verdict is 'at least one piece pushed' for a magnet and 'only pulled' for 'probably plain iron'.
  - Think: A pull can fool you, and a push cannot. What other tests are good at saying no but not yes?
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap places. Now your child prepares five mystery pieces for the parent, in secret, with letters A to E. The parent tests both ends with the hanging magnet and gives a verdict.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are the teacher. Hide which pieces are magnets, and let me test them. Then tell me if I got them right.  
  *Child mode:* Now you hide the magnets. Let your parent test them. Were they right?
  - Note: Let your child explain the push test back to you in their own words. This is the best way to find out what they have understood.
### a3 · Two Faces of a Fridge Magnet: and cut it in two (25 min)
- **record:** A table: front, back, half 1 front, half 2 front, with the number of staples each held. Draw the cut magnet with the staples on it.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the front of the magnet, which is the side that grips the fridge. Ask your child to guess how many staples the front will pick up, and how many the back will pick up. Then lay the front on the heap of staples and lift it straight up. Count the staples.  
  *Parent says:* The front of this magnet sticks to the fridge. Will the back stick just as well? Guess how many staples each side will lift, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Does the back stick as well as the front? Guess how many staples each lifts. Why?
  - Ask: Do you think the two faces will hold the same number of staples? (good: Yes, it is one magnet.; No, the front is the part that sticks.; The front will hold more staples than the back.)
  - Note: A bendy magnet is weak, so it may lift only a few staples. That is fine. If it lifts none, try tiny paper clips or a pinch of steel wool, or press the magnet on the fridge and note how many sheets of paper it can hold.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn the magnet over and lay the back on the staples. Lift it and count. Write both numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now try the back. How many staples does it lift? Is it the same as the front?  
  *Child mode:* Test the back face on the staples. Count them. Is it the same as the front?
  - Check: The front of a bendy fridge magnet lifts many staples, and the back lifts almost none. What is the best explanation? → **The magnet is made with stripes of north and south, so its field mostly reaches out of the front**
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts the magnet into two equal halves with scissors. Test the front of each half on the staples, and count. Write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Now a grown-up cuts the magnet in two. Is each half still a magnet? Is each half as strong as the whole? Test and see.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts the magnet in two. Test each half on the staples. Is each half a magnet?
  - Ask: Did you get one magnet and one piece of plain rubber? (good: No, both halves are magnets.; Each half has its own strong face.; Each half has its own north and south, so each half is a magnet.)
  - Note: Each half should still lift staples, usually about half as many as the whole, because it has half the area. Cut with sharp scissors by pulling the blade through the rubber in one go. Keep the small pieces away from children under five.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the two halves front to front and slide one over the other slowly. Notice what it feels like. Then press one half to the fridge and count how many sheets of paper it holds. Write what you found.  
  *Parent says:* Slide one half over the other, front to front. Do you feel any bumps or clicks? That feeling is the stripes lining up and slipping past each other.  
  *Child mode:* Slide the two halves over each other. Do you feel little bumps?
  - Note: Not every magnet sheet feels bumpy, because the stripes can be very fine. If you feel nothing, say so. The result is still honest. A magnetic viewing film shows the stripes in science museums.
  - Think: A magnet cut in two gives two magnets, each with its own two sides. What do you think would happen if you cut one of the halves in two again?
### a4 · Magnet Against the Earth: how many grams can your magnet hold? (30 min)
- **record:** A table with the grams of rice each time: nail touching the magnet, nail with one sheet of paper between, and a thick bolt touching the magnet.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the magnet face down at the table edge, so its face overhangs. Stick the nail to its face. Tie the cup to the nail's thread. Ask your child to guess how many grams of rice the magnet will hold, and why.  
  *Parent says:* The Earth is pulling this nail down. The magnet is pulling it up. How much rice can we put in the cup before the Earth wins? Make a guess, and give a reason.  
  *Child mode:* The Earth pulls the nail down. The magnet pulls it up. How much rice can the cup hold? Guess.
  - Ask: How many grams of rice do you think the cup can hold before the nail falls? (good: About 100 grams.; A lot, because the magnet is strong.; Hardly any.)
  - Note: A small ceramic magnet usually holds the nail and some rice, maybe 50 to 300 grams in all. A neodymium magnet may hold much more. Put a cushion on the floor below to catch the cup.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add rice to the cup, one spoonful at a time, slowly. Stop when the nail comes off the magnet. Weigh the cup with the rice, or count the spoonfuls. Repeat three times.  
  *Parent says:* Add a spoonful of rice. Wait. Add another. Keep going slowly. When the nail falls, we know the Earth's pull has beaten the magnet's pull. Write down the weight.  
  *Child mode:* Add rice, a spoonful at a time. When the nail falls, stop. Weigh the rice.
  - Note: One level tablespoon of rice weighs about 10 to 15 grams, so counting spoonfuls gives a rough guess if you have no scale. The weight of the nail and cup counts too, but is small. Write down the biggest weight held, not the first drop.
  - Check: The nail falls when the cup of rice is heavy enough. What does this mean? → **The Earth's pull on the cup, nail and rice has become bigger than the magnet's pull**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put one sheet of paper between the nail and the magnet. Repeat the test. Then use a thick bolt without paper. Write all three weights.  
  *Parent says:* Now a sheet of paper sits between the magnet and the nail. Does the magnet hold more or less? Then we try a thick bolt in place of the nail.  
  *Child mode:* Put a sheet of paper between the magnet and the nail. Test again. Then try a thick bolt.
  - Ask: Why might the magnet hold less when there is paper in between? (good: The paper keeps the nail further from the magnet, and the pull is weaker further away.; There is a small gap.; The pull of a magnet drops quickly with distance.)
  - Note: Expect the paper to cut the weight held a lot, because the pull falls quickly with distance. A thick bolt usually holds more than a thin nail, because there is more iron to magnetise.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write your three numbers. Ask your child which was biggest, which smallest, and why. Then ask what the magnet holds in terms of everyday things, for example a few packets of biscuits.  
  *Parent says:* Which test held the most? Which held the least? What does this tell you about the magnet's pull?  
  *Child mode:* Which held the most? Which held the least? Why?
  - Think: A tiny magnet beats the pull of a whole planet at a gap of a few millimetres. Why do you think it loses at a gap of a metre?
### a5 · Hidden Magnets at Home: where are they, and how do you know? (25 min)

### a6 · Magnetic Memory: write your name in ups and downs (25 min)
- **record:** Your strip with the arrows, the letters written under it, and a note of how many arrows your name used.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the code. Up is 1 and down is 0. Each letter uses five arrows. A is the number 1, B is 2, C is 3, and so on. Write the number in five places: 1 is 00001, 2 is 00010, 3 is 00011, 4 is 00100, 5 is 00101.

  *Child mode:* Up is 1. Down is 0. Each letter is five arrows. A is 00001. B is 00010.
  - Ask: How would you write the letter D with five arrows? (good: D is the number 4, so 00100: down, down, up, down, down.; D is number 4, written 00100 with five arrows.; D is the fourth letter, so its number is 4.)
  - Note: Reading from the right, the places are worth 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16. So 00101 is 4 plus 1, which is 5, the letter E. Help your child read a place at a time, from the right.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write your child's first name in the code. Look up each letter's number, turn it into five arrows, and lay the arrows along the strip. Leave one empty square between letters.  
  *Parent says:* Write your name in ups and downs. Find each letter's number. Turn it into five arrows. Put them along the strip.  
  *Child mode:* Find each letter's number. Write it with five arrows. Lay them on the strip.
  - Note: Letters 1 to 26 need numbers up to 11010 for Z. Take your time. The number for K is 11, which is 01011.
  - Check: In the code, each arrow is up or down. What does an arrow stand for on a real hard disk? → **A tiny region of magnetic material that points one way or the other**
- **s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap strips with a friend or a parent. Each reads the other's name from the arrows. Then ask the closing question.  
  *Parent says:* Swap strips. Read your friend's name from the arrows. One at a time, five arrows, one letter. What does it say?  
  *Child mode:* Swap strips. Read the name. Five arrows make one letter.
  - Note: The answer is hard. A soft material would let the pattern fade. This is why hard disks use hard magnetic coatings.
  - Think: A real hard disk can only keep this pattern if its tiny magnetised regions stay put for years. What kind of magnetic material does that need, soft or hard?
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now mix up some arrows on a strip by flipping three of them. Ask a friend to read it. Talk about what happened to the name, and why a hard disk needs a hard magnetic material.  
  *Parent says:* If a few ups and downs flip by mistake, what happens to the name? Hard disks use hard magnetic materials so that this does not happen.  
  *Child mode:* Flip three arrows on a strip. Read the name now. What went wrong?
  - Think: A soft material would let the arrows flip by themselves. How would a hard disk behave?

### Magnets and Magnetic Materials, Class 4 (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A magnet attracts a steel nail. Which sentence is true?  
   Options: Only the magnet pulls / Only the nail pulls / The magnet and the nail pull each other equally / The bigger one pulls harder  
   **Answer:** The magnet and the nail pull each other equally. *Why:* A pull always comes as a pair of equal forces, one on each thing. Picking 'only the magnet pulls' comes from watching the nail move, because the magnet is usually held in a hand.
2. (mcq) A magnet sits on a light raft, and a nail sits on another raft of the same mass. They float 8 cm apart. What happens?  
   Options: Only the nail raft moves / Only the magnet raft moves / Both rafts move towards each other and meet near the middle / Neither raft moves  
   **Answer:** Both rafts move towards each other and meet near the middle. *Why:* The pulls are equal and the masses are equal, so both rafts move the same distance. Picking one raft moving means forgetting that the pull works both ways.
3. (mcq) The same magnet pulls two rafts, but one raft is three times as heavy. Which statement is right?  
   Options: The heavy raft is pulled three times as hard / The light raft is pulled harder / The pulls are equal, so the light raft moves further / Neither raft moves  
   **Answer:** The pulls are equal, so the light raft moves further. *Why:* The pulls on both are equal. The same pull makes a light raft move faster and further than a heavy raft. The light raft is not pulled harder.
4. (truefalse) Two plain iron nails with no magnet near them pull each other strongly.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A plain nail is not a magnet, so it has no pull of its own. A nail only becomes a magnet when a magnet is near it.

   **Answer:** Hang the magnet on a thread. Bring one end of the piece near, then the other end. If either end pushes the magnet away, the piece is a real magnet. If both ends only pull, it is plain iron.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a push is the proof, and a pull is not.

A strong answer can give an example of each.

1. (mcq) A magnet attracts a nail. What does the nail do to the magnet?  
   Options: Attracts it with an equal force / Nothing / Attracts it with a smaller force / Repels it  
   **Answer:** Attracts it with an equal force. *Why:* Pulls always come in pairs of equal size. The nail seems to do nothing because the magnet is usually held in a hand.
2. (mcq) Two rafts of equal mass carry a magnet and a nail. Where do they meet?  
   Options: In the middle / Close to the magnet's start / Close to the nail's start / They do not meet  
   **Answer:** In the middle. *Why:* The pulls are equal and the masses are equal, so both move the same distance and meet in the middle.
3. (mcq) The same pull acts on a light thing and a heavy thing. Which moves further?  
   Options: The light thing / The heavy thing / They move the same / Neither  
   **Answer:** The light thing. *Why:* A pull makes a light thing speed up more than a heavy thing, so the light thing moves further.
4. (mcq) A steel nail near a magnet picks up a pin. What has happened to the nail?  
   Options: It has become a magnet for as long as the magnet is near / It has become lighter / It has become a different metal / It has become hot  
   **Answer:** It has become a magnet for as long as the magnet is near. *Why:* The magnet turns the domains in the nail to point one way. The nail is now an induced magnet.
5. (mcq) Two pins hang from one pole of a magnet. Their tips spread apart. Why?  
   Options: Both tips are the same pole, so they repel / The pins are bent / The tips attract the table / The pins are heavy  
   **Answer:** Both tips are the same pole, so they repel. *Why:* Both pins become magnets with the same pole at their tips. Two poles of the same kind repel.
6. (mcq) A mystery metal piece pushes a hanging magnet away at one end. What is the piece?  
   Options: A real magnet / Plain iron / Wood / Aluminium  
   **Answer:** A real magnet. *Why:* Only a magnet can push another magnet away.
7. (mcq) A mystery piece pulls the hanging magnet at both ends and never pushes. What is it most likely?  
   Options: Plain iron or steel / A strong magnet / A plastic spoon / A rubber band  
   **Answer:** Plain iron or steel. *Why:* A plain magnetic piece pulls at both ends. A plastic spoon or rubber band would not move the magnet at all.
8. (mcq) A steel needle keeps its pins for days. A plain nail drops its pins in minutes. Which is the harder magnetic material?  
   Options: The needle / The nail / They are the same / Neither is a magnetic material  
   **Answer:** The needle. *Why:* In the needle the domains stay turned. In the nail they turn back.
9. (mcq) What should the core of an electromagnet in a crane be made of?  
   Options: Soft iron / Hard steel / Wood / Glass  
   **Answer:** Soft iron. *Why:* Soft iron loses its magnetism when the current stops, so the crane can drop its load.
10. (mcq) Which can weaken a magnet?  
   Options: Heating it, hammering it, or putting a strong opposite magnet near it / Holding a nail for a long time / Putting paper over it / Keeping it in a dry box  
   **Answer:** Heating it, hammering it, or putting a strong opposite magnet near it. *Why:* Heat, hard knocks and strong opposite fields shake the domains. Holding a nail takes nothing from a magnet.
11. (mcq) Why are some bar magnets stored with an iron keeper across their poles?  
   Options: It gives the field an easy loop, which helps the magnet stay strong / It makes the magnet lighter / It stops rust / It makes the pull stronger on the table  
   **Answer:** It gives the field an easy loop, which helps the magnet stay strong. *Why:* A keeper closes the loop of the field. This stops the magnet's own poles from turning its domains backwards.
12. (mcq) A bendy fridge magnet grips on its front and hardly on its back. What is the reason?  
   Options: Thin stripes of north and south make the field reach out of the front only / The back is made of rubber and the front of steel / The front is a bigger magnet / The back is dirty  
   **Answer:** Thin stripes of north and south make the field reach out of the front only. *Why:* The maker magnetises thin stripes side by side. The loops between neighbours reach out of one face and cancel at the other.
13. (mcq) A hard disk stores information as tiny regions magnetised up or down. Why must the coating be a hard magnetic material?  
   Options: So the pattern stays for years / So the disk is heavy / So the disk is shiny / So the disk is hot  
   **Answer:** So the pattern stays for years. *Why:* A soft material would let the pattern fade. Hard material keeps the domains turned.
14. (mcq) A rubbed comb picks up paper scraps but not a steel pin. What does that show?  
   Options: The comb's pull is static electricity and not magnetism / The comb is a strong magnet / The pin is too heavy / The paper is magnetic  
   **Answer:** The comb's pull is static electricity and not magnetism. *Why:* A magnet picks up pins. The comb does not, so its pull is a different one.
15. (mcq) Scientists agree that a magnetic field works as a description of the pull around a magnet. What do they still argue about?  
   Options: Exactly what a field is / Whether magnets attract iron / Whether nails can be magnetised / Whether pulls come in pairs  
   **Answer:** Exactly what a field is. *Why:* The pulls, the pairs and the nails are well tested. What a field is, underneath, is still being discussed.

## Applications
- Scrapyard cranes with electromagnets lift cars and steel, and drop them when the electricity is switched off.
- Electric bells, relays and door locks use a soft iron core that is a magnet only while current flows.
- Cupboard catches and fridge door seals use permanent magnets that pull a door shut and let go to a finger.
- Hard disks, cassette tapes and old bank cards store information in tiny magnetised regions of hard magnetic material.
- Loudspeakers and earphones use a permanent magnet and a coil of wire to turn electricity into sound.
- Electric fans, mixers and washing machines have motors with soft iron cores and permanent magnets.
- Transformers use thin sheets of soft iron to carry the changing magnetic field from one coil to another.
- Engineers listen to the crackle of magnetised steel to check whether a steel part was heated properly.
- Magnetic locks on shop doors hold a door shut with an electromagnet and open when the current stops.
- Magnetic separators in recycling plants and flour mills pull iron out of mixed waste and food.
- Magnetic levitation trains float on a cushion of magnetic force, with an equal and opposite force on the track.

## Open questions
- **What is a magnetic field, underneath?** Scientists can describe it very well and light is a wave of it. Whether it is a thing in its own right or only a very good description is still argued.
- **Why is gravity so much weaker than the magnetic force?** Physicists have measured the difference, but nobody yet knows why the forces have the strengths that they do.
- **Can we make a very strong magnet from common, cheap elements?** The strongest magnets use rare earth metals, which are hard to dig and separate. Scientists are looking for new materials that do not need them.
- **How exactly do the edges of domains move when a magnet is brought near?** Scientists can see domain edges with special microscopes. Predicting how every flip happens in a real, messy piece of steel is still hard.
- **Is there a limit to how much information a magnetic disk can hold?** The tiny regions cannot be made too small or heat makes them flip. Engineers keep finding new tricks, and nobody is sure where the limit lies.
- **Why does the magnet pull in pairs at all?** The rule about pairs of forces has never failed in a test. Why nature is built this way is a question physics describes but does not answer.

## Connected maths
- The magnet raft and the nail raft start 8 centimetres apart. The nail raft is three times as heavy. The lighter raft moves 3 times as far as the heavier one. How far does each raft move before they meet? **The magnet raft moves 6 centimetres. The heavy raft moves 2 centimetres.**. Together they must cover 8 centimetres. The light raft moves 3 parts and the heavy raft moves 1 part, so there are 4 parts. 8 divided by 4 is 2 centimetres a part. The heavy raft moves 1 part, which is 2 centimetres. The light raft moves 3 parts, which is 6 centimetres. 6 plus 2 is 8, which checks. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A magnet held 240 grams of rice. With one sheet of paper between the magnet and the nail it held 80 grams. How many times as much did it hold with no paper? **3 times as much.**. 240 divided by 80 is 3. So the magnet held 3 times as much with no paper. Check: 80 plus 80 plus 80 is 240. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- After stroking, a needle held 6 staples. The next day it held 5. What fraction of its staples did it still hold? And what fraction had it lost? **It still held five sixths and had lost one sixth.**. It started with 6 staples. 5 out of 6 is five sixths. It lost 1 out of 6, which is one sixth. Five sixths plus one sixth makes one whole. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- The nail held 9 staples right after stroking, and 2 the next day. The needle held 6, then 5. Which one lost a bigger fraction of its staples? **The nail lost a bigger fraction.**. The nail lost 7 out of 9, which is more than three quarters. The needle lost 1 out of 6, which is less than a fifth. So the nail lost a much bigger fraction, even though it started with more staples. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In the code for the magnetic memory, each letter is a number from 1 to 26, written with five arrows. Up is 1 and down is 0. The places are worth 16, 8, 4, 2 and 1. M is the 13th letter. Write 13 with five arrows, using U for up and D for down. **D U U D U.**. 13 is 8 plus 4 plus 1. So the 8 place, the 4 place and the 1 place are up, and the 16 place and the 2 place are down. In order, the arrows are down, up, up, down, up. That is 01101. (queues `math.number-sense.place-value.L4`)
- A toy maker makes 200 magnetic trains. Each train needs 1 small magnet that costs 6 rupees and 3 steel nails that cost 1 rupee each. How much does she spend on magnets and nails for all 200 trains? **1,800 rupees.**. One train needs 6 rupees for the magnet and 3 rupees for the nails, which is 9 rupees. 200 trains cost 200 times 9. 200 times 9 is 1,800 rupees. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L4`)
- Made-up results: nails of length 2 cm, 4 cm and 6 cm gave tip gaps of 0.5 cm, 1 cm and 1.5 cm. If the pattern continued, what tip gap would a 10 cm nail give? Why might a real nail give less? **2.5 centimetres. A real nail may give less because the pull between the tips gets weaker with distance, and the nail's own weight pulls the tips down.**. The gap grows by 0.5 cm for every 2 cm of nail. 10 cm is 5 steps of 2 cm, so the gap is 5 times 0.5, which is 2.5 cm. Real patterns often flatten out as the numbers get bigger. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- Look at the kitchen scale. The pointer is halfway between the 200 gram mark and the 250 gram mark. A cup of rice and a nail hang from a magnet that just lets go at that reading. How many grams is that? **225 grams.**. Halfway between 200 and 250 is 200 plus half of 50. Half of 50 is 25. 200 plus 25 is 225 grams. The weight is the Earth's pull on the cup, nail and rice. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L4`)
- The first train used a magnet that made the carriage rush from 5 cm away. The second used a weaker magnet that started to pull only from 3 cm away. How many centimetres closer must the carriage start with the weaker magnet? **2 centimetres closer.**. 5 take away 3 is 2. The carriage must start 2 centimetres closer with the weaker magnet. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
