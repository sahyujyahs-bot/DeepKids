# The charge ladder: which materials give electrons, which take them, and where the charge goes
`sci.electricity.static-and-charge.L4`

**Summary.** A plain hand pulls a charged balloon, and so does a charged ruler. So how can you tell which one is really charged? Only a push proves it. In this unit you rub six materials on wool and on polythene, and use the pushes to build a ladder of who gives electrons and who takes them. A material in the middle gives to the rungs below it and takes from the rungs above it. So plus and minus describe how two things meet, not what one thing is. You also follow the charge as it runs through metal and a body into the Earth, and find out why that matters in a printing press, a petrol tanker and a storm.

**Intro.** *Rub a balloon on your hair and hang it from a thread. Hold your open hand near it. The balloon swings towards your hand. Now hold a ruler you rubbed on wool near it. The balloon swings away. One thing pulled and one thing pushed. Which of them proves that something is charged?* Your hand has no extra charge, yet it pulled the balloon. The ruler pushed, and a push needs charge on both sides. In this unit you use pushes to find out which materials give away electrons and which take them. You hang a foil ball that changes its mind, peel tape into twins, and build a ladder from your own results. Then you follow the charge as it runs away through a steel spoon or a hand, and find out why a printing press in Sivakasi and a petrol tanker both need that to happen.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that rubbing only moves electrons from one thing to another, so one thing gains exactly what the other loses.
1. I can explain why only a push, not a pull, proves that a thing is charged.
2. I can rank materials by which takes electrons and which gives them, and use my ladder to predict a pair I have not tried.
3. I can explain why a material in the middle of the ladder can give electrons to one partner and take them from another.
4. I can explain how charge runs through conductors, stays on insulators, and is carried away by earthing, damp air or a sharp point.
5. I can explain that the push between charges gets weaker quickly with distance and stronger with more charge.
6. I can explain how a spark and lightning happen, and why static helps in a printer and is dangerous near fuel and dust.

## Terms
- **Electron**: A tiny particle with a minus charge that circles the middle of an atom. Electrons are the charges that move when you rub things. *e.g.* When a balloon is rubbed on wool, electrons move from the wool to the balloon.
- **Plus and minus charge**: The two kinds of electric charge. Two of the same kind push apart. One of each kind pull together. *e.g.* A balloon rubbed on wool has extra minus charge, so it pushes away another rubbed balloon.
- **Neutral**: Having equal plus and minus charge, so there is no extra charge to feel from far away. *e.g.* A plain steel spoon is neutral until something gives it or takes from it a charge.
- **Conservation of charge**: Charge is never made or destroyed. It only moves from one place to another, so what one thing gains, another loses. *e.g.* If the balloon gains a million electrons from the wool, the wool loses exactly a million.
- **Contact charging**: Charge moving between two materials when they touch and then come apart. Rubbing helps because it makes more touching. *e.g.* Clear tape pulled off a table leaves both the tape and the table with a charge.
- **Charge ladder**: A list of materials in order. A material higher on the ladder gives electrons to one lower down. The scientific name is the triboelectric series. *e.g.* On most ladders wool is near the top and polythene is near the bottom, so wool gives electrons to polythene.
- **Repel**: To push away. Only two things with the same kind of charge repel each other. *e.g.* Two balloons rubbed on hair hang apart in a V, because they repel.
- **Polarisation**: When a charged thing comes near a neutral one, the neutral thing's plus and minus charges shift a little, so the near side has the opposite charge. This is why a charged thing can pull a neutral one. *e.g.* A rubbed balloon pulls a bit of paper, even though the paper has no extra charge.
- **Conductor**: A material that lets charge move through it easily. *e.g.* A steel spoon is a conductor, so a charge on it runs away into your hand.
- **Insulator**: A material that does not let charge move through it easily, so charge stays where it was put. *e.g.* A plastic spoon is an insulator, so a charge on it stays put.
- **Earthing**: Joining something to the ground with a conductor, so any extra charge can flow away into the huge Earth. *e.g.* A petrol tanker trails a metal chain or strap on the road, so the charge from its movement can drain into the ground.
- **Discharge**: When extra charge leaves a charged thing, slowly through the air or a conductor, or suddenly as a spark. *e.g.* A charged foil ball discharges when you touch it with your finger.
- **Spark**: A sudden jump of charge through air, which glows and crackles because the air along the path is heated. *e.g.* A tiny spark may jump from your finger to a metal door handle on a dry day.
- **Volt**: A unit for how strongly charge is pushed to move. A bigger number of volts can push a spark across a bigger gap of air. *e.g.* A torch cell is 1.5 volts. A spark you can feel needs about 3,000 volts.
- **Electric field**: The space around a charge where another charge feels a push or a pull. *e.g.* A rubbed balloon has an electric field, so it can lift your arm hairs without touching them.
- **Ion**: An atom or a piece of a molecule that has gained or lost electrons, so it carries a charge. *e.g.* Salt in water splits into ions, which is why salty water carries charge.

## Explanations
### Rubbing moves electrons, and charge is never made · *defined*
**Definition:** Rubbing, or touching and pulling apart, moves electrons from one material to another. The total charge stays the same, so one material gains exactly what the other loses.

Every atom has a plus centre, made of protons, and minus electrons around it. The protons are locked in the middle of the atom and stay put. Some electrons on the outside can be moved. When two different materials touch and come apart, some electrons stay with one and are left behind by the other. Now one has extra minus charge, and the other has missing minus, which acts like extra plus. Nothing was made. Charge only moved. Michael Faraday showed in 1843 that the two charges always come in equal pairs, by catching a charged thing inside a metal pail and measuring.
- e.g. A balloon rubbed on hair takes electrons from the hair, so the balloon is minus and the hair is plus.
- e.g. Clear tape peeled off a table leaves one side of the pair minus and the other plus, in equal amounts.
- e.g. A comb that pulls paper has taken or given a tiny fraction of its electrons, not made new ones.
### Only a push proves a thing is charged · *defined*
**Definition:** Two charged things with the same kind of charge repel. A pull can come from a charge on one thing and nothing on the other, so only repelling shows that both are charged the same way.

A charged balloon pulls on a plain, neutral hand or wall. That surprises people. The balloon's charge shifts the neutral thing's own plus and minus charges a little, so the near side gets the opposite charge and the pull wins slightly. This is called polarisation. So a pull cannot tell you if the other thing is charged. A push can. The only way to push a minus balloon away is with another minus charge. That is why a scientist who wants to test a charge looks for repelling, and why the tests in this unit all watch for a push.
- e.g. A rubbed balloon swings towards a plain hand, so the pull does not prove the hand is charged.
- e.g. A rubbed balloon swings away from a ruler rubbed on wool, so both have the same kind of charge.
- e.g. A bit of paper jumps to a comb because of polarisation, not because the paper is charged.
### The charge ladder · *defined*
**Definition:** A charge ladder lists materials in order. When two are rubbed together, the one higher up gives electrons and the one lower down takes them. Its scientific name is the triboelectric series.

If you rub many pairs of materials and note who gets the minus charge each time, an order appears. Wool and hair are near the top. They give electrons to almost everything. Polythene, PVC and Teflon are near the bottom, and take electrons from almost everything. Paper and cotton are in the middle, and charge weakly with each other. The ladder lets you predict a pair you have never tried: whichever is lower gets the minus. Published ladders agree about the top and the bottom. They disagree about neighbours, and the order shifts with dryness, how clean the surface is and how hard you rub. So a ladder is a good guide for far-apart materials, and a poor one for close neighbours.
- e.g. Wool rubbed on polythene: wool is higher, so polythene gets the minus.
- e.g. Cotton rubbed on a plastic ruler: cotton is higher, so the ruler gets the minus.
- e.g. Paper rubbed on cotton: they are neighbours, so the charge is weak and the answer unreliable.
### A material in the middle both gives and takes · *defined*
**Definition:** On a ladder, a material gives electrons to every rung below it and takes electrons from every rung above it. Plus and minus therefore depend on the partner, not only on the material.

Take cotton. Rub it with wool, and wool is higher, so the cotton takes electrons and becomes minus. Rub the same cotton with polythene, and polythene is lower, so the cotton gives electrons and becomes plus. The cotton did not change. Its partner did. So you cannot call cotton a plus material or a minus material. You can only say what it does against a partner. Only the two ends of the ladder always do the same: the top gives and the bottom takes. Everything between is both, depending on who it meets.
- e.g. Cotton is minus against wool and plus against polythene.
- e.g. Paper is minus against hair and plus against PVC.
- e.g. Wool is plus against nearly everything, because it sits near the top.
### Plus and minus are names chosen before electrons were found · *defined*
**Definition:** The names plus and minus were chosen in the 1700s. Later, electrons were found to be the particles that move, and they carry the charge called minus.

Charles du Fay, in Paris in 1733, found that there are two kinds of charge. Benjamin Franklin, in 1747, said there is one electric fluid, and a thing has either too much of it or too little. He guessed that glass rubbed with silk gets too much, so he called its charge plus. In 1897 J. J. Thomson in Cambridge found the electron, a small particle that carries the charge now called minus. Glass rubbed with silk actually loses electrons, so Franklin had it backwards. The names stayed, because they work in the sums. So an electron has a minus charge only because of a guess made 150 years before it was found.
- e.g. Glass rubbed with silk loses electrons, so it is plus.
- e.g. A balloon rubbed on hair gains electrons, so it is minus.
- e.g. Moving electrons are minus charges, even though the name was chosen by guessing.
### Why does rubbing move charge? Scientists still argue · *debated*
Everyone agrees that touching two materials and pulling them apart can move charge. Nobody agrees yet on exactly what moves, or why one material wins over another. Even the same material can charge itself: in 2011 a team led by Bartosz Grzybowski found that one piece of plastic can have patches of plus and minus on its own surface, like a mosaic. And in 2014 researchers found that small grains of one material charged larger grains of the very same material. A simple ladder cannot explain that.
- e.g. Clear tape peeled from the same roll has plus and minus patches.
- e.g. Small grains of one material can charge larger grains of that same material.
- e.g. Water on a surface can change the charge a material takes.
- **The electron view:** Materials hold their outer electrons with different strengths. When two touch, electrons move from the one that holds them loosely to the one that holds them more strongly.
- **The ion view:** Thin films of water on every surface carry ions. These ions may move from one surface to the other, and the surface that gains the ions gets the charge. This view explains why humidity changes results so much.
- **The material view:** Tiny bits of one material may be torn off and stick to the other, carrying their charge with them. Some measurements show this happening. In other measurements it does not.
### Conductors carry charge away, insulators keep it · *defined*
**Definition:** A conductor lets charge move through it freely. An insulator holds charge where it is.

Metals have outer electrons that are free to drift from atom to atom, so a charge put on one end of a steel spoon spreads out and runs into whatever touches it. Plastic, dry wood, glass and rubber hold their electrons tightly, so a charge put on one spot stays there. Your body is a conductor, because it is full of salty water. That is why you cannot charge a steel spoon by rubbing it while holding it in your hand. The charge runs through you to the floor. It is also why a charged plastic ruler loses its charge when you touch it with a wet finger.
- e.g. A steel spoon held in the hand cannot stay charged.
- e.g. A plastic comb keeps its charge for a minute or more on a dry day.
- e.g. A wet finger removes a charge in an instant.
### Earthing: the Earth as a giant store · *defined*
**Definition:** Joining a charged thing to the ground with a conductor lets its extra charge flow into the Earth, which is so big that it hardly changes.

The Earth is a conductor, and it is enormous. If you give it a few billion extra electrons, or take a few billion away, nothing about it changes that anyone can measure. So it works like a bottomless drain or a bottomless store. When you touch a charged metal thing while standing on the floor, any extra charge can run through you to the Earth. This is why a petrol tanker trails a metal chain or strap, why a three-pin plug has an earth pin, and why an aircraft has small wire wicks on its wings. It is also why touching an earthed metal pipe calms the static on a paper sheet.
- e.g. A petrol tanker trails an earthing strap so charge cannot build up.
- e.g. A three-pin plug has a long earth pin joined to the building's earth.
- e.g. A person touching a metal almirah on a dry day may feel a small spark as charge drains away.
### The push gets weaker quickly with distance · *defined*
**Definition:** The push or pull between two charges gets bigger with more charge and much smaller with more distance. Twice as far apart means only a quarter of the force.

Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured this in France in 1785, with a very sensitive hanging balance. Two charges twice as far apart push a quarter as hard. Three times as far apart, a ninth as hard. Scientists call this an inverse square law. Sunlight also follows it: twice as far from a torch means the same light spread over four times the area. Gravity follows it too. The reason is geometry: whatever spreads out from a point in all directions thins out over the surface of a bigger and bigger sphere. This is why a balloon lifts paper at 1 centimetre but not at 20, and why the charge on a single balloon can lift your hair but not move the table.
- e.g. A balloon that lifts paper at 1 centimetre has a quarter of the pull at 2 centimetres.
- e.g. Two balloons with twice the charge each push four times as hard at the same distance.
- e.g. A torch's light on a wall is a quarter as bright twice as far away.
### A sharp point lets charge escape · *defined*
**Definition:** Charge crowds at sharp points of a conductor. The electric field there is strong enough to tear electrons from air, so charge leaks away into the air.

On a round metal ball, charge spreads out evenly. On a pointed piece of metal, charge crowds towards the tip, and the push on the air there is very strong. The air near the tip breaks down a little and carries the charge off in a faint, silent stream. Benjamin Franklin noticed this in the 1740s, and it is the reason a lightning conductor has a pointed top. It is also why the tinsel bars in printing presses are made of thin metal threads: each thread is a very fine point that lets charge leak away from passing paper. Scientists call this a corona discharge.
- e.g. A lightning conductor on a building has a pointed tip.
- e.g. A printing press uses a bar of fine metal threads to remove charge from paper.
- e.g. A pin held near a charged balloon makes the balloon lose its charge quickly.
### Sparks and lightning · *defined*
**Definition:** A spark happens when the push on the air between two charged things is so strong that the air suddenly lets charge through. Lightning is the same, on a giant scale.

Air is an insulator, until the push on it is strong enough. That takes about three million volts across each metre of air, or about 3,000 volts for each millimetre. A spark you can just feel from a door handle is across about one millimetre, so it takes about 3,000 volts. It hurts little because the amount of charge is very small and it passes in a flash. Lightning has hundreds of millions of volts and a huge amount of charge, so it is deadly. A bolt starts as a faint feeler that steps down from the cloud. When it is close to the ground, a bright return stroke travels up the same path from the ground to the cloud. The flash we see is the return stroke. The heated air bursts outward, which we hear as thunder.
- e.g. A static shock from a car door is a small spark.
- e.g. A lightning flash is a spark many kilometres long.
- e.g. Thunder is the sound of air heated to about 30,000 degrees Celsius bursting outward.
### Static at work and static as a danger · *defined*
**Definition:** Static charge is used to put powder or paint exactly where we want it, and it is dangerous where sparks can set fire to fuel vapour or fine dust.

A laser printer charges a drum with a pattern, and the charged toner powder sticks only to the pattern. A paint spray gives each droplet the same charge, so droplets push apart into a fine mist and are pulled to the object being painted. A power station chimney can pass its smoke through charged plates so that the dust sticks to the plates instead of going into the sky. The same push is a problem in a flour mill or a fireworks factory, where one spark can ignite fine dust. At a petrol pump, fuel flowing through a hose builds up charge, and a spark could set fire to the vapour. So these places keep the air damp, wear cotton, and earth every metal part.
- e.g. A printer's toner sticks only where the drum is charged.
- e.g. A power station's chimney plates catch dust from smoke.
- e.g. A fuel tanker, a flour mill and a firework factory all earth their metal parts.
### Living things and electric fields · *idea*
The air near the ground has a faint electric field, about 100 volts for every metre of height. In 2013 Dominic Clarke and colleagues showed that bumblebees can sense the weak electric field around a flower, and learn to tell flowers apart by it. A flying bee collects a small plus charge from the air, and a flower is usually minus, so pollen may jump to her without her touching it. In 2018 Erica Morley and Daniel Robert showed that spiders lift off on silk threads in an electric field, so charge helps them fly. Some fish, sharks and the platypus sense the fields made by other animals. Humans have no sense for it. Scientists do not yet know how common this is, or how many animals use it.
- e.g. A bumblebee senses the electric field of a flower before landing.
- e.g. A spider lifts on a silk thread in an electric field.
- e.g. A shark finds a hidden fish by the faint field the fish's muscles make.
- **Researchers in this field:** Many animals live in a world full of electric fields and use them to find food and to move. People simply cannot sense them, so we had to build instruments to find out.
- **Careful sceptics:** Some of the animal studies are small, and an animal reacting to a field does not prove she feels it as we feel touch. More careful tests are still being done.

## Mostly agreed
- Rubbing, or touching and pulling apart, moves electrons from one material to another, and the protons stay in the middle of the atoms. *How we know:* A charge on a rubbed thing always has an equal and opposite charge on the thing it was rubbed with, as Faraday's pail experiment of 1843 and every later measurement show.
- Charge is never made or destroyed. It is only moved from one place to another. *How we know:* No experiment has ever found charge appearing from nothing or vanishing, and measurements of charge have been made to extreme accuracy.
- There are two kinds of charge. The same kinds repel, and different kinds attract. *How we know:* Du Fay's gold-leaf tests of 1733 and every test since: a rubbed glass tube and a rubbed resin rod act in opposite ways on the same leaf.
- The force between two charges gets four times smaller each time the distance doubles. *How we know:* Coulomb's balance measurements of 1785, and modern tests of this law that have found no difference from it.
- Charge moves freely through metals and through salty water, and hardly at all through dry plastic, glass or rubber. *How we know:* Measurements of how easily a charge flows through many materials, which sort them into conductors and insulators.
- Lightning is a giant spark of electricity from charge that has separated in a storm cloud. *How we know:* In 1752 sparks were drawn from an iron rod during a storm, and modern cameras and radio detectors record the current in every flash.

## How we found out
**Charles du Fay in Paris, Benjamin Franklin in Philadelphia and J. J. Thomson in Cambridge, 1733 (du Fay's two kinds of charge), 1747 (Franklin's plus and minus) and 1897 (Thomson's electron).** In the early 1700s, people knew that rubbed amber and glass pulled light things towards them. Most thought this was one kind of electric power. In 1733 Charles du Fay in Paris hung a thin sheet of gold leaf on a thread. He touched it with a rubbed glass tube, and the glass then pushed it away. Then he held a rubbed piece of resin near, and the leaf rushed towards it. So there were two kinds of charge, and the same kinds push apart. In 1747 Benjamin Franklin in Philadelphia suggested there was only one electric fluid, and a thing had too much or too little of it. He guessed that glass rubbed with silk had too much, and called that plus. In 1897 J. J. Thomson in Cambridge found the electron, the small particle that moves. Rubbed glass loses electrons. Franklin's guess was backwards, but the names plus and minus stayed.  
*What people believed before:* People thought rubbing made only one kind of electric power, and later that the glass gained a fluid. Du Fay's gold leaf, pushed by glass and pulled by resin, showed two kinds. The electron, found in 1897, showed that rubbed glass loses something instead of gaining it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Friction makes the charge.** (Many students and some old textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: Rubbing makes more charge than gentle touching, and rubbing warms things.  
  Against: Clear tape pulled off a table is charged, and nobody rubbed it. Touching and separating is enough. Rubbing only helps by putting more of the two surfaces in contact. The charge is moved, not made by heat.
- **Static electricity and the electricity in wires are two different kinds.** (Many scientists in the early 1800s; *rejected*)  
  For: A rubbed rod made sparks, and a battery made a steady push. They looked and behaved very differently.  
  Against: In 1833 Michael Faraday compared electricity from rubbing, from batteries, from magnets, from heat and from electric fish. He found they all did the same things. It is the same charge, behaving differently because of how it is arranged and moving.
- **Two pieces of the same material cannot charge each other.** (Textbooks for many decades; *debated*)  
  For: A ladder puts the same material at the same rung, so neither should win.  
  Against: In 2011 Baytekin and colleagues, and in 2014 Waitukaitis and colleagues, found that identical materials can charge each other, because their surfaces are patchy or because of their size. Our simple ladder does not explain it.
- **Lightning never strikes the same place twice.** (A common saying; *rejected*)  
  For: Most places are hit rarely, and a strike can seem like a once-only event.  
  Against: Tall pointed things are hit again and again. The Empire State Building in New York is struck about twenty times a year.
- **Electric charge is a fluid that is poured from one thing to another.** (Benjamin Franklin and many of his time; *rejected*)  
  For: It explained that charge can flow, that it is not made or lost, and it let Franklin make good predictions.  
  Against: Charge is carried by particles, electrons mostly, which cannot be divided into tiny drops of fluid. The fluid picture was useful, but it led to a wrong guess about which way charge moves when glass is rubbed.

## Roots
We have built a ladder of who gives electrons and who takes them, and followed charge into the ground. Now we ask what the ladder tells us about what a thing is.

**Deep truth.** Cotton took electrons from wool and gave them to polythene. So cotton is neither a plus material nor a minus material. Plus and minus only describe how two things meet. Alone, a thing has no sign.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call a material positive or negative, as if the label belonged to the thing by itself. The ladder shows that the same cotton is minus against wool and plus against polythene, so the label belongs to the pair.  
*Evidence:* In the Charge Ladder, each material was rubbed on both wool and polythene, and the pushes on the balloon showed which way the electrons went. A material in the middle of the finished ladder takes from the rung above it and gives to the rung below it.

- *who am i:* Your body is a conductor, so charge does not stay on you for long. It runs through you into the floor, and you are a path as well as a person.
- *what is life:* A flying bee collects a small plus charge from the air, and a flower joined to the ground holds the opposite kind. Pollen can leap between them, so living things are part of the ladder too.
- *what is this world:* A spark, a tanker's chain and a lightning bolt all end in the same place, the Earth, which is large enough to take charge from anything.

One piece of cotton has no sign of its own. Put it next to wool, and it takes electrons. Put it next to polythene, and it gives them away. Charge never appeared from nowhere in any of your tests. It only moved, and the ground is the biggest place it can go. A clinging sheet, a spark and a lightning bolt are all the same thing, charge moving between things that happen to meet.

- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika, chapter 15:** Nagarjuna argued that nothing has a nature of its own that stays the same whatever it meets. Things appear only in dependence on other things. (The same cotton gave electrons to one partner and took them from another. Its sign was not in the cotton alone. It was in the meeting.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 2:** Being and non-being give birth to each other. Hard and easy complete each other. Long and short shape each other, and high and low rest on each other. (A rung on the ladder is high only when something lower is there to give to, and low only when something higher is there to take from.)
- Science (settled): Only the two ends of the charge ladder always give or always take. A material in the middle gives to the rungs below it and takes from the rungs above it. Published ladders agree about the ends and disagree about close neighbours.
- Science (debated): Why touching two materials moves charge between them is not yet agreed. Electrons, ions and tiny pieces of material have each been seen moving, and the same material can even charge itself.
- Other view, Many materials scientists: Each material does have its own nature. It holds its outer electrons with a certain strength, and that can be measured. The ladder simply shows those strengths in order. A sign changes with the partner, but the strength belongs to the thing.
- Practice, Giver and taker (3 min): Sit comfortably and close your eyes. Breathe in slowly and think that this air was made by plants. Breathe out slowly and think that this air goes to plants. Do this five times. Are you a giver or a taker?
- Ask: The same cotton is plus with one partner and minus with another. Is anything plus or minus all on its own?
- Ask: Charge is never made or destroyed. Where did the balloon's extra electrons come from, and where would they go if you touched it with a wet finger?
- Ask: A bee and a flower hold opposite charges, and pollen jumps across. Which of them is the giver and which is the taker?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Cotton takes electrons from wool and gives them to polythene. Is cotton a plus material or a minus material? (hint: Think about what changed between the two rubs, and what did not.)
- *deep:* Charge cannot be made or destroyed. Where was the minus charge on your balloon before you rubbed it? (hint: Think about the wool or hair, and about what a neutral thing really holds.)
- *deep:* A charged balloon pulls a plain wall. How does a wall with no extra charge feel the balloon, and how fast does it find out when the balloon moves? (hint: Think about polarisation and the electric field around the balloon.)
- *big:* Plus and minus were chosen by a guess, and the guess turned out backwards. If a name is wrong but works, should we change it? (hint: Think about what a name is for, and what it would cost to change every book and every wire.)
- *big:* A bee can feel a flower's electric field and you cannot. Does that mean you and the bee live in different worlds? (hint: Think about what a sense does, and what you can know that a bee cannot.)
- *big:* Over two thousand people in India die each year from lightning, mostly farmers working in open fields. Whose job is it to warn them, and how could the science help? (hint: Think about who gets warnings in time, and which of them can see a storm coming.)
- *big:* Static is a nuisance in a printing press and a tool in a laser printer. Is static good or bad? (hint: Think about whether a thing can be harmful by itself, or only in a place.)
- *big:* People have known about rubbed amber since ancient times, and scientists still argue about why it works. Why might a simple everyday thing stay a mystery? (hint: Think about how hard it is to see what happens on a surface a few atoms thick.)

## Story: The Sheets That Would Not Lie Flat
*A made-up story set in a real town. Sivakasi is famous for printing and fireworks. The science is real: paper pulled over rollers picks up charge, and presses use an earthed bar of fine metal threads to take it away. The people and the press are imagined.*

In Sivakasi, in the weeks before Deepavali, the printing presses never slept. Thenmozhi was ten. Every afternoon she carried her mother's lunch to the Lakshmi Offset Press in a steel tiffin carrier, and sat on a stool by the wall while her mother worked.

Her mother was Karpagam, and she ran the big printing machine. It took in a thin sheet of paper at one end. It printed red, green and gold on it, with a roar and a clatter. Then it slid the sheet out into a tray at the other end, and the sheet was a label for a box of crackers. A thousand sheets an hour should have made a neat, flat pile.

But that August the sheets would not lie flat. They slid out of the machine and rose at their edges like petals. They leaned away from each other. Some slid sideways off the pile, and some stuck to the iron frame of the tray. Karpagam had to straighten the pile by hand every few minutes, and the machine had to slow down. The orders were getting late.

Mr. Raghunathan, the owner, walked up and down with his hands behind his back. "It is the paper," he said. "The new supplier has sent us bad paper." So he brought out the old paper from the store. It did exactly the same.

"Then it is the machine," he said. He called Murugan, the mechanic, who opened the machine and looked for three hours. He found nothing wrong.

Thenmozhi ate her lunch and watched. She noticed a few things that nobody else was looking at. The sheets on top of the pile stood up the most. They leaned away from each other, like people who have just quarrelled. When she held out her arm, a loose sheet floated up and clung to her nylon sleeve, and the fine hairs on her arm stood up.

"Amma," she said, "the sheets do not like each other. They push."

"Paper does not push," said her mother, not unkindly. "Finish your lunch."

But Thenmozhi remembered something from school. Her science teacher, Mrs. Selvarani, had hung two balloons from one thread. She had rubbed both on her hair, and they swung apart. "Only things with the same kind of charge push each other away," Mrs. Selvarani had said.

So if the sheets were pushing, they all had the same kind of charge. But where did they get it? Thenmozhi looked at the rollers. Every sheet was pulled over the same rollers, a thousand times an hour. That was rubbing, wasn't it? And every sheet was rubbed in exactly the same way.

She had an idea for a test. The day was hot and dry. She picked up her steel tiffin carrier by its handle, and gently touched its lid to the top of the pile. The sheets under the lid sank flat, as if they had breathed out.

She touched the other corner. The sheets sank flat again. Then she tried the wooden handle of a broom in the same way. The sheets did not move at all.

"Mr. Raghunathan," she said, "please come and see."

He came, with his hands behind his back and one eyebrow raised. She showed him the steel and the wood, twice each. Steel made the sheets sink, and wood did nothing.

"The paper is not bad," she said. "It is full of charge from the rollers. The steel lets the charge run out of the paper, through my hand and my body, down into the floor. Wood will not let it go."

"Charge," said Mr. Raghunathan slowly. "Paper is paper." But then he stopped. "Last Thursday it rained all day," he said. "And the paper lay perfectly flat. I thought we had been lucky."

"Damp air lets charge leak away by itself," said Thenmozhi. "On a dry day it has nowhere to go."

Everyone in Sivakasi respects a spark. Mr. Raghunathan's cousin ran a cracker unit two lanes away, and he had never allowed anything near it that could jump a spark. He looked at the pile of sheets for a long time.

"Where does the charge go?" he said. "The machine stands on rubber pads, to stop the shaking. It cannot go into the floor from the machine."

"Then we must give it a path," said Thenmozhi. "A metal one, that goes to the ground."

Karpagam went to the cupboard in the corner, where she kept her good saree. She took out an old one with a silver zari border that was too worn to wear. She unpicked a long length of the shiny thread, which is made of fine metal. She wound one end round the iron pillar, which went down into the concrete floor. Then she spread the thread out at the other end, into a brush of a hundred fine strands, so that the paper slid right under them as it came out.

The machine started. The first sheet slid out under the silver strands and landed flat. So did the second. So did the hundredth. The pile grew straight and neat, as it should have.

"A hundred fine points," said Murugan, scratching his head. "The charge jumps off the paper into the air at each tip, and then goes down the thread and the pillar."

Mr. Raghunathan did not say anything for a while. Then he ordered a proper bar of fine steel bristles from Chennai, and until it came, the silver zari stayed where it was.

That Deepavali, labels printed on those sheets went onto cracker boxes in shops from Madurai to Mumbai. Nobody who bought them knew that the paper had once refused to lie flat, or that a girl on a stool had worked out why.

**For the parent:** For the parent: paper pulled over rollers touches and separates from them again and again, so it picks up a charge. Every sheet meets the same roller in the same way, so every sheet gets the same kind of charge. Sheets with the same charge push each other apart, which is why the pile fanned out. Neutral things such as a sleeve or the iron frame are pulled by the charged sheets. A steel carrier in her hand worked as a conductor, and the charge ran through her body to the floor. Wood is an insulator and did nothing. Damp air carries charge away by itself. The machine stood on rubber, so the charge had no path. Fine metal threads leak charge into the air at each tip, and an earth wire carries it down, which is what real press static eliminators do. Thenmozhi also did a fair test: she tried steel and wood twice each.

- Ask: Why would every sheet from the same rollers get the same kind of charge, and what does that do to a pile of sheets?
- Ask: The steel carrier made the sheets sink flat, but a wooden broom handle did nothing. What does that tell you about steel and wood?
- Ask: The same paper lay flat after a day of rain. Why might damp air stop a pile from fanning out?
- Ask: A hundred fine threads took the charge away faster than one thick wire might. Why might a point be better than a flat plate at letting charge go?

## Games
### g1 · Ladder Duel: who takes the electrons? (20 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the 12 cards and put them face down in a pile. Lay the ladder board where everyone can see it.; The reader turns up two cards, with the material sides showing. The backs stay hidden. Neighbours are unreliable, so if the two cards are less than 3 rungs apart, put one back and draw another.; Every other player writes or says which of the two materials will get the minus charge when they are rubbed together. The reader then turns the cards over.; The card with the bigger rung number is lower on the ladder. It takes the electrons, so it gets the minus. A player with the right answer scores one point.; A player who also says why, using the words give, take or electrons, scores one more point.; In round 2, the reader turns up three cards. Players name the one that is minus against both others, the one that is plus against both others, and the one in the middle. Getting all three right scores 2 points.; In round 3, the reader picks a card in the middle of the ladder, from rung 4 to rung 9. Players say its sign against a card two rungs above it and a card two rungs below it. A correct answer scores 2 points.; After each card, the reader's job passes to the player on the left. With two players, swap after every card.; Playing alone, draw two cards each turn and write your guess before you check. Score yourself in the same way. You win with 10 points from 12 cards.; The game ends when each player has been reader twice, or the pile is gone. The player with the most points wins. A tie is shared.
- **Printable board: 12 rungs: the charge ladder, from top (gives electrons) to bottom (takes electrons):** Rung 1: Human hair; Rung 2: Wool; Rung 3: Nylon; Rung 4: Silk; Rung 5: Paper; Rung 6: Cotton; Rung 7: Dry wood; Rung 8: Rubber, such as a balloon; Rung 9: Polyester; Rung 10: Polystyrene, such as a clear plastic ruler; Rung 11: Polythene, such as a carry bag; Rung 12: PVC, such as a water pipe
- **Printable cards: 12 two-sided cards: a material on the front, its rung and a note on the back:** Human hair (back: Rung 1 of 12. The top. It gives electrons to nearly everything.); Wool (back: Rung 2 of 12. Near the top. It gives electrons to almost everything below it.); Nylon (back: Rung 3 of 12. It gives electrons to everything below it, and takes them from hair and wool.); Silk (back: Rung 4 of 12. Gives to the rungs below it, takes from the rungs above it.); Paper (back: Rung 5 of 12. In the middle. It charges weakly with its close neighbours.); Cotton (back: Rung 6 of 12. In the middle. It takes from wool and gives to polythene.); Dry wood (back: Rung 7 of 12. In the lower middle. Damp wood loses its charge quickly.); Rubber, such as a balloon (back: Rung 8 of 12. Tables disagree about where rubber sits, so trust it only against far-away rungs.); Polyester (back: Rung 9 of 12. Many clothes are made of it. It takes electrons from most materials above it.); Polystyrene, such as a clear plastic ruler (back: Rung 10 of 12. Near the bottom. It takes electrons from most materials above it.); Polythene, such as a carry bag (back: Rung 11 of 12. Near the bottom. It takes electrons from nearly everything above it.); PVC, such as a water pipe (back: Rung 12 of 12. The bottom. It takes electrons from nearly everything. Some tables put Teflon even lower.)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 12 cards and the ladder board. Shuffle the cards and put them face down. Give each player paper and a pencil for guesses.  
  *Parent says:* These cards are twelve materials on a charge ladder. In each round, you guess which one takes the electrons. Let us look at the board first.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and the ladder. Shuffle the cards.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play round 1. The reader turns up two cards at least 3 rungs apart. Everyone guesses which will get the minus charge. Turn over the backs and score. Play six pairs.  
  *Parent says:* Two cards are up. Which one will take the electrons and get the minus charge? Write your guess, then we turn the cards over.  
  *Child mode:* Two cards are up. Which gets the minus? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why does the lower card get the minus charge? (good: Because it takes electrons from the higher card.; Because it is lower on the ladder.)
  - Note: Ask for the reason every time. The rule is always the same: the lower card takes electrons and gets the minus.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play round 2 with three cards at a time, and then round 3 with a middle card. Score the points as in the rules.  
  *Parent says:* Three cards are up. Which is minus against both the others? Which is plus against both? Which is in the middle?  
  *Child mode:* Three cards are up. Which is minus, which is plus, which is in the middle?
  - Ask: Why can the middle card be plus with one card and minus with another? (good: It gives to the ones below it and takes from the ones above.; Its partner changes.)
  - Note: The card in the middle is both. This is the main idea of the ladder: plus and minus belong to the meeting, not to the material.
  - Check: A card is in the middle of the ladder. It meets one card above it and one below it. What happens? → **It is minus with the one above and plus with the one below**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the points. Compare this ladder with the one from your own experiment. Where do the two ladders disagree? Talk about why.  
  *Parent says:* Count the points. Now compare this ladder with the one you built yourself. Do they match? Where do they differ, and why might that be?  
  *Child mode:* Count your points. Compare this ladder with your own ladder.
  - Note: Your ladder from the experiment may differ from this one. That is a normal result. Ladders agree about the ends, and not about close neighbours. Real data from your own home is worth more than a table in a book.
  - Think: If two published ladders disagree about close neighbours, which should you trust?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Charge Ladder: who gives electrons, and who takes them? (45 min)
- **question:** When five different things are each rubbed on wool and on polythene, which of them take electrons and which give them? Can the results be put in a ladder that predicts a pair you have not tried?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess for each of the five things. After wool, will it push the balloon away? After polythene, will it push or pull? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Every thing gets ten strokes, always in one direction. Every thing is held 3 centimetres from the balloon, at the same height, for 5 seconds. Each thing is wiped clean before the next test, and the hanging balloon is charged again on wool after every three tests. The same wool, the same polythene and the same room are used all the way through. Only the thing being rubbed changes.
- **safety:** Balloons and small cut pieces can choke young children, so keep them away from children under three. Do this in a room away from the kitchen gas.
- **record:** Fill in the results table for all five things. Then lay the seven cards in a ladder with wool at the top and polythene at the bottom, and draw it.
- **results table:** Thing | After wool: R, A or 0 | After polythene: R, A or 0 | Its rung on my ladder
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay out the five things, the wool and the polythene. Ask your child to guess for each thing, and to say why. Write every guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will rub five things on wool, and then on polythene. Which will take electrons and push our balloon away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which things will push the balloon away. Say why.
  - Ask: Which thing do you think will charge the most, and why? (good: The balloon, because it worked before.; The plastic ruler, because plastic holds charge.; I am not sure, but the cloth looks too soft.)
  - Note: Write the guesses and reasons at the top of the table. Do not correct them. Some will be right by luck, and that is fine.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Blow up the first balloon and tie 40 centimetres of thread to it. Tie the other end to a pencil. Tape the pencil to the edge of a table so the balloon hangs free, 30 centimetres from any wall. Rub the hanging balloon ten times along the wool, in one direction. Step back until it stops swinging. Hold your plain hand 5 centimetres away and watch the balloon.  
  *Parent says:* This hanging balloon is our detector. We charge it on wool. Now hold your plain hand near it. What does it do?  
  *Child mode:* Hang a balloon from the table edge. Rub it ten times on wool. Hold your hand near.
  - Ask: The balloon leaned towards your plain hand. Does that mean your hand is charged? (good: No. A pull does not prove it.; A plain hand has no extra charge, but it still pulled.)
  - Note: The balloon should lean gently towards your hand. If it does not, rub it ten more times. On a damp day, use an air-conditioned room, or blow a hair dryer on the wool and the balloon for 30 seconds first. Charge fades in about a minute, so you will recharge it often.
  - Check: The charged balloon leans towards your plain hand. What does that show? → **A pull alone does not prove the other thing is charged**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the plain, unrubbed ruler 3 centimetres from the balloon, at the same height. Watch for 5 seconds. Write R if the balloon is pushed away, A if it is pulled closer, and 0 if nothing happens.  
  *Parent says:* First, a control. This ruler has not been rubbed. What does the balloon do? Write R, A or 0.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the plain ruler near the balloon. Write R, A or 0.
  - Ask: Why do we test a ruler we have not rubbed? (good: To see what a plain ruler does.; To have something to compare with.; To check the ruler is not already charged.)
  - Note: Often the balloon leans a little towards the plain ruler. That is polarisation, and it is why we trust only a push. If the balloon moves away from a plain ruler, the ruler already has a charge. Wipe it with a damp cloth, dry it, and try again.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the folded wool flat on the table. Rub the first thing along the wool, ten strokes, always one way. Carry it to the balloon and hold it 3 centimetres away. Watch for 5 seconds and write R, A or 0. Wipe the thing with a damp hand, then dry it. Do all five things. Recharge the balloon on the wool after every three tests.  
  *Parent says:* Ten strokes, one way, then carry it over. Does the balloon move away, move closer, or stay still? Write R, A or 0.  
  *Child mode:* Rub a thing ten times on wool. Hold it near the balloon. Write R, A or 0.
  - Ask: What does an R tell you about the thing you rubbed? (good: It has the same kind of charge as the balloon.; It took electrons from the wool.; Both are minus.)
  - Note: Expect the ruler, the comb and the balloon to push the hanging balloon away. The paper tube and the cotton often give a weak push or nothing. If nothing moves at all, give twenty strokes, move the thing in slowly from the side, or try again in a drier room. Write 0. A weak charge is a real result.
  - Check: A ruler rubbed on wool pushes the balloon away. What do you know about the ruler? → **It has the same kind of charge as the balloon**
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the polythene flat on the table. Rub the first thing along the polythene, ten strokes, always one way. Carry it to the balloon and hold it 3 centimetres away. Watch for 5 seconds and write R, A or 0. Wipe the thing clean. Do all five things. Recharge the balloon on the wool after every three tests.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same five things on polythene. Does each one push the balloon away, pull it closer, or do nothing? Write R, A or 0.  
  *Child mode:* Now rub each thing on polythene. Hold it near the balloon. Write R, A or 0.
  - Ask: A thing pulls the balloon after polythene, and pushed it after wool. What could that mean? (good: It took electrons from wool and gave some to polythene.; It is between them.; It has the opposite charge with polythene.)
  - Note: Expect different results from step 4. The paper tube and the cotton often pull the balloon after polythene, because they gave electrons to the polythene. Remember that a pull is only a hint. Do not worry if some results are messy. Write down what you saw.
- **s6 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut seven small cards: wool, polythene, and the five things. Put wool at the top and polythene at the bottom. A thing that pushed the balloon after wool goes below wool. A thing that pushed it after wool and pulled it after polythene goes in the middle. Now pick two things that are far apart on your ladder. Say which will get the minus charge. Rub them together, test each one on the balloon, and check.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Put the cards in a ladder. Pick two far apart. Rub them together. Test your guess.
  - Ask: Which thing gives electrons to some partners and takes them from others? (good: The one in the middle.; Cotton, or paper.; The thing that pushed after wool but pulled after polythene.)
  - Note: Only after they have said what they saw, explain: each thing can give to the ones lower on the ladder and take from the ones higher. So a thing in the middle is plus with one partner and minus with another. Your ladder may not match a book's. Books disagree about close neighbours, and damp air changes the order.
  - Think: Which of your five things sits in the middle of the ladder, and what does that mean?
### e2 · Two Balloons: does twice the rubbing push twice as far? (30 min)
- **question:** Two balloons rubbed the same number of times hang apart in a V. Does ten rubs make the gap twice as big as five rubs? Does twenty rubs make it four times as big?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess how far apart the balloons will hang after 5, 10 and 20 rubs. Will 20 rubs give a gap four times as big as 5? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Both balloons are the same size, hang on threads of the same length from the same pencil, and are rubbed on the same wool with the same number of strokes. The gap is measured 10 seconds after hanging, between the same two knots. The balloons are cleared of charge between levels. Only the number of rubs changes.
- **safety:** Keep balloons and bits of burst balloon away from children under three.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each number of rubs, using the gap beyond the resting gap.
- **results table:** Rubs on each balloon | Gap between knots, try 1 (cm) | Gap between knots, try 2 (cm) | Gap beyond the resting gap (cm)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the two balloons, the wool and the ruler. Ask for a guess about the gap after 5, 10 and 20 rubs, and for a reason. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* We will rub two balloons the same amount and see how far apart they hang. Will 20 rubs push them four times as far as 5 rubs? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how far apart the balloons will hang. Say why.
  - Ask: Do you think the gap will double when the rubs double? Why? (good: Yes, because twice the charge pushes twice as hard.; No, because the balloons are heavy and pull back.; Not sure. Let us test.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Both a doubling guess and a not-doubling guess are good starting points.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tie each thread to a loop. Slip both loops over the middle of the pencil. Tape the pencil to the edge of a table so the balloons hang free and touch each other. Measure the distance between the two knots. Write it down as the resting gap.  
  *Parent says:* Both balloons hang from one point and touch each other. Measure the gap between the knots. This is the resting gap, before any charge.  
  *Child mode:* Hang both balloons from one pencil. Measure the gap between the knots.
  - Note: The resting gap will be about 20 centimetres, the width of a balloon. Measure from knot to knot every time. Keep the balloons away from walls.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lift both loops off the pencil. Rub the first balloon 5 times on the wool, then the second balloon 5 times. Slip the loops back over the pencil. Wait 10 seconds. Measure the gap between the knots. Repeat once more. Then touch both balloons with a damp hand and wait 30 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Five rubs for each balloon, always the same direction. Hang them up again. Wait ten seconds, and measure the gap. Write it down.  
  *Child mode:* Rub each balloon 5 times. Hang them. Measure the gap.
  - Ask: Why do the balloons hang apart now? (good: They both have the same kind of charge.; They both took electrons from the wool.; Two minus charges push each other away.)
  - Note: Measure at 10 seconds each time, because the charge leaks away. Do two tries. If the gap is nearly the same as the resting gap, rub six more times.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Repeat step 3 with 10 rubs on each balloon. Do two tries, and clear the charge with a damp hand afterwards.  
  *Parent says:* Now ten rubs each. Hang them up, wait ten seconds, and measure. Is the gap twice as big as with five rubs?  
  *Child mode:* Rub each balloon 10 times. Hang them. Measure the gap.
  - Ask: Is the gap twice as big as before? What does it tell you? (good: It is bigger but not twice as big.; More rubbing gives more charge, so more push.; Maybe some charge leaked away.)
  - Note: Do not tell your child the answer. The gap should grow, but usually by less than double.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Repeat step 3 with 20 rubs on each balloon. Do two tries, and clear the charge with a damp hand afterwards.  
  *Parent says:* Now twenty rubs each. Hang them up, wait ten seconds, and measure. How does this gap compare with the others?  
  *Child mode:* Rub each balloon 20 times. Hang them. Measure the gap.
  - Check: Two balloons rubbed 20 times each hang further apart than after 5 rubs. What does this show? → **More rubbing gave each balloon more charge, so they push harder**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Subtract the resting gap from each gap. Draw a bar chart with one bar for 5, 10 and 20 rubs. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each number of rubs. Say what you saw.
  - Note: After they have said what they saw, explain: more rubs moved more electrons, so each balloon had more charge and pushed harder. But the gap did not grow in step, for two reasons. The push gets weaker as the balloons move apart. And the balloons can hold only so much charge before it leaks into the air as tiny sparks you cannot see.
  - Think: If you could rub forever, would the gap keep growing?

### a2 · Charge Walk: which things let charge run away? (20 min)

  *Parent does:* Line up the seven things and a wet fingertip. Ask your child to guess, for each thing, if it will let the charge run away or keep it. Write the guesses down.

  *Child mode:* Sort the things into two groups. Say what the first group shares.
  - Note: A strong answer says that the charge ran along metal or through the body into the floor. After they have said it, tell them that the Earth works as a giant store for charge.
  - Think: Where did the charge go when it ran away?
### a3 · Tape Twins: one peel, two charges (20 min)

  *Parent says:* We have two pairs of strips. The strips on the table are B for bottom. The strips on top are T. Before we peel them, what do you think will happen?  
  *Child mode:* Stick two strips on the table. Press two more on top. Label them B and T.
  - Ask: What do you think will happen when we peel a pair apart, and why? (good: They will stick to each other.; They might get charged, like the balloon.; Nothing will happen.)
  - Note: Press each strip down well with a finger, but leave the handles free. Keep the strips clean and dry. A bit of dust spoils the result, and a damp day weakens it.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the handles of the first pair. Peel both strips off the table together in one smooth pull. Then pull the two strips apart quickly. Hang each strip by its handle from the edge of the table. Do the second pair in the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Peel both strips off together. Then pull them apart fast. Hang them by their handles. What do you notice about how they hang?  
  *Child mode:* Peel a pair off the table. Pull the strips apart fast. Hang them.
  - Ask: What do you notice about how the strips hang? (good: They swing towards or away from things.; They are not straight.; They seem to cling to me.)
  - Note: Pull the strips apart in one quick movement, because slow pulling lets the charge leak. Strips should swing towards your hand. If they do not, try a drier room.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Bring the two T strips close to each other. Then bring the two B strips close to each other. Then bring a T strip close to a B strip. Write push or pull for each.  
  *Parent says:* Bring two T strips together. Do they push or pull? Now two B strips. Now a T and a B. Write down what you see.  
  *Child mode:* Bring the strips close. Do they push or pull? Write it down.
  - Ask: Which pairs of strips pushed apart, and which pulled together? (good: T and T pushed. B and B pushed. T and B pulled.; The same labels pushed, and different labels pulled.)
  - Note: Expect two Ts to push apart, two Bs to push apart, and a T and a B to pull together. This shows two kinds of charge from one peel. Do not say yet which is plus and which is minus. Nobody can tell from this test alone.
  - Check: Two T strips push apart. A T strip and a B strip pull together. What does this show? → **T and B carry opposite kinds of charge**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to explain what happened. Ask where the charges came from when nobody rubbed anything.  
  *Parent says:* Nobody rubbed these strips. Where did the charge come from? Why do the top and bottom strips have opposite charges?  
  *Child mode:* Where did the charge come from? Say what you think.
  - Note: A strong answer says that peeling moved electrons from one strip to the other, so one has extra and the other has a shortage. Pressing them together lets the electrons go back, and the strips become neutral again.
  - Think: If the charges are equal and opposite, what would happen if you pressed the two strips together again?

### p1 · Our Home Ladder: does the order change on a damp day? (75 min, 3 days)
- **record:** A table with three columns: the thing, its result on the dry day, its result on the damp day. Then a final ladder with all nine rungs.
- **schedule:** day 1: Choose four new things. Guess their rungs. Test each on wool and on polythene in a dry room. (30 min); day 2: Test the same four things in a damp place, for example a bathroom after a hot shower. (25 min); day 3: Compare the two days. Draw your final ladder with all nine rungs. (20 min)

  *Parent does:* Choose four new things that are dry and not metal. Lay them out and guess where each will sit on your ladder from the Charge Ladder test. Write the guesses and reasons in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* We will add four new things to our ladder. Where will each one sit? Is it near wool, near polythene, or in the middle? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Pick four new things. Guess where each goes on your ladder.
  - Note: Do not worry about being right. Writing the guess first is what matters. If you have no thermocol, use a plastic bottle, a foam slipper or a nylon cloth.
- **s2 (20 min)**

  *Child mode:* Test each new thing on wool and polythene. Write R, A or 0.
  - Check: A new thing pushes the balloon away after wool, and also after polythene. Where does it sit on the ladder? → **Below both wool and polythene**
- **s3 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On the second day, hang the balloon in a damp place. Test the same four things on wool and polythene in the same way. Write R, A or 0 for each, in a new column.

  - Wait: Do this on another day, in a damp place such as a bathroom after a hot shower.

  *Parent does:* Put the two columns side by side. Did the order of the things change? Draw your final ladder with all nine rungs. Write three sentences about what you found.  
  *Parent says:* Look at both columns. Did the order change on the damp day? Or only how strongly they pushed? What do you think that tells us?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the two days. Draw your ladder with nine rungs.
  - Note: Many families find that the order stays but the pushes weaken, because water on the surface lets charge leak. Some find the order shifts a little. Both are real results. Published ladders also disagree, partly for this reason.
  - Think: If damp air weakens the pushes but keeps the order, what is the water doing?
  - Wait: Do this on the third day.

## Worksheets
### Charge, pushes and the ladder (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A balloon is rubbed on wool and becomes charged. What actually moved?  
   Options: Electrons moved from the wool to the balloon / New charge was made by the rubbing / Protons moved from the wool to the balloon  
   **Answer:** Electrons moved from the wool to the balloon. *Why:* Electrons are the small particles on the outside of atoms, and some can be moved. Protons are locked in the middle of the atoms and stay put. The rubbing made no new charge, it only moved some.
2. (truefalse) When a balloon is rubbed on wool, new electric charge is made.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Charge is never made or destroyed. Rubbing moves electrons from the wool to the balloon, so the wool loses exactly as much as the balloon gains.
3. (fill) A pull does not prove that a thing is charged. Only a ____ does.  
   **Answer:** ['push', 'repel', 'repelling']. *Why:* A charged balloon pulls neutral things too. Only two things with the same kind of charge push each other apart, so a push is real proof.
4. (mcq) A charged balloon swings towards a plain wooden stick. What does this show?  
   Options: Nothing for certain, because even neutral things are pulled / The stick is charged / The balloon has lost its charge  
   **Answer:** Nothing for certain, because even neutral things are pulled. *Why:* The balloon's charge shifts the stick's own plus and minus charges, so the nearer side has the opposite charge. That gives a small pull on a stick with no extra charge. Only a push proves that both are charged.
5. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Conductor → lets charge move through it easily; Insulator → keeps charge where it was put; Earthing → joining a thing to the ground so charge can drain away; Polarisation → a neutral thing's charges shift when a charged thing comes near. *Why:* A conductor passes charge on and an insulator holds it. Earthing joins a thing to the ground. Polarisation is why a charged thing can pull a neutral one.
6. (sort) Sort each thing by what it does with a charge put on one end.  
   **Answer:** steel spoon → Lets charge run away; copper wire → Lets charge run away; salty water → Lets charge run away; plastic spoon → Keeps the charge; glass tumbler → Keeps the charge; rubber slipper → Keeps the charge. *Why:* Metals and salty water have charges that are free to move, so a charge spreads out and runs away. Plastic, glass and rubber hold their charges tightly, so a charge stays where it was put.
7. (truefalse) On most charge ladders, a wool shawl rubbed on polythene leaves the polythene with the minus charge.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Wool is near the top of the ladder and gives electrons. Polythene is near the bottom and takes them, so it ends up minus.
8. (order) Put these in order on a charge ladder, from the top (gives electrons) to the bottom (takes electrons).  
   **Answer:** ['Human hair', 'Cotton', 'Polyester', 'PVC']. *Why:* Hair is at the top and gives electrons to almost everything. PVC is at the bottom and takes them from almost everything. Cotton is in the middle and polyester is lower down.
9. (write) Ria rubs a ruler on wool, and a hanging charged balloon swings towards the ruler. Ria says the ruler is therefore charged. Is she right? What test would settle it?  
   **Answer:** Not necessarily. A pull can happen with a neutral ruler. A push settles it: if the balloon swings away from the ruler, both have the same kind of charge.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a pull is not proof, because charged things pull neutral things too, and that a push is the test.
10. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Steel spoon, copper wire, plastic spoon, iron nail.  
   **Answer:** The plastic spoon, because it is an insulator. The other three are metals that let charge run away.. *Why:* Metals are conductors, because some of their electrons are free to move. Plastic holds its electrons tightly and keeps a charge where it was put.
11. (mcq) Why does a petrol tanker trail a metal chain or strap on the road?  
   Options: It lets charge from the tanker's movement drain into the ground / It makes the tanker heavier / It warns other drivers  
   **Answer:** It lets charge from the tanker's movement drain into the ground. *Why:* Moving fuel and moving parts can build up charge, and a spark could set fire to the vapour. A metal strap to the ground is earthing: it gives the charge a path to drain away.
12. (draw) Draw a charge ladder with three rungs: wool at the top, cotton in the middle and polythene at the bottom. Draw arrows showing which way electrons go between each neighbouring pair.  
   **Answer:** A ladder with wool, cotton and polythene from top to bottom, and arrows pointing down from wool to cotton and from cotton to polythene.. *Why:* Electrons go from the higher rung to the lower rung. So the arrows between each pair point down. Cotton has an arrow coming in from wool and one going out to polythene.
13. (fill) Charge can be moved from one thing to another, but it can never be made or ____.  
   **Answer:** ['destroyed', 'lost']. *Why:* This is called conservation of charge. What one thing gains, another loses, so the total stays the same.
14. (count) Four tape strips are peeled apart in two pairs. Two strips are labelled T and two are labelled B. Every T pushes the other T away, and every B pushes the other B away. How many pairs of strips push each other apart?  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Strips with the same kind of charge push. The two Ts make one pair and the two Bs make another pair. That is 2 pairs. The other four pairs, each with a T and a B, pull together.
15. (perspective) Selvi and Imran have a ladder. Cotton took electrons from wool, and it gave electrons to polythene.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason. Imran's view fits both results, since cotton took from wool and gave to polythene.. *Why:* Selvi only looked at one pair. Both results together show that cotton is minus with a partner above it and plus with a partner below it.
16. (mcq) Clear tape is peeled off a table, and nobody rubs it, yet it ends up charged. What does this show?  
   Options: Touching and pulling apart is enough to move charge / Tape always carries a charge / Friction heat makes the charge  
   **Answer:** Touching and pulling apart is enough to move charge. *Why:* Nothing rubbed the tape, so heat from rubbing cannot be the cause. Touching and then separating two materials is enough to move electrons from one to the other. Rubbing only helps by making more of the two surfaces touch.
### Distance, points and sparks (stretch) (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A bit of paper has no extra charge, yet it jumps up to a rubbed comb. Why?  
   Options: The comb shifts the paper's own charges, so the near side gets the opposite charge and is pulled / The paper has secretly gained a charge / The air pushes the paper up  
   **Answer:** The comb shifts the paper's own charges, so the near side gets the opposite charge and is pulled. *Why:* This is polarisation. The comb's minus charge pushes the paper's electrons slightly away, so the near side of the paper is plus. A near plus side is pulled more strongly than the far minus side is pushed, so the paper rises.
2. (mcq) A piece of cotton meets wool, and later meets polythene. Which is right?  
   Options: Cotton is minus with wool and plus with polythene / Cotton is plus with both / Cotton is minus with both  
   **Answer:** Cotton is minus with wool and plus with polythene. *Why:* Wool is higher on the ladder, so it gives electrons to cotton. Polythene is lower, so cotton gives electrons to it. The cotton plays both roles.
3. (truefalse) A material can be plus with one partner and minus with another.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Plus and minus depend on where the two materials sit on the ladder. A material in the middle gives to those below it and takes from those above it.
4. (fill) If two charges are moved twice as far apart, the push between them becomes ____ as big.  
   **Answer:** ['a quarter', 'one quarter', '1/4', 'a fourth']. *Why:* The force follows the inverse square law. Twice as far means 2 times 2, which is 4, so the force is divided by 4.
5. (count) Two charges 2 centimetres apart push each other with a force of 36 units. How big is the push when they are 6 centimetres apart?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* 6 centimetres is 3 times as far as 2 centimetres. The force divides by 3 times 3, which is 9. 36 divided by 9 is 4 units.
6. (match) Match each happening to what explains it.  
   **Answer:** A pin held near a charged balloon makes it lose its charge → A sharp point lets charge leak into the air; A tanker's trailing chain → Earthing carries charge to the ground; A paper bit jumping to a comb → Polarisation of a neutral thing; Thunder → Air heated by a spark bursting outward. *Why:* A point has a very strong field that tears charge into the air. A chain gives charge a path to the ground. A comb shifts a paper bit's charges. A spark heats air so quickly that it bursts, and we hear that as thunder.
7. (sort) Sort each use of static charge by whether it is used on purpose or guarded against.  
   **Answer:** a laser printer's toner powder → Used on purpose; chimney plates that catch smoke dust → Used on purpose; electric paint spray → Used on purpose; fuel flowing into a tanker → A danger to guard against; flour dust in a mill → A danger to guard against; a fireworks factory → A danger to guard against. *Why:* Printers, chimney plates and paint sprays use the push and pull of charge to put powder or droplets in the right place. Fuel, flour dust and fireworks can be set alight by one spark.
8. (order) Put these in order for a lightning flash.  
   **Answer:** ['Charge separates inside the storm cloud', 'A faint feeler steps down from the cloud towards the ground', 'A bright return stroke travels up the same path', 'The heated air bursts outward and we hear thunder']. *Why:* Charge builds up first. A feeler finds a path to the ground. The bright stroke that we see goes up that path. The air it heats bursts outward with a bang.
9. (write) Why can the Earth act as a bottomless drain for extra charge?  
   **Answer:** The Earth is a conductor and it is enormous, so a few billion electrons added or taken away change it by a tiny amount that nobody can measure. So charge can always run into it.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the Earth conducts and is very large, so adding or taking away charge hardly changes it.
10. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? A lightning conductor's tip, a brush of thin metal threads, a pin near a charged balloon, a smooth round steel ball.  
   **Answer:** The smooth round ball, because charge spreads evenly over it. The other three are sharp points that let charge leak into the air.. *Why:* Charge crowds at sharp points and the field there is strong enough to carry charge into the air. A smooth ball holds its charge more evenly.
11. (truefalse) A small spark from a door handle has about as much energy as a lightning flash.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The spark you feel carries a tiny amount of charge and passes in a moment. Lightning carries a huge amount of charge and has hundreds of millions of volts, so it is deadly.
12. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still not agree about?  
   Options: Exactly what moves, and why, when two materials touch and part / Whether the same kinds of charge push apart / Whether charge is ever made from nothing  
   **Answer:** Exactly what moves, and why, when two materials touch and part. *Why:* The other two are settled. What moves, and why one material wins over another, is still argued about. Electrons, ions and tiny bits of material have all been proposed.
13. (perspective) A printing press has paper sheets that cling together on dry days. Two workers disagree about the best fix.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks one, gives a reason, and names a check, such as measuring if the sheets still cling after the fix. Both work, and many presses use both.. *Why:* The bar gives charge a path to the ground. Damp air weakens the charge everywhere. Each has a cost, and a test shows which is better.
14. (draw) Draw two balloons hanging from one point after both are rubbed the same way. Show the forces on one balloon with arrows.  
   **Answer:** Two balloons hanging in a V from one point. On each balloon, an arrow down for its weight, an arrow along the thread, and an arrow pointing sideways away from the other balloon.. *Why:* The sideways push comes from the other balloon's charge. Weight pulls down, and the thread pulls along its length. The balloon rests where all three balance.
15. (write) Moving electrons are called minus, though the names plus and minus were chosen before electrons were found. Explain why the names stayed.  
   **Answer:** The names were a guess that happened to work in the sums. Changing them would mean changing every book and every wire, and the electron is just the thing that carries the minus charge.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a name is a label that works, even if the guess behind it was backwards.
16. (mcq) A balloon pulls paper bits strongly at 1 centimetre. How strong is the pull at 3 centimetres, if the law is the same?  
   Options: One ninth as strong / One third as strong / The same  
   **Answer:** One ninth as strong. *Why:* 3 centimetres is 3 times as far. The pull divides by 3 times 3, which is 9. That is why a balloon that lifts paper at 1 centimetre does almost nothing at 10.
17. (fill) A spark can jump through air when the push on the air is about ____ volts for every millimetre.  
   **Answer:** ['3,000', '3000', 'three thousand']. *Why:* Air is an insulator until the push is strong enough. About 3,000 volts for each millimetre is where it gives way. So a 1 centimetre spark needs about 30,000 volts.
### Open questions and clever tests (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) A student rubs a comb on cotton and finds that the comb pushes a charged balloon away. She says cotton must be a plus material. Why is that claim too quick?  
   **Answer:** She tested cotton against only one partner. Cotton might be plus with that comb and minus with a different partner higher up the ladder. Plus and minus belong to a pair.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a result with one partner does not tell you what happens with another, because the sign depends on the pair.
2. (mcq) Wool and nylon are close neighbours on the ladder. Why is a ladder a poor guide to rubbing them together?  
   Options: Their difference is so small that damp air, dirt or how hard you rub can reverse the result / Wool and nylon are both insulators / Wool and nylon have no electrons  
   **Answer:** Their difference is so small that damp air, dirt or how hard you rub can reverse the result. *Why:* Materials far apart give a big, steady difference. Close neighbours give a small difference, which small changes in the surface can flip. Published tables disagree about neighbours for this reason.
3. (count) A balloon has taken 60,000,000,000 electrons from a woollen shawl. How many electrons has the shawl lost?  
   **Answer:** 60,000,000,000. *Why:* Charge is never made or destroyed. Whatever the balloon gained, the shawl lost, so the shawl lost exactly 60,000,000,000 electrons.
4. (perspective) Two scientists argue about why rubbing moves charge between two plastics.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks one, gives a reason, and suggests a test such as repeating the rubbing in very dry air or in a vacuum, where there is no water film.. *Why:* Nobody has settled it. Both ideas have evidence behind them, and good tests change one thing, such as the water, and see what stays the same.
5. (write) Design a fair test to find out if damp air changes the order of materials on your ladder. Say what you would keep the same, and what you would change.  
   **Answer:** Rub the same materials, with the same number of strokes, against the same wool and polythene, and test with the same hanging balloon. Do it in dry air first, then in damp air. Only the dampness changes.. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing only. The materials, the strokes, the pads and the detector must stay the same, so any difference comes from the damp air.
6. (mcq) Why does a brush of many thin metal threads remove charge from paper better than one flat metal strip?  
   Options: Each thread tip is a sharp point, and charge leaks into the air easily from a point / Threads are lighter / A flat strip has no electrons  
   **Answer:** Each thread tip is a sharp point, and charge leaks into the air easily from a point. *Why:* Charge crowds at points and the field there is strong enough to carry charge into the air. A hundred tips leak a lot more charge than one smooth edge.
7. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? A tanker's trailing chain, an earth pin on a plug, a pointed lightning rod, a rubber mat.  
   **Answer:** The rubber mat, because it is an insulator. The other three are conductors that carry charge to the ground.. *Why:* Rubber keeps charge from moving. A chain, an earth pin and a lightning rod are metal paths that carry charge safely into the earth.
8. (truefalse) If the Earth takes a few billion electrons from you, scientists can easily measure that the Earth's charge has changed.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Earth is so enormous that a few billion electrons make no difference that any instrument can find. That is why it works as a bottomless store.
9. (fill) The two charges made by rubbing are always equal in size and ____ in kind.  
   **Answer:** ['opposite']. *Why:* One material gains exactly the electrons the other loses. So one has extra minus, and the other has an equal shortage, which acts as plus.
10. (match) Match each happening to the idea behind it.  
   **Answer:** Tape pulled off a table is charged → Touching and parting moves electrons; A pin near a charged balloon drains it → Charge leaks from a sharp point; A bee and a flower hold opposite charges → Opposite charges pull pollen across; Two rubbed balloons hang apart → Same charges repel. *Why:* Each happening has a charge idea behind it. Touching and parting moves electrons. A point leaks charge. Opposite charges attract and the same repel.
11. (draw) Draw a bumblebee flying towards a flower. Mark the kind of charge on the bee and on the flower, and draw an arrow for the pull on a grain of pollen.  
   **Answer:** A bee marked plus and a flower marked minus, with an arrow from the pollen grain on the flower towards the bee.. *Why:* A flying bee gathers a small plus charge from the air, and a flower joined to the ground is minus. Opposite charges attract, so a pollen grain can jump towards the bee.
12. (write) Tape peeled from a table is charged, though nothing rubbed it. What does this say about the idea that friction heat makes the charge?  
   **Answer:** It says the idea is wrong or at least not needed. Peeling makes no friction heat, yet the tape is charged. Touching and parting is enough.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the tape as evidence against the idea, since the charge appeared with no rubbing.
13. (mcq) A neutral metal ball on a thread jumps to a charged balloon, touches it, and is then pushed away. Why is it pushed away after touching?  
   Options: It took some of the balloon's charge, so both have the same kind of charge now / It became heavier / The balloon lost all its charge  
   **Answer:** It took some of the balloon's charge, so both have the same kind of charge now. *Why:* At first, polarisation pulls the neutral ball to the balloon. Touching lets electrons run onto the ball. Then both have minus charge, so they push apart.
14. (truefalse) The bright flash of a lightning bolt travels from the cloud down to the ground.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A faint feeler steps down from the cloud first. When it is near the ground, the bright return stroke travels up the same path, and that is the flash we see.
15. (count) Two charges 3 centimetres apart push with a force of 18 units. What is the force when they are only 1 centimetre apart?  
   **Answer:** 162. *Why:* 1 centimetre is one third of 3 centimetres, so it is 3 times as close. The force multiplies by 3 times 3, which is 9. 18 times 9 is 162 units.
16. (perspective) Researchers say a bumblebee senses the electric field of a flower. Some scientists want more evidence.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks one, gives a reason, and suggests a test, such as hiding all other clues and seeing if the bees still choose by field.. *Why:* Both views are fair. A good test removes every other clue and sees whether the bee still chooses by the field.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A comb rubbed on a shawl gains 5 billion electrons. How many has the shawl lost?  
   Options: 5 billion / 10 billion / None, since charge was made  
   **Answer:** 5 billion. *Why:* Charge is never made or destroyed. What one thing gains, the other loses.
2. (mcq) Which result proves that a rubbed straw is charged?  
   Options: It pushes away a charged balloon / It picks up bits of paper / It sticks to a wall  
   **Answer:** It pushes away a charged balloon. *Why:* Paper and walls are pulled even when they are neutral. Only a push shows that two things have the same kind of charge.
3. (mcq) On a ladder, wool is rung 2 and PVC is rung 12. Wool is rubbed with PVC. Which gets the minus charge?  
   Options: PVC / Wool / Both get a minus  
   **Answer:** PVC. *Why:* PVC is lower on the ladder, so it takes electrons from wool. The material that gains electrons is minus.
4. (mcq) Paper is rung 5 on a ladder. It meets wool at rung 2 and PVC at rung 12. What happens?  
   Options: Paper is minus with wool and plus with PVC / Paper is plus with both / Paper is minus with both  
   **Answer:** Paper is minus with wool and plus with PVC. *Why:* Paper takes electrons from the rung above it, wool, and gives electrons to the rung below it, PVC. A material in the middle plays both roles.
5. (mcq) Why does a charge put on a steel spoon disappear when you hold the spoon in your hand?  
   Options: The charge runs through the spoon and your body to the floor / Steel makes charge vanish / Steel makes new charge of the opposite kind  
   **Answer:** The charge runs through the spoon and your body to the floor. *Why:* Steel and your body are both conductors. The charge spreads out and runs away to the Earth.
6. (mcq) Why does touching an earthed metal pipe calm static on a sheet of paper?  
   Options: The pipe gives the charge a metal path to the ground / The pipe is colder than the paper / The pipe is heavier than the paper  
   **Answer:** The pipe gives the charge a metal path to the ground. *Why:* An earthed pipe is joined to the ground by metal. Charge flows along it into the large Earth.
7. (mcq) Two charged balloons are 4 centimetres apart. They are moved to 8 centimetres apart. What happens to the push between them?  
   Options: It becomes a quarter as big / It becomes half as big / It stays the same  
   **Answer:** It becomes a quarter as big. *Why:* The force follows the inverse square law. Twice the distance gives a quarter of the force.
8. (mcq) Why do more rubs make two balloons push each other further apart?  
   Options: More electrons moved, so each balloon has more charge / The balloons get hotter / The wool pushes them  
   **Answer:** More electrons moved, so each balloon has more charge. *Why:* Each rub moves more electrons. More charge on each balloon means a bigger push between them.
9. (mcq) What makes a spark jump across a gap of air?  
   Options: The push on the air gets strong enough for the air to let charge through / Air is a conductor / The air is damp  
   **Answer:** The push on the air gets strong enough for the air to let charge through. *Why:* Air is an insulator until the push is about 3,000 volts for each millimetre. Then it gives way, and charge jumps across.
10. (mcq) Which place most needs to guard against static sparks?  
   Options: A flour mill / A library / A bus stop  
   **Answer:** A flour mill. *Why:* Fine flour dust in the air can burn very fast if a spark lights it. Mills keep the air damp and earth every metal part.
11. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: What exactly moves, and why, when two surfaces touch and part / Whether charge is ever made from nothing / Whether the same kinds of charge push apart  
   **Answer:** What exactly moves, and why, when two surfaces touch and part. *Why:* The other two are settled. Electrons, ions and tiny pieces of material have each been proposed for the first.
12. (mcq) Why does the phrase 'cotton is a plus material' mislead?  
   Options: Cotton is plus with some partners and minus with others / Cotton has no charge at all / Cotton is always minus  
   **Answer:** Cotton is plus with some partners and minus with others. *Why:* Plus and minus depend on the partner. Cotton gives electrons to rungs below it and takes them from rungs above it.

## Applications
- Laser printers and photocopiers, which put black powder only where a pattern of charge has been drawn.
- Chimney dust catchers in power stations, which charge the dust so it sticks to plates.
- Electric paint spraying, where charged droplets push apart into a fine mist and are pulled to the object.
- Earthing straps on petrol and diesel tankers, which stop charge building up near fuel vapour.
- Static bars in printing and packing plants, which remove charge from paper and plastic sheets.
- Lightning conductors on tall buildings, which carry a strike safely to the ground.
- The earth pin on a three-pin plug, which gives stray charge a path to the ground.
- Anti-static wrist straps and bags for computer chips, which stop a tiny spark from ruining them.
- Air purifiers that charge dust and smoke so they stick to plates.
- Flour mills and fireworks factories that keep the air damp and earth every metal part to avoid sparks.

## Spotted around us
- A petrol or diesel tanker on a highway with a thin metal chain or strap dragging on the road.
- A pointed metal rod on top of a tall building, a school roof or a water tower, with a thick wire running down its side.
- The long earth pin on a three-pin plug, which is longer than the other two pins.
- Hair rising and crackling when you pull off a synthetic dupatta or a woollen sweater on a dry winter evening.
- A small shock when you touch a metal door handle or a car door after sliding across the seat on a dry day.
- A polythene bag clinging to your hand or arm when you try to shake it off.
- Dust that settles thickly on a TV screen or a fan blade, because they are slightly charged and pull dust to them.
- Fine dust or flour clinging to the inside of a plastic jar or a plastic bag.

## Open questions
- **Why exactly does rubbing two materials move charge between them?** Electrons, ions and tiny bits of material have each been seen moving in some tests and not in others. Laboratories still disagree, and it is hard to look at a surface only a few atoms thick.
- **Why do two pieces of the very same material sometimes charge each other?** A simple ladder cannot explain it. Research since 2011 shows patchy surfaces and size differences matter, but no one theory covers all the results.
- **How does the very first spark of a lightning bolt start inside a storm cloud?** The push inside a cloud seems too weak to break down air, yet lightning starts. Scientists have ideas about ice crystals and cosmic rays, and none is settled.
- **How many animals sense electric fields, and what do they use them for?** Bees, spiders, sharks and some fish have been studied, but most animals have not. Many studies are small and need to be repeated.
- **Do the dust storms of the Thar desert or of Mars make lightning too?** Rubbing grains of sand charge each other, and lightning has been seen in some dust storms on Earth. Whether it happens in every big storm, and on Mars, is not yet clear.
- **Why are charges made only in whole units of one electron?** Every charge ever measured is a whole number of electron charges, but no one knows why charge comes in these units.

## Connected maths
- In the Charge Ladder, there are 7 cards: wool, polythene and five things. How many different pairs can you make from 7 cards, if each pair is counted once? **21 pairs.**. The first card makes a pair with the 6 others. The next card makes a new pair with 5 others, then 4, then 3, then 2, then 1. 6 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 is 21. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L4`)
- Two charges 2 centimetres apart push with a force of 36 units. What is the force at 4 centimetres apart, and at 6 centimetres apart? **9 units at 4 centimetres, and 4 units at 6 centimetres.**. 4 centimetres is 2 times as far, so the force divides by 2 times 2, which is 4. 36 divided by 4 is 9. 6 centimetres is 3 times as far, so the force divides by 3 times 3, which is 9. 36 divided by 9 is 4. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A rubbed balloon might carry about 60,000,000,000 extra electrons. In India we count in crores, and 1 crore is 10,000,000. How many crore electrons is that? **6,000 crore.**. 60,000,000,000 divided by 10,000,000 is 6,000. So the balloon carries about 6,000 crore extra electrons. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- In the Two Balloons test, one student measured the gap beyond the resting gap after 10 rubs three times: 7 centimetres, 9 centimetres and 8 centimetres. What is the middle value of the three? **8 centimetres.**. Put the numbers in order: 7, 8, 9. The middle one is 8. The middle value is a fair summary, even if one try went wrong. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- In the Two Balloons test, 5 rubs gave a gap of 4 centimetres beyond the resting gap, and 20 rubs gave 10 centimetres. The number of rubs became 4 times as large. How many times as large did the gap become? **2 and a half times.**. 10 divided by 4 is 2 and a half. The rubs went up 4 times, but the gap went up only 2 and a half times. So doubling the rubbing does not double the push. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A static spark needs about 3,000 volts for each millimetre of air it jumps. How many volts are needed for a spark that jumps 3 millimetres, and for one that jumps 1 centimetre? **9,000 volts for 3 millimetres, and 30,000 volts for 1 centimetre.**. 3 millimetres is 3 times 3,000, which is 9,000. 1 centimetre is 10 millimetres, and 10 times 3,000 is 30,000. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- Two balloons hang from one point after being rubbed. Each thread leans 25 degrees away from straight down. What is the angle between the two threads? **50 degrees.**. Each thread leans 25 degrees to its own side. The two angles add up: 25 + 25 = 50 degrees between the threads. (queues `math.lines-angles.angles.L4`)
- Five families did the Charge Ladder. The number of their five things that pushed the balloon after wool was 5, 4, 5, 3 and 5. After polythene it was 2, 1, 3, 2 and 2. How many more pushes were there after wool than after polythene in total? **12 more pushes.**. After wool: 5 + 4 + 5 + 3 + 5 = 22. After polythene: 2 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 10. 22 take away 10 is 12 more. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
