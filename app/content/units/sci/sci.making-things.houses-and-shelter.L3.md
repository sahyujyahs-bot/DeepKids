# Houses and shelter: what keeps a home standing, dry and safe
`sci.making-things.houses-and-shelter.L3`

**Summary.** Push the top of a square frame sideways, and it leans over and folds flat. Push a triangle frame made of the same strips, and it keeps its shape. That is why roofs, towers and bridges are full of triangles. A shelter keeps us safe from rain, sun, heat, cold, wind and animals. People build it from what is close by: mud, bamboo, stone, grass, wood or brick. So a house in rainy Kerala has a steep tiled roof, and a house in dry Ladakh has a flat mud roof. New designs use less material and stand up better to floods and earthquakes. Safety rules keep builders and families from getting hurt.

**Intro.** *Make a square from four strips of card, joined at the corners with pins. Lay it flat and push one side gently. It leans over and squashes flat. Now make a triangle from three strips and push it the same way. Why does the triangle not lean?* Every home has to stay standing. Wind pushes on it, its own heavy roof presses down on it, and sometimes the ground shakes. In this unit you test frames, roofs and tiles at home, and find out how builders all over India make houses that stand up, keep the rain out and keep people safe. You also meet houses made of mud, bamboo, stone and grass, and the people who are designing new ones.

## Terms
- **Shelter**: A place that protects a living being from rain, sun, heat, cold, wind and danger. A house is a shelter for people. *e.g.* A bus stop roof is a shelter when it rains. A crow's nest is a shelter for her eggs.
- **Material**: What a thing is made of. *e.g.* A door can be made of wood, steel or plastic. Each one is a different material.
- **Natural material**: A material taken from nature and used with little change, such as mud, stone, bamboo, wood or grass. *e.g.* A Toda hut in the Nilgiris is made of bamboo, cane and thatch.
- **Frame**: The skeleton of a building: the poles, beams or strips that hold it up. Walls and roof are fixed onto the frame. *e.g.* A wedding pandal has a frame of poles tied with rope. The cloth is put on later.
- **Joint**: The place where two parts of a frame meet and are fixed together. *e.g.* In your card frame, every pin is a joint.
- **Rigid**: Keeps its shape when it is pushed. A rigid frame does not lean or fold. *e.g.* Your card triangle is rigid. Your card square is not.
- **Brace**: An extra strip or pole fixed from corner to corner across a frame, so that it cannot lean. A brace turns a square into two triangles. *e.g.* A wooden gate often has one diagonal plank across it. That plank is a brace.
- **Truss**: A frame made of many triangles joined together. It is strong but uses little material. *e.g.* Many roofs, mobile phone towers and railway bridges are trusses.
- **Load**: The weight or push that a building or frame has to hold up. *e.g.* A roof's load is its own weight, plus rain, and people or water tanks on top.
- **Slope**: How steeply a surface tilts. A steep slope is high at one end and low at the other. *e.g.* Rain runs off a steep roof quickly. On a flat roof it can stand in puddles.
- **Thatch**: A roof made of thick layers of dry grass, straw, reeds or palm leaves. *e.g.* Some village houses in Tamil Nadu and Kerala have thatch made of dried coconut leaves.
- **Earthquake**: A sudden shaking of the ground, caused by huge blocks of rock deep under the ground slipping past each other. *e.g.* In January 2001, a big earthquake shook Kutch in Gujarat.

## Explanations
### What a shelter does · *defined*
**Definition:** A shelter is a place that protects a living being from the weather and from danger, so they can rest, sleep and keep their young and their food safe.

Shelter is one of our basic needs, along with air, water, food and clothing. A house keeps off rain and sun, keeps out cold wind, and keeps out snakes, rats and thieves. People are not the only ones who build shelters. A male baya weaver bird weaves a hanging nest of grass strips. His problem is that snakes and other hunters eat eggs and chicks. So the nest hangs from a thin branch, often over water, and the way in is a long tube that opens downwards. A hunter finds it very hard to reach inside. Ants dig rooms under the ground, where it stays cool and damp. A shelter must suit the weather of its place and the life of whoever lives in it.
- e.g. A city flat keeps a family dry in the monsoon and cool in summer.
- e.g. A baya weaver's nest hangs over water, with its door at the bottom.
- e.g. Ants keep their eggs in rooms under the soil, out of the hot sun.
### Why a triangle keeps its shape · *defined*
**Definition:** A triangle frame is rigid: it cannot change shape unless one of its sides gets longer, shorter or breaks. A square frame can lean over without any side changing length.

Join three strips into a triangle. Once the three lengths are fixed, there is only one shape they can make. To lean, one strip would have to stretch or squash, and strong strips do not. Join four strips into a square, and the corners can turn like hinges. The square leans into a diamond and then folds flat, though every strip stays the same length. Add one strip from one corner to the opposite corner. Now the square is two triangles, and it is rigid. That extra strip is called a brace. A frame made of many triangles is called a truss. Builders use trusses in roofs, towers, cranes and bridges, because they hold a big load with little material.
- e.g. A roof of a shed or a factory is often held up by wooden or steel triangles.
- e.g. A mobile phone tower is a tall truss made of steel triangles.
- e.g. The Howrah Bridge in Kolkata is a steel bridge made of many triangles.
### Houses are built from what is near · *defined*
**Definition:** Long ago, people built homes from materials found close by. The weather of the place decided what kind of house kept people safe and comfortable.

Carrying heavy materials far is hard, so people used what they had. In Kerala and coastal Karnataka, many old houses have walls of laterite, a reddish stone cut from the ground, and steep roofs of clay tiles for the heavy monsoon rain. In Assam, some families, such as the Mising people, build bamboo houses raised on poles. When the river floods, the water flows under the floor. In Ladakh, rain and snow are light, so houses have thick walls of sun-dried mud bricks and flat mud roofs. Thick mud walls are slow to heat up in the day and slow to cool at night. In the Nilgiris, the Toda people built small huts with a rounded roof of bamboo, cane and thatch. In Kutch, round mud houses called bhungas have light cone-shaped roofs. Today many homes are made of brick, cement, steel and glass, which can be carried a long way by truck.
- e.g. A Kerala house with a steep tiled roof.
- e.g. A Mising house on bamboo stilts in Assam.
- e.g. A flat-roofed mud house in Ladakh.
- e.g. A round bhunga with a cone roof in Kutch, Gujarat.
### Roofs and rain · *defined*
**Definition:** A sloping roof lets rain run off before it can soak in or leak. Roof tiles overlap, with each higher tile lying on top of the one below it, so water always runs onto the outside of the next tile.

Water flows downhill. On a flat roof it can stand in puddles and slowly find its way through cracks. On a steep roof it runs off in seconds. That is why places with very heavy rain, like Kerala, the Konkan coast and the north-east, have steep roofs. Places with little rain, like Ladakh and much of Rajasthan, often have flat roofs, which people also use for drying grain and sleeping on hot nights. Tiles must overlap the right way. If the higher tile lies on top of the lower one, water runs from one tile onto the next and off the edge. If the lower tile lies on top, water runs into the gap and drips inside. Mangalore tiles, made in Mangaluru since the 1860s, have grooves that lock each tile to its neighbours.
- e.g. Steep tiled roofs in Kerala villages.
- e.g. Flat mud roofs in Ladakh, used for drying apricots and storing hay.
- e.g. The tin sheet over a balcony slopes outwards, so rain falls off the edge.
### Wind and earthquakes push sideways · *defined*
**Definition:** Weight pushes a building down, but wind and earthquakes push it sideways. A building stays up in an earthquake if its frame cannot lean and its heavy parts cannot fall.

Most walls are good at holding weight that presses straight down. A sideways push is harder. It is like your push on the card square. Builders stop this in three main ways. They add braces and triangles, so the frame cannot lean. They tie walls together with bands of concrete or wood, so the walls move as one piece. And they keep roofs light, because a heavy roof that falls is the most dangerous part. In the Kutch earthquake of 26 January 2001, many round bhungas with light roofs in the Banni area were reported to have stood, while many newer buildings fell. Round walls have no long flat side for the shaking to push over. A badly built concrete building can be more dangerous than a well-built mud one.
- e.g. Tall buildings have braces, or a very stiff central core, to stand up to wind.
- e.g. Engineers test model buildings on shaking tables before building real ones.
- e.g. Rules for building in earthquake areas ask for bands of concrete tying the walls together.
### New designs: less material, more comfort · *debated*
People keep inventing new ways to build. Laurie Baker, an architect born in England who made his home in Kerala, built with local brick and stone. His rat-trap bond lays bricks on their edges, with an air gap inside the wall. It uses about a quarter fewer bricks and keeps rooms cooler. His jaali walls, with holes between the bricks, let breeze through. Other builders prefer concrete and steel, which are strong, quick to build tall, and easy to find. People still argue about which is better, because the answer depends on the place, the money, the weather and how well the house is built.
- e.g. Brick walls with gaps in a pattern, called jaali, in many Laurie Baker buildings in Thiruvananthapuram.
- e.g. Tall blocks of concrete flats in Chennai, Bengaluru and Hyderabad.
- e.g. Houses built with blocks of pressed mud and a little cement, made near the building site.
- **An architect who builds with mud and brick:** Local mud and brick cost less, need little energy to make, and keep rooms cool without a fan or AC. A well-made mud wall can last a hundred years.
- **A builder who builds with concrete:** Concrete with steel rods inside is very strong and can be built many floors high, which a crowded city needs. It does not wash away in heavy rain.
- **An engineer who studies earthquakes:** The material matters less than how well it is built. Braces, bands that tie the walls, and a light roof save more lives than any one material.
### Safety rules when building and at home · *defined*
**Definition:** Safety rules are steps that stop people getting hurt. On a building site and at home, most injuries come from falls, falling objects, sharp tools and electricity.

Builders wear a hard helmet, because a falling brick or tool can hurt the head. They wear strong shoes, because nails and broken glass lie on the ground. Workers high up wear a harness tied to the building, so a slip does not become a fall. Bright jackets help drivers of trucks and cranes see them. At home, the same dangers are smaller but real. Children do not climb on balcony walls, grills or water tanks. Wet floors are wiped at once. Broken switches and loose wires are left for a grown-up to fix. In this unit, a grown-up does every step with a sharp point, a knife or a hammer.
- e.g. A worker on scaffolding wearing a yellow helmet and a harness.
- e.g. A sign at a building site saying 'Helmets must be worn'.
- e.g. Grills or a net on a high balcony so that nobody can fall.

## Mostly agreed
- A triangle frame keeps its shape when pushed. A square frame leans unless it has a brace. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with card strips and pins, as in the Push Test. Engineers have used this in bridges and roofs for hundreds of years.
- Sloping roofs shed rain faster than flat roofs. *How we know:* Water flows downhill. You can see it on the tray roof in the Rain on the Roof test, and in every monsoon.
- Traditional houses often suit their weather well, because people improved them over many generations. *How we know:* Studies of mud houses in hot, dry places show their thick walls keep rooms cooler in the day than thin walls do.
- Most deaths in earthquakes are caused by buildings falling, not by the shaking itself. *How we know:* Reports after earthquakes all over the world, including Latur in 1993 and Kutch in 2001, show this.

## How we found out
**Fazlur Rahman Khan, an engineer born in Dhaka, with the architect Bruce Graham, in Chicago, The 1960s. The John Hancock Center was finished in 1969..** In the 1960s, buildings were getting taller, and engineers had a problem. A tall building is pushed sideways by the wind, like a tree. Most engineers thought the answer was a thick skeleton of columns and beams with very stiff joints. But each extra floor then needed more and more steel, so very tall buildings cost far too much. Fazlur Rahman Khan, an engineer born in Dhaka in 1929, thought about it differently. He worked in Chicago, in the United States, with the architect Bruce Graham. Khan saw that the outside walls could work like one stiff hollow tube. For the John Hancock Center, a 100-storey tower finished in 1969, he put huge diagonal steel braces across the outside, making giant triangles. The triangles stopped the building leaning in the wind. The tower used about as much steel for each floor as an ordinary 50-storey building. The old idea, that height needed ever thicker skeletons, was shown wrong. Triangles, placed well, did the work instead.  
*What people believed before:* Engineers thought that a taller building needed a thicker and heavier skeleton of columns and stiff joints, so very tall towers would cost too much steel. Khan showed that big diagonal braces making triangles on the outside could hold the building firm against wind with far less steel.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A square is stronger than a triangle, because it has more sides.** (A common first guess among children and many grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Four strips use more material than three, so it seems they should be stronger.  
  Against: In the Push Test, the square leans and folds flat with a gentle push. The triangle does not, because three fixed sides allow only one shape.
- **Concrete houses are always safer than mud houses.** (Many people today; *debated*)  
  For: Concrete with steel rods inside is very strong, and does not wash away in rain.  
  Against: In the 2001 Kutch earthquake, many newer buildings fell while many old bhungas stood. A badly built concrete house with a heavy roof can be more dangerous than a well-built mud one.
- **Thick, heavy walls and roofs make a house safe in an earthquake.** (An old belief in many places; *rejected*)  
  For: Heavy walls feel solid and are hard to push over by hand.  
  Against: In the Latur earthquake in Maharashtra in 1993, many heavy stone walls and roofs fell on people asleep at night. A shaking ground pushes heavy things harder, so heavy roofs are more dangerous.
- **Mud houses are old-fashioned and only for the poor.** (Many people in towns and cities; *debated*)  
  For: Most new houses in cities are made of concrete, and mud walls need care after heavy rain.  
  Against: Mud is cheap, cool in summer, and can be used again. Architects in India and around the world now build new mud houses on purpose, for families who can afford anything.

## Roots
We have tested frames, roofs and tiles, and met houses from all over India. Now we ask what building a shelter tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your square frame folded flat at one push, but your triangle frame kept its shape. Both were made of the same card strips and pins. Three sides of fixed length fit together in only one shape, while four sides can lean into many. So a roof or tower stays firm because its parts form triangles.  
*Habit it breaks:* We usually think a building is strong because its material is strong, thick or heavy. But your two frames used the same strips, and one folded while the other held. How the parts are joined decides as much as what they are made of.  
*Evidence:* In the Push Test, the top corner of the square moved many centimetres and the square folded flat. The triangle's corner moved less than one centimetre. One diagonal strip across the square stopped it leaning too.

- *who am i:* Your skeleton is your frame. When you stand with your feet apart, your legs and the floor make a triangle, and you are much harder to push over.
- *what is life:* Every living being needs shelter. A baya weaver weaves a hanging nest, ants dig rooms under the soil, and people build houses.
- *what is this world:* Everything that stands is pushed all the time, by its own weight, by wind and by the ground. It stays standing because its parts push back.

A Toda hut, a Kerala tiled house, a Ladakh mud house and a block of flats in Chennai look nothing alike. They are made of grass, clay, mud or concrete, by different people, for different weather. Yet under every one is the same simple rule: a frame stays firm when its parts make triangles. One plain fact about three sides, found again and again by builders who never met, takes shape as thousands of different homes.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** We cut out doors and windows to make a room. It is the empty space inside that makes the room useful. (Your frames and a house's walls hold their shape so that an empty space can stay open inside. We build the walls, but we live in the space they hold.)
- **Dhammapada 153 to 154:** The Buddha spoke of seeking the builder of a house. He said that its rafters were broken and its ridge-pole was shattered, so that house would not be built again. (The Buddha used a house of rafters and a ridge-pole as a picture of the self. A house is only parts held together at their joints. When the joints and beams fail, there is no house left, only poles.)
- Science (settled): A triangle of fixed sides cannot change shape, so trusses made of triangles hold big loads with little material. This is used in roofs, cranes, towers and bridges.
- Science (debated): How well traditional mud houses stand up to earthquakes, compared with concrete houses, depends a lot on how each one is built. Engineers are still studying this.
- Other view, Le Corbusier, a famous architect, in his book Vers une architecture, which means Towards an Architecture (1923): He wrote that a house is a machine for living in. In this view, a house is a useful tool, like a car, built as well and as cheaply as possible. It carries no deeper meaning about life, and reading one into it is our own idea.
- Practice, Stand like a triangle (3 min): Stand up with your feet close together. I will press very gently on your shoulder. Did you wobble? Now stand with your feet wide apart. Your legs and the floor make a triangle. I will press again, just as gently. Did you wobble now? Close your eyes and take five slow breaths. Feel the floor pushing up through your feet. Feel how steady you are.
- Ask: Your square and your triangle were made of the same strips. Which matters more for a strong frame: the strips, or the way they are joined?
- Ask: A house is walls around an empty space. Is the house the walls, or the space, or both?
- Ask: A baya weaver was never taught how to weave a nest. How do you think the first weavers worked out what makes a good one?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A triangle stays rigid and a square leans. Why does having one more side make a frame weaker, not stronger? (hint: Push the corners of your square. Which parts turn? Do the strips get longer or shorter, or only the corners?)
- *deep:* Your body has a skeleton, like a house has a frame. Where in your body are there triangles? (hint: Stand with your feet apart and look at your legs and the floor. Look at your arm when you put your hand on your hip.)
- *big:* A bird, an ant and a person all build shelters. Is a nest a home in the same way a house is? (hint: Think about what each one is for: sleeping, keeping young safe, keeping food, being together.)
- *big:* A house is walls around an empty space. Which part do we live in: the walls or the space? (hint: Think about a room with no door or window, and a room with no walls at all.)

## Story: The Pandal That Leaned
*Pandals of casuarina poles tied with coir rope are really built for weddings and festivals in Tamil Nadu. Meenakshi, Selvi, Arun and this wedding are made up.*

Meenakshi was nine, and her cousin Lakshmi was getting married on Sunday. The wedding lunch would be on the terrace of Paati's house in Madurai. On Thursday morning, a lorry brought a load of long, thin casuarina poles and bundles of coir rope.

Selvi Athai, Meenakshi's aunt, built pandals for a living. A pandal is a big shade of cloth held up on a frame of poles. Selvi Athai had built pandals for temple festivals, school sports days and hundreds of weddings. This time she said her son Arun could build the frame himself. Arun was twelve. He had watched his mother work since he was small.

Arun worked hard all day. He stood the poles up in rows, each one in a heavy pot of sand. He tied long poles across the top, from post to post, with tight coir knots. By evening the frame stood on the terrace like a big grid of squares. Meenakshi carried rope and water and held the ladder.

"Not bad," said Arun, wiping his face. "Tomorrow we put the cloth on."

That night the wind came. It was the end of the monsoon, and the wind blew hard across the terraces. Meenakshi woke up and heard the poles creaking. She ran upstairs in her nightie.

The whole frame was leaning. Every pole had tipped the same way, like a row of people pushed by a crowd. The squares between the poles had become long, thin diamonds. Not one knot had come undone. Not one pole had broken. But the pandal was leaning over towards the edge of the terrace.

Arun came up behind her. "Amma!" he shouted down the stairs. "Come quickly!"

Selvi Athai came up with a torch. She did not shout. She walked once round the frame and pushed one pole with her hand. The whole frame swayed.

"Your knots are good," she said to Arun. "Your poles are good. Something is missing. Meenakshi, give me four of those short sticks from the broom."

Meenakshi pulled four thin sticks out of the coconut broom. Selvi Athai tied them at the ends with thread to make a small square. She put the little square on the floor and pushed one corner with her finger. It leaned and fell flat, just like the pandal.

Then she took three sticks and tied them into a triangle. "Push this one," she said.

Meenakshi pushed. She pushed harder. The triangle slid across the floor, but it did not change shape at all.

"Why doesn't it lean?" asked Arun.

"Try to make it lean," said his mother. "Which stick has to change?"

Meenakshi looked at it for a long time. "To lean, one stick would have to get longer, or shorter," she said slowly. "And the sticks can't. But in the square, none of the sticks has to change. Only the corners turn."

"That's it," said Selvi Athai. She tied one more broom stick across the little square, from one corner to the opposite corner. Now there were two triangles inside the square. Meenakshi pushed it. It did not lean.

Arun's face went red. "I forgot the cross poles," he said. "I've seen you tie them a hundred times. I thought they were just for hanging lights."

"Everyone forgets something on their first pandal," said Selvi Athai. "I forgot the cross poles on mine, too. My mother made me take the whole thing down."

They worked until the wind dropped. Selvi Athai held the torch. Arun climbed the ladder. Meenakshi passed up rope. At each end of the pandal, and in the middle, they tied a long pole from the top of one post down to the foot of the next. Every one made a triangle. As each pole was tied, the frame stood a little straighter. By the time the sky turned grey, the pandal stood upright, and the wind could not move it.

On Sunday, the pandal was covered in white cloth and strings of jasmine and marigold. Two hundred guests ate sambar, rice and payasam on banana leaves underneath it. Nobody looked at the cross poles. They were hidden behind the cloth.

But Meenakshi knew they were there. After lunch she found Arun on the stairs, eating a third laddu.

"Next year," she said, "I'm building one."

"You'll need cross poles," said Arun.

"I know," said Meenakshi. "I'll need triangles."

**For the parent:** A frame of squares can lean even when every knot and pole is strong, because its corners can turn. A triangle cannot lean unless one of its sides changes length. So one cross pole, which turns a square into two triangles, keeps the whole pandal upright.

- Ask: No knot came undone and no pole broke. So why did the pandal lean?
- Ask: How did the little square of broom sticks help Meenakshi understand the big pandal?
- Ask: Why did the triangle slide across the floor without changing shape?
- Ask: Arun thought the cross poles were only for hanging lights. Why do people sometimes copy what they see without knowing why it is done?
- Ask: Where in your home or street could you add a cross piece to stop something wobbling?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Push Test: square frame or triangle frame? (35 min)
- **question:** When we push the top of a frame sideways, which shape keeps its shape best: a square, a triangle, or a square with one strip from corner to corner?
- **predict:** Look at the strips. A square uses four strips and a triangle uses only three. Which frame do you think will lean the most when you push it? Which will lean the least? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same kind of strips, the same pins and the same push for every frame. Only the shape changes. Hold the bottom strip still each time. Push each frame 3 times, because one push can be a fluke.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the holes with the compass point or a sharp pencil, pressing the card onto an eraser. Keep split pins away from babies, who may swallow them.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the three frames, before and after the push, and write one sentence under each drawing.
- **results table:** Frame | My guess: will it lean? (a lot, a little, not at all) | Push 1: how far the top corner moved (cm) | Push 2 (cm) | Push 3 (cm) | Did it fold flat? (yes or no)
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut 7 card strips, each 2 cm wide and 15 cm long. 2. Cut one strip 25 cm long. 3. Make a hole 1 cm from each end of the short strips.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to build three frames, like the skeletons of tiny houses. First we need strips. I will make the holes with the compass point.  
  *Child mode:* Help cut strips of card. Each one is 2 cm wide and 15 cm long. A grown-up makes a hole near each end.
  - Note: Press the card onto an eraser and twist the compass point to make each hole. The holes should be a little loose round the pin, so the corners can turn. Cereal box card works well. Thin paper is too floppy.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Join four strips into a square, with a split pin at each corner. 2. Join the other three strips into a triangle. 3. Keep the long strip for later.  
  *Parent says:* Let's join the strips. Push a split pin through two holes and open its legs flat at the back. Make a square, then a triangle.  
  *Child mode:* Join four strips into a square with split pins. Then join three strips into a triangle.
  - Note: Open the pin's legs flat, but do not press the joint tight. The corners should be able to turn freely, like a hinge. If you used twist ties or thread, keep them loose too.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold up the square and the triangle. 2. Ask your child which one will lean more when pushed. 3. Write the guess and the reason in the table.  
  *Parent says:* The square has four strips and the triangle has only three. When I push the top sideways, which one will lean more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the square and the triangle. Which will lean more when you push it? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will lean less? (good: The square has more strips, so it is stronger.; The triangle has a pointed top, so it is harder to push.; I don't know, let's test it.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Many children guess the square is stronger because it has more strips. That is a fine guess. The test will answer it.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the square flat on the table. 2. Tape its bottom strip down firmly. 3. Lay the ruler beside the top strip, with 0 at the top left corner. 4. Push the top strip sideways with one finger, gently. 5. Read how far the corner moved.  
  *Parent says:* Push the top strip sideways, about as hard as you press a doorbell. Watch the ruler. How many centimetres did the corner move?  
  *Child mode:* Tape the bottom strip down. Push the top strip sideways, gently. Read the ruler. How far did the corner move?
  - Note: The square will lean into a diamond and keep going until it is almost flat. The corner usually moves 10 to 14 cm. Lift it back to a square before each push. Write all three pushes.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Untape the square. 2. Tape the triangle's bottom strip down. 3. Push the top corner sideways in the same way, three times. 4. Measure each time.  
  *Parent says:* Now the triangle. Same push, same ruler. Watch the corner closely.  
  *Child mode:* Now push the triangle the same way, three times. Measure how far the corner moves.
  - Note: The triangle's top corner should move less than 1 cm, only as much as the loose holes allow. If the card bends, use thicker card. A triangle that slides across the table needs its bottom strip taped more firmly.
  - Check: When you pushed the triangle, why did it not lean like the square? → **To lean, one of its sides would have to get longer or shorter**
- **s6 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the square back together as a square. 2. Lay the long strip from the bottom left corner to the top right corner. 3. Mark where it crosses the top right pin. 4. A grown-up makes a hole there and pins it on.  
  *Parent says:* What if we add one strip across the square, from corner to corner? Guess first: will it still lean?  
  *Child mode:* Add the long strip across the square, from one corner to the opposite corner. Will it lean now? Guess first.
  - Note: Hold the square straight while you mark the second hole, so the brace fits exactly. The long strip shares a pin at each of its two corners.
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Push the braced square three times, the same way, and measure.  
  *Parent says:* Same push again. How far does the corner move now?  
  *Child mode:* Push the square with the long strip. Measure how far it moves.
  - Note: It should now move less than 1 cm, like the triangle. The long strip has turned the square into two triangles. This strip is called a brace.
- **s8 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things: what I think now, what I saw, and why it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.
  - Ask: Why did the square fold, though no strip got longer or shorter? (good: Because the corners turned like hinges.; Because a square can change shape without changing its sides.)
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A strong answer names the shape, gives the numbers from the table, and says that the triangle's sides cannot change length.
  - Think: Look at a gate, a chair or a ladder at home. Can you find a brace that turns a square into triangles?
### e2 · Rain on the Roof: how steep should it be? (25 min)
- **question:** Does water run off a steep roof faster than a flat one, and does any water stay behind on a flat roof?
- **predict:** We will pour water on a roof that is flat, a little tilted and very tilted. Which roof will be dry first? Will any water stay on the flat one? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same roof, the same 4 spoons of water and the same pouring place each time. Only the slope changes. Wipe the roof dry between tries. Do each slope twice.
- **safety:** Wipe up spilled water at once, so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw the three roofs and colour any puddles left on them.
- **results table:** Slope | Books under the high end | My guess: seconds to stop dripping | Try 1 (seconds) | Try 2 (seconds) | Water left in puddles? (yes or no)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the tray flat on a table, with one edge over a bowl. 2. Show the cup of water and the spoon.  
  *Parent says:* This tray is our roof. Rain falls on it. Where will the water go if the roof is flat? Where will it go if we tilt it?  
  *Child mode:* The tray is a roof. Will rain run off a flat roof? What about a tilted one?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess for each slope: flat, one book and four books. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which roof will be dry first? Will any water stay on the flat roof? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which roof will be dry first. Will water stay on the flat one? Why?
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. Do not explain yet.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour 4 spoons of water onto the middle of the flat tray. 2. Start the stopwatch. 3. Stop it when no drop has fallen for 5 seconds, or after 60 seconds. 4. Wipe dry. 5. Repeat with one book, then four books.  
  *Parent says:* Pour slowly, all in the same place. Start the clock now. Tell me when the drips stop.  
  *Child mode:* Pour 4 spoons in the middle. Start the clock. Stop it when the drips stop.
  - Note: On the flat tray, most water stays in a puddle, so write 'more than 60' and 'yes'. On one book, water creeps slowly and some drops stay behind. On four books, it runs off in a few seconds. Do each slope twice.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the results together and talk about where houses have steep roofs.  
  *Parent says:* Kerala gets very heavy rain in the monsoon. Ladakh gets very little. Which roof would you build in each place? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Kerala gets lots of rain. Ladakh gets very little. Which roof suits each place?
  - Check: Why do many old houses in Kerala have steep roofs? → **Rain runs off a steep roof quickly, before it can soak in or leak**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think now, what they saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you think now, what you saw, and why it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think now. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Note: A strong answer gives the times, says water flows downhill, and links steep roofs to places with heavy rain.
### e3 · Which Way Should the Tiles Overlap? (25 min)
- **question:** Does it matter which way roof tiles overlap? Which way keeps the tissue underneath dry?
- **predict:** On roof A, each higher tile lies on top of the tile below it. On roof B, each lower tile lies on top of the tile above it. Which roof will keep the tissue dry? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same tiles, the same slope and the same 4 spoons of water for both roofs. Only the way the tiles overlap changes. Do each roof twice, with a fresh dry tissue.
- **safety:** Wash the milk packets and dry them before cutting. A grown-up helps with the scissors.
- **record:** Draw a side view of both roofs, with arrows showing where the water went. Mark where the tissue got wet.
- **results table:** Roof | How the tiles overlap | My guess: will the tissue get wet? | Try 1: wet spots on the tissue | Try 2: wet spots on the tissue
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut a slit 3 cm wide down the middle of each card, from just below the top to just above the bottom. 2. Tape 4 plastic tiles in rows across each card, covering the slit.  
  *Parent says:* The slit in the card is like the gaps in a real roof. If water gets through the tiles, it will drip through the slit onto the tissue.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts a slit in each card. Tape 4 plastic tiles over the slit.
  - Note: On roof A, start taping at the bottom: tape the lowest tile first, then lay each higher tile so it covers the top 2 cm of the one below. On roof B, start at the top, so each lower tile covers the bottom 2 cm of the one above. Tape each tile along its two side edges only, so water can run over or under the tile edges.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show both roofs side by side and ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the edges of the tiles on each roof. When water runs down, which roof will let it in? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at both roofs. Which one will let water through? Why?
  - Note: Ask your child to run a finger down each roof, from top to bottom. On roof B the finger catches on each tile edge. That is where water gets caught too.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tilt each roof on two books over a dry tissue. 2. Pour 4 spoons of water slowly along the top edge. 3. Lift the roof and look at the tissue. 4. Repeat with fresh tissues.  
  *Parent says:* Pour slowly along the top. Now lift the roof. Is the tissue wet? Where?  
  *Child mode:* Tilt each roof over a tissue. Pour water along the top. Lift it up. Is the tissue wet?
  - Note: Under roof A the tissue should stay dry, because water runs from the top of each tile onto the outside of the next. Under roof B, water runs into the edge of each tile, under it, and through the slit. You should see wet spots.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think now, what they saw and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you think now, what you saw, and why the water went where it did.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think. Say what you saw. Say why.
  - Check: On a real roof, how must the tiles overlap to keep the rain out? → **Each higher tile lies on top of the tile below it**

## Activities
### a1 · What Is Our Home Made Of? A home walk (10 min)
- **safety:** Look at the balcony from inside, and don't climb on anything to see the roof.
- **record:** Make a table: part of the house, material, and why that material.
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Walk to the main door, a window, the floor, a wall and the ceiling. 2. At each one, ask what it is made of. 3. Write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Let's be detectives in our own home. What is this door made of? Why do you think the builder chose that?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the door, a window, the floor and a wall. What is each made of? Why?
  - Ask: Why is the window made of glass, not wood? (good: So light can come in.; So we can see out.)
  - Note: Walls in most flats are brick or concrete blocks, covered with cement plaster and paint. Floors are often tiles, stone or concrete. Under the plaster, a concrete roof has steel rods inside.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask which materials came from nature with little change, and which were made in a factory.  
  *Parent says:* Which of these came from nature, like wood or stone? Which were made in a factory, like glass or cement?  
  *Child mode:* Which materials came from nature? Which were made in a factory?
  - Note: Wood and stone come from nature. Glass is made from sand, and cement from limestone and clay, but both are heated in factories. Every material started in nature.
### a2 · Triangle Hunt (15 min)
- **safety:** Look from inside a window or a balcony with a grill. Never lean over a balcony wall.
- **record:** Draw five triangles you found. Under each, write what it holds up.
- **s1 (8 min)**

  *Child mode:* Find triangles that hold something up. Look at home and out of the window. Draw them.
  - Note: Good finds: the bracket under a shelf, the legs of a folding stand, a ladder leaning on a wall with the floor, a mobile tower, a crane, the steel roof of a bus stand or a railway station.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at photos of the Howrah Bridge in Kolkata, or a railway bridge near you. Count the triangles on one side.  
  *Parent says:* This is the Howrah Bridge in Kolkata. Its sides are made of steel triangles. Why do you think the builders used so many?  
  *Child mode:* Look at a photo of a steel bridge. Count the triangles. Why so many?
  - Check: Why does a steel bridge have triangles along its sides? → **Triangles keep their shape, so the bridge does not lean or sag**

### p1 · Build a Newspaper Hut You Can Sit In (90 min, 2 days)
- **safety:** Build on the floor, away from the stove and lamps. Don't climb on the hut.
- **record:** Draw your hut and mark every triangle. Write where it leaned and what you did to fix it.
- **schedule:** day 1: Roll 30 newspaper sticks. Tape them into triangles, and test a square of sticks against a triangle. (40 min); day 2: Join the triangles into a hut. Cover it with a sheet. Push it gently to test it, and add braces where it leans. (50 min)
- **s1 (25 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay a double sheet of newspaper flat. 2. Roll it tightly from one corner around a pencil. 3. Slide the pencil out and tape the end. 4. Make 30 sticks.  
  *Parent says:* We need about 30 sticks. Roll tightly, because a tight roll is much stronger than a loose one.  
  *Child mode:* Roll newspaper tightly from one corner. Tape the end. Make 30 sticks.
  - Note: Rolling from the corner gives a long, thin, strong stick about 80 cm long. Share the rolling. Aim for sticks of about the same length.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape four sticks into a square and three into a triangle. 2. Stand each upright and push the top gently.  
  *Parent says:* Remember the card frames? Let's see if big newspaper frames behave the same way.  
  *Child mode:* Make a big square and a big triangle. Push each one. What happens?
  - Note: The square will lean, just like the card one. The taped corners hold a little, but still bend.
- **s3 (35 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2: 1. Tape three triangles together at their sides to make a tent shape, like a tall pyramid. 2. Add more triangles until it is big enough to sit in. 3. Tape each joint firmly.  
  *Parent says:* Let's build our hut from triangles. Where will the door be?  
  *Child mode:* Join your triangles into a hut. Leave a gap for the door.
  - Note: A simple shape is a square base with four triangles leaning in to meet at the top, with a brace across each side of the base. Leave one side open as a door.
  - Wait: Come back to this on day 2.
- **s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Push gently on each side. 2. Where it leans, add a stick from corner to corner. 3. Cover it with the sheet and sit inside.  
  *Parent says:* Push it gently. Does it lean anywhere? What can we add to stop it?  
  *Child mode:* Push your hut gently. If it leans, add a brace. Then go inside.
  - Check: One side of your hut leans when you push it. What is the best fix? → **Add a stick from one corner of that side to the opposite corner**

## Challenges
### c1 · The Tower That Stands in the Fan's Wind (35 min)
- **problem:** Build the tallest tower you can from newspaper and tape. It must stay standing for 30 seconds while a table fan blows on it.
- **your turn:** Now design your own tower. Draw it first, build it, then test it in the fan's wind. Change one thing and test again.
- **constraints:** Use only 10 sheets of newspaper and 1 metre of tape.; The tower must stand on its own, with nothing holding it.; Put the fan on its highest speed, 1 metre away.
- **general:** Roll the newspaper into sticks and tape them into a tower of triangles.; Make the base wide and the top narrow, like a mobile tower.; Add braces across each side so the tower cannot lean.
- **creative:** Make the tower a hollow tube with diagonal strips on the outside, like Fazlur Rahman Khan's John Hancock Center.; Leave gaps in the sides so the wind can pass through, as it does through a steel tower.; Fold paper into a fan shape for the walls, because folds make paper stiff.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand one rolled newspaper stick upright in front of the fan. 2. Switch the fan on.  
  *Parent says:* Let's see the problem first. What does the wind do to a single stick?  
  *Child mode:* Stand one stick up. Turn on the fan. What happens?
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw three tower ideas together before building.  
  *Parent says:* Engineers draw before they build. What shapes did we learn are good at not leaning?  
  *Child mode:* Draw three ideas for a tower. Use what you learned about triangles.
- **s3 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Build the best idea. 2. Measure its height. 3. Test it in the fan for 30 seconds. 4. Improve it and test again.  
  *Parent says:* Build it and test it. If it falls, look at where it bent first.  
  *Child mode:* Build your tower. Measure it. Test it in the wind. Fix it and try again.
  - Note: The tower often bends at a joint low down. A wider base and braces near the bottom help most.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what they changed and why it helped.  
  *Parent says:* What did you change after the first test? Why did it help?  
  *Child mode:* What did you change? Why did it help?
  - Note: A strong answer names a change, such as a wider base or a brace, and links it to the tower not leaning.
### c2 · The Flood-Proof House (30 min)
- **problem:** Families in Assam live with floods every year. Build a small model house that stays dry when water 3 cm deep flows round it in a tray.
- **your turn:** Now design your own flood-proof house. Pour the water in slowly, then quickly. Does your house stay dry, and stay standing?
- **constraints:** The house must be at least 8 cm tall.; Use only things from home.; A small paper square inside the house must stay dry.
- **general:** Raise the house on stilts, like a Mising house in Assam.; Make the floor of something that floats.; Build a wall of clay or card around the house.
- **creative:** Make a house that floats and is tied by a string so it rises with the water.; Put stilts in upside-down paper cups so they stand firm on the tray.; Add braces between the stilts so they do not lean when water pushes them.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at a photo of a house on stilts in Assam during a flood.  
  *Parent says:* When the river floods, the water flows under this house. Why does the family stay dry?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the house on stilts. Why does the family stay dry in the flood?
- **s2 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Build a house. 2. Put the tissue inside on the floor. 3. Stand it in the tray. 4. Pour water slowly until it is 3 cm deep. 5. Check the tissue.  
  *Parent says:* Build it, then we will flood the tray. Pour slowly. Is the tissue still dry?  
  *Child mode:* Build your house. Put the tissue inside. Flood the tray. Is the tissue dry?
  - Note: Stilts often lean when the water pushes them. Braces between the stilts fix that. Mop up spills straight away.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what worked and what they would change.  
  *Parent says:* What kept the floor dry? What would you change for a bigger flood?  
  *Child mode:* What kept your house dry? What would you change?
  - Note: A strong answer explains that raising the floor above the water keeps it dry, and that stilts need braces.

## Worksheets
### Frames and Homes (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In the Push Test, you pushed the top of a square card frame sideways. What happened?  
   Options: It stayed square / It leaned into a diamond and folded flat / One strip broke  
   **Answer:** It leaned into a diamond and folded flat. *Why:* The corners of a square turn like hinges, so the square leans and folds without any strip changing length.
2. (mcq) Which of these frames keeps its shape when pushed?  
   Options: A square / A rectangle / A triangle  
   **Answer:** A triangle. *Why:* A triangle cannot change shape unless one side changes length. Squares and rectangles can lean by turning their corners.
3. (fill) A strip fixed from one corner of a frame to the opposite corner, so it cannot lean, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['brace']. *Why:* A brace turns a square into two triangles.
4. (truefalse) A square frame is stronger than a triangle frame, because it has more strips.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. In the Push Test, the square folded flat and the triangle did not. The number of strips matters less than the shape they make.
5. (match) Match each house to the place it suits.  
   **Answer:** Steep tiled roof → Kerala, very heavy rain; Bamboo house on stilts → Assam, yearly floods; Flat mud roof and thick walls → Ladakh, cold and very little rain; Round mud house with a light roof → Kutch, heat and earthquakes. *Why:* Each house suits the weather and dangers of its place.
6. (sort) Sort these building materials. Did they come from nature with little change, or were they made in a factory?  
   **Answer:** mud → From nature; bamboo → From nature; glass → Made in a factory; stone → From nature; cement → Made in a factory; dry grass for thatch → From nature; steel rods → Made in a factory. *Why:* Mud, bamboo, stone and grass are used almost as they are found. Glass, cement and steel are made by heating materials from nature in factories.
7. (write) Kavya, a girl in Class 3, says, "My card square folded, so the card must be weak." What would you tell her?  
   **Answer:** The card is not weak. The triangle was made of the same card and it did not fold. The square folds because its corners can turn, so its shape can change without any strip bending.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the triangle as evidence that the material is the same, and says the shape is the reason.
8. (draw) Draw a square frame. Then draw one strip that stops it leaning. Colour the two triangles it makes.  
   **Answer:** A square with one diagonal from one corner to the opposite corner, with the two triangles coloured.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts the brace from corner to corner, not across the middle of a side.
9. (mcq) Why do many houses in Assam stand on bamboo stilts?  
   Options: So floodwater can flow under the floor / So the house is cooler in winter / Because bamboo is too short for walls  
   **Answer:** So floodwater can flow under the floor. *Why:* When the river floods, water flows under the raised floor, so the family and their things stay dry.
10. (label) Label the parts of this house: roof, wall, frame, brace, door.  
   **Answer:** ['roof', 'wall', 'frame', 'brace', 'door']. *Why:* A strong answer puts the brace across the frame from corner to corner.
11. (truefalse) On a model roof, rain runs off faster when the roof is steep.

### Roofs, Tiles and Triangles (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (write) In the tile test, the tissue under roof B got wet, but the tissue under roof A stayed dry. Explain why, using the word 'overlap'.  
   **Answer:** On roof A, each higher tile overlaps the one below it, so water runs from the top of one tile onto the outside of the next and off the edge. On roof B, each lower tile overlaps the one above, so water runs into the edge of each tile, under it, and through the gap.. *Why:* A strong answer follows the water down the roof and says where it got in.
2. (perspective) A family in Chennai is building a new house.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and backs it with a fact, such as concrete's strength, a jaali wall's breeze, or the Push Test showing braces matter. It then admits a fair point from another side, because each choice has real costs and gains.. *Why:* There is no single right answer. Builders and architects really disagree about this.
3. (mcq) Mythili, a girl in Class 4, adds a strip across her square frame, from the middle of the top to the middle of the bottom. Will it stop the square leaning?  
   Options: Yes, any extra strip stops leaning / No, it makes two smaller squares, which can still lean / Only if the strip is thicker  
   **Answer:** No, it makes two smaller squares, which can still lean. *Why:* A strip from the middle of the top to the middle of the bottom makes two smaller rectangles, not triangles. Rectangles still lean. The brace must go from corner to corner.
4. (draw) Draw a side view of a roof with four tiles overlapping the right way. Add arrows to show where rain runs.  
   **Answer:** Four tiles in a slope, each higher tile lying over the top of the one below, with arrows running down over each tile and off the bottom edge.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the higher tile on top every time, and the arrows never go under a tile.
5. (mcq) In the Kutch earthquake of 2001, many bhungas with light roofs stood. Why is a light roof safer in an earthquake?  
   Options: A light roof is cheaper / If a light roof falls, it is far less likely to hurt people, and shaking pushes light things less / A light roof keeps the house cool  
   **Answer:** If a light roof falls, it is far less likely to hurt people, and shaking pushes light things less. *Why:* The shaking ground pushes heavy things harder, and a falling heavy roof is the main danger. A light roof both moves less and hurts less if it falls.
6. (write) Houses in Ladakh have flat roofs, but houses in Kerala have steep roofs. Explain why, using what you saw in the Rain on the Roof test.  
   **Answer:** In the test, water stayed in a puddle on the flat tray but ran off the steep tray in seconds. Kerala gets very heavy rain, so it needs steep roofs to shed water. Ladakh gets very little rain, so a flat roof is fine and can be used for drying food.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the test results, then links each roof to each place's rain.
7. (sort) Sort these. Will each one keep its shape when pushed, or lean?  
   **Answer:** a gate with a diagonal plank → Keeps its shape; a gate with no diagonal plank → Leans; a ladder leaning on a wall, with the floor → Keeps its shape; a square of four sticks → Leans; a triangle of three sticks → Keeps its shape; a tall rectangle shelf with no back → Leans. *Why:* Anything split completely into triangles keeps its shape. A shape with four or more sides and no braces can lean.
8. (truefalse) In an earthquake, the ground pushes a building mostly sideways, not only down.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Shaking moves the ground from side to side, so walls and frames are pushed sideways, like your push on the card square.
9. (label) Label the parts of a baya weaver's nest: branch, nest room, entrance tube, water below.  
   **Answer:** ['branch', 'nest room', 'entrance tube', 'water below']. *Why:* A strong answer shows the entrance tube at the bottom, which makes it hard for snakes to get in.
10. (mcq) Laurie Baker's rat-trap bond lays bricks on their edges, with a gap inside the wall. What does the gap do?  
   Options: Saves bricks and keeps the room cooler / Lets rats live in the wall / Makes the wall heavier  
   **Answer:** Saves bricks and keeps the room cooler. *Why:* The air gap means fewer bricks are needed, and still air slows heat coming through the wall. The name comes from the shape of the brick pattern, not from rats.
11. (fill) A frame that keeps its shape when pushed is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['rigid']. *Why:* Rigid means it does not lean or fold. A triangle is rigid.
12. (write) Meera, a girl in Class 3, says the pandal in the story leaned because Arun's knots were loose. Is she right? What really happened?  
   **Answer:** No. Not one knot came undone and no pole broke. The pandal leaned because its frame was made of squares, which can lean when their corners turn. The cross poles made triangles and stopped it.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the story's evidence and names the missing triangles.
13. (order) Put these roofs in order, from the one where 4 spoons of water stopped dripping first to the one where it stayed longest.  
   **Answer:** ['a tray on four books', 'a tray on one book', 'a flat tray']. *Why:* The steeper the roof, the faster the water ran off. On the flat tray it stayed in a puddle.

15. (mcq) Thilaga, a girl in Class 3, wants a light frame for a balcony tent. Which one will stay up best in the wind?  
   Options: Four sticks in a square / Sticks in triangles, with a brace on each side / One tall stick in the middle  
   **Answer:** Sticks in triangles, with a brace on each side. *Why:* Triangles and braces stop the frame leaning in a sideways push from the wind. A square can fold, and one stick can tip over.

It then admits a fair point: how well a house is built matters more than the material.. *Why:* Both views are held by real experts. A strong answer shows it understands the trade-off.
3. (mcq) Fazlur Rahman Khan put huge diagonal braces on the outside of the John Hancock Center in Chicago. What did they do?

   **Answer:** They made giant triangles, so the tower did not lean in the wind, using less steel. *Why:* The diagonals made giant triangles across the outside walls. Triangles cannot lean, so the whole building stayed stiff, and it used about as much steel for each floor as a 50-storey building.

   **Answer:** Any shelter whose frame is split into triangles, with a roof sloping to shed rain, and an opening for the bird.. *Why:* A strong design uses triangles or braces in every side and a sloping roof, and thinks about what the bird needs.
5. (write) A Toda hut in the Nilgiris has a very small door. The Nilgiris are cool and misty. What problem do you think the small door solves for the family?  
   **Answer:** A small door lets less cold air in and less warm air out, so the hut stays warmer. It is also easy to close.. *Why:* A strong answer links the door to keeping warmth in, in a cool place. It is fine to say this is a guess.
6. (mcq) A frame with six sides leans when pushed. What is the smallest number of strips you need to add so it cannot lean?  
   Options: 1 / 3 / 6  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Three strips from one corner to three far corners split a six-sided shape into four triangles. With fewer, a four-sided part is left, and it can lean.
7. (truefalse) In a city, building tall blocks of flats lets many families live on a small piece of land.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Building upwards puts many homes on the same ground, which matters where land is scarce and costly.

   **Answer:** It shows strength does not come only from the material. The shape and the way the parts are joined matter too. The same card was floppy as a square and firm as a triangle.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the Push Test as evidence and separates the material from the shape.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does a shelter protect us from?  
   Options: Only from rain / Rain, sun, heat, cold, wind and danger / Only from animals  
   **Answer:** Rain, sun, heat, cold, wind and danger. *Why:* A shelter protects from all of these. Choosing only one leaves out the others.
2. (mcq) You push a square frame of card strips sideways. What happens?  
   Options: It stays square / It leans and folds flat / It becomes a triangle  
   **Answer:** It leans and folds flat. *Why:* The square's corners turn like hinges, so it leans. Choosing 'stays square' usually means thinking more strips make it stronger.
3. (mcq) Why can a triangle frame not lean?  
   Options: It is made of stronger card / To lean, one side would have to change length / It is smaller than a square  
   **Answer:** To lean, one side would have to change length. *Why:* Three fixed lengths make only one shape. The card was the same in both frames, so the material is not the reason.
4. (mcq) Ananya, a girl in Class 3, has a wobbly square frame. Where should she add a strip to stop it leaning?  
   Options: From one corner to the opposite corner / Along the bottom / Across the middle, side to side  
   **Answer:** From one corner to the opposite corner. *Why:* A strip from corner to corner, a brace, makes two triangles. Along the bottom or across the middle leaves shapes that can still lean.
5. (mcq) Which of these is built from many triangles?  
   Options: A mobile phone tower / A brick wall / A glass window  
   **Answer:** A mobile phone tower. *Why:* A mobile tower is a truss of steel triangles. A brick wall is a block, and a window is one sheet.
6. (mcq) Why do many houses in Ladakh have flat mud roofs?  
   Options: Ladakh gets very little rain / Ladakh has no wood at all / Flat roofs keep out snakes  
   **Answer:** Ladakh gets very little rain. *Why:* With very little rain, water rarely stands on the roof, and the flat roof is useful for drying food.
7. (mcq) Families in parts of Assam build houses on bamboo stilts. Why?  
   Options: To see further / So floodwater can flow under the floor / To catch more wind  
   **Answer:** So floodwater can flow under the floor. *Why:* Rivers in Assam flood every year. A raised floor keeps the family and their things dry.
8. (mcq) Water was poured on a flat tray and on a tray tilted on four books. What happened?  
   Options: Both dried at the same time / The water ran off the tilted tray fast and stayed on the flat one / The water stayed on the tilted tray  
   **Answer:** The water ran off the tilted tray fast and stayed on the flat one. *Why:* Water flows downhill. The steeper the slope, the faster it runs off.
9. (mcq) How must roof tiles overlap to keep rain out?  
   Options: Each higher tile lies on top of the tile below / Each lower tile lies on top of the tile above / They should not overlap at all  
   **Answer:** Each higher tile lies on top of the tile below. *Why:* Then water runs from one tile onto the outside of the next and never slips under an edge. In the tile test, the other way let water through.
10. (mcq) Why does a builder working high up wear a harness?  
   Options: To look smart / So that a slip does not become a fall / To carry bricks  
   **Answer:** So that a slip does not become a fall. *Why:* Falls are one of the biggest dangers on a building site. A harness tied to something strong catches the worker.
11. (mcq) Which is a good safety rule at home?  
   Options: Climb on the balcony wall to see better / Leave a wet floor to dry by itself / Never climb on balcony walls or grills  
   **Answer:** Never climb on balcony walls or grills. *Why:* Falls from balconies are a real danger. Wet floors should be wiped at once.
12. (mcq) In an earthquake, why is a heavy roof more dangerous than a light one?  
   Options: Heavy roofs are pushed harder by the shaking and hurt more if they fall / Heavy roofs let rain in / Heavy roofs are hotter  
   **Answer:** Heavy roofs are pushed harder by the shaking and hurt more if they fall. *Why:* The shaking pushes heavy things harder, and a falling heavy roof is the main danger. In Kutch in 2001, many bhungas with light roofs stood.

## Applications
- Roofs of houses, sheds, railway stations and factories are held up by trusses made of triangles.
- Mobile phone towers, electricity pylons and cranes are tall frames of steel triangles.
- Steel railway and road bridges, such as the Howrah Bridge in Kolkata, use triangles along their sides.
- Gates, ladders, folding stools and shelf brackets use braces and triangles so they do not wobble.
- Tall buildings use diagonal braces or a very stiff core to stand up to wind and earthquakes.
- Wedding and festival pandals are tied with cross poles so the wind cannot push them over.
- Sloping roofs and overlapping tiles keep homes dry in the monsoon.
- Houses on stilts keep families dry in flood areas.

## Open questions
- **How exactly do baya weavers learn to weave such good nests?** Young males build better nests with practice, and females choose among nests. How much is learned and how much they are born knowing is still being studied.
- **What is the best way to build homes that are cheap, cool and safe in earthquakes, for millions of families?** Engineers, architects and builders still argue about it, because the answer depends on the place, the money, the materials and how carefully the house is built.
- **Can we predict exactly when and where an earthquake will happen?** Scientists can say which areas are at risk, but nobody can yet say the day or hour of an earthquake.
- **Could houses one day be grown, from fungi or plants, instead of built?** Scientists are testing building blocks made from fungi and plant fibres. Nobody knows yet whether they will last as long as brick.

## Connected maths
- In the Push Test, the top corner of the square moved 12 cm. The top corner of the triangle moved half a centimetre. How many centimetres further did the square's corner move? **11 and a half centimetres further.**. 12 cm take away half a centimetre is 11 and a half centimetres. The square leaned more than 20 times as far as the triangle. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Divya, a girl in Class 3, wants to build 4 card triangles. Each triangle needs 3 strips. How many strips does she need? **12 strips.**. 4 triangles with 3 strips each is 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12. Or 4 times 3 is 12. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- A square frame needs 4 strips. A square with a brace needs 5 strips. A triangle needs 3. How many strips do you need to make one of each? **12 strips.**. 4 + 5 + 3 = 12. You can add the 5 and the 3 first to make 8, then add 4 to make 12. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In the Rain on the Roof test, water stopped dripping after 4 seconds on four books, 20 seconds on one book, and still stood in a puddle after 60 seconds on the flat tray. How many times as long did the water take on one book as on four books? **5 times as long.**. 20 seconds divided by 4 seconds is 5. So the gentler slope took 5 times as long. Check: 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 20. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- An ordinary wall needs about 100 bricks. A rat-trap bond wall of the same size needs about a quarter fewer. About how many bricks does the rat-trap wall need? **About 75 bricks.**. A quarter of 100 is 25, because 100 shared into 4 equal parts is 25 each. 100 take away 25 is 75. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- A pentagon frame has 5 sides. Two braces from one corner split it into triangles. How many triangles are there? Draw it to check. **3 triangles.**. Two strips from one corner reach the two far corners. They cut the five-sided shape into 3 triangles. A shape with 6 sides needs 3 braces and makes 4 triangles. The number of triangles is always 2 less than the number of sides. (queues `math.shapes-2d.recognising-shapes.L3`)
