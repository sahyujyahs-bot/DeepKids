# Is my food pure? Adulteration, simple tests, and what a test cannot tell
`sci.food.is-my-food-pure.L5`

**Summary.** A spoon of turmeric stirred into water leaves one glass almost clear and turns another bright yellow at once. What does the difference say about what was in the packet? In this lesson you learn how food is secretly changed to make money, and how that can cheat or poison a family. You try tests with water, wet paper and a slanting plate on samples a grown-up has secretly changed. You find out what such tests cannot show, how laboratories and the law go further, and why 'pure' is harder to define than it sounds.

**Intro.** *Pour a little milk into a steel tumbler and look at it. Smell it. If someone had added a quarter of a tumbler of water to the packet this morning, could you tell?* Most of what matters about food is hidden from our eyes, nose and tongue. That is what makes cheating possible. For as long as food has been sold, some sellers have mixed in cheaper things, and buyers and rulers have looked for ways to catch them. Today you become a tester. You will learn the tricks, the tests, the limits of the tests, and the honest answer to how worried a family needs to be.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what food adulteration is, and give examples of adding, removing and swapping.
1. I can explain why adulteration happens and why a buyer usually cannot see it.
2. I can tell apart adulteration that cheats and adulteration that poisons, with an example of each.
3. I can carry out simple tests on turmeric, chilli powder, tea, sugar and milk, and say what difference each test uses.
4. I can say what a home test cannot show, and why passing a test is not proof that food is pure.
5. I can explain why 'pure' has more than one meaning, and why some additions to food are not adulteration.
6. I can say what families and the law in India can do about adulteration.

## Terms
- **pure**: Made of one substance only, or in food law, meeting a written standard with nothing hidden. *e.g.* A packet says 'pure milk'. A chemist says milk is a mixture, and the law says it is pure if it meets the standard.
- **adulteration**: Secretly adding, removing or swapping something in food so that the seller gains and the buyer is cheated or harmed. *e.g.* Brick powder is mixed into chilli powder and sold as pure chilli powder.
- **adulterant**: The cheaper or harmful thing that is secretly put into food. *e.g.* In sugar mixed with chalk powder, the chalk is the adulterant.
- **adding**: Putting a cheaper or harmful thing into a food without saying so. *e.g.* A seller stirs water into milk before filling the packets.
- **removing**: Taking out a valuable part of a food and still selling it as the whole food. *e.g.* Cream is skimmed off milk, and the milk is still sold as full-cream.
- **swapping**: Selling one food in place of a costlier one. *e.g.* Cheap long-grain rice sold as basmati rice.
- **profit**: The money a seller has left after paying for everything. *e.g.* Water costs almost nothing, so a seller who sells it as milk keeps nearly all the money as profit.
- **by weight**: Sold according to how heavy it is, so heavier means more money. *e.g.* Chilli powder is sold by weight, so a heavy dust mixed in adds to the weight and costs the seller little.
- **label**: The writing on a packet that says what the food is, who made it and what it holds. *e.g.* The label on a salt packet says iodised salt and shows the maker's address.
- **supply chain**: The line of people through whom food passes from the farm to the kitchen. *e.g.* Turmeric goes from a farmer to a trader, a grinder, a packer, a shop and then to your home.
- **trader**: A person who buys food from farmers and sells it on. *e.g.* A trader in Erode buys turmeric roots from many farms and sells them to mills.
- **wholesaler**: A seller who buys in very large amounts and sells to shops. *e.g.* A wholesaler buys a lorry of rice and sells a sack at a time to the shops.
- **nourishment**: The things in food that the body uses to grow, mend and work. *e.g.* Milk gives protein, fat, calcium and other nourishment to a growing child.
- **poison**: A substance that harms the body, even in small amounts. *e.g.* Lead is a poison that harms a child's growing brain.
- **dye**: A colouring that soaks into cloth, paper or food and stains it. *e.g.* A cloth dye that stains your fingers yellow should never be in a sweet.
- **pigment**: A coloured powder that does not dissolve in water and is used in paints. *e.g.* Lead chromate is a bright yellow pigment made for paint, not for food.
- **lead**: A heavy grey metal that is a poison. The body mistakes it for calcium and stores it in the bones. *e.g.* Lead was once added to paints and petrol, and it can reach food through paint pigments.
- **lead chromate**: A yellow paint pigment that contains lead, and that has been added to turmeric to make it look bright. *e.g.* Researchers in Bangladesh traced high lead in village women's blood to lead chromate in turmeric.
- **argemone**: A thorny wild plant with yellow flowers whose seeds look like mustard seeds and give a poisonous oil. *e.g.* In 1998 argemone seeds pressed with mustard made oil that made thousands of people in Delhi ill.
- **epidemic dropsy**: An illness with swollen legs and breathlessness, caused by eating oil that holds argemone oil. *e.g.* Many families in Delhi who cooked with one kind of loose mustard oil became ill at the same time.
- **additive**: A substance added to food openly, within a legal limit and for a stated reason. *e.g.* A permitted yellow colour named on a sweet packet is an additive.
- **fortification**: Adding a nutrient to food openly so that more people get enough of it. *e.g.* Iodine is added to salt, and vitamins A and D to some oil and milk.
- **iodised salt**: Salt that has iodine added to it. All salt sold for eating in India has had to be iodised since 2006. *e.g.* The salt packet on the shelf says iodised salt, and iodine helps the thyroid gland.
- **permitted colour**: A food colour that the law allows in certain foods, up to a set limit. *e.g.* India allows eight artificial colours in certain foods, and each must be named on the label.
- **preservative**: A substance that slows the spoiling of food. *e.g.* Salt in pickle is a preservative that stops the mango from spoiling.
- **mixture**: Two or more substances together, which can often be separated again. *e.g.* Milk is a mixture of water, fat, protein, milk sugar and calcium.
- **mineral**: A simple substance from rocks and soil, such as calcium, iron or salt, that the body needs in small amounts. *e.g.* The calcium in milk is a mineral, and bones are built from it.
- **standard**: A written rule that says what a food must be, such as the least fat that milk must hold. *e.g.* The standard for toned milk says how much fat it must have.
- **toned milk**: Milk that has had some fat taken out and sold openly under that name. *e.g.* A packet that says toned milk is not adulterated, because it says what was done.
- **property**: A way in which a thing behaves or looks that can be noticed or measured. *e.g.* Sinking in water is a property of a stone, and floating is a property of a dry leaf.
- **dissolve**: To mix into a liquid so completely that the pieces can no longer be seen. *e.g.* Sugar dissolves in water, and the water stays clear.
- **settle**: To sink slowly to the bottom of a liquid and stay there. *e.g.* Chalk powder in water makes the water cloudy and then settles as a white layer.
- **float**: To stay on top of a liquid instead of sinking. *e.g.* A dry tea leaf floats on water for a while.
- **magnet**: A piece of iron or steel, or a special stone, that attracts iron. *e.g.* A magnet drawn through tea leaves picks up any iron filings and leaves the tea.
- **iodine**: A dark brown liquid from a medical shop that turns starch blue-black. It stains skin and must never be swallowed. *e.g.* A grown-up puts a drop of iodine on a slice of potato, and the potato turns blue-black.
- **starch**: The store of energy in many plants, in grains, potatoes and rice, which turns blue-black with iodine. *e.g.* A drop of iodine turns a slice of boiled potato blue-black because the potato is full of starch.
- **curcumin**: The yellow substance in turmeric that gives it its colour and does not dissolve well in water. *e.g.* Turmeric powder in still water gives only a pale yellow haze, because curcumin hardly dissolves.
- **detergent**: A cleaning liquid or powder that makes thick bubbles in water. *e.g.* A drop of washing-up liquid in a bottle of water makes a mountain of foam when it is shaken.
- **lather**: A thick, lasting foam made of many small bubbles. *e.g.* Shaking soap and water in a bottle makes a lather that stays for minutes.
- **lactometer**: A weighted glass float that sinks deeper in thin milk and floats higher in thick milk. *e.g.* A dairy dips a lactometer into each can to check the milk quickly.
- **chromatography**: A way of separating coloured mixtures by letting water carry them up paper, so that each colour travels a different distance. *e.g.* A black felt-pen dot on wet paper spreads into bands of different colours.
- **band**: One coloured stripe on a chromatography paper, made by one substance. *e.g.* A green dot on wet paper may split into a yellow band and a blue band.
- **detect**: To find that something is there. *e.g.* A magnet detects iron filings in tea.
- **limit of a test**: The smallest amount of an adulterant that a test can notice, and everything it cannot show at all. *e.g.* A drop of milk on a plate cannot show a spoon of water in a whole tumbler.
- **nectar**: The sweet liquid that flowers make. Bees collect it and turn it into honey. *e.g.* A bee visiting a mustard flower sucks up the nectar from the flower.
- **syrup**: A thick sweet liquid made by dissolving a lot of sugar in a little water. *e.g.* A syrup of sugar and water can be as thick as honey.
- **NMR**: A laboratory test that puts a sample inside a very strong magnet and reads how it answers. Each mixture gives its own pattern, like a fingerprint. *e.g.* In 2020 a German laboratory used NMR to test honey from 13 Indian brands.
- **stand-in measurement**: An easy thing that we measure because it usually goes along with the thing we truly care about. *e.g.* Thickness stands in for quality in honey, and nitrogen stands in for protein in milk.
- **protein**: The nourishment in food that the body uses to build muscle and to mend itself. *e.g.* Milk, dal, eggs and paneer hold protein.
- **nitrogen**: A gas in the air, and also an element that every protein holds. *e.g.* A laboratory measures the nitrogen in milk to estimate how much protein it holds.
- **melamine**: A white chemical used to make plastics. It holds a lot of nitrogen and forms stones in the kidneys. *e.g.* In 2008 melamine in milk made thousands of babies in China ill.
- **density**: How much a thing weighs for its size. *e.g.* A steel spoon has a high density, and the same size of foam has a low one.
- **microscope**: An instrument with lenses that makes tiny things look much larger. *e.g.* Under a microscope, starch grains from potato and rice have different shapes.
- **DNA**: The molecule inside every cell that carries a living being's inherited instructions. *e.g.* A laboratory reads the DNA of a rice grain to tell basmati from a look-alike.
- **survey**: A study that tests many samples of the same food to find out how common a fault is. *e.g.* A national survey tested thousands of milk samples from towns across India.
- **sample**: A small amount taken from a larger amount so that it can be tested. *e.g.* A teaspoon of turmeric from a packet is a sample of that packet.
- **contaminant**: A harmful thing that gets into food by accident, from soil, water, air or storage. *e.g.* Aflatoxin, a poison made by a mould, can get into milk when the cow eats mouldy feed.
- **ethylene**: A gas that fruits make themselves, which tells them to ripen. *e.g.* A banana in a paper bag ripens faster because her own ethylene stays near her.
- **ripening**: The changes that make a fruit soft, sweet and coloured. *e.g.* A green mango turns yellow, soft and sweet as she ripens.
- **calcium carbide**: Grey stones that give off a gas when they get wet. Using them to ripen fruit is banned in India. *e.g.* A trader hid calcium carbide in a box of green mangoes, and the law forbids it.
- **kesari dal**: A pulse that looks like toor dal, from the grass pea plant. Eaten as the main food for months, it can cause lathyrism. *e.g.* Kesari dal has long been mixed into toor dal and besan because it costs less.
- **lathyrism**: An illness that makes the legs weak or stiff for life, caused by eating mostly one pulse for months. *e.g.* In famine years, many people who ate little but grass pea dal became unable to walk properly.
- **drought**: A long time with too little rain. *e.g.* In a drought most crops die, but the grass pea plant keeps growing.
- **licence number**: The 14-digit number that an Indian food business must print on its packets. *e.g.* The 14-digit number on a biscuit packet shows that the maker is registered.
- **sub-standard**: Food that falls short of the written standard but is not necessarily harmful. *e.g.* Milk with less fat than the standard asks for is sub-standard.
- **misbranded**: Food whose label says something that is not true. *e.g.* A packet that says cow ghee but holds another fat is misbranded.
- **unsafe**: Food that can harm health. *e.g.* Turmeric with lead chromate is unsafe.
- **batch number**: A number on a packet that tells which lot it was made in, so that a bad lot can be traced. *e.g.* If a batch of biscuits is found bad, shops can pull back only the packets with that batch number.
- **complaint**: A report to the food authority that a food looks or tests wrong. *e.g.* A family keeps the packet and the bill and sends a complaint through the Food Safety Connect app.

## Explanations
### Adulteration is secretly making food worse in order to make money · *defined*
**Definition:** Food adulteration is secretly changing a food so that it is worth less or does harm. The seller adds something cheap or harmful, takes out a valuable part, or swaps in a cheaper food. The seller gains and the buyer does not know.

There are three ways to adulterate a food. The first is to add something cheaper, such as water to milk. The second is to take something valuable out, such as the cream from milk, and sell what is left as whole milk. The third is to swap, such as selling dried papaya seeds as black pepper, because the two look alike. In each case two things are true. The food is worth less than the buyer paid for. And the buyer is not told. The secrecy is the heart of it. A shop that sells milk mixed with water and says so on the label is selling a different product. A shop that does the same and says nothing is adulterating.
- e.g. Adding: chalk powder in sugar, brick powder in chilli powder, water in milk.
- e.g. Removing: tea leaves that have already been used once, dried, coloured and sold again.
- e.g. Swapping: a cheaper oil sold as coconut oil or as mustard oil.
### Why it is done: food is sold by weight and by looks · *defined*
**Definition:** Adulteration pays when a cheap substance can add weight, volume or an attractive look to a food that sells for more.

Follow the money with a sum. A seller has 100 litres of milk and sells it at 60 rupees a litre. That brings 6,000 rupees. If the seller adds 25 litres of water, there are 125 litres to sell, which brings 7,500 rupees. The water cost almost nothing, so the extra 1,500 rupees is profit. The same sum works for anything sold by weight. Looks pay too. Buyers pick the reddest chilli powder and the yellowest turmeric, so a little dye makes poor spices sell as good ones. The more costly the food, the more a cheat gains. That is why saffron, honey, ghee, spices and oils have been adulterated more than rice or potatoes.
- e.g. Saffron is among the costliest foods by weight, and dyed threads of other plants have been sold as saffron for centuries.
- e.g. A kilogram of chilli powder with 100 grams of brick powder in it has only 900 grams of chilli.
### The buyer cannot see inside the packet, and the chain from farm to kitchen is long · *defined*
**Definition:** A seller knows more about a food than a buyer does. When buyers cannot tell good from bad, cheating is hard to punish and honest sellers are undercut.

You can judge a brinjal by looking. You cannot judge a spoon of ghee, a packet of turmeric or a bottle of honey that way. The seller, or someone further back, knows what went in. You do not. In 1970 the American economist George Akerlof showed what follows from this. If buyers cannot tell good from bad, they will not pay extra for good. Then the honest seller, whose costs are higher, loses customers to the cheat. Food also passes through many hands: farmer, trader, grinder, packer, wholesaler, shop. Each hand is a place where something can be added, and each can blame the hand before.
- e.g. A pale, honest turmeric powder sits unsold beside a bright one, and the shopkeeper learns which to stock.
- e.g. A milk seller who buys from twenty farms cannot easily know which can was watered.
- **George Akerlof, in his paper 'The Market for Lemons' in 1970:** The trouble starts with hidden knowledge, not with bad people. Where quality cannot be seen, even a market full of mostly honest sellers drifts towards the worst goods. Labels, brands, testing and guarantees are all ways to let the buyer see.
- **Small traders and vendors:** They are the ones the inspector can reach, so they are the ones fined. Often they sold exactly what the wholesaler gave them. They ask that testing be done further up the chain, where the mixing is done in bulk.
- **Public-interest groups, such as the Centre for Science and Environment in Delhi:** Buyers have power when they act together. A food sells only if someone buys it. When test results are made public, informed buyers can change what shops stock, often faster than fines do.
### Two kinds of harm: some adulterants cheat you, and some poison you · *defined*
**Definition:** An adulterant may be harmless in itself and still rob the eater of money and nourishment. Another may be a poison. The law treats both as offences, and the second as far more serious.

Water added to milk is not a poison, if the water is clean. But a child who drinks that milk gets less protein, less fat and less calcium from each tumbler than the family paid for. Over months, that is a real loss. This is harm by cheating. Other adulterants harm by what they are. A dye made for cloth or paint may damage the body. A seed that looks like mustard may carry a poison. So for any adulterant, ask two questions. What is the eater losing? And what is the eater getting that should not be in food at all? The worst cases answer both.
- e.g. Cheats: water in milk, starch in paneer, used tea leaves.
- e.g. Poisons: lead chromate, a paint pigment, in turmeric. Metanil yellow, a cloth dye, in sweets and dal. Argemone oil in mustard oil.
- e.g. Both at once: milk that is watered, and then thickened and whitened again with detergent or urea.
### A slow poison: lead in turmeric, stored in a child's bones · *defined*
**Definition:** Lead chromate is a bright yellow paint pigment that has been added to turmeric to improve its colour. Lead builds up in the body and harms the growing brain.

Turmeric roots that are old or poorly dried give a dull powder. Some processors in South Asia have polished roots with lead chromate, a yellow pigment made for paint, to make them glow. Lead is a metal that the body mistakes for calcium. So the body stores lead in bones, where it stays for years, and leaks back into the blood. In a growing child, lead harms the brain and lowers the ability to learn. The World Health Organization says that no level of lead in the blood is known to be safe. In 2019 Jenna Forsyth and colleagues in Bangladesh traced high lead in the blood of village women to their turmeric. They worked with the health research centre icddr,b. Some turmeric samples held lead far above the legal limit.
- e.g. After the findings, Bangladesh's food authority warned traders and fined sellers. It checked markets with a hand-held scanner. A study in 2023 found that lead in market turmeric had fallen sharply.
- e.g. A study published in 2024 found some turmeric samples from Patna, Karachi and Peshawar far above the limit. India's limit for lead in turmeric is 10 parts in a million by weight.
### A fast poison: argemone oil in mustard oil, Delhi, 1998 · *defined*
**Definition:** Epidemic dropsy is an illness caused by eating oil that contains oil from the seeds of the Mexican prickly poppy, Argemone mexicana.

The Mexican prickly poppy is a thorny plant with yellow flowers who grows wild on roadsides across India. Their seeds are small, round and dark, and look very like mustard seeds. Their oil contains a poison called sanguinarine. When the seeds are pressed together with mustard, by accident or on purpose, the oil looks and smells normal. People who cook with it for some days get swollen legs, breathlessness and heart trouble. The illness is called epidemic dropsy. In August 1998 it struck Delhi. About 3,000 people fell ill and about 60 died. The city banned the sale of loose mustard oil for a time. The illness had been described in Kolkata as early as 1877, and Indian doctors in the 1920s and 1930s traced it to argemone.
- e.g. Food officers can tell the two seeds apart with a hand lens. A mustard seed is smooth. An argemone seed is rough and pitted, with a tiny point at one end. Crushed, mustard is yellow inside and argemone is white. A family should not collect or crush wild seeds to try this. The plant is poisonous, and a laboratory is the safe way to be sure.
- e.g. Outbreaks have also been recorded in Gwalior, Lucknow and Kolkata.
### Not every addition is adulteration: additives and fortified foods are declared on the label · *debated*
**Definition:** A food additive is a substance added openly, within legal limits, for a stated purpose. Fortification is the open adding of a nutrient. Both appear on the label. An adulterant is hidden.

If adding things to food were always wrong, salt would be in trouble. Since 2006, all salt sold for eating in India must have iodine added, because iodine protects the thyroid gland and the growing brain. Adding a nutrient in this way is called fortification. Fortified packets carry a blue '+F' mark. Many foods also contain permitted additives. India allows eight artificial colours in certain foods, each within a set limit. A preservative slows spoiling. What separates these from adulteration? Three things. The substance has been tested and is allowed. The amount is within the limit. And the label says so. A permitted colour used secretly to hide stale food breaks the third rule and becomes adulteration.
- e.g. Allowed and declared: iodine in salt, vitamin A and D in fortified oil and milk, a permitted yellow colour named on a sweet packet.
- e.g. Never allowed in food: rhodamine B, a pink dye for cloth. In 2024 Tamil Nadu and Karnataka banned cotton candy coloured with rhodamine B after tests found the dye in samples.
- **Food regulators such as the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India:** An additive is acceptable when tests show it is safe at the amount used, when it serves a purpose, and when it is declared. The dose decides the safety.
- **Some doctors, parents and consumer groups:** Artificial colours add nothing to nourishment. They make food look better than it is, most of all food aimed at children. They ask why any risk, however small, should be accepted for looks alone.
- **Critics of fortification, such as India's Right to Food Campaign:** Adding a nutrient to everyone's salt, rice or oil treats everyone alike, though needs differ. They prefer that families be able to afford varied food. Supporters answer that fortified salt has sharply reduced goitre and has cost very little.
### 'Pure' has no single meaning: even pure milk is a mixture · *debated*
**Definition:** In chemistry, pure means one substance only. In food law, pure means meeting a written standard with nothing hidden. In everyday talk it often means natural or untouched. These meanings do not agree.

A packet says 'pure milk'. Ask a chemist, and she will say that milk is not pure at all. In every 100 grams of cow's milk, about 87 grams are water. The rest is fat, protein, milk sugar and minerals such as calcium, many hundreds of substances in all. Turmeric, honey and ghee are mixtures too. So the chemist's meaning, one substance only, fits almost no food. The law uses a different meaning. Milk is pure if it meets a standard, such as a minimum amount of fat, and has nothing added. Many shoppers mean a third thing: natural, as it came from the cow, the bee or the plant. Yet the cow's own milk changes with her feed and the season, and some natural things are poisons.
- e.g. Buffalo milk has about twice the fat of cow's milk. Both are 'pure milk', and they are different mixtures.
- e.g. The argemone seed is completely natural.
- e.g. 'Toned' milk has had some fat removed and is sold openly under that name, so it is not adulterated.
- **Chemists:** Pure means one kind of substance. By that meaning, distilled water and refined white sugar are among the purest things in a kitchen, and nobody thinks they are the best foods.
- **Food law in India and most countries:** Pure is a promise, not a property. A food is pure if it is what the label says, meets the written standard for that food, and hides nothing.
- **Chandogya Upanishad, chapter 7, section 26, verse 2:** 'When the food is pure, the being becomes pure. When the being is pure, memory becomes firm.' The commentator Shankara lived in about the 8th century CE. He read 'food' here as everything taken in through all the senses, not only what is eaten. Others read it as the food on the plate.
- **The Buddha, in the Amagandha Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.2:** What makes a person impure is cruelty, lying and cheating, not the kind of food that is eaten. On this view purity lies in conduct and not in substances.
### How any simple test works: find one way the food and the adulterant behave differently · *defined*
**Definition:** A test uses one property in which the adulterant differs from the food. A property is a way a thing behaves. Sinking, floating, dissolving, being pulled by a magnet and changing colour with a chemical are all properties.

An adulterant is chosen because it looks like the food. So looking will not find it. A test works by finding some other way in which the two differ. Does one dissolve in water and the other not? Sugar dissolves and chalk does not. Does one sink and the other float? Does a magnet attract one? Iron filings jump to a magnet and tea leaves do not. Does one change colour with a certain chemical? Iodine turns starch blue-black and leaves pure milk alone. Every test in this lesson, and every test in a laboratory, is built on one such difference. Before you run any test, say aloud which difference it uses. Then you will know what it can find, and what it cannot.
- e.g. Sugar and chalk: the difference is dissolving in water.
- e.g. Tea leaves and iron filings: the difference is attraction to a magnet.
- e.g. Milk and added starch: the difference is the colour with iodine.
### Tests with a glass of water: turmeric, chilli powder, sugar and tea · *defined*
**Definition:** Many powdered adulterants can be shown up in plain water, because they dissolve, settle or release colour differently from the food.

The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India publishes a booklet of home tests called DART, which stands for Detect Adulteration with Rapid Test. Several need only a clear glass of water. Turmeric: add a teaspoon of powder and do not stir. Turmeric's own yellow substance, curcumin, hardly dissolves in water, so natural powder sinks and leaves the water pale. An added dye dissolves and colours the water strongly at once. Chilli powder: sprinkle some on the water. Artificial colour runs down in streaks. Brick powder sinks fast and feels gritty at the bottom. Sugar: stir a spoonful until it dissolves. Chalk powder stays as a white layer. Tea: spread a pinch of leaves on wet white paper. Added colour stains the paper within a minute.
- e.g. Black pepper in water: the booklet says pepper sinks and papaya seeds float.
- e.g. Coffee powder in water: coffee floats for a while, and chicory, the roasted root of a plant, sinks quickly and leaves brown trails.
- e.g. Tea leaves and a magnet: iron filings cling to the magnet.
### Tests for milk: a slanting plate, a shake, and a drop of iodine · *defined*
**Definition:** Added water, detergent and starch each change one thing about milk that can be seen at home. Water changes how a drop flows, detergent changes how the milk froths, and starch changes its colour with iodine.

Milk is a costly food bought every day, so it has a long list of adulterants. Three can be looked for at home. Water: put one drop of milk on a polished plate held at a slant. Whole milk moves slowly and leaves a white trail. Watered milk runs down fast and leaves little or no trail. Detergent: shake a little milk with the same amount of water in a closed bottle. Milk gives a thin layer of froth. Milk with detergent gives a thick, lasting lather. Starch: a grown-up boils a few spoons of milk, cools it, and adds two or three drops of tincture of iodine from a medical shop. A blue-black colour means starch was added to thicken watered milk.
- e.g. The same iodine test works on paneer and khoya, which are sometimes bulked out with starch.
- e.g. Dairies measure added water with a lactometer, a weighted glass float that sinks deeper in thinner milk.
### Colours that run: separating a dye from a food on wet paper · *defined*
**Definition:** Chromatography is a way of separating mixed substances by letting a liquid carry them along paper or another material. Substances that cling less to the paper travel farther.

The tea test on wet paper is a small case of a powerful method. When water creeps through paper, it carries along anything that dissolves in it. Different dyes cling to paper by different amounts, so they travel different distances and spread into separate bands. The method is called chromatography, which means 'colour writing'. The Russian-Italian botanist Mikhail Tsvet invented it in the early 1900s to separate the green and yellow colours in leaves. Food laboratories use far finer versions today to name the exact dye in a sweet. At home it shows two things. A natural colour that will not dissolve in water stays put. And a single 'green' colouring often turns out to be a blue dye and a yellow dye mixed.
- e.g. Rub green peas or green chillies with a piece of wet white cotton. A green stain on the cotton suggests added colour.
- e.g. Put a dot of green food colouring near the bottom of a strip of filter paper or tissue and stand the strip in a little water. In some minutes the dot may separate into blue and yellow.
### What a home test cannot tell: passing a test is not proof of purity · *defined*
**Definition:** Each test detects one kind of adulterant, above a certain amount. A food that passes may still contain a different adulterant, or the same one in a smaller amount.

A test can say 'I found this.' It can never say 'There is nothing here.' There are two reasons. First, each test looks for one difference only. The magnet finds iron and is blind to dye. Second, every test has a smallest amount it can notice. A little added water will not change how a drop of milk runs. The next card shows the same limit with honey.
- e.g. A turmeric sample that leaves the water pale has passed the dye test. That test says nothing about lead chromate, which does not dissolve in water.
- e.g. A magnet pulled through a packet of turmeric finds no iron. It tells you nothing about dye, chalk or lead.
### Honey: a test for thickness lets a thick syrup pass · *debated*
**Definition:** Home tests for honey check only that it is thick, and a thick sugar syrup passes them too. In 2020 an advanced laboratory test marked ten of thirteen brands as failing, and what that proves is still argued over.

Honeybees make honey from nectar by drying it until it is too thick for microbes to live in. Home tests for honey, such as a drop holding together in water, check only that thickness. A thick sugar syrup passes just as well. In December 2020 the Centre for Science and Environment in Delhi, led by Sunita Narain, sent honey of 13 brands to a laboratory in Germany. The laboratory used an advanced test called NMR. Ten of the 13 brands failed it, though most had passed India's standard tests.
- e.g. A drop of honey that stays together in water has shown only that it is thick. A thick syrup of sugar and water also stays together.
- e.g. Adulterated honey also undercuts beekeepers, whose bees pollinate crops and wild plants.
- **The Centre for Science and Environment, in its 2020 honey report:** Syrups were being designed and sold specially to pass the official tests. So standards must keep moving to newer methods, and results must be made public.
- **Some honey companies and food scientists, in reply:** The advanced method, called NMR, was not then part of India's legal standard. It also depends on a library of known honeys, which had few Indian samples. A failure on it, they said, is a reason to look closer and not a proof of fraud.
### Cheats aim at the test: the lactometer, and the melamine milk of 2008 · *defined*
**Definition:** A test often checks one easy property as a stand-in for quality. A cheat can then add something that restores that property, while the food stays poor or becomes dangerous.

Each new test changes the cheating. Water makes milk thinner, and a lactometer detects thin milk. So cheats learned to add sugar, starch or salt, which bring the thickness back. Then came tests for sugar, starch and salt. The most terrible case came in China in 2008. Dairies there tested milk for protein by measuring nitrogen, an element that proteins contain. Some suppliers watered the milk and then added melamine, a chemical used to make plastics, which is rich in nitrogen. The test read 'plenty of protein'. Melamine forms stones in the kidneys. Some 300,000 babies fed on formula made from this milk fell ill, and six died. The test had measured something easy that usually goes along with protein, and the cheats supplied that thing alone.
- e.g. The nitrogen test was invented by the Danish chemist Johan Kjeldahl in 1883 and is still used. Laboratories now also test for melamine directly.
- e.g. In a school, marks stand in for learning. A student who copies supplies the marks without the learning. It is the same pattern.
### Density and the microscope: two old ways to tell one thing from another · *defined*
**Definition:** A laboratory can tell foods apart by density and by looking at tiny grains under a microscope. Density is how much a thing weighs for its size.

A story from Sicily, about 250 BCE, says that Archimedes checked a gold crown for hidden silver. He compared its weight with the water it pushed aside. The Roman writer Vitruvius told the story more than 200 years later, and historians doubt its details. The principle is sound, and a lactometer uses it. The microscope came next. Starch grains from potato, rice and wheat each have their own shape, so a trained eye can name a hidden flour.
- e.g. A lactometer is a weighted glass float. It sinks deeper in thin milk, because thin milk weighs less for its size.
- e.g. Under a microscope, starch grains from potato and rice have different shapes.
### Fingerprints of molecules and DNA: how modern laboratories name an adulterant · *defined*
**Definition:** Modern machines read a pattern from a sample, like a fingerprint. Laboratories also read DNA, which carries a living being's inherited instructions.

Modern machines shine light on a sample, or hold it inside a very strong magnet, and read how it answers. Each mixture of molecules answers in its own pattern, a kind of fingerprint. The honey test called NMR works this way. DNA is the molecule in every cell that carries a living being's inherited instructions. Each species has its own DNA, so a laboratory can tell one species from another, for example beef from horse.
- e.g. In January 2013 food inspectors in Ireland used DNA tests on beef burgers and found horse meat. The finding led to checks and recalls across Europe.
- e.g. Scientists at the Centre for DNA Fingerprinting and Diagnostics in Hyderabad developed a DNA test that tells true basmati rice from look-alike long-grain varieties.
- e.g. Hand-held scanners that read metals by X-rays can find lead in turmeric in under a minute, in the market itself.
### How common is adulteration? The answer depends on what is counted · *debated*
**Definition:** A survey tests many samples of a food and counts how many fail. The count depends on what counts as failing: a quality standard, an accidental contaminant, or a harmful adulterant.

Is most Indian milk adulterated? Two national surveys by the food authority seem to disagree. In 2011, a quick survey of 1,791 samples reported that about 68 in every 100 did not meet the standard. In 2018, a larger survey of 6,432 samples reported that 93 in every 100 were safe to drink. Both can be true. The 2011 number counted every sample that fell short in any way, and most fell short through added water or low fat. The 2018 number asked a narrower question: is anything harmful in it? Only 12 samples held a harmful adulterant. About 6 in every 100 held traces of a mould poison that had come through the cattle's feed, which nobody had added on purpose. About 4 in 10 were safe but below the standard for fat or other solids.
- e.g. In 2018 and 2019 a message passed round on phones. It claimed that the World Health Organization had warned that 87 out of every 100 Indians would get cancer by 2025 from adulterated milk. The World Health Organization said it had issued no such warning.
- e.g. A substance that gets into food without anyone intending it, such as a mould poison or a pesticide trace, is called a contaminant, not an adulterant.
- **The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, on its 2018 survey:** Milk in India is largely safe. The main problems are quality, meaning thin milk, and contamination from feed. Frightening claims do harm by making families give up a nourishing food.
- **Consumer groups and some dairy scientists:** A child given thin milk every day is being cheated of food, and that deserves to be counted as a serious failure. Surveys also sample on chosen days. They may miss the festival seasons, when demand is high and adulteration is said to rise.
### Ripening fruit: a banned shortcut that copies the fruit's own signal · *defined*
**Definition:** Mangoes and bananas make a gas called ethylene, and the gas tells them to ripen. Calcium carbide gives off a similar gas along with poisonous impurities. India bans it for ripening fruit.

A mango picked hard and green can still ripen, because the mango makes a gas called ethylene. Ethylene is a signal. When fruits sense it, they soften, sweeten, change colour and make more ethylene, which sets off the fruits nearby. That is why one ripe banana in a bag ripens the rest. Traders want fruit that travels hard and ripens on arrival. India allows ripening rooms that release small, measured amounts of ethylene gas, which is the fruit's own signal. The banned shortcut is calcium carbide, a grey stone used in welding. With moisture, carbide gives off acetylene, a gas that acts on fruit much as ethylene does. Industrial carbide also carries traces of arsenic and phosphorus, which are poisons, and the workers who handle it are harmed first.
- e.g. A carbide-ripened mango is often evenly yellow outside while still pale, sour and hard near the seed.
- e.g. At home, raw mangoes ripen in a few days wrapped in paper or buried in rice or hay, which holds their own ethylene close.
- e.g. In 1934 the British scientist Richard Gane showed that ripening apples themselves give off ethylene.
### Kesari dal: a banned adulterant, or a drought food wrongly blamed? · *debated*
**Definition:** Kesari dal is the split seed of the grass pea, Lathyrus sativus. Eaten as the main food for months, it can cause lathyrism, a lasting paralysis of the legs. Its sale has been banned in most of India since 1961, and scientists disagree on whether the ban should stay.

Grass peas are tough plants. They grow in drought and in flooded fields where other pulses die. Their dal looks like toor dal and costs much less, so it has long been mixed into toor dal and besan. The seeds contain a substance that harms nerves. In famines, when people ate little else for two or three months, many were left unable to walk properly. The illness is called lathyrism. In 1961 India banned the sale of kesari dal, and it became a classic adulterant. Yet the plant is still grown and eaten in parts of Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar and West Bengal, and lathyrism has become very rare.
- e.g. Under a lens, kesari dal is wedge-shaped with one slanting side, while toor dal is rounded.
- e.g. Plant breeders in India have developed grass pea varieties with much less of the harmful substance.
- **An expert panel of the Indian Council of Medical Research, in 2016:** The panel advised lifting the ban. It reasoned that harm came only when the dal was nearly the whole diet for months. With a mixed diet and the new low-toxin varieties, the risk is very small. A hardy, protein-rich crop is valuable to poor farmers in dry years.
- **Doctors and consumer groups who want the ban kept:** If sale were legal, nobody could stop cheap kesari dal being mixed secretly into costlier dals. The poorest people are the most likely to eat a lot of the cheapest dal. They are also the least able to keep their diet mixed.
- **Farmers who grow and eat it:** Their families have eaten it for generations, soaked and cooked in ways that wash out part of the harmful substance. To them the ban punished a food of the poor, while the fault lay with famine.
### Rules against adulteration are ancient, and India's present law dates from 2006 · *defined*
**Definition:** The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006, is India's food law. It created the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, which sets standards, licenses food businesses and tests food.

Wherever markets grew, rules against food fraud followed. India's present law is the Food Safety and Standards Act of 2006, which replaced the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act of 1954. It set up the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, known as FSSAI. Every food business must hold an FSSAI licence or registration, and its 14-digit number is printed on the packet. The law sorts bad food into three kinds. Sub-standard food falls short of the standard. Misbranded food has a false label. Unsafe food can injure health. Fines run up to 5 lakh rupees for sub-standard food. If unsafe food causes a death, the punishment can be prison for life.
- e.g. AGMARK, a mark under a law of 1937, certifies that a farm product such as ghee, honey or spices was graded and tested.
- e.g. A licence number on a packet tells you that the business is registered and can be traced. It does not tell you that this packet was tested.
- **Kautilya's Arthashastra, book 4, chapter 2, composed between about the 2nd century BCE and the 3rd century CE:** The chapter is about protecting people against merchants. It sets a fine of 12 panas for adulterating grains, oils, alkalis, salts, scents and medicines with similar articles of no quality. It also sets penalties for false weights and measures.
- **The market inspectors, called muhtasib, of Arab and Persian cities, as described by Ibn al-Ukhuwwa of Egypt in the early 14th century CE:** His handbook tells the inspector how to check each trade in turn, such as bakers, milk sellers and spice sellers. It lists the tricks to look for. The inspector's duty came from a religious command to promote what is right in public life.
- **Lawmakers in 19th-century Britain before 1860:** They held that the state should not interfere in trade, and that buyers must look after themselves. This view lost ground once chemists and microscopists showed adulterants that no buyer could ever detect.
### What a family can do: buy whole, look closely, test, keep the packet, and report · *defined*
**Definition:** A household lowers its risk by choosing foods that are hard to adulterate and by checking labels and samples. It reports suspected cases to the food safety authority.

No family can test everything, and none needs to. A few habits cover most of the risk. Buy spices whole where you can, and grind them at home or at a mill you can watch. A whole turmeric root or peppercorn is far harder to fake than a powder. Be a little wary of the brightest colour on the shelf. Check a packet for its FSSAI number, the maker's address, and the batch number, which lets a bad lot be traced. Now and then, run the water tests on powders bought loose. If a test shows something, keep the packet and the bill. India's food authority takes complaints through its Food Safety Connect app and through each state's food safety office. Mobile testing vans called Food Safety on Wheels visit markets and test samples that people bring.
- e.g. A housing society or a school class that tests ten samples together and shares the results has more effect than one family alone.
- e.g. The National Consumer Helpline can be reached by dialling 1915.

## Mostly agreed
- Food fraud has turned up in every country and century in which anyone tested for it. *How we know:* Pliny the Elder, in his Natural History, book 12, written in the 1st century CE, complained that pepper in Rome was adulterated with juniper berries. Frederick Accum found it across London in 1820. Inspectors in Ireland found horse meat sold as beef in 2013. Each time, new tests showed frauds that had been going on unnoticed.
- Iodine turns starch blue-black, and this colour change is a reliable sign of starch. *How we know:* The French chemists Jean-Jacques Colin and Henri-François Gaultier de Claubry reported the reaction in 1814, soon after iodine was discovered. It has been used as a test ever since. The colour appears because iodine slips inside the coiled chains of the starch molecule.
- Children take in far more of the lead they swallow than adults do. *How we know:* The World Health Organization has summarised the measurements. Young children absorb about four to five times as much of the lead in their food as adults do. Their brains are also still being built. This is why lead in a family's spices harms the youngest most.

## How we found out
**Frederick Accum, Arthur Hill Hassall, Thomas Wakley, Harvey Wiley, 1820 to 1954.** For a long time the rule of the market was 'let the buyer beware'. It seemed fair, because in a small market a buyer could look, smell and taste, and knew the seller's face. As towns grew and food came from far away, that stopped being true. In 1820 Frederick Accum, a German chemist working in London, published a book of chemical tests. It showed chalk and alum in bread, red lead in cheese and copper in pickles. Merchants were furious, and little changed. Between 1851 and 1854 the English doctor Arthur Hill Hassall examined about 2,500 samples of food under a microscope for the medical journal The Lancet. Its editor, Thomas Wakley, printed the results with the names and addresses of the shops. Hassall could see what no buyer could: ground-up roots in coffee, foreign starches in flour. Britain passed its first food adulteration law in 1860. In the United States, the chemist Harvey Wiley fed common preservatives to volunteers from 1902 to study their effects. This led to a food law in 1906. India passed the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act in 1954. Long before any of this, in India, the Arthashastra of Kautilya already fined sellers who mixed goods (see the law card).  
*What people believed before:* It is the buyer's own job to notice bad food, so no law is needed. It was believable while buyers could judge food with their senses. The microscope and chemistry showed adulterants that no buyer could ever see, smell or taste.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Branded food in sealed packets is safer than food sold loose.** (Food regulators in many countries, and most food companies; *debated*)  
  For: A sealed packet with a licence number and a batch number can be traced back to its maker, who has a name to lose. Loose food passes through open sacks and many hands. Delhi banned loose mustard oil after the 1998 poisoning for this reason.  
  Against: The honey investigation of 2020 found that most of the failing samples were from large brands. A packet hides the food from the buyer's eyes entirely. Loose whole foods bought from a seller you know can be seen, smelled and tested. Packets also cost more, which matters to poor families.
- **Many packaged foods that are legally pure are harmful because of how heavily they are processed, and should worry us more than old-style adulteration.** (Carlos Monteiro, a Brazilian nutrition scientist, who proposed the idea of 'ultra-processed' food in 2009; *debated*)  
  For: Many studies link diets high in ultra-processed foods with obesity, diabetes and heart disease. In a trial run by Kevin Hall in 2019, people offered ultra-processed meals ate about 500 kilocalories more a day than when offered unprocessed meals. That is about the energy in five bananas. Nothing in such food is hidden or illegal.  
  Against: Critics say the category is too loose. It puts packaged wholemeal bread and fizzy drinks in one group. They argue that the harm comes from too much sugar, salt and fat and too little fibre, which is already known. They add that processing also makes food safe and cheap.
- **If it is natural, it is safe. Only 'chemicals' are harmful.** (A widespread popular belief, used in much food advertising; *rejected*)  
  For: Many of the worst adulterants are factory-made: paint pigments, cloth dyes, melamine. Foods eaten for thousands of years have had a long trial.  
  Against: Everything is made of chemicals, water and turmeric included. Argemone oil and the nerve-harming substance in kesari dal are natural. So are the poisons made by moulds on badly stored groundnuts. Whether a substance harms depends on what it is and how much is eaten, not on where it came from.

## Roots
You have tested food for what was secretly added to it. Now we ask what 'pure' really points to, and what the food you eat tells you about yourself and the world.

**Deep truth.** You tested milk and found extra water. But the milk was mostly water before anyone touched it. Turmeric, honey and ghee are each made of many substances. So 'pure' never meant that a food is one thing alone. Your tests were finding something hidden by a person, and purity turned out to be honesty between people about a mixture.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think adulteration means that a bad substance got into the food, as if the fault sat in the food. But clean water in milk is not a bad substance, and the milk was already mostly water. The fault lies in what was promised and not given.  
*Evidence:* In the mystery cups test, the watered milk and the whole milk contained exactly the same kinds of substance. The only difference was the amount of water and the fact that nobody had been told. The tests detected the first, and the wrong lay in the second.

- *who am i:* The calcium in your bones and the protein in your muscles arrived as food. Lead, if it is in your food, is stored in the same bones. So your body is built from what you were given to eat, including the parts nobody mentioned. You are made of other people's care, and sometimes of their carelessness.
- *what is life:* Every living being tests what they take in. A rat nibbles a new food and waits. A bee's antennae judge nectar. A bacterium senses sugar and swims towards the sugar, and senses a poison and turns away. To be alive is to keep choosing what may cross from outside to inside.
- *what is this world:* A spoon of turmeric holds soil from a field, rain, a farmer's season, a miller's hands and a trader's choices. If any of them fails, the failure reaches your plate. The world is joined so closely that no meal comes from one place or one person.

This lesson set out to catch cheats and found something else as well. No food is one thing alone, and nobody eats alone. Each mouthful is a mixture made by plants, animals, microbes, weather and many people, most of whom you will never meet. Adulteration is a break in that chain at the one place where a person chose to hide something. One school of Indian thought, Advaita Vedanta, holds that consciousness is the ground from which the eater, the food and the grower all arise. On this view, eating is that one ground passing through its own forms. Many scientists do not accept this, and would say it more simply: atoms are shared and recycled among living things. Both views are given below. Both accounts agree that the line between your food and you lasts only a few hours.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad, part 3, sections 2 and 7:** Bhrigu, a student, is told by his father to find out what everything comes from. His first answer is food: beings are born from food, live by food, and return to food. Later the text gives a rule: 'Do not speak ill of food.' (The student's first answer is the one biology confirms: bodies are built from what is eaten. The Upanishad treats this as a first step, and Bhrigu goes on to deeper answers. The lesson stays at the first step and finds it already large.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, a Vietnamese Buddhist teacher, in 'The Heart of Understanding', 1988:** He holds up a sheet of paper and says that a cloud is floating in it. Without the cloud there is no rain, without rain no tree, and without the tree no paper. He calls this interbeing: a thing is made of everything that is not that thing. (The story's turmeric holds a field, a mill and a road full of buyers in the same way. The lesson's science traced each of those links one by one, and found that a fault in any of them shows up in the powder.)
- **Kautilya's Arthashastra, book 4, chapter 2:** A ruler must protect people from merchants who use false weights or sell mixed goods as good ones. (This is a book of government, not of the spirit, and it is here for that reason. It shows an old recognition that honesty about food cannot be left to each buyer's eyes. It has to be kept up by people together.)
- Science (settled): The atoms of the body are steadily replaced by atoms from food and water. Studies begun by Rudolf Schoenheimer in the 1930s used food labelled with traceable atoms. They showed that even fat and protein in the body are constantly taken apart and rebuilt.
- Science (settled): Rats who fall ill after a new taste avoid that taste afterwards, even when the illness comes hours later. John Garcia and colleagues reported this in 1955. Many other animals show the same learning.
- Other view, The Buddha in the Amagandha Sutta, Sutta Nipata 2.2, and separately the Charvaka thinkers of ancient India: The Buddha taught that no food makes a person pure or impure, and that only actions do. On that view the search for pure food can become a distraction from how one lives. The Charvakas held that a person is the body and nothing more. For them, food builds the body and matters for that reason alone, and there is no deeper joining to be found in a meal.
- Practice, One mouthful, traced back (3 min): Take one small piece of food: a grain of cooked rice, or a pinch of dal. Before you eat it, look at it for three slow breaths. In your mind, walk backwards along its journey. The kitchen. The shop. The lorry. The mill. The field. The rain. Do not hurry. Now eat it slowly, and notice the moment when you can no longer say where the food ends and you begin.
- Ask: How many people and other beings touched that one grain before you did? Can you count them?
- Ask: If your body is built from food, and food is built from soil, water and air, where does 'you' begin?
- Ask: When a seller hides something in food, what exactly is broken: the food, or something between people?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Milk is already mostly water. So why is adding more water to it wrong? (hint: Imagine the packet said 'milk with one fifth water' and cost less. Would anything still be wrong? What changed?)
- *deep:* If a food passes every test you have, do you know that it is pure, or only that you have not found anything? (hint: Think about what each test was looking for, and about the adulterants nobody has thought of yet.)
- *big:* A test for protein was fooled by a chemical that copied only the thing the test measured. Where else in life do we measure one easy thing and trust that it stands for something bigger? (hint: Think of marks and learning, of likes and friendship, of price and quality.)
- *big:* Buyers choose the brightest turmeric, and some sellers add colour to supply it. Who is responsible for the dye: the seller, the buyer, or both? (hint: Ask what would happen to an honest seller if every buyer kept choosing by colour.)
- *deep:* Iodine added to salt is welcomed, and chalk added to sugar is a crime. What exactly makes one adding good and the other bad? (hint: Try each possible answer, such as 'it is harmless', 'it is useful', 'it is declared', on both cases and see which survive.)
- *big:* Kesari dal harmed people in famines and feeds people in droughts. Should a food be banned because it is dangerous when it is the only food? (hint: Think about who eats the cheapest dal, who grows the hardiest crop, and who gets to decide.)
- *deep:* Every food is a mixture, and every plant and animal takes in what is in the soil, water and air. Can any food be called pure? (hint: Decide first what you want the word to promise. Then ask if anything could keep that promise.)
- *big:* You trust hundreds of strangers every time you eat. What makes that trust reasonable, and what would make it foolish? (hint: List who handled your last meal before it reached you, and what would let you check each one.)
- *deep:* A rat tastes a tiny bit of a new food and waits to see what happens. You run a water test on turmeric. Are the two of you doing the same thing? (hint: Compare what each tester is trying to find out, and what each uses as a tool.)

## Story: The Colour They Asked For
*A made-up story set in Erode, Tamil Nadu, which is a real centre of the turmeric trade. The people and the mill are imagined. What is real: turmeric powders differ naturally in colour, from pale yellow to deep orange, depending on the variety and on how much curcumin the roots hold. Researchers in Bangladesh found that buyers' liking for bright yellow turmeric was one reason processors added lead chromate. The water test is from the food authority's booklet.*

The grinding mill on Brough Road in Erode had been Kanagavalli's for eleven years, and her mother's before that. On Saturdays her daughter Thenmozhi, who was ten, sat on the flour sacks and did her homework in the smell of chilli and coriander.

This Saturday there was a problem, and the problem was a customer.

'It is dull,' said the woman, holding the open packet of turmeric powder to the light. 'Last month I bought from the new shop near the bus stand. Theirs was like gold. Yours is like dust. Are you mixing something?'

Kanagavalli wiped her hands. 'I am mixing nothing. These are this year's roots from Kodumudi. I boiled, dried and ground them myself. This is their colour.'

'Then the roots are bad,' said the woman, and she left without buying.

She was the third that week. Thenmozhi watched her mother stand quite still beside the grinder.

In the afternoon a man came with a sample bag. He was a trader named Ramasamy, friendly, with a pen in his pocket. He put a pinch of brilliant yellow powder on the counter. 'Polish,' he said. 'Two spoons to a sack of roots before grinding. Your turmeric will shine like the bus-stand shop's. Everyone is using it. The customers want it. You are only giving them what they ask for.'

'What is it?' said Kanagavalli.

'Colour,' said Ramasamy. 'Only colour.'

Thenmozhi had been reading her science book. She slid off the sacks. 'Amma, can I have two glasses of water?'

She dropped a spoon of her mother's turmeric into one glass and did not stir. The powder sank in a soft cloud. The water above stayed nearly clear, the faintest yellow.

'And a little of the bus-stand shop's,' she said. Her mother found the neighbour's packet. Thenmozhi dropped a spoon of that into the second glass. Yellow streamed off the powder as it fell, and in a few seconds the whole glass was the colour of a marigold.

'Turmeric's own colour does not like water,' said Thenmozhi. 'It stays in the powder. So that colour is something else. A dye.'

Ramasamy smiled. 'Clever girl. But my polish is not that cheap dye. Try it.' He tipped his pinch into a third glass.

The yellow powder sank straight to the bottom, heavy as sand. The water above stayed clear. Clearer than her mother's.

'You see?' said Ramasamy. 'It passes your test.'

Thenmozhi stared at the glass. Her test had said: nothing found. And yet the powder at the bottom was plainly not turmeric. It was too bright and too heavy, and no root had ever made it. 'It passed,' she said slowly, 'because my test only asks one question. Does the colour run in water? I did not ask anything else.' She looked up at the trader. 'Why is it so heavy?'

Ramasamy stopped smiling. He folded his sample bag. 'It is what painters use,' he said, not to her. 'It has lead in it. I do not eat turmeric from the shops I sell to.' He said it as a plain fact, the way a man mentions the weather, and he went out.

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Kanagavalli took a piece of cardboard and a marker. She wrote in Tamil, in large letters: OUR TURMERIC IS PALE BECAUSE NOTHING IS ADDED. BRING A GLASS OF WATER AND SEE. She set two glasses on the counter beside it, one pale and settled, one marigold.

'Will they come back?' asked Thenmozhi.

'Some,' said her mother. 'The ones who look.'

Thenmozhi sat down again with her homework and did not do it. She was thinking about the woman who had held the packet to the light. That woman had not wanted lead. She had wanted gold-coloured turmeric, because gold looked like good. And up and down the road, traders had heard her, and had given her the colour.

So who had put it there?

**For the parent:** The dye was added by a seller, but the wish for it began with buyers who judged turmeric by brightness. And the test that caught one adulterant was blind to a worse one, because a test answers only the question it was built to ask.

- Ask: The trader's powder passed Thenmozhi's water test. What other difference between that powder and turmeric could a new test use?
- Ask: The customer wanted bright turmeric and did not want lead. Did she have any part in the lead being there? Give your reasons.
- Ask: Kanagavalli put two glasses on the counter. Why might letting buyers see a test work better than telling them 'my turmeric is pure'?

## Experiments
### e1 · The mystery cups test: can simple tests find what a grown-up has secretly added? (63 min, 2 days)
- **question:** A grown-up has secretly changed some cups of food. Can a glass of water, a wet paper and a slanting plate show which cups were changed? How little can each test notice?
- **predict:** Before any test, guess for each set which cup was changed and which test will show it. For the milk, guess how much added water the plate will be able to notice. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Each pair is tested with the same amount of powder, the same amount of water and the same wait. For the milk, every cup uses the same clean plate, the same slant and the same size of drop. Each drop starts on the same line at the same moment. The milk drops are run twice, and the second time the cups are in a different order on the plate. This is because the place on the plate can change how a drop runs. A sample that seems to show something is tested a second time with fresh water, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Do not taste or drink anything in the cups, because chalk and sugar look the same. Label every cup 'NOT FOR EATING' and keep the cups away from small children and pets. The grown-up crushes the chalk inside a plastic bag and tips it out slowly, so that nobody breathes the dust. Food colouring stains hands and cloth, so cover the table with newspaper. Wash hands after the test.
- **record:** Fill in the table as you go. After the reveal, tick each set that you named correctly. Then write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why. Write the smallest amount of added water that the plate noticed, or write that it noticed none.
- **results table:** Set and cup | What I saw in the test | My guess: changed or not changed | What the grown-up reveals | Did the test notice it?
- **schedule:** day 1: A grown-up dries colour onto tea leaves and onto turmeric powder, out of the child's sight, and leaves them overnight. (10 min); day 2: A grown-up finishes the secret cups. Then the child predicts, tests the four sets, learns the answers and tests the family's own samples. (53 min)
- **s1 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* On day 1, out of the child's sight, make the changed tea and turmeric. Spread 1 teaspoon of dry tea leaves on a plate. Drop 4 or 5 drops of red, green or blue food colouring onto them, then stir. Stir a pinch of yellow food colouring powder into 1 teaspoon of turmeric on another plate. If you only have liquid yellow colouring, stir in 1 drop. Leave both plates in a fan's breeze or in the sun. Wait at least 3 hours, or overnight, until they are completely dry. Break up any lumps with a spoon. Keep 1 teaspoon of plain tea and 1 teaspoon of plain turmeric dry in other cups.  
  *Parent says:* This part is for the grown-up. Please keep the plates where your child will not see them.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to do this part when you are not looking. They will secretly change some tea and some turmeric.
  - Note: Use a colour that is not brown for the tea, so that natural tea stain and added colour look different. If the powders are still damp, the test result will be muddy, so dry them completely. The turmeric may clump after drying. Crumble it with a spoon before use.
  - Wait: Leave the coloured tea and turmeric to dry for at least 3 hours or overnight
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* On day 2, finish the secret cups. Make 9 cups labelled by set. S1 and S2 hold sugar. Put 2 teaspoons of sugar in each, and stir a quarter teaspoon of chalk powder into one of them. T1 and T2 hold tea: one has plain tea and one has the coloured tea. H1 and H2 hold turmeric: one plain and one coloured. M1, M2 and M3 hold milk. Use the same spoon for all, and put 4 spoons of liquid in each. One has 4 spoons of milk and no water. One has 3 spoons of milk and 1 spoon of water. One has 2 spoons of milk and 2 spoons of water. Flip a coin to decide which cup of each pair is changed. Draw three slips from a bowl to decide the milk order. Write the answers on a sheet of paper, fold it, and put it out of reach of the child.  
  *Parent says:* I have made nine cups in four sets. In each set of two, one cup has been changed. In the milk set, one cup is plain milk, one has a little water, and one has a lot. You will find which, and you will not know until the end.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up has made nine cups for you. In each pair, one cup has been changed. Your job is to find the changed one.
  - Note: Do not mix milk cups in advance, because the milk will spoil. Do the milk last, and wash the spoon between cups. The secret key must stay folded until step 8. Put the cups in the order S, T, H and M on a tray.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the four sets of cups. Ask for a guess for each set, and a reason. Write the guesses in the table. Say that for the milk the guess is about how much water the plate can notice. Do not correct any guess.  
  *Parent says:* In each pair, one cup was changed. Which test do you think will find it? How much water in the milk do you think the plate can notice? Tell me what you think and why.  
  *Child mode:* Guess which cup in each set was changed and why. Write your guesses in your table.
  - Ask: How could a changed cup behave differently from a plain one? (good: It might dissolve, or float, or sink, or leave a colour behind.; The changed one may look the same but act differently in water.)
  - Note: Do not say who is right. Every guess is a good guess at this point. The tests will answer.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the two sugar cups. Half fill two tumblers with plain water. Stir the contents of S1 into one tumbler with a clean spoon for 20 seconds. Stir S2 into the other tumbler with a second spoon. Look at both tumblers after 30 seconds and again after 2 minutes. Hold a dark cloth or paper behind the tumblers to see any cloudiness. Write what you see. Then say which cup you think was changed.  
  *Parent says:* Stir each one for 20 seconds. What does each glass look like now, and after 2 minutes? Which cup do you think was changed?  
  *Child mode:* Stir each cup into its own glass of water. Look at the water now and after 2 minutes. Which one is cloudy?
  - Ask: What difference between sugar and the added powder does this test use? (good: Sugar dissolves and the powder does not.; The changed one stays cloudy and settles as a white layer.)
  - Note: A quarter teaspoon of chalk makes a clear white cloud, and then a white layer on the bottom within 2 minutes. If both glasses look clear, the chalk was too little or too coarse. Say 'nothing found by this test' and keep going. That is a real result.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the two turmeric cups. Put 1 teaspoon of H1 gently on the top of one tumbler of still water. Put 1 teaspoon of H2 on the other tumbler. Do not stir. Look after 1 minute and after 5 minutes. Look for streaks of colour running down from the powder, and notice how fast the water changes colour. Write what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Do not stir. Watch the powder. Does any colour run down into the water? How quickly? Which cup do you think was changed?  
  *Child mode:* Put each powder on still water without stirring. Watch for colour running down. Which one runs bright yellow fastest?
  - Ask: Why should we not stir the water? (good: Stirring would spread colour and powder by force, and we would not see which one dissolves by itself.; We want to see what each powder does on its own.)
  - Note: Natural turmeric tints the water a faint pale yellow after some minutes, because a little of the curcumin does dissolve. Added colour shows up much faster, as bright streaks. What matters is how quickly and how strongly the water changes. If both look alike after 5 minutes, say 'nothing found by this test'.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the two tea cups. Lay a square of white napkin or tissue on each plate. Wet both with a little water, until damp but not floating. Sprinkle a pinch of T1 on one and a pinch of T2 on the other. Wait 1 minute. Lift a few leaves and look at the paper underneath. Look again after 10 minutes while you do the next test. Write what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Is the paper stained under each pinch? What colour is the stain? What about after 10 minutes?  
  *Child mode:* Sprinkle each tea on wet paper. Wait 1 minute. Lift the leaves and look at the paper.
  - Ask: Both teas may stain the paper after a long time. So which stain tells you more, the first one or the last? (good: The first stain, because added colour comes off at once and natural tea comes off slowly.; How fast the colour appears matters as much as whether it appears.)
  - Note: Added colour makes a bright red, green or blue stain at once. Natural tea leaves stain the paper brown slowly, over many minutes. Both are real. The test looks at the colour and its speed.
- **s7 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the three milk cups. Wash and dry a steel plate so that it is free of grease. Tilt it on 3 books so that it slopes steeply, about as steep as a roof. Mark a pencil or tape line across the top. Use a separate clean spoon for each cup. Let 1 drop from each spoon tip fall onto the plate on that line, about 3 centimetres apart. Start all three drops together. Watch for 10 seconds and look at how far each one ran and whether it leaves a white trail. Wipe the plate and run it a second time with the cups in a different order. Rank the three drops from the one that moved slowest to the one that moved fastest.  
  *Parent says:* Put the drops down together. Which runs farthest? Which leaves a white trail? Which cup do you think holds the most water?  
  *Child mode:* Put three drops of milk on the slanting plate together. Which runs fastest? Which leaves a white trail?
  - Ask: If the cup with a lot of water and the plain cup both look the same, what does that tell you? (good: The test cannot notice that much water, or the plate is greasy.; A test may be too weak to find the difference. That is a result, not a failure.)
  - Note: Plain milk drags and leaves a white trail. The cup with half water usually runs much faster and leaves a thin trail. The cup with a quarter water is often hard to tell from plain milk. If all three run alike, wash the plate with soap, dry it and tilt it more. If they still run alike, write 'nothing found by this test'.
- **s8 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at your table. Write your final answer for each set, and the milk ranking from most water to least. Then ask the grown-up to unfold the key and read the answers. Tick each set that you named correctly. For the milk, write the smallest amount of added water that the plate noticed.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me your final answers. Then we will open the key and see.  
  *Child mode:* Write your final answers. Then a grown-up reads the key. Tick the ones you got right.
  - Ask: Which set was easiest and which was hardest? Why? (good: The test that uses a big difference was easiest, such as dissolving or colour running.; The milk with a little water was hardest, because the difference was small.)
  - Note: If your child got some wrong, say that the tests have limits, and that is exactly what the experiment is for. Never say the child failed.
  - Think: What would you need to know before you could be sure that 'nothing found' meant nothing was there?
- **s9 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Test the family's own samples. Take four samples from the kitchen: a teaspoon of turmeric, a teaspoon of sugar, a pinch of tea leaves and a spoon of milk. Run the same tests as before. Put the sugar and turmeric in water and the tea on wet paper. Put a drop of the family's milk on the plate. Put a drop of the cup you named plain milk beside it, and a drop of plain water. For each, write 'found something' or 'nothing found by this test'. Never write 'pure'. If a test shows something, repeat it once with fresh water, and keep the packet and the bill.  
  *Parent says:* Here are our own foods. What does each test say? We will write 'found something' or 'nothing found by this test'. We never write 'pure'. Why not?  
  *Child mode:* Test your family's own turmeric, sugar, tea and milk. Write 'nothing found by this test' or 'found something'. Never write 'pure'.
  - Ask: A kitchen turmeric leaves the water pale. What exactly can you say? (good: Nothing found by the dye test.; The test says nothing about lead chromate or any other adulterant that it cannot see.)
  - Note: Most home samples will pass, and that is the expected and honest result. A passing sample is not a proof of purity. If something does show, do not conclude that the shop cheated. Try a second brand, and a whole spice if you have one. If you are still worried, keep the packet and the bill and report it. The neighbourhood test project shows how to write a report.
- **s10 (4 min)**

  *Parent says:* Nothing we tested is eaten or drunk. Pour it all away and wash everything. Then write what you think, what you saw and why.  
  *Child mode:* Pour everything away and wash the cups. Write what you think, what you saw and why.

  - Think: If a seller wanted to fool every test you used today, what could the seller add?
### e2 · Whose colour is it? Splitting colours on wet paper (25 min)
- **question:** A dot of green food colouring or black felt-pen ink is placed on paper, and water creeps up the paper. Does each dot stay one colour, or does it split into several?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will each dot stay one colour or split into more? Which dot will split into the most colours? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Every strip is the same paper, the same width and the same length. Each dot is the same size and the same height above the water. The water is the same depth and the strips wait for the same 10 minutes. The paper is changed only in the colour of the dot. Each colour is tried on two strips, because one strip can be spoiled by a drip.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw each strip and colour in the bands. Work out, for each band, its distance divided by the distance that the water travelled. Write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Colour dot | Colours seen after 10 minutes | Distance the water travelled (millimetres) | Distance of the farthest band (millimetres)
- **e2s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut 6 strips, each 2.5 centimetres wide and 12 centimetres long. On each strip draw a pencil line 2 centimetres from one end. Put a small dot on the line, about the size of a pinhead. Use a toothpick dipped in food colouring for the green, and the felt pens for black and brown. Make 2 strips for each colour. Let the dots dry for 1 minute.  
  *Parent says:* Make a small dot on each pencil line. Keep it small. Which dot do you think will stay one colour?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a pencil line near the bottom of each strip. Put one small dot on each line.
  - Note: A big dot spreads into a blur. Keep it as small as a pinhead. Washable pens give clear bands. A permanent marker does not dissolve in water, so it will not move. If your black pen stays one colour, try a green or a purple pen.
- **e2s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the guesses and the reasons, and write them down. Then pour 1 centimetre of water into each tumbler, so that the water is below the pencil line.  
  *Parent says:* Which dots will split into more colours? Why do you think so? Make a guess before the water arrives.  
  *Child mode:* Guess which dots will split. Pour 1 centimetre of water into each glass.
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. If the water is higher than 1 centimetre, it will cover the dots and wash the colour into the glass.
- **e2s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the top of each strip to a pencil laid across a tumbler. Lower the strip until the bottom touches the water. The dot must stay above the water. Watch for 10 minutes. Look every 2 minutes and say what you see. After 10 minutes, take out each strip and quickly mark where the water reached with a pencil, before the paper dries.

  - Note: Most green food colourings split into a blue and a yellow band. Some black pens split into pink, blue and yellow. If a dot does not split, say so. Some colours are single dyes. A strip that drifts into the glass can be started again.

  *Child mode:* Measure how far the water went and how far each colour went. Draw each strip.
  - Note: Fractions such as 0.4 and 0.8 are enough. The same band on the same paper should give about the same number on two strips. If two strips of one colour disagree a lot, a drip probably spoiled one.
  - Think: If a sweet's colour splits into three, what might that mean? What would it not prove?

(15 min)

### ch1 · Build a milk-flow tester: a plate, a slope and a ruler (35 min)
- **problem:** Build a milk-flow tester from things at home. It must tell plain milk from half-and-half watered milk. Two different people must get the same ranking.

Use the same spoon tip to make a drop of about the same size. Put all the drops on the line together. Measure how far each ran in 10 seconds with a ruler. Do it three times and write the distances.

## Worksheets
### Adding, removing, swapping and the first tests (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (match) Match each trick to its kind of adulteration.  
   **Answer:** chalk powder stirred into sugar → adding; cream skimmed off milk sold as full-cream → removing; cheap long-grain rice sold as basmati → swapping; water poured into a milk can → adding. *Why:* Adding puts in a cheaper thing, removing takes out a valuable part and swapping sells one food as another. In every case the change is secret.
2. (sort) Sort each one into adulteration or not adulteration.  
   **Answer:** iodine in salt, allowed and named on the packet → Not adulteration; brick dust secretly mixed into chilli powder → Adulteration; toned milk sold under the name toned milk → Not adulteration; starch secretly mixed into paneer → Adulteration; vitamins A and D in fortified oil, named on the label → Not adulteration; cloth dye in a sweet → Adulteration. *Why:* What matters is whether the addition is secret, unallowed or harmful. Open, allowed and labelled additions are not adulteration.
3. (count) Water (10 litres) is added to 40 litres of milk at 50 rupees a litre. How many extra rupees?  
   **Answer:** 500. *Why:* Sold as milk, 50 litres at 50 rupees brings 2,500 rupees, and 40 litres brings 2,000. The difference is 500 rupees for water that cost almost nothing.
4. (truefalse) A buyer can usually find adulteration in a sealed packet by looking and smelling.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* Adulterants are chosen so that they look and smell like the food, so the buyer's eyes and nose cannot find them.
5. (mcq) Why is saffron cheated far more often than potatoes?  
   Options: Little weight, big price, so cheating pays / Saffron is easier to grow / Potatoes are never sold by weight  
   **Answer:** Little weight, big price, so cheating pays. *Why:* Cheating pays most where a food costs a lot for its weight. Potatoes are sold by weight too, but they cost little, so the gain is small.
6. (mcq) Which adulterant harms mainly by what it is, and not only by cheating?  
   Options: Clean water in milk / Used tea leaves in fresh tea / Lead chromate in turmeric  
   **Answer:** Lead chromate in turmeric. *Why:* Lead chromate is a poison that builds up in the body. Clean water and used leaves cheat the buyer of food but are not poisons themselves.
7. (fill) Lead is stored in the body's ____ because the body mistakes it for calcium.  
   **Answer:** ['bones']. *Why:* The body uses calcium to build bones and takes up lead by mistake, and it stays there for years.
8. (order) Put the steps of a fair slanting-plate test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Wash and dry the plate', 'Tilt it on the same books', 'Put a drop of each milk on the same line together', 'Compare how far each drop ran in 10 seconds']. *Why:* A clean plate and the same slope keep the test fair. Starting drops together on one line means that only the milk differs.
9. (truefalse) A magnet can find brick powder in chilli powder.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* A magnet attracts iron only, and brick powder is not iron. A magnet test suits iron filings in tea.
10. (mcq) Chalk is stirred into sugar. Which difference does the water test use?  
   Options: Sugar floats and chalk sinks / Sugar dissolves and chalk does not / Sugar is white and chalk is grey  
   **Answer:** Sugar dissolves and chalk does not. *Why:* Sugar vanishes into water, and chalk stays as a cloud and then a layer. Colour is not the difference, since both are white.
11. (draw) Draw the slanting-plate test. Show the plate, the books, the line and three drops of milk.  
   **Answer:** A plate tilted on books, a line across its top, and three drops starting together. Their trails are short, medium and long.. *Why:* A fair test needs the same plate, the same slope and a common start line, so that only the milk differs.
12. (write) A sample passed every test. Write one honest sentence for the family notebook.  
   **Answer:** A good answer says 'nothing found by these tests' and does not say 'pure'. It may add what the tests could not find.. *Why:* A test can say 'I found this' but never 'there is nothing here', so the honest words are 'nothing found'.
13. (perspective) Is milk pure?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer sees that each meaning is useful for something. The chemist's meaning fits no food, the legal one lets inspectors act and the shopper's one can include natural poisons.. *Why:* The three meanings answer different questions, and the word pure alone does not tell you which one is meant.
14. (mcq) A pink dye made for cloth is found in cotton candy. Why is it adulteration and not a permitted addition?  
   Options: Pink is not a food colour / Cotton candy is not a real food / Not allowed in food, and hidden  
   **Answer:** Not allowed in food, and hidden. *Why:* Eight artificial colours are allowed in certain foods, in set amounts, and named on the label. A cloth dye is not one of them.
15. (write) Name two things on a packet that a family can check before buying, and say what each tells.  
   **Answer:** The 14-digit licence number shows that the business is registered. The batch number lets a bad lot be traced. The date, the address and the ingredients also count.. *Why:* A label cannot show a secret addition, but it makes tracing and reporting possible.
16. (mcq) A family finds that a test shows something odd in a packet of turmeric. What should they do first?  
   Options: Throw the packet away quietly / Taste a little to check / Tell nobody / Repeat it, keep the packet, ask for help  
   **Answer:** Repeat it, keep the packet, ask for help. *Why:* One try can be a fluke, so repeat it. Keeping the packet and the bill lets the food authority test it, and tasting an unknown sample is never safe.
### What a test can and cannot tell (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A honey sample passes the test of a drop that holds together in water. What does this show?  
   Options: That the honey is made from nectar / Only that it is thick / That no sugar was added  
   **Answer:** Only that it is thick. *Why:* The test checks thickness, and bees make honey by drying nectar until it is thick. A syrup can be as thick, so it passes too.
2. (truefalse) A test that finds nothing proves that a food is pure.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* A test can find only what it was built for, and only above its smallest amount. 'Nothing found' is all it can say.
3. (fill) Before anyone adds anything, about ____ parts in every 100 parts of milk are water.  
   **Answer:** ['87']. *Why:* Milk is a mixture, and most of it is water. Adding water changes the mix, not the kind of substance.
4. (mcq) On wet paper, one colour travels farther than another. What does this show?  
   Options: It clings less, so water carries it farther / It is the better dye / It is heavier  
   **Answer:** It clings less, so water carries it farther. *Why:* Each dye clings to paper by a different amount, so the water carries them different distances. That separates the dyes.
5. (sort) Which test would catch each adulterant?  
   **Answer:** chalk in sugar → Glass of water; iron filings in tea → Magnet; water in milk → Slanting plate; starch in paneer → Iodine drop; brick powder in chilli powder → Glass of water. *Why:* Each test uses one difference: dissolving or sinking, attraction to iron, how fast a drop runs, and colour with starch.
6. (match) Match each adulterant to the difference that its test uses.  
   **Answer:** iron filings in tea → attracted by a magnet; starch in paneer → turns blue-black with iodine; papaya seeds in pepper → float while pepper sinks; water in milk → runs fast down a slope. *Why:* A test works by finding one difference between the food and the thing that was added.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the drop that runs slowest on a slanting plate to the one that runs fastest.  
   **Answer:** ['Plain whole milk', 'Milk with a quarter water', 'Milk with half water', 'Plain water']. *Why:* More water makes the liquid thinner, so the drop runs faster. The quarter-water milk is close to plain milk, so the first two are hard to tell apart.
8. (write) A dairy checks milk with a lactometer. Say how cheats fooled it, and why.  
   **Answer:** The lactometer notices thin milk. Cheats added sugar, starch or salt to bring the thickness back. The test measured one property that stands for quality, and the cheats supplied only that property.. *Why:* When a test checks one property, cheats can restore that property alone.
9. (mcq) A seller adds clean water to milk. Why does the law still treat it as an offence?  
   Options: Clean water is a poison / Milk must never be mixed with anything / The family pays for more than it gets  
   **Answer:** The family pays for more than it gets. *Why:* Harm by cheating is real, even when nothing poisonous is added. The family loses money and nourishment.
10. (write) Iodine in salt is welcome and chalk in sugar is a crime. Say what three things separate them.  
   **Answer:** The iodine has been tested and is allowed, it is within a limit and the label says so. The chalk is hidden, unallowed and a cheat.. *Why:* An addition is not adulteration if it is allowed, within the limit and declared.
11. (perspective) Is food in sealed packets safer than loose food?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer notes that the 2020 honey tests found failures in big brands, and that tracing needs a packet. Neither view is always right.. *Why:* Evidence supports packets for some foods, such as oils, and not for all. Cost also decides what a family can do.
12. (truefalse) Toned milk sold under that name is adulterated milk.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* It has had fat taken out openly, and it says so on the packet, so nobody is cheated about what it is.
13. (count) Turmeric has lead at 30 parts per million. The limit is 10. How many times the limit?  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* 30 ÷ 10 = 3. The sample holds three times the limit.
14. (draw) Draw a paper strip after 10 minutes in water, with a green dot split into two colours.  
   **Answer:** A strip standing in water with a pencil line near the bottom. A yellow band travelled farther and a blue band stayed lower. A mark shows where the water reached.. *Why:* Different dyes cling by different amounts, so they separate into bands at different heights.
15. (mcq) A label says 'no added colour', but a laboratory finds a dye. Which kind of bad food is this?  
   Options: Sub-standard, because it is cheap / Misbranded, because the label says something untrue / Unsafe, because it has a label  
   **Answer:** Misbranded, because the label says something untrue. *Why:* A false label makes the food misbranded. A dye may also make it unsafe, but the label is the first fault.
16. (write) A family's test shows something. Write what they should keep and what they should do next.  
   **Answer:** Keep the packet, the bill and the batch number. Repeat the test once. Report it to the food authority through the Food Safety Connect app or the state food safety office.. *Why:* Evidence kept is what the food authority needs to trace and test the lot.
### Faulty claims and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A girl says, 'My turmeric left the water pale, so it has no lead.' What is wrong with her claim?  
   Options: The water was too cold / Turmeric never holds lead / The test misses lead, which does not dissolve  
   **Answer:** The test misses lead, which does not dissolve. *Why:* A test can find only what it uses a difference for. Lead chromate hardly dissolves, so a water test cannot show it.
2. (mcq) A school tests 10 samples and finds nothing. Which claim can the school fairly make?  
   Options: All the food in the town is pure / These tests found nothing in these 10 samples / Nobody cheats in this town  
   **Answer:** These tests found nothing in these 10 samples. *Why:* Ten samples are a small part of a town's food, and the tests have limits. The honest claim is only about what was tested.
3. (truefalse) A new test can raise the cases reported, though the food did not change.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* A new test finds what the old tests could not see. So counts show what is looked for as well as what exists.
4. (fill) Milk has 87 parts water in every 100. A half-milk, half-water mix has ____ parts water in every 100.  
   **Answer:** ['93.5']. *Why:* Half water and half milk gives (100 + 87) ÷ 2 = 93.5 per hundred parts of water. The jump from 87 is only 6.5, so a test can easily miss it.
5. (write) Many Delhi families fell ill together in 1998. What does that say about loose oil from one source?  
   **Answer:** One source of oil reached many families, so one poisoned batch harmed many. It is hard to trace loose oil, and it shows why batch numbers and sealed packets help.. *Why:* A long chain with loose food makes one fault spread, and a lack of tracing makes it hard to stop.
6. (order) Order from easiest to hardest to find with a glass of water and a spoon.  
   **Answer:** ['Chalk in sugar', 'Dye in turmeric', 'Water in milk, a quarter', 'Lead chromate in turmeric']. *Why:* Chalk settles in water and a dye runs. A small amount of added water barely changes a drop. Lead chromate cannot be found without a laboratory.
7. (perspective) Kesari dal is banned in most of India. Should it stay banned?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer asks who eats it, how much and for how long, and whether the low-toxin varieties are used. No view is marked right, because scientists still disagree.. *Why:* The harm comes from eating mostly one pulse for months, so the question is not simply safe or unsafe.
8. (write) A rat tastes a little new food and waits. You test turmeric in water. Is that the same thing?  
   **Answer:** Both test before trusting, in small amounts, and both can miss what the test cannot show. The rat tests with her body and a delay, and you use a chosen difference.. *Why:* Testing a little before trusting a lot is a way living beings protect themselves, and every test has limits.
9. (mcq) Which is the worst way to say how common adulteration is?  
   Options: Giving one percentage without saying what was counted / Saying how many samples were tested / Saying what the standard was  
   **Answer:** Giving one percentage without saying what was counted. *Why:* A survey may count failing a quality standard, finding an accidental contaminant or finding a harmful adulterant. These give very different numbers.
10. (sort) Sort each one into a stand-in measurement or a direct check.  
   **Answer:** nitrogen read as protein → Stand-in measurement; a magnet pulling iron out of tea → Direct check; a drop that holds together read as honey → Stand-in measurement; reading the DNA of a rice grain → Direct check; bright yellow read as good turmeric → Stand-in measurement. *Why:* A stand-in measures an easy thing that usually goes with quality. A cheat can supply the easy thing without the quality.
11. (match) Match each situation to the reason a test failed.  
   **Answer:** melamine fooled a protein test → the test measured nitrogen, which melamine has; watered milk passed a lactometer → sugar was added to bring back the thickness; failing honey passed home tests → the tests checked only thickness; lead was missed by a water test → lead chromate does not dissolve. *Why:* Each test measured one property, and the cheat or the chemistry supplied that property.
12. (draw) Draw the chain from a turmeric field to your kitchen. Mark where you can look and where you cannot.  
   **Answer:** A row of boxes: field, trader, mill, packer, shop, kitchen. A tick where someone can see the food. A cross where the food is hidden, such as inside a sealed sack or packet.. *Why:* Cheating can enter wherever the food is hidden from the next person.
13. (count) India's limit for lead in turmeric is 10 milligrams in every kilogram. How many milligrams are allowed in a 500 gram packet?  
   **Answer:** 5. *Why:* 500 grams is half a kilogram, so half of 10 is 5 milligrams.
14. (truefalse) Calcium carbide gives a gas like ethylene, so it is a safe way to ripen mangoes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* The gas it gives off is similar to ethylene, but the stones carry poisonous impurities. The use is banned in India. Ethylene itself is permitted.
15. (write) 'Natural' and 'safe' mean the same thing. Give one example from this lesson that shows they do not.  
   **Answer:** Argemone seeds are completely natural but poison mustard oil. Kesari dal is natural and can cause lathyrism when eaten alone for months.. *Why:* Nature makes poisons as well as foods, so a word like natural cannot stand in for a test or a standard.

1. (mcq) Which one is adulteration?  
   Options: A permitted colour named on a sweet packet / Brick dust secretly mixed into chilli powder / Toned milk sold as toned milk  
   **Answer:** Brick dust secretly mixed into chilli powder. *Why:* Adulteration is secret. Open, allowed and labelled additions are not. A child who picks another answer may think any addition counts.
2. (mcq) A seller secretly skims the fat from milk and still calls it full-cream. What kind of adulteration is this?  
   Options: Adding a cheaper thing / Swapping one food for another / Removing a valuable part  
   **Answer:** Removing a valuable part. *Why:* The seller takes out the valuable fat and keeps the name. Adding and swapping would need something new to be put in.

   Options: Loses customers to the bright turmeric / Gets more customers / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** Loses customers to the bright turmeric. *Why:* If buyers cannot tell good from bad, they may not pay more for the honest one, and the cheat can win. Akerlof wrote about this in 1970.

   Options: Potatoes, because they are everywhere / Plain water, because it is free / Saffron, because a small weight costs a lot

## Applications
- The Food Safety and Standards Authority of India sends mobile testing vans, called Food Safety on Wheels, to markets in many states. Families can bring a sample to be tested for common adulterants. Ask your local food safety office where the van visits.
- Dairies measure milk at the gate with a lactometer, and many test for fat and added water. If a farmer's milk is found watered, it can be refused or priced lower, which pays an honest farmer better.
- After researchers in Bangladesh linked lead in village blood to turmeric, the government warned traders, checked markets and fined sellers. Lead in market turmeric later fell. Testing and law together can work.
- Erode in Tamil Nadu is one of India's big turmeric centres. A trader there can send a sample of a lot to a laboratory before it is ground. Then a problem is found before it reaches a thousand kitchens.
- Honey from a beekeeper you know, bought near the hive, has a short chain. A short chain means you can ask the beekeeper how it was made, which a long chain does not allow.
- A school canteen can buy whole spices and grind them in the school kitchen. Then nobody can add anything. It costs more time, and it can feed many children safely.
- The Food Safety Connect app and the helpline 1915 let any family report a suspected case. A kept packet and bill help the food officer trace the lot back through the chain.
- Scientists at the Centre for DNA Fingerprinting and Diagnostics in Hyderabad developed a DNA test for basmati rice. It helps show that a sack is truly basmati. Then honest farmers are paid fairly.

## Spotted around us
- At the kitchen counter, hold a packet of turmeric or a bag of rice next to the same food sold loose. Notice that the packet has a printed licence number and a batch number, and the loose food has none. Ask what that tells you about who can trace it.
- On a milk packet in the fridge, look for the word toned, double toned or full cream. Notice that the packet names what has been done to the milk, and that this is why it is not adulterated.
- At the sweet shop, look at a very bright orange or pink sweet next to a pale one. Notice the colour, and ask what the packet says about colour. A permitted colour must be named.
- At the fruit seller's stall in mango season, look at a pile of mangoes. Notice whether the colour is even and whether the skin is spotted. Ask how the seller ripens them. Fruit ripen with a gas they make themselves, and stones that give off a similar gas are banned.
- At the grocery, pick up a bottle of mustard oil or ghee and look at its seal and its label. Notice if the seal is whole, and if the batch number and date are printed.
- In the tea cup, drop a pinch of tea leaves into plain water. Watch how fast the colour comes out. Notice that natural tea colours water slowly, and that this is one reason added colour can be found.
- At the corner shop, look at the salt packet. Notice the word iodised and the blue plus F mark. Ask why a nutrient that is added openly is not adulteration.
- At the weekly market, notice how many hands a sack of turmeric or rice passes through between the field and your bag. Ask which of them you could ask a question.

## Open questions
- **Is there any amount of an artificial food colour that is truly without effect on children?** In 2007 Donna McCann and colleagues in Britain tested mixtures of some colours and a preservative. Some children became more restless after drinking them. Later reviews judged the effect small and uncertain. Regulators in different countries drew different conclusions from the same studies.
- **Should kesari dal be legal to sell in India?** The science of low-toxin varieties and mixed diets suggests the risk is small. Can it be sold openly without being mixed secretly into other dals? And who would bear the risk? These are questions about markets and fairness, and no experiment has settled them.
- **Can testing ever get ahead of adulteration, or will cheats always design around each new test?** Every test so far has been followed by adulterants designed to pass it. Methods that read the whole fingerprint of a food are harder to fool, but they are costly and need large libraries of genuine samples. Nobody knows if a test can exist that cannot be gamed.
- **How much of the harm linked to heavily processed food comes from the processing itself?** Studies link such diets to illness, and one controlled trial showed people eat more of them. Scientists do not agree on the cause: the ingredients, the softness and speed of eating, the additives, or something not yet identified.
- **How much lead are Indian children taking in from spices, compared with other sources?** Lead also comes from old paint, battery recycling, some cookware and some cosmetics. Few studies in India have measured all the sources in the same homes, so the share from adulterated spices is not known.

## Connected maths
- A cup of whole milk is about 87 per cent water. A cup holds 4 spoons, made of 2 spoons of milk and 2 spoons of added water. About what share of the cup is water? Compare it with plain milk. **About 93.5 per cent, compared with 87 per cent. The share rises by only about 6.5 percentage points.**. 2 spoons of milk hold 2 × 0.87 = 1.74 spoons of water. Add 2 spoons of added water to get 3.74 spoons of water in 4 spoons. 3.74 ÷ 4 = 0.935, which is 93.5 per cent. Plain milk is 87 per cent, so half-and-half milk is only about 6.5 points higher. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A seller has 100 litres of milk. Imagine a test that notices added water only above 30 parts in every 100 of the mixture. How many litres can the seller add and not be noticed? **Up to about 42 litres is not noticed. About 43 litres or above would be.**. Water added is x litres, and the mixture is 100 + x litres. We want x ÷ (100 + x) to be 0.30. So x = 0.30 × (100 + x), which gives 0.7x = 30 and x is about 42.9 litres. Anything under 42 litres stays under the limit of the test. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- India's limit for lead in turmeric is 10 parts per million, which is 10 milligrams in every kilogram. A sample holds 300 times the limit. How many milligrams of lead are in a 100 gram packet? **300 milligrams, which is 0.3 grams.**. 300 times 10 is 3,000 milligrams in each kilogram. A 100 gram packet is one tenth of a kilogram, so 3,000 ÷ 10 = 300 milligrams. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- A survey tested 6,432 milk samples. It found 12 samples of unsafe adulterated milk, and about 41 out of every 100 samples below quality standards. About how many samples were below standard? About how many times more is that than 12? **About 2,637 samples, which is roughly 220 times as many as 12.**. 41 ÷ 100 × 6,432 = 2,637 samples. And 2,637 ÷ 12 is roughly 220. The two counts differ because they count different things. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- In the chromatography test, the water travels 60 millimetres up the paper. A yellow band stops 54 millimetres up and a blue band stops 24 millimetres up. Work out each band's distance divided by the water's distance. Which dye clings more to the paper? **Yellow 54 ÷ 60 = 0.9, blue 24 ÷ 60 = 0.4. The blue dye clings more.**. A band that moves almost as far as the water, 0.9, clings little to the paper. A band that moves less, 0.4, clings more. The same dye on the same paper gives about the same number each time, so the number helps name a dye. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is food adulteration? → Secretly adding, removing or swapping something in food so that the seller gains and the buyer is cheated or harmed.
- Name one thing that can be added to milk to cheat the buyer. → Water. Starch, sugar, salt or detergent are others.
- True or false: if you can see no difference, the food has nothing added. → False. Adulterants are chosen because they look like the food.
- Why does adulteration pay more with saffron than with potatoes? → A small weight of saffron costs a lot, so a cheap stand-in earns a lot.
- Is iodine added to salt adulteration? Why or why not? → No. It is allowed, within a limit, and the label says so.
- Chalk is stirred into a glass of water. What does it do? → It makes the water cloudy and then settles as a white layer. It does not dissolve.
- What do we write if a test finds nothing? → Nothing found by this test. We do not write pure.
- About how much of milk is water? → About 87 parts in every 100.
