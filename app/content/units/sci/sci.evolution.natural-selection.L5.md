# How species change: natural selection, adaptation and new species
`sci.evolution.natural-selection.L5`

**Summary.** A cactus has spines and a waxy skin, and each fits the desert so well that it looks planned. Yet no cactus changed itself to suit the desert. In this unit you will hunt beans on two cloths for three generations, and test two beaks in a drought year.

**Intro.** *A camel is born with wide feet that do not sink in sand. A lotus is born with air tubes in their stalks. Nobody fitted these parts. So how did each living being come to suit their place so well?* For a long time, many people thought each animal changed their own body by trying hard. They thought the change was then passed on. In this unit you test a different answer with your own hands. You count the seeds in one bhindi to see how many young one plant could have. You hunt beans on a dark cloth and a pale cloth for three generations, and count who is left. You pick up seeds with tweezers and with roti tongs, in a wet year and in a drought. Then you find out how one bird became two species in the hills of South India. You also learn why your own cells hold partners that were once free bacteria.

## Terms
- **Variation**: Variation means the differences between living beings of the same kind. No two are exactly alike. *e.g.* Look at ten kabuli chana. They differ in size, shape and wrinkles, though all came from chana plants.
- **Inherit**: To inherit a feature is to get it from your parents through their genes. Genes are instructions stored in DNA inside every cell. *e.g.* A calf inherits the shape of her horns from her parents. She does not inherit a scar her mother got from a fence.
- **Generation**: A generation is all the young born at about the same time. Parents are one generation and their children are the next. *e.g.* You, your mother and your grandmother are three generations. Mosquitoes can pass through a generation in two or three weeks.
- **Population**: A population is all the living beings of one kind who live in one place. They can breed with each other. *e.g.* All the house crows in Chennai form one population. All the chital deer in Bandipur form another.
- **Natural selection**: Natural selection is the way a population changes over generations. Members whose features suit the place leave more young, so those features become more common. *e.g.* In a drought on a small island, finches with deeper beaks survived more often. The next generation had deeper beaks on average.
- **Adaptation**: An adaptation is an inherited feature that helps a living being survive and have young in their place. *e.g.* A camel's wide feet are an adaptation to soft sand. A lotus's air-filled stalks are an adaptation to airless mud.
- **Environment**: The environment is everything around a living being. It includes the weather, the water, the food and all the other living beings there. *e.g.* The environment of a Thar desert lizard is hot sand, little water, insects to eat and hawks overhead.
- **Predator**: A predator is an animal who hunts and eats other animals. *e.g.* A tiger is a predator of chital deer. A house lizard is a predator of moths.
- **Prey**: Prey are the animals a predator hunts. One animal can be a predator and also be prey. *e.g.* A frog is prey for a snake, and the same frog is a predator of flies.
- **Camouflage**: Camouflage is a colour, pattern or shape that makes a living being hard to see against their background. *e.g.* The orange oakleaf butterfly of Indian forests closes their wings and looks like a dry leaf.
- **Mimicry**: Mimicry is when one species looks like another species that predators avoid. *e.g.* The female common mormon butterfly is harmless. Many look like the common rose butterfly, who tastes bad to birds.
- **Mutation**: A mutation is a change in DNA, made by chance when a cell copies DNA. Mutations are where new variations come from. *e.g.* One chance mutation long ago let some adult humans keep digesting milk. Their children inherited it.
- **Species**: A species is a group of living beings who can breed together and have young who can also have young. Scientists do not all agree on this meaning. *e.g.* Horses and donkeys are two species. Their young, the mule, cannot have young of her own.
- **Selective breeding**: Selective breeding is when people choose which plants or animals become parents, to get features that people want. *e.g.* Farmers bred cabbage, cauliflower and knol khol from one wild plant, by saving seed from the plants they liked.
- **Symbiosis**: Symbiosis means two different species living closely together. In many cases both partners gain. *e.g.* A lichen on a tree trunk is a fungus and an alga living as one. The alga makes sugar and the fungus holds water.
- **Mitochondria**: Mitochondria are tiny parts inside your cells that release energy from food using oxygen. Their ancestors were free bacteria. *e.g.* A muscle cell in your leg holds thousands of mitochondria. Each has their own small loop of DNA.
- **Coevolution**: Coevolution is when two species change over generations in answer to each other. *e.g.* As deer became faster, the tigers who hunted them became better at stalking. Each selected the other.
- **Extinct**: A species is extinct when their last member has died. More than 99 in every 100 species that ever lived are extinct. *e.g.* The cheetah became extinct in India in the 1950s. Cheetahs from Africa were brought to Kuno in 2022.

## Explanations
### Natural selection in four steps · *defined*
**Definition:** Natural selection is the way a population changes over generations. Members whose features suit the place leave more young, so those features become more common.

Natural selection needs four things. First, the members of a population vary. Second, many of their variations are inherited. Third, more young are born than can survive. Fourth, members whose features suit the place survive more often and leave more young. When this repeats for many generations, the whole population changes. No single member has to change. The place does the selecting, through its weather, food, predators and diseases. Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace both worked this out, and published it together in 1858.
- e.g. In the Bean Generations Test, you start with 15 dark and 15 pale beans on a dark cloth. The hunter catches more pale beans, so each new generation has more dark beans.
- e.g. In 1977, a drought on the island of Daphne Major left only large, hard seeds. Finches with deeper beaks survived more often, and their chicks inherited deeper beaks.
- e.g. DDT is a poison that was sprayed on mosquitoes across India from the 1950s. A few mosquitoes survived it and had young. In many places DDT stopped working within some years.
- e.g. In Gorongosa in Mozambique, ivory hunters killed elephants with tusks during a war from 1977 to 1992. Before the war about 2 in 10 females had no tusks. Among females born after it, about 3 in 10 had none.
### Adaptations: features that fit a place · *defined*
**Definition:** An adaptation is an inherited feature that helps a living being survive and have young in their environment.

Every place sets problems. A hot, dry place has little water and strong sun. A wet place has water all around but little air in the mud. A cold place has snow, frozen ground and little food in winter. An adaptation is a feature that solves one of these problems. A way of behaving can be an adaptation too, such as sleeping through the winter or feeding only at night. A living being is born with their adaptations. Natural selection built each one over many generations, because members with a slightly better version left more young. Most adaptations also have a cost. Thick fur keeps a yak warm in Ladakh, and would overheat the same yak in Chennai.
- e.g. Hot and dry: a cactus has spines in place of flat leaves, a waxy skin and a thick stem that stores water. A camel has wide feet for sand and a hump of fat as a food store. Long eyelashes keep out blowing sand. The khejri tree of the Thar has roots that go many metres down to water.
- e.g. Wet: a lotus has flat floating leaves that reach the light. Their stalks are full of air channels that carry air down to the roots. A fish has gills that take in oxygen dissolved in water. A duck has webbed feet. Mangrove trees in the Sundarbans push breathing roots up out of the mud. Seabirds such as gulls have waterproof feathers, and glands near the eyes that remove salt.
- e.g. Cold: a deodar or a fir has a cone shape and sloping branches, so heavy snow slides off. Their needles have a waxy coat and lose little water, and thick bark protects the trunk. A snow leopard has thick fur and wide, furry paws. A Himalayan marmot hibernates, which means sleeping deeply through the winter.
- e.g. Behaviour: bar-headed geese migrate over the Himalaya to spend the winter in India. The Indian gerbil of the Thar stays in a cool burrow by day and feeds at night.
### Predators and prey change each other · *defined*
**Definition:** A predator has adaptations for catching prey, and prey have adaptations for escaping. Each one is part of the other's environment, so each selects the other.

Predators need to find, reach and hold their prey. Many have eyes at the front of the head, which lets them judge distance. Others have speed, a smooth narrow shape called streamlined, camouflage, venom, traps such as webs, sharp teeth and claws, or teamwork. Prey need to notice danger and get away. Many have eyes at the sides of the head, which lets them see almost all around. Others live in herds, give alarm calls, run fast, or carry shells. Some fight back with horns, stings or spines. Some hide by camouflage. Some warn predators with bright colours that they taste bad. Some harmless species copy those colours, which is called mimicry. When prey become faster over generations, only the better hunters among the predators eat well and leave young. Each change in the predators selects a change in the prey, and each change in the prey selects a change in the predators. Scientists call this coevolution.
- e.g. A tiger has forward-facing eyes, stripes that hide her in tall grass, and soft paws for stalking. A chital deer has eyes at the sides, lives in a herd and gives a sharp alarm call.
- e.g. Dholes, the wild dogs of Indian forests, hunt in packs. A spider traps insects in a web. A cobra stops prey with venom.
- e.g. An Indian star tortoise pulls into a hard shell. A porcupine raises long quills.
- e.g. In 1862 Henry Walter Bates described harmless butterflies in the Amazon that look like bad-tasting ones. In India, many female common mormon butterflies look like the bad-tasting common rose.
### Where new variations come from: mutation · *defined*
**Definition:** A mutation is a change in DNA, made by chance when a cell copies DNA. Mutations are the source of new variations.

DNA holds the instructions for building a body, written in a code of four chemical letters. Each time a cell divides, the cell copies billions of letters, and a few copying mistakes slip through. Radiation and some chemicals can also damage DNA and cause mutations. These mistakes are mutations. Most mutations change nothing that matters. Some do harm, and a few help. A mutation in an egg cell, a sperm cell or a seed can be inherited. Each human baby is born with roughly 60 new mutations that neither parent had. A mutation appears by chance. It does not appear because the living being needs it. In 1952 Esther Lederberg and Joshua Lederberg showed this with bacteria. An antibiotic is a medicine that kills bacteria. Bacteria that could survive an antibiotic were already present before the bacteria ever met the antibiotic. So the variation comes first, and selection comes afterwards. Having two parents also makes variation, because each child gets a new mix of their parents' genes.
- e.g. In the activity One New Bean, a single dark bean among 29 pale beans stands for a new mutation. On a dark cloth, the dark bean's descendants can spread through the group in a few generations.
- e.g. A chance mutation lets some adult humans keep digesting the sugar in milk. In peoples who herded cattle, this mutation spread over the last several thousand years.
- e.g. Most mutations are never noticed. You carry dozens that do nothing you can see.
### How one species becomes two · *defined*
**Definition:** A new species can form when a population is split into groups that cannot meet. Each group changes in their own way, until the two can no longer breed together.

First, something splits a population. It may be a sea, a river, a mountain range or a stretch of land where that species cannot live. Next, each group meets different conditions. Different mutations appear in each group, and natural selection keeps different variations. The groups cannot mix their genes, so the differences add up. After many thousands of generations, the two groups may differ in size, colour, song and the season when they breed. If they meet again, they no longer choose each other as mates, or their young cannot have young. Now they are two species. This is how one ancestor can leave many species. The two cloths in the Bean Generations Test show the start of this: two groups that begin the same and end different.
- e.g. The sholakili is a small songbird of cool hilltop forests in the Western Ghats. The Palghat Gap is a hot, low gap about 30 kilometres wide, between Coimbatore and Palakkad. North of the gap lives the Nilgiri sholakili. South of the gap lives the white-bellied sholakili. They were one species millions of years ago.
- e.g. About 18 species of Darwin's finches live on the Galápagos Islands and Cocos Island. All descend from one species that arrived more than a million years ago.
- e.g. Blind catfish called Horaglanis live in wells and underground water in Kerala. Their ancestors were cut off from surface streams and changed in the dark.
- e.g. On Daphne Major, a male finch from another island arrived in 1981. Within a few generations, his descendants were breeding only with each other.
### What is a species? · *debated*
Scientists use the word 'species' every day. Yet they do not agree on one meaning that works for all of life. The reason comes from how species form. One species becomes two slowly, so at any moment some groups are part of the way there. Nature has no sharp line for us to find.
- e.g. A horse and a donkey can have a mule, but a mule cannot have young. So horses and donkeys count as two species.
- e.g. Lions and tigers have had cubs together in zoos, and some of those cubs have had young. Yet in the wild they live as two species.
- e.g. Greenish warblers live in a ring of forests around the Tibetan Plateau. Neighbouring groups breed together along the ring, but where the two ends meet in Siberia, the birds do not.
- **Ernst Mayr, a German-American biologist, in 1942:** A species is a group whose members can breed with each other in nature and have fertile young. Members of two species cannot. This works well for most animals with two parents.
- **Scientists who study bacteria and many plants:** Bacteria copy themselves and never breed in pairs, and many plant species cross easily. So the breeding rule fails for most of life. They group living beings by how different their DNA is.
- **Scientists who study fossils:** Nobody can test whether two fossil animals could breed. They must use the shape of bones and shells, and accept that the line between species is partly our choice.
### Cooperation in evolution: Lynn Margulis and the cell inside a cell · *defined*
**Definition:** Symbiosis is two different species living closely together. Sometimes two partners join so fully that they become one new kind of living being.

Inside almost every cell of your body are mitochondria, which release energy from food using oxygen. In 1967 the American biologist Lynn Margulis argued that mitochondria were once free bacteria. More than 1.5 billion years ago, a larger cell took in a bacterium and did not digest them. The bacterium gained food and shelter. The larger cell gained a partner who could use oxygen to release far more energy. Their descendants could no longer live apart. Later, some of these cells took in a second partner, a bacterium that could photosynthesise, which means making food from sunlight. That partner became the chloroplast, the green part of a plant cell. Her paper is said to have been turned down by about fifteen journals before one printed it. The evidence is strong. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA, in a loop like the DNA of bacteria. They make more of themselves by splitting in two, as bacteria do. They are wrapped in two membranes. In the 1970s and 1980s, scientists read their DNA and found that it matches the DNA of living bacteria. So every animal, plant and fungus descends from a partnership. Natural selection still works on partners: a partnership that helps both leaves more young.
- e.g. A lichen is a fungus and an alga, or a fungus and a photosynthesising bacterium, living as one body.
- e.g. Pulse plants such as chana and moong house bacteria in small lumps on their roots. The bacteria turn nitrogen from the air into food the plant can use.
- e.g. A coral animal houses tiny algae who share the sugar they make from sunlight.
- e.g. A cow cannot digest grass alone. Microbes in her stomach break the grass down, and both are fed.
- e.g. Each kind of fig tree has their own kind of tiny fig wasp. The wasp carries the fig's pollen, and the fig feeds the wasp's young.
### Do living beings help steer their own evolution? · *debated*
Living beings choose where to live, what to eat and whom to mate with. Many also build: nests, burrows, dams and webs. These choices change which features help them, so they change the selection on their own descendants. Scientists agree that this happens. They disagree about how much it matters.
- e.g. Thousands of years ago, some peoples began to herd cattle and drink their milk. That choice made the milk-digesting mutation useful, and it spread in those peoples.
- e.g. Earthworms change the soil they live in by pulling leaves down and mixing it. Charles Darwin wrote his last book about them, in 1881. Each new generation of earthworms inherits soil that their ancestors changed.
- e.g. A beaver builds a dam and makes a pond. The beaver's young grow up in a pond, and selection works on them there.
- **Most biologists, following Darwin:** Mutations arise by chance, and the environment selects among them. A habit such as dam-building is itself an inherited feature that selection shaped. Nothing more is needed to explain evolution.
- **Mary Jane West-Eberhard, an American biologist, in 2003:** Behaviour and flexible bodies often change first. An animal tries a new food or a new place. Later, selection favours genes that suit the new way of life. She wrote that genes are often followers, not leaders.
- **Scientists who study niche construction, such as John Odling-Smee and Kevin Laland:** Living beings make part of their own environment and pass that changed environment to their young. So young inherit two things, their genes and a changed place, and both shape evolution.
### 'Fittest' does not mean strongest, and evolution has no top · *defined*
**Definition:** In biology, 'fit' means suited to the place and so leaving more surviving young there. It does not mean strong, fast, big or clever.

The phrase 'survival of the fittest' was first used by Herbert Spencer in 1864, and Darwin later borrowed it. Many people take it to mean that the strongest win. The evidence says otherwise. Which feature is fit depends on the place and the year. A deep beak is fit in a drought. A small beak is fit after heavy rain. Being small, slow or blind can be fit. Evolution also has no ladder with humans at the top. Every species alive today has an equally long history, going back to the same first cells. A bacterium, a moss, a crow and a human are all well adapted to the lives they lead. Humans are one animal species among millions, still evolving like the rest.
- e.g. In the Drought Year Test, the roti tongs collect enough food in the drought year. The tweezers collect enough in the wet year. Neither beak is the better beak everywhere.
- e.g. Blind catfish in Kerala's wells have no working eyes. In total darkness, eyes cost energy and bring nothing, so fish without them left more young.
- e.g. Penguins descend from birds who flew. Their wings became flippers, which are better for swimming after fish.
- e.g. Bacteria have lived on Earth for more than 3.5 billion years. They live in more places than any animal.

## Mostly agreed
- Members of every population vary, and many variations are inherited. *How we know:* Anyone can measure the variation in a handful of seeds or a class of children. Plant and animal breeders have used inherited variation for thousands of years, and DNA now shows the differences directly.
- Natural selection changes populations, and it has been measured as it happens. *How we know:* Rosemary and Peter Grant measured finch beaks on Daphne Major for 40 years. They saw beaks change after droughts and rains. Bacteria become resistant to antibiotics, and mosquitoes to poisons, within years.
- New variations begin as chance mutations. They do not appear because they are needed. *How we know:* In 1952 Esther and Joshua Lederberg tested bacteria with an antibiotic. The bacteria able to survive were present before the antibiotic was added.
- All living beings share one ancestor, and new species form when populations split. *How we know:* All life uses the same DNA code. Family trees built from DNA match those built from fossils and bodies. Islands and separated hilltops hold close relatives that differ from each other.
- Mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from bacteria that once lived free. *How we know:* Both have their own loop of DNA, both multiply by splitting, and their DNA matches that of living bacteria.
- A feature gained during one lifetime, such as strong muscles or a scar, is not passed on through the genes. *How we know:* In the 1880s August Weismann cut the tails off mice for many generations. Every new litter was born with tails of normal length.

## How we found out
**Rosemary Grant and Peter Grant, biologists born in Britain, with their student Peter Boag, 1973 to 2012. The drought was in 1977..** In 1859 Charles Darwin wrote that natural selection works too slowly for anyone to watch. For more than a hundred years, most scientists agreed. In 1973 Rosemary and Peter Grant began work on Daphne Major, a tiny island in the Galápagos. They caught the finches, put coloured rings on their legs and measured every beak. In 1977 almost no rain fell. The small, soft seeds ran out, and only large, hard seeds were left. Of about 1,200 medium ground finches, fewer than one in five lived. The Grants and their student Peter Boag compared the survivors with the birds who had died. The survivors' beaks were a little deeper on average, less than a millimetre, but deep enough to crack the hard seeds. Their chicks inherited deeper beaks. After heavy rains in 1983, small seeds were plentiful, and birds with smaller beaks did better. The Grants kept measuring for 40 years. Natural selection had been seen and measured, one season at a time.  
*What people believed before:* That natural selection is always too slow to see in one human lifetime. The Grants measured a change in the average beak after a single dry year.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Animals change their bodies by effort, and pass the change to their young.** (Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, a French naturalist, in 1809; *rejected*)  
  For: It seems to fit what we see. A giraffe's long neck looks like the result of stretching for leaves over many generations.  
  Against: A weightlifter's child is not born with big muscles. Weismann's mice were born with tails after many generations of cut tails. The genes in egg and sperm cells are not rewritten by exercise.
- **Natural selection is always too slow for a person to see.** (Charles Darwin and most scientists until the 1970s; *rejected*)  
  For: Big changes, such as fins turning into legs, do take millions of years. Nobody has watched one of those happen.  
  Against: The Grants measured a change in finch beaks after one drought year. Bacteria in laboratories and insects on farms change within a few years.
- **The biggest steps in evolution came from living beings joining together, not from small mutations.** (Lynn Margulis, in books up to 2002; *minority*)  
  For: The first complex cell came from two beings joining. So did the first plant cell. Lichens and corals are also joined beings.  
  Against: Most biologists accept those cases but say they are rare. They find that most new species arise from mutations and selection in separated groups, with no joining.
- **Most changes in DNA neither help nor harm, and they spread by chance alone.** (Motoo Kimura, a Japanese biologist, in 1968; *accepted*)  
  For: When scientists compare the DNA of related species, most differences sit where they change nothing in the body. Chance explains those well.  
  Against: Features such as beaks, eyes and wings fit their uses too well for chance alone. Selection is still needed to explain adaptations. Scientists argue about the share of each.
- **Living beings steer their own evolution through what they choose and build.** (Mary Jane West-Eberhard, John Odling-Smee, Kevin Laland and others; *debated*)  
  For: Herding cattle made a milk-digesting mutation useful in humans. Beavers, earthworms and termites live in places their ancestors built.  
  Against: Many biologists reply that choosing and building are inherited features too, already explained by natural selection. They see no need for a new idea.

## Roots
We have learned how natural selection fits living beings to their places, how species split, and how partners join. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Bean Generations Test, no bean changed colour. Yet after three hunts, one group was mostly dark and the other mostly pale. Each hunt removed the beans that were easy to see, and only survivors left young. So a group of living beings can change over generations while no member changes.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'the camel adapted to the desert', as if each camel reshaped their own body to suit the sand. That way of speaking hides where the change happens. A camel does not change in her own lifetime. The change happens from one generation to the next, depending on which members of the group go on to have young.  
*Evidence:* On the dark cloth, the count of dark beans rose from 15 to about 28 in three hunts. On the pale cloth the count fell towards 0. Every bean kept the colour they began with. On Daphne Major, the average finch beak grew deeper after the 1977 drought, because shallow-beaked birds died. Each living bird kept the beak they had.

- *who am i:* Every one of your ancestors lived long enough to have a child, all the way back to the first cells. Inside your cells live mitochondria, whose ancestors were free bacteria.
- *what is life:* Life makes more young than can survive, and makes each one a little different. Those differences are how life comes to fit new places.
- *what is this world:* Each place shapes the living beings in it, and they also shape the place and each other. Over many generations, tigers caught the slower deer, so deer became faster. In the same way, deer escaped the noisier tigers, so tigers became better at creeping up quietly.

All living beings descend from one ancestor. Chance mutations make each family line a little different, and different places keep different variations. In this way one species becomes two, and two become many. Sometimes two different kinds of living being join together instead. A bacterium and a larger cell did this long ago, and together they became the first complex cell. So life has both branched apart into new species and joined together into new kinds of cell.

- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), final paragraph:** Darwin describes a tangled bank full of plants, birds, insects and worms, all depending on each other. His last words are: 'from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved.' (This is the unit's science in its first author's words: one beginning, many forms, and the change still going on.)
- **Udana 1.1, a discourse of the Buddha on dependent arising:** “Iti imasmiṁ sati idaṁ hoti, imassuppādā idaṁ uppajjati” When this is, that is. From the arising of this comes the arising of that. (No species has their shape by themselves. A cactus has spines because of the dry places where cacti lived. A chital has an alarm call because tigers hunt chital. Each feature depends on the place and on the other beings in it.)
- **Peter Kropotkin, Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902):** Kropotkin watched animals through Siberian winters. He wrote that in harsh places, the animals who help each other are the ones who survive best. (Lynn Margulis later showed that helping can go as far as two beings becoming one cell. Cooperation is one of the things natural selection keeps.)
- Science (settled): All living beings on Earth share one ancestor.
- Science (settled): Mitochondria descend from bacteria that joined a larger cell more than 1.5 billion years ago.
- Science (debated): The choices and behaviour of living beings often change first. Genes that suit the new way of life become common afterwards.
- Other view, Jacques Monod, a French biologist, in Chance and Necessity (1970): Monod wrote that every new feature of life begins as a chance mistake in DNA, kept or removed by selection. He saw no plan, no purpose and no oneness behind it. In his view humans appeared by chance in a universe that has no interest in them.
- Practice, The unbroken line (3 min): Let's sit still and look at one hand. Your hand was built from instructions you got from your parents. They got theirs from their parents. Breathe in slowly, and think of one generation back. Breathe out. With each breath, go back one more generation. Every one of them lived long enough to have a child. Not one link is missing, all the way back to the first cells. Now look out of the window at any bird or plant. Their line is unbroken too, and far back it joins yours.
- Ask: No bean changed, yet the group changed. Where exactly did the change happen?
- Ask: Your line of ancestors and a crow's line meet somewhere far back. What does that make you and the crow?
- Ask: Your cells hold partners that were once free bacteria. Where do 'you' end and 'they' begin?
- Ask: If tigers shaped the deer and deer shaped the tigers, can either be understood alone?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* In the Bean Generations Test, no bean changed colour, yet the group changed. So what is it that evolves: one living being, or a population? (hint: Think about what you counted. Could you have seen any change by watching a single bean?)
- *deep:* A mutation is a copying mistake. Can a mistake be a good thing? (hint: Think about the single dark bean in One New Bean. What would have happened on the dark cloth with no new bean at all?)
- *deep:* A sholakili chick always belongs to the same species as her parents. So when did one species become two? (hint: Think about a child growing taller. On which day did she stop being short?)
- *big:* The mitochondria in your cells descend from free bacteria. Are you one living being, or many living together? (hint: Your mitochondria have their own DNA and split on their own. But neither you nor they can live apart.)
- *big:* If the place decides which features help, can any species be called better than another? (hint: Remember which beak did well in the drought year and which did well in the wet year.)
- *big:* Mosquitoes kept up with our poisons within a few years. A bustard cannot keep up with power lines. Why can some species keep up with fast change and others not? (hint: Compare how many young each has, and how long a generation takes.)

## Story: What the Dry Year Left
*Thenmozhi, Paati, Murugesan Mama and their fields are made up, and so are the counts. These things are real: the Kolli Hills are in Namakkal district in Tamil Nadu, and the road up has seventy hairpin bends. Farmers there grow ragi and other millets, and many keep their own mixed seed. Storing seed in a clay pot with ash and dried neem leaves is an old practice. In a mixed crop, plants that ripen early and root deeply are likeliest to give grain in a dry year.*

Every October, Thenmozhi rode the bus up seventy hairpin bends to her grandmother's village in the Kolli Hills. Paati grew ragi on a slope behind the house. Thenmozhi thought it was the untidiest field on the whole hill.

Next door, Murugesan Mama's ragi stood like children at a school assembly. Every plant was the same height, and every head of grain turned brown in the same week. He bought his seed each year in a sealed packet from a shop in Namakkal.

Paati's field had tall plants and short ones. Some heads were already brown while others were still green. Some heads were closed like fists and some were open like fingers. Paati had never bought a seed in her life. She kept her own, as her mother had, in a clay pot with ash and dried neem leaves.

"Why don't you sow Mama's seed?" Thenmozhi asked. "His field looks so neat."

"Neat is nice," said Paati, "in a good year."

The year Thenmozhi turned ten, the rain came in June and then stopped. July was dry. August was dry. When her bus came round the last bend in October, the hill was the wrong colour.

Murugesan Mama's field was straw from end to end. Every plant was still the same height. Every plant had died in the same week. He stood at the edge with his hands behind his back and said nothing.

Paati's field looked almost as bad. Most of her plants were straw too. But here and there a plant was still standing, green at the foot, with a small head of grain. Thenmozhi walked every row and counted them. There were thirty-one, in a field that had held thousands.

She looked at them closely. Nearly all were short plants, the ones she had thought ugly. Their heads were small, and they had ripened early.

"A whole field," said Thenmozhi, "for two handfuls."

Paati did not seem upset. She cut the thirty-one heads herself, one at a time, and tied them in a separate cloth. That evening she rubbed out the grain. Thenmozhi fetched the cooking pot.

"Not that pot," said Paati. "These we do not eat."

"But we have so little!"

"These are the ones who managed," said Paati. She poured the grain into the clay seed pot and pressed the lid down.

Before she left, Thenmozhi pulled up two plants from the field. One was a tall dead plant and one was a short living plant. The tall plant's roots were a shallow mat, no longer than her hand. The short plant's roots went down longer than her arm. She turned them over and over, and did not know what to make of them.

The next June, Paati sowed the grain from the thirty-one plants at the top of the slope. She sowed her old mixed seed lower down. The rain was fair that year and the year after, and Thenmozhi forgot about the roots.

When she was twelve, the rain failed again. Her bus came round the last bend, and Murugesan Mama's new packet of seed had become straw, exactly as before. Paati's lower field was mostly straw. But at the top of the slope the ragi stood. The plants were thin and their heads were small, but they were alive. Thenmozhi began to count them and gave up after four hundred.

She sat down on the stone bund at the edge of the field. Paati had not carried more water up the slope. Nobody could, on that hill. The plants at the top had not grown deep roots because the year was dry. They were the children of the thirty-one, and the thirty-one had been deep-rooted and quick from the day they sprouted.

Then she saw the whole of it. The first dry year had gone through the mixed field and removed every plant that needed more water than there was. It had left the few who could do with less. Their grain had grown into plants like themselves. Now there were hundreds.

"Paati," she said, "you did not change the ragi. The dry year chose. You only kept what the dry year left."

Paati laughed. "My mother said the same thing in fewer words. She said the bad year is the real seed keeper."

That evening Thenmozhi noticed a second clay pot, behind the first. "What is in that one?"

"The tall ones," said Paati. "And the late ones."

"But they die in a dry year! Why keep them?"

"In a dry year, yes. Fifteen years ago it rained for forty days without a stop. The short, early ones ripened in the rain, and their grain rotted on the stalk. The tall, late ones fed this house. I do not know which year is coming, kanna. So I keep some of everyone."

Thenmozhi went out and looked at the lower field for a long time. The field was not untidy. Every kind of plant there had come through some year that had finished off the others.

In the morning she asked for a spoonful of grain from each pot, to take down the seventy bends to her balcony in Salem.

**For the parent:** For the parent: this is natural selection in a field. The ragi plants varied in height, roots and ripening time, and they passed these features to their seed. The drought removed the plants that needed more water. The survivors became the parents, so the next crop was mostly deep-rooted and early. No plant changed during the dry year. The second pot shows that no feature is best in every year. A population with more variation can come through more kinds of trouble. Murugesan Mama's plants were all alike, so one bad year took them all.

- Ask: Why did all of Murugesan Mama's plants die in the same week?
- Ask: Thenmozhi said the dry year chose. What did she mean?
- Ask: Did any ragi plant grow deeper roots because the year was dry? How do you know?
- Ask: Why does Paati keep the seed of tall, late plants that die in a dry year?
- Ask: Which is safer for a farmer over twenty years: a field where all plants are alike, or a mixed field? Give your reasons.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Bean Generations Test: does one hunter change two groups in the same way? (40 min)
- **question:** Two groups of beans start exactly the same, with 15 dark beans and 15 pale beans. One group lives on a dark cloth and one on a pale cloth. The same hunter hunts both for three generations. Do the two groups end up the same?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. After three hunts, how many of the 30 beans on the dark cloth will be dark? How many on the pale cloth? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both groups start with 15 dark and 15 pale beans. The same hunter hunts both, and catches exactly 15 beans in every hunt. The beans are scattered over the same area, in the same light. Every survivor gets one young bean of the same colour. Only one thing changes: the colour of the cloth.
- **safety:** Dry beans are a choking risk for children under three. Keep them off the floor afterwards.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows, for generations 1, 2, 3 and 4. For each cloth, write the number of dark beans and pale beans at the start of each generation. The two numbers must add up to 30.
- **results table:** Generation | Dark cloth: dark beans, pale beans | Pale cloth: dark beans, pale beans
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the two kinds of beans and the two cloths. 2. Explain the rules of the hunt. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason for each cloth, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* These beans are a population of animals, and you are their predator. You will hunt them for three generations on each cloth. After three hunts, how many of the 30 beans on the dark cloth will be dark? How many on the pale cloth? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the beans and the two cloths. Guess how many dark beans each cloth will have at the end. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think the two cloths will end the same, or end differently? (good: Pale beans are easy to see on the dark cloth, so I will catch more of them.; I pick without looking at colour, so they will stay half and half.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say the numbers will stay at 15 and 15. Their reason is that the hunter is not trying to choose a colour.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the dark cloth on a bed or the floor, about 1 metre wide. Leave it a little rumpled. 2. Count out 15 dark and 15 pale beans. 3. Draw the results table and write 15 and 15 for generation 1. 4. The hunter turns away. Scatter the 30 beans all over the cloth.  
  *Parent says:* This is generation 1: fifteen dark and fifteen pale. Turn away while I scatter them. Do not peek.  
  *Child mode:* Spread the dark cloth. Count 15 dark and 15 pale beans. Turn away while a grown-up scatters them.
  - Note: Folds and a towel's loops give the beans somewhere to hide, as grass does for a real animal. A cloth pulled perfectly flat makes every bean easy to see.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Say 'go'. The hunter picks up beans one at a time with one hand, and drops each into the bowl. 2. Count aloud and say 'stop' at 15. 3. Collect the 15 survivors from the cloth and sort them by colour. 4. Add one new bean of the same colour for each survivor. 5. Write the new numbers for generation 2.  
  *Parent says:* Go! Take the first bean you see each time, as fast as you can. Stop at fifteen. Now let's count who survived. Each survivor has one young of the same colour.  
  *Child mode:* Pick up 15 beans, one at a time, fast. Take the first bean you see. Then count the survivors.
  - Note: The beans caught are 'eaten' and go back to the spare bowls. Check that the new generation adds up to 30.
  - Check: In the Bean Generations Test, each surviving bean gets one young bean of the same colour. What does this stand for? → **Young inherit features from their parents**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The hunter turns away. Scatter generation 2. 2. Hunt 15 again, count the survivors, add their young and write generation 3. 3. Do the same once more and write generation 4.  
  *Parent says:* Generation 2 is on the cloth. Turn away, then hunt fifteen again. We will do this for generation 3 as well.  
  *Child mode:* Hunt 15 beans again. Count the survivors and add their young. Then hunt once more.
  - Note: If the numbers stay close to 15 and 15, the beans are too easy to see. Rumple the cloth more, spread the beans wider and ask the hunter to go faster.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put all the beans back in their bowls. 2. Spread the pale cloth in the same place. 3. Start again with 15 dark and 15 pale beans. 4. Run three hunts exactly as before, and write generations 2, 3 and 4 in the second column.  
  *Parent says:* Now the same hunter meets the same starting group in a different place. Fifteen dark and fifteen pale again, on the pale cloth.  
  *Child mode:* Now use the pale cloth. Start with 15 and 15 again. Hunt three times and write the numbers.
  - Note: Keep everything else the same: the area, the light, the speed and the rule of 15.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw a line chart. Put generations 1 to 4 along the bottom and the number of dark beans, 0 to 30, up the side. 2. Plot the dark cloth as one line and the pale cloth as another. 3. Label each line.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw both groups on one chart. Each line shows the number of dark beans in one group. Both lines start at fifteen.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line chart of the dark beans. Draw one line for each cloth. Both start at 15.
  - Note: A line chart suits these results because each generation follows from the one before.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table and the chart together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your two lines. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Did any bean change colour during the test?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Did any single bean change colour? Then how did the group change? (good: No bean changed.; The easy-to-see beans were caught, and only the survivors had young.; The group changed because its members were replaced.)
  - Ask: The hunter was the same on both cloths. Why did the two groups go opposite ways? (good: The place was different.; On each cloth a different colour was hard to see.)
  - Note: In most homes the dark cloth ends with 24 to 30 dark beans. The pale cloth ends with 0 to 6. Write down what you really found. The reason: the hunter catches the beans she sees first, so the beans that match the cloth survive more often. Only survivors have young, and young inherit their parent's colour. So the matching colour becomes more common in each generation. This is natural selection. No bean changed, and the hunter never tried to choose a colour. The model leaves three things out. Real young are never exact copies of a parent. New variations appear through mutation. And real prey also run, hide and learn.
  - Think: What would the two groups look like if they stayed apart for a thousand generations?
### e2 · The Drought Year Test: which beak finds enough food when the seeds change? (30 min)
- **question:** A finch with a small beak and a finch with a big beak live on the same island. In a wet year there are many small seeds. In a drought, almost only big seeds are left. Does the same beak find enough food in both years?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which beak will collect enough seeds in the wet year? Which in the drought year? Is one beak better in both? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same person uses both beaks, with the same hand. Every try lasts 30 seconds. Seeds are lifted one at a time from the plate into a bowl that stands 30 centimetres away. Each beak is tried 3 times on each kind of seed, because one try can be a fluke. Only two things change: the beak and the kind of seed.
- **record:** Draw a table with one row for the tweezers and one row for the tongs. Write the three tries and the average for rice, then for groundnuts. Then write how many seeds each beak collected from the wet year plate and from the drought year plate.
- **results table:** Beak | Rice grains in 30 seconds: try 1, try 2, try 3, average | Groundnuts in 30 seconds: try 1, try 2, try 3, average | Wet year plate: seeds collected | Drought year plate: seeds collected
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the tweezers, the tongs, the rice and the groundnuts. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* The tweezers are a small beak and the tongs are a big beak. In a wet year there are many small seeds. In a drought, almost only big seeds are left. Which beak will find enough food in each year? Is one beak better in both? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the two beaks and the two seeds. Which beak is better in each year? Say why.
  - Note: Many children say the big beak is better at everything. Write the guess down and let the test answer it.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 40 rice grains on the plate. Stand the bowl 30 centimetres away. 2. Your child lifts grains one at a time with the tweezers for 30 seconds. Count the grains in the bowl. 3. Do this 3 times, then 3 times with the tongs. 4. Change the rice for 20 groundnuts and try each beak 3 times again. 5. Work out the four averages.  
  *Parent says:* One seed at a time, from the plate to the bowl. You have thirty seconds. Ready, go! We will try each beak three times on each seed.  
  *Child mode:* Lift seeds one at a time for 30 seconds. Count them. Try each beak 3 times on each seed.
  - Note: To find an average, add the three counts and divide by 3. Put the seeds back on the plate after every try. A seed that falls outside the bowl does not count.
  - Check: In the Drought Year Test, why do you try each beak 3 times on each seed? → **One try can be a lucky or unlucky fluke**
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make the wet year plate: 60 rice grains and 2 groundnuts, mixed. Give each beak 30 seconds. 2. Make the drought year plate: 3 rice grains and 20 groundnuts, mixed. Give each beak 30 seconds. 3. A bird needs 10 seeds to live. Write 'lives' or 'dies' for each beak in each year.  
  *Parent says:* This plate is a wet year. This plate is a drought year. A bird must collect ten seeds in thirty seconds to live. Let's see who lives in each year.  
  *Child mode:* Try each beak on the wet year plate. Then try the drought year plate. Ten seeds means the bird lives.
  - Note: If both beaks collect 10 on a plate, raise the number a bird needs to 12. Say so in your table. Write what really happens.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 2. Ask what the next generation of beaks would look like after each year. 3. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. If only the survivors have chicks, what beaks will the chicks have after a drought? What about after a wet year?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Is one beak the better beak? (good: No. Each is better in one kind of year.; It depends on which seeds there are.)
  - Ask: After many drought years in a row, what would most beaks on the island be like? (good: Big, because the big-beaked birds lived and had chicks.; Deep beaks would become common.)
  - Note: In most homes the tweezers lift 12 to 20 rice grains and the tongs only 3 to 6. With groundnuts the result reverses. So the small beak lives in the wet year and the big beak lives in the drought. The reason: a beak is an adaptation to a food, and which beak is fit depends on the food that year. On Daphne Major in 1977, a drought left only large, hard seeds. Finches with deeper beaks could crack them, and survived more often. After heavy rain in 1983, small seeds were plentiful and smaller beaks did better. Our model is not exact. A real big beak cracks hard seeds, while the tongs only grip big ones.
  - Think: An island has drought years and wet years in turn. Why might it be useful for the finch population to keep both kinds of beak?

### a1 · Count the Seeds in One Bhindi: how many young could one plant have? (12 min)

### a2 · One New Bean: can a single new variation spread? (15 min)
- **record:** For each run, write the number of dark beans at the start of generations 1 to 5.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Spread the dark cloth as in the Bean Generations Test. 2. Count 29 pale beans and 1 dark bean. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This group is all pale, on a dark cloth. One bean has a new mutation and is dark. After four hunts, how many dark beans will there be? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* There are 29 pale beans and 1 dark bean. Guess the number of dark beans after four hunts. Say why.
  - Note: The rules are the same as in the Bean Generations Test. The hunter turns away, then catches 15 beans, one at a time. Each survivor gets one young of the same colour.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Scatter the beans and hunt 15. 2. Count the surviving dark beans and double them. Fill up with pale young until there are 30. 3. Do four hunts and write the number of dark beans each time.

### Natural Selection and Adaptations (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these must be true for natural selection to work?  
   Options: Members of a population differ from each other / All members are exactly alike / Every young one survives / Parents teach their young  
   **Answer:** Members of a population differ from each other. *Why:* Selection needs differences to select among. If all were alike, the survivors would be no different from the rest. Choosing 'parents teach' mixes up learning with inherited features.
2. (fill) An inherited feature that helps a living being survive in their place is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['adaptation']. *Why:* An adaptation solves a problem set by the place, such as heat, cold or a predator.

   **Answer:** A cactus's spines → losing water in dry air; A camel's wide feet → sinking into soft sand; A lotus's air channels → no air in pond mud; A deodar's sloping branches → heavy snow; A yak's thick coat → cold on high mountains. *Why:* Each feature answers one problem of the place.    **Answer:** The hunter saw the pale beans more easily and caught more of them. More dark beans survived. Only survivors had young, and the young had their parent's colour.    **Answer:** ['Members of a population are born a little different', 'More young are born than can survive', 'Those whose features suit the place survive more often', 'Their features become more common in the next generations']. *Why:* Variation comes first.    **Answer:** Mosquitoes have a new generation every few weeks. *Why:* Selection works generation by generation. A mosquito generation takes weeks and an elephant generation takes about 20 years. Elephants do vary, so 'do not vary' is wrong.
7. (sort) Sort these pairs: do both partners gain, or does one gain while the other is harmed?  
   **Answer:** bee and pumpkin flower → Both gain; tick and dog → One gains, one is harmed; coral animal and coral algae → Both gain; koel and the crow who raises her chick → One gains, one is harmed; fig tree and fig wasp → Both gain; chana plant and root bacteria → Both gain. *Why:* The bee, the coral and the fig each give and get. A tick takes blood and gives nothing. A koel leaves her egg for a crow to raise, and the crow's own chicks may lose out.
8. (write) Hunters killed elephants with tusks for 15 years. Afterwards, more females were born without tusks. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Some females had no tusks because of an inherited variation. Hunters killed elephants with tusks, so tuskless females survived more often. They had calves, and their daughters often inherited no tusks. So the share of tuskless females went up.. *Why:* A strong answer says the tuskless variation was already there, and the hunting changed who survived to have calves. No elephant lost her tusks to escape hunters.
9. (mcq) A helpful mutation appears in one animal. Is the mutation certain to spread through the population?  
   Options: No, bad luck may kill that animal / Yes, always / Only if the animal wants that / Only in plants  
   **Answer:** No, bad luck may kill that animal. *Why:* While a variation is rare, chance matters a great deal. The one animal who carries the mutation may be eaten before having young, as the single dark bean sometimes is.
10. (truefalse) True or false: bacteria alive today are less evolved than humans.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every species alive today has an equally long history. Bacteria have been adapting for more than 3.5 billion years, and live in more places than any animal.
11. (draw) Draw a large cell taking in a small bacterium. Show the two membranes around the bacterium afterwards.  
   **Answer:** Three steps. The large cell's membrane folds in around the bacterium. The fold pinches off. The bacterium now sits inside with their own membrane, plus a second one from the large cell.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows where the second membrane came from: the outer skin of the larger cell.
12. (fill) When two species change over generations in answer to each other, scientists call it ____.  
   **Answer:** ['coevolution']. *Why:* Deer and tigers, figs and fig wasps, and flowers and bees have each shaped the other.
13. (odd) In three of these pairs, both partners gain. Which pair is the odd one out?  
   Options: Fungus and alga in a lichen / Coral animal and coral algae / Mosquito and human / Chana plant and root bacteria  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A mosquito takes blood and may pass on disease, so the human loses. In the other three pairs, each partner gives the other something useful.
14. (mcq) A weed in rice fields looks almost exactly like young rice. Farmers weed by hand. Why did this look spread?  
   Options: Farmers pulled out the weeds that looked different / The weed wanted to hide / Rice plants taught the weed / Water changed the weed's shape  
   **Answer:** Farmers pulled out the weeds that looked different. *Why:* Weeds that looked unlike rice were seen and pulled out. Weeds that happened to look like rice survived and set seed. The farmers' hands did the selecting, without meaning to.
15. (write) Some fish that live in dark wells have no eyes. Is losing eyes a step backwards? Give your reasons.  
   **Answer:** No. In total darkness eyes are useless, and they cost energy to build. Fish without eyes had more energy for other things and left more young. The fish became better fitted to the dark.. *Why:* A strong answer says that 'fit' depends on the place, and that evolution has no forwards or backwards.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which list gives the things natural selection needs?  
   Options: Variation, inheritance and more young than can survive / Strength, speed and size / Sunlight, water and soil / Wanting, trying and learning  
   **Answer:** Variation, inheritance and more young than can survive. *Why:* Selection needs differences that are inherited, and more young than the place can support. Choosing 'wanting, trying and learning' is the old idea that effort changes what is inherited.
2. (mcq) A beetle population goes from mostly green to mostly brown in 20 generations. What changed?  
   Options: The share of brown beetles in the population / Each green beetle slowly turned brown / The beetles chose a new colour / The sun faded them  
   **Answer:** The share of brown beetles in the population. *Why:* More brown beetles survived and had young in each generation, so their share grew. No single beetle changed colour.
3. (mcq) Why does a cactus have spines where most plants have flat leaves?  
   Options: Spines lose far less water than flat leaves / Spines drink the rain / Spines make more food / Spines feel the wind  
   **Answer:** Spines lose far less water than flat leaves. *Why:* Water escapes from a leaf's surface, and a spine has very little surface. The green stem makes the food. Spines also keep thirsty animals away.
4. (mcq) A plant has large floating leaves and stalks full of air channels. Where does this plant most likely live?  
   Options: In a pond / In a desert / On a snowy mountain / On a sand dune  
   **Answer:** In a pond. *Why:* Floating leaves reach the light on water, and air channels carry air to roots in airless mud. Both are adaptations to a wet place.
5. (mcq) A chital deer has eyes at the sides of the head. What is the main gain?  
   Options: Seeing danger from almost all around / Judging the distance for a leap onto prey / Seeing in the dark / Keeping out sand  
   **Answer:** Seeing danger from almost all around. *Why:* Side eyes give prey a very wide view. Forward eyes, which judge distance, are found in predators such as tigers and owls.
6. (mcq) Over many generations, deer become faster. What is likely to happen to the tigers who hunt them?  
   Options: Faster or stealthier tigers leave more young / Tigers stay the same for ever / Tigers choose to eat grass / Each tiger trains her legs for her cubs  
   **Answer:** Faster or stealthier tigers leave more young. *Why:* Only the tigers who can still catch deer eat well and raise cubs. So the prey select the predator, as the predator selects the prey. This is coevolution.
7. (mcq) When does a helpful mutation first appear?  
   Options: By chance, before being useful / As soon as the place changes / When the animal tries hard / When parents teach their young  
   **Answer:** By chance, before being useful. *Why:* A mutation is a chance copying mistake. It is already present when conditions change, and selection then spreads it.
8. (mcq) What usually has to happen first for one species to split into two?  
   Options: Two groups are kept apart for long / One animal changes shape / Food runs out everywhere / A predator eats every member  
   **Answer:** Two groups are kept apart for long. *Why:* While groups can mix, they stay one species. A barrier lets each group change in their own way until they can no longer breed together.
9. (mcq) Which fact supports the idea that mitochondria were once free bacteria?  
   Options: They have their own bacteria-like DNA / They are found only in plants / They are made of fat / They are the biggest part of a cell  
   **Answer:** They have their own bacteria-like DNA. *Why:* Mitochondria carry a loop of DNA that matches living bacteria, and they multiply by splitting. They are found in animals, plants and fungi.
10. (mcq) After one year of drought, the finches on an island had deeper beaks on average. Why?  
   Options: More deep-beaked birds survived and had chicks / Each finch stretched their beak on hard seeds / Dry air makes beaks swell / The birds wanted bigger beaks  
   **Answer:** More deep-beaked birds survived and had chicks. *Why:* Birds with shallow beaks could not crack the hard seeds, and many starved. The survivors passed deeper beaks to their chicks. No bird's own beak grew.
11. (mcq) In biology, what does 'fittest' mean in the phrase 'survival of the fittest'?  
   Options: Best suited to the place / Strongest / Fastest / Biggest  
   **Answer:** Best suited to the place. *Why:* Fit means fitting the place. A small beak is fittest in a wet year and a deep beak in a drought. Strength is only sometimes what suits a place.
12. (mcq) Why can mosquitoes adapt to a poison faster than bustards adapt to power lines?  
   Options: Mosquitoes have many young, a few weeks apart / Mosquitoes think faster / Bustards do not vary / Power lines are stronger than poison  
   **Answer:** Mosquitoes have many young, a few weeks apart. *Why:* Selection needs many young to select among, over many generations. A mosquito lays hundreds of eggs and a generation takes weeks. A bustard lays one egg a year.

## Applications
- Doctors ask you to finish a full course of antibiotics, and not to take them for colds. Every use of an antibiotic selects for bacteria that can survive that antibiotic.
- A new flu vaccine is made every year, because flu viruses mutate. The ones our bodies do not recognise spread.
- Farmers are advised to change the insect poison they use from season to season. Then no one poison selects resistant insects for long.
- Plant breeders in India used selective breeding to make rice that survives flooding, such as Swarna-Sub1. They also made rice that copes with drought, such as Sahbhagi Dhan.
- Seed banks, such as the National Gene Bank in New Delhi, store many thousands of crop varieties. They keep the variation that future breeding and selection will need.
- Wildlife corridors join separated forests, so that elephants and tigers from different groups can meet and mix their genes.
- Engineers copy natural selection in computers. A program makes many varied designs, keeps the best and varies them again. NASA flew a spacecraft antenna designed this way in 2006.

## Spotted around us
- Street dogs in any Indian town differ in size, coat and ears. That is variation within one species.
- Pigeons on a building ledge come in grey, white, brown and speckled. City pigeons descend from birds that people once bred.
- Cabbage, cauliflower and knol khol lie side by side at the vegetable shop. Farmers bred all three from one wild species.
- Mosquito coils and sprays work less well in some places than they did years ago. The mosquitoes there descend from survivors.
- A peepal or banyan tree has figs with tiny holes. Each kind of fig has a partner wasp, small enough to crawl inside.
- Grey-green lichen patches grow on old tree trunks and rocks in hill stations such as Ooty, Munnar and Shillong. Each patch is a fungus and an alga living as one.
- A cactus or an aloe in a pot on the balcony shows desert adaptations: thick parts that store water, and a waxy skin.
- Lotus leaves float on temple tanks from Madurai to Puri. Cut a lotus stalk, sold as a vegetable, and you see the air channels.
- In paddy fields, a grass weed grows that looks very like young rice. Hand weeding selected the look-alikes.

## Open questions
- **Can a new species form without any barrier, in the middle of the parent population?** A few cases in fish and insects suggest that this can happen. Scientists disagree about how often this happens.
- **Which large cell took in the bacterium that became the mitochondrion, and how?** DNA points to a group of microbes called Asgard archaea, first found in 2015. One was grown in a laboratory in 2020, after twelve years of work. How the joining happened is still argued over.
- **If life on Earth began again, would evolution give similar animals and plants?** Some scientists think chance events would send life a different way each time. Others point out that eyes and wings evolved many times over. Nobody can rerun the whole experiment.
- **How much of the difference between species is due to selection, and how much to chance?** Most changes in DNA seem to make no difference to survival. Scientists are still measuring what share of evolution selection explains.
- **How often does a new habit come first, with the genes changing afterwards?** There are good examples, such as milk drinking in humans. Scientists disagree about whether this is common or rare.

## Connected maths
- In Count the Seeds in One Bhindi, one pod held 60 seeds, and one plant gives about 20 pods. How many seeds does one plant make? If every seed grew and did the same, how many seeds would there be the next year? **1,200 seeds from one plant, and 1,440,000 seeds the next year.**. 60 × 20 = 1,200 seeds from one plant. Suppose all 1,200 grew into plants that each made 1,200 seeds. There would be 1,200 × 1,200 = 1,440,000 seeds. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- On the dark cloth, the group always has 30 beans. It had 15 dark beans in generation 1 and 20 in generation 2. What fraction of the group was dark each time? **One half in generation 1, and two thirds in generation 2.**. 15 out of 30 is 15 ÷ 30 = one half. 20 out of 30 is 20 ÷ 30 = two thirds. So the dark share grew from one half to two thirds in one hunt. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L5`)
- On the pale cloth, generation 2 had 22 pale beans out of 30. What percentage of the group was pale? Round to the nearest whole number. **About 73 per cent.**. 22 ÷ 30 = 0.733. Multiply by 100 to get 73.3. So about 73 per cent of the group was pale. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- On the dark cloth, the number of dark beans went 15, 20, 26, 28. The rise was 5, then 6, then only 2. Why did the rise slow down, when the hunter did not change? **By generation 3 only 4 pale beans were left. So the hunter had to catch mostly dark beans to reach 15.**. In generation 3 there were 26 dark and 4 pale beans. Even if the hunter caught all 4 pale beans, 11 of the 15 caught had to be dark. With few pale beans left to remove, the dark share could not rise much more. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L5`)
- In One New Bean, the dark beans doubled after every hunt: 1, 2, 4, 8. If they keep doubling, after how many hunts would the dark beans pass the group size of 30? **After 5 hunts.**. Keep doubling: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. The fifth doubling gives 32, which is more than 30. So one new variation could fill a group of 30 within 5 generations. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In the 1977 drought on Daphne Major, fewer than 1 in 5 of about 1,200 medium ground finches survived. Take 180 as a round example. What fraction survived, and what percentage died? **3 in every 20 survived, which is 15 per cent. So 85 per cent died.**. 180 ÷ 1,200 = 18 ÷ 120 = 3 ÷ 20. As a percentage, 3 ÷ 20 = 15 per cent. 100 − 15 = 85, so 85 per cent died. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
