# Feelings: how they show in your body, and how we read them in others
`sci.health.feelings-and-wellbeing.L3`

**Summary.** When you feel scared, your heart beats faster before you have even thought about it. Why? In this lesson you will name feelings and find where you feel them in your body, test which feelings can be read from faces, and play a feelings charades game.

**Intro.** *Try this with someone at home. Make the most scared face you can, with your mouth closed. Then make a surprised face, mouth closed. Can they tell which is which? Most people find these two faces very hard to tell apart. So how do we ever know what someone else is feeling?* Every day you read faces without thinking about it. You can tell when a parent is tired, or when a friend is upset. In this lesson you test how good you really are at it. You play a face game, find out which feelings are easy to read and which get mixed up, and learn what a feeling does inside your body. You also find out what keeps your body and your feelings well, and why kindness and friendship matter to the body as well as the heart.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name at least 10 feelings and say where I feel some of them in my body.
1. I can explain how a strong feeling changes the body: the heart, the breath, the tummy and the face.
2. I can run a fair test of reading feelings from faces, and say which feelings were easy to read and which got mixed up.
3. I can explain why a face is only a clue to a feeling, and why asking is the surest way to know how someone feels.
4. I can say what keeps my body and my feelings well: sleep, food, water, play, friends and talking about feelings.
5. I can give examples of kindness, respect and friendship, and say what scientists found about how kindness affects the one who gives.
6. I can tell how Duchenne and Darwin studied faces, and which wrong idea about faces they showed was wrong.

## Terms
- **Emotion**: A strong feeling, such as joy, fear, anger or sadness, that changes your body and what you want to do. *e.g.* When a dog barks suddenly, fear makes your heart race and your body jump back.
- **Feeling**: What an emotion is like from the inside, for the person having it. People often use 'feeling' and 'emotion' to mean the same thing. *e.g.* Before a dance show, you may feel butterflies in your tummy. That is how nervousness feels from the inside.
- **Facial expression**: The shape your face makes when small muscles under the skin pull on it. Many expressions go with feelings. *e.g.* A frown pulls the eyebrows down and together.
- **Empathy**: Understanding what another person feels, and feeling some of it with them. *e.g.* Your friend drops her ice cream, and you feel a little sad too.
- **Well-being**: Being well in both body and feelings: healthy, rested, and mostly calm and happy, with people to turn to. *e.g.* A good night's sleep, a full tummy, an hour of play and a chat with a friend all add to your well-being.
- **Kindness**: Doing something good for someone because you care about how they feel, without wanting anything back. *e.g.* Sharing your umbrella with a classmate at the bus stop in the rain.
- **Respect**: Treating other people's bodies, feelings and choices as important, even when they are different from yours. *e.g.* Asking your little cousin 'Can I hug you?' and waiting for the answer.
- **Friendship**: A close bond between people who like each other, trust each other and care about each other's feelings. *e.g.* Two friends take turns choosing the game, so both of them get to pick.
- **Adrenaline**: A chemical messenger that two small glands above your kidneys pour into your blood when you are scared or excited. It makes your heart beat faster and gets your body ready to act. *e.g.* Adrenaline is why your heart thumps after a near fall on the stairs.
- **Duchenne smile**: A smile that lifts the cheeks and crinkles the skin at the outer corners of the eyes, as well as turning up the mouth. It is named after Guillaume Duchenne, a French doctor who studied face muscles. *e.g.* When someone laughs at a joke, little lines appear at the corners of their eyes.
- **Contagious**: Spreading from one person to another. Yawns, laughs and moods can be contagious, as well as some illnesses. *e.g.* One person in the room yawns, and soon two others yawn too.
- **Rasa**: In Indian dance and drama, the feeling a performance brings out in the people watching. The old book called the Natya Shastra describes eight rasas, and later writers added a ninth, peace. *e.g.* A Kathakali dancer from Kerala shows fear with wide eyes and trembling hands, and the audience feels it too.

## Explanations
### What a feeling is · *debated*
Everyone has feelings, but scientists still argue about what a feeling really is. They agree that an emotion involves the brain, the body and what you are thinking about, all at once. They disagree about whether a few feelings are built in from birth, the same in every person, or whether each person builds their feelings from body changes and what they have learned.
- e.g. A baby cries and startles at a loud noise long before learning any words for feelings.
- e.g. Some people cry when they are very happy, so tears do not always mean sadness.
- **Paul Ekman and many scientists who followed him:** There are a few basic emotions, such as happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise and disgust. Each comes with its own face, and people everywhere make and recognise those faces.
- **Lisa Feldman Barrett and other scientists:** There is no fixed face or body pattern for each feeling. People scowl when angry only some of the time. The brain builds each feeling from what the body is doing and what the person has learned, so feelings differ from place to place and person to person.
### A feeling happens in your whole body · *defined*
**Definition:** A strong emotion changes the body. When you are scared or excited, your body releases adrenaline, a chemical messenger. Adrenaline makes the heart beat faster and the breath quicker, and blood moves towards the big muscles. When you calm down, the heart and breath slow again.

These changes come before you choose them. If a cooker whistle blows behind you, you jump before you know what the noise was. Fear gets your body ready to run or fight. This is useful when there is real danger. The 'butterflies' in your tummy before a test are part of the same change, because blood moves away from the tummy towards the muscles. Happy feelings change the body too: you may feel light, warm or full of energy, and your face smiles.
- e.g. Your heart thumps after a near fall on the stairs.
- e.g. Your face goes hot and red when you feel shy or embarrassed.
- e.g. Your hands sweat a little before you go on stage.
### How a face shows a feeling · *defined*
**Definition:** Dozens of small muscles under the skin of the face pull on it to make expressions. Some move the eyebrows, some the eyelids and cheeks, and some the lips.

Different feelings move different parts. Surprise and fear both lift the eyebrows and open the eyes wide, which is why they are easy to mix up. In fear, the eyebrows are also pulled together and the lips stretch back. Anger pulls the eyebrows down and together. Disgust wrinkles the nose and lifts the upper lip. A real, happy smile usually lifts the cheeks and crinkles the corners of the eyes. A polite smile often moves only the mouth. But many people can make the eye crinkle on purpose, so even that is a clue, not a proof.
- e.g. Cover your mouth and frown at a mirror. Your eyebrows still show it.
- e.g. A Kathakali dancer shows nine feelings with the face, especially the eyes and eyebrows.
- e.g. Look at an old photo where someone smiled for the camera. Are their eye corners crinkled?
### Reading feelings is a guess, so we ask · *defined*
**Definition:** When we read a feeling from a face, we use a few clues, such as the eyebrows, eyes and mouth. Then we guess the rest from what is happening around us. Empathy means understanding what another person feels, and feeling some of it with them. It starts with this guess. Asking the person to check it makes the guess kind and correct.

A face can match more than one feeling. A wide-eyed face could be scared, surprised or excited. A frowning face could be angry, worried or just thinking hard. People also hide feelings, or show a polite face when they feel something else. So a good friend notices the clues and then asks: 'You look upset. Are you all right?' Asking also shows respect, because the other person gets to say what they feel instead of being told.
- e.g. A new girl in class looks cross on her first day, but she is really nervous.
- e.g. A man frowns at the newspaper. He is not angry. He just cannot read the small print without his glasses.
- e.g. A friend laughs after falling, but her knee really hurts.
### What keeps you well · *defined*
**Definition:** Well-being means caring for the body and the feelings together. Children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night, about an hour of active play every day, good food and enough water, and people they can talk to.

Body and feelings affect each other. After a bad night's sleep, small things feel bigger and you get cross more easily. Running, cycling or dancing makes most people feel calmer and happier afterwards. Hunger can make you grumpy. Feelings affect the body too: worry can give you a tummy ache or keep you awake. Talking about a feeling with someone you trust, and putting it into words, often helps the feeling settle.
- e.g. You get grumpy before lunch and cheerful after it.
- e.g. After an hour of playing in the building compound, you sleep well.
- e.g. Telling a parent or a friend about a bad day at school makes it feel smaller.
### Kindness, respect and friendship · *defined*
**Definition:** Kindness is doing good for someone because you care how they feel. Respect is treating other people's bodies, feelings and choices as important. Friendship is a bond of liking, trust and care between people.

Scientists have found that very young children help others without being asked. In one study, toddlers of 18 months picked up a clothes peg a grown-up had dropped and handed it back, with no reward. In another study, children under two looked happier when they gave a treat to a puppet than when they got a treat themselves. Grown-ups who spent a little money on others also said they felt happier than those who spent it on themselves. Respect means asking before touching someone, listening when they say no, and not laughing at feelings that are different from yours. Respect also means seeing a person who is physically or mentally challenged as a whole person. A child who uses a wheelchair, or who learns and thinks in a different way, has feelings and choices that count as much as anyone's.
- e.g. Helping a neighbour carry vegetables up the stairs.
- e.g. Asking before you borrow your sister's crayons.
- e.g. Two friends agree to take turns picking the game.
### Do animals have feelings? · *debated*
Animals have feelings. A dog wags and wriggles when someone she loves comes home, and a crow calls loudly and dives when someone goes near the nest. Rats make high squeaks, too high for us to hear, when they are tickled, and they come back to the hand for more. Darwin wrote a whole book comparing feelings in people and animals. Scientists still argue about how much an animal's feelings are like ours.
- e.g. A dog lowers her ears and tail when scolded.
- e.g. A cat purrs and kneads when stroked.
- e.g. Monkeys groom each other, which keeps them clean and keeps their friendships strong.
- **Jaak Panksepp and many animal scientists:** Mammals share the same deep brain parts for feelings such as fear, play and caring for young, so they feel joy, fear and grief much as we do.
- **More cautious scientists:** We can see what animals do, but we cannot ask them what it feels like. We should describe their behaviour carefully and not assume their feelings are the same as ours.

## Mostly agreed
- Strong emotions change the body: the heart rate, breathing, sweating and face. *How we know:* Doctors and scientists have measured these changes in many thousands of people. You can feel your own heart racing after a fright.
- People in every part of the world smile, frown, laugh and cry. *How we know:* Researchers have watched and photographed people in many countries, including people who had never seen films or photos.
- Children need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night to stay well. *How we know:* Children's sleep doctors studied how sleep affects attention, mood, learning and health, and agreed on these hours in 2016.
- Even very young children help and share without being taught or rewarded. *How we know:* In experiments, toddlers of 18 months handed back things grown-ups dropped, and children under two looked happier giving treats than getting them.

## How we found out
**Charles Bell, a Scottish surgeon. Guillaume Duchenne, a French doctor. Charles Darwin, an English naturalist, and the visitors at his home who took part in his test., 1806 (Bell's book), 1862 (Duchenne's photographs), 1868 (Darwin's test with visitors) and 1872 (Darwin's book on the expression of emotions).** Two hundred years ago, the Scottish surgeon Charles Bell taught that people have special face muscles made only for showing feelings, and that no animal has them. In 1862, Guillaume Duchenne, a French doctor, tested face muscles one by one. He touched an old man's face with gentle electric currents, and each current made one muscle move. Duchenne photographed the faces this made. He found that a happy smile uses two muscles: one turns up the mouth, and one around the eye lifts the cheek and crinkles the eye corner. A put-on smile often uses only the mouth. Charles Darwin used Duchenne's photographs. In 1868, at his home in England, he showed 11 of them, one at a time, to more than 20 visitors and asked what each face showed. Almost everyone agreed on some faces, but argued about others. Darwin also studied faces in monkeys, apes and dogs. In 1872 he wrote that our faces share these muscles with other animals, inherited from shared ancestors. Bell had been wrong: our face muscles are not only ours.  
*What people believed before:* Charles Bell taught that people were given special muscles only for showing feelings, and that animals do not have them. Darwin compared faces of people, apes, monkeys and dogs, and showed that we share these muscles and many expressions with other animals.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Every feeling has one face, and people everywhere read it the same way.** (Paul Ekman's work from the 1960s, and many books and posters for children; *debated*)  
  For: Ekman showed photos of faces to the Fore people in Papua New Guinea, who had seen very few photos or films, and they matched most faces to the same feelings as Americans did.  
  Against: Later studies found people often do not make the 'right' face for a feeling, and that people in some places read the same faces differently. The Fore people also mixed up fear and surprise.
- **Holding a pencil in your teeth, so your face is forced into a smile, makes cartoons seem funnier.** (A famous study in Germany in 1988; *debated*)  
  For: The first study found people rated cartoons a little funnier with the pencil in their teeth.  
  Against: In 2016, 17 labs repeated the test and found no difference. A big study in 2022 found that posing a smile changed people's mood only a little.
- **Hitting a pillow when you are angry gets the anger out.** (A common piece of advice; *rejected*)  
  For: People often feel they have let off steam afterwards.  
  Against: In a 2002 study, people who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who annoyed them were more aggressive afterwards, not less. Calming down, moving for fun and talking it over worked better.
- **Boys should not cry.** (An old saying in many homes; *rejected*)  
  For: Some people think crying shows weakness.  
  Against: Every person has the same tear glands and the same feelings. Crying is one way the body shows sadness or pain, and it often brings help and comfort from others.

## Roots
We have learned how feelings change the body and show on the face, and how hard faces can be to read. Now we ask what feelings tell us about ourselves and each other.

**Deep truth.** In the Face Test, happy faces were easy to name, but scared and surprised often got mixed up. Both lift the eyebrows and open the eyes wide. So a face gives a few clues, and the watcher guesses the rest. To know how a friend really feels, ask them.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk as if we can see a feeling on a face, like reading a word on a page. But a face shows only a few muscle shapes, and the same shapes fit more than one feeling. What we call 'seeing' a feeling is really a guess.  
*Evidence:* In the Face Test, scared and surprised were often confused, and in the Real or Polite test some polite smiles fooled the guesser. Scientists found the same: the Fore people of Papua New Guinea and Darwin's visitors also disagreed about some faces.

- *who am i:* Your feelings are part of your body: your heart, your breath and your face all join in.
- *what is life:* Living beings feel, and many show it. A dog, a crow and a baby all show feelings without words.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of other people, and you can see only part of what each one feels. Asking is how you find out the rest.

A feeling starts in one person, but it does not stay there. It shows on their face, and a friend who sees it often copies that face a little without noticing. A yawn or a laugh can spread round a room until many people are doing the same thing. In this small way people share a feeling, and that sharing is called empathy.

- **Tirukkural 706 (chapter 71, 'Reading signs'), by Thiruvalluvar:** “அடுத்தது காட்டும் பளிங்குபோல் நெஞ்சம் / கடுத்தது காட்டும் முகம்.” As a crystal shows the colour of what is next to it, the face shows what is strongly felt in the heart. (Thiruvalluvar saw that strong feelings show on the face. The Face Test found that too, and also that some faces are hard to read.)
- **Natya Shastra, chapter 6 (on rasa):** “तत्र विभावानुभावव्यभिचारिसंयोगाद्रसनिष्पत्तिः” A feeling in a play is shown through outward signs, such as the face, the eyes and the body, and the audience comes to taste that feeling themselves. (The outward signs are the same face and body changes this lesson studied. The audience tasting the feeling is a kind of empathy.)
- **Mencius, Book 2A, chapter 6:** “今人乍見孺子將入於井，皆有怵惕惻隱之心。 / 非所以內交於孺子之父母也，非所以要譽於鄉黨朋友也，非惡其聲而然也。” Anyone who suddenly sees a small child about to fall into a well feels alarm and pity at once, before thinking of any reward. (The feeling comes before the thought, just as you jump at a cooker whistle before you know what the noise was. Caring for others starts as a feeling in the body.)
- Science (settled): People often copy each other's faces very slightly without noticing, for example smiling a little when they see a smile.
- Science (debated): Whether a few feelings have the same face in every person everywhere is still argued about.
- Science (open): What a feeling is, and how the body and brain together make it, has no agreed answer.
- Other view, Many psychologists and brain scientists: Copying a face or catching a yawn is something human brains are built to do, because it helped our ancestors live in groups. It shows that brains copy each other. It does not show that people share one feeling.
- Practice, Find the feeling in your body (3 min): Let's sit still and close our eyes. Take three slow breaths. Now notice your body. Is your heart fast or slow? Is your tummy tight or soft? Are your hands warm or cool? Now give the feeling a name, quietly, in your head. Open your eyes. What did you find?
- Ask: When you guessed a feeling wrong in the Face Test, was the face wrong, or was your guess wrong?
- Ask: Can you feel something without your body changing at all?
- Ask: If a friend smiles but feels sad, which one is the truth about their feeling?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* When you feel sad because your friend is sad, whose feeling is it: yours or your friend's? (hint: Your own heart and face changed, so it happened in your body. But it began with your friend's feeling. Can a feeling belong to two people?)
- *deep:* When a cooker whistle blows behind you, your heart races before you know what the noise was. So which came first, the feeling or the thought? (hint: Think about the cooker whistle. Did you think 'I am scared' and then jump, or jump first?)
- *deep:* If someone smiles but feels sad inside, which one is the truth? (hint: Is the smile a lie, or is it a kindness, or something else? What would you want a friend to do?)
- *big:* A dog, a crow and a baby all show feelings without words. Did living beings have feelings before they had words? (hint: Darwin thought our faces came from animals long ago. What would a feeling be for, if not for talking?)
- *deep:* Is a feeling something that happens to you, or something you do? (hint: Can you choose to stop a feeling? Can you choose what you do with it?)

## Story: The New Girl Who Looked Cross
*Nila, Arjun, Ishita and their families are made up. The facts in the story are true: fear can make a face look cross or blank, and asking is the surest way to know how someone feels.*

Nila was in Class 3 at a school in Madurai, and she was good at faces. She could tell when her little brother Arjun was about to cry, because his chin wobbled first. She could tell when Amma was tired, because Amma rubbed her eyes and said 'Hmm?' to every question. Nila was proud of this. She called herself a face detective.

One Monday in June, a new girl came to Nila's class. Her name was Ishita, and her family had just moved from Kolkata. The teacher asked her to sit next to Nila. Ishita sat down, put her bag on her lap and held it tight. She did not smile. Her eyebrows were pulled down and together, and her lips were pressed into a thin line. She looked straight ahead at the board.

'She looks cross,' Nila thought. 'She doesn't want to be here, and she doesn't want to sit with me.'

At lunch, Nila sat with her old friends under the neem tree. Ishita ate her lunch alone on the steps. Nila saw her open a steel tiffin box, look inside, and close it again without eating.

That evening Nila told Amma about the new girl. 'She's very cross,' said Nila. 'She frowned all day. She didn't even talk to anyone.'

Amma was folding clothes. She stopped. 'Did you ask her how she was?'

'No,' said Nila. 'I could see. I'm a face detective.'

Amma laughed softly. 'Do you remember your first day at swimming class? You stood at the edge of the pool with your arms folded. The coach thought you were sulking. Were you?'

Nila remembered. Her tummy had felt tight and cold, and her legs had felt like they were made of water. 'I was scared,' she said.

'And what did your face look like?'

Nila thought about it. She went to the mirror in the hall and tried to remember that feeling. Her eyebrows pulled together. Her lips pressed shut. She looked, she had to admit, quite cross.

'Oh,' said Nila.

Arjun had been listening from the sofa. 'So scared can look like cross?' he asked.

'Sometimes,' said Nila slowly. She sat down next to him. 'A face is only a clue, Arjun. It's like a footprint. A footprint tells you someone walked here, but not why they were walking. You have to find out the rest.' Arjun nodded seriously, the way he did when his big sister explained things.

'How do you find out the rest?' he asked.

Nila looked at Amma. Amma raised her eyebrows and waited.

'You ask,' said Nila.

The next morning, Nila sat down next to Ishita. Ishita was holding her bag tightly again, and her eyebrows were pulled together again. Nila took a breath. Her own heart was beating fast, because asking felt a bit scary too.

'Are you all right?' Nila asked. 'Yesterday you looked a bit upset.'

Ishita looked at her. For a moment she said nothing. Then her chin wobbled, just like Arjun's did. 'I don't know anyone,' she said quietly. 'Everyone speaks Tamil and I only know a few words. And Ma packed luchi, and I was scared everyone would laugh at it, so I didn't eat.'

Nila felt a little sad, right in her chest, as if Ishita's feeling had jumped across to her. She also felt a bit ashamed that she had guessed wrong.

'Nobody will laugh,' said Nila. 'I'll teach you some Tamil. You can teach me some Bangla. And I've never tasted luchi.'

At lunch, Ishita sat under the neem tree with Nila and her friends. She opened her tiffin box. Inside were puffy, golden luchis and a little potato curry. Nila tried one and her eyes went wide. 'It's like a poori, but softer!' she said. Ishita laughed, and this time her cheeks lifted and the corners of her eyes crinkled. Nila did not need to ask what that face meant. But she asked anyway, just to be sure.

'Are you happy now?'

'Yes,' said Ishita. 'Romba happy,' she added, which was her first Tamil word. Everyone laughed, and the laughing spread round the circle like a yawn.

That night, Nila wrote in her notebook: 'A face detective looks for clues. A good friend asks.'

**For the parent:** A face is a clue to a feeling, not the whole answer. Fear can look like anger. The only way to know for sure is to ask.

- Ask: Why did Nila think Ishita was cross? What clues did she use?
- Ask: What was Ishita really feeling? Why did her face look cross?
- Ask: Nila's heart beat fast before she asked. Why was asking scary for her too?
- Ask: Have you ever been scared and someone thought you were angry or sulking?

## Games
### g1 · Feelings Charades: guess it, then help (20 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the cards and put them face down.; On your turn, read a card silently. Act the feeling with your face and body for a count of five. No words or sounds.; The others guess the feeling. A right guess gets the guesser 1 point.; Then the actor reads the card aloud. Anyone who can say one kind thing to do for a person in that situation gets 1 point.; Playing alone? Act each card in a mirror. Say the feeling, then say a kind thing you could do.; After 12 cards, the player with most points wins. If points are equal, both win.
- **Printable cards: 24 situation cards to cut out:** Your kite string snaps and the kite flies away.; You find a puppy under the stairs of your building. [picture]; Your friend wins the drawing prize, and you hoped to win.; A cockroach runs over your foot. [picture]; A relative you love gives you a surprise gift.; Someone cuts in front of you in the queue.; You smell the milk and it has gone sour.; You go on stage for the Bihu dance programme.; Your best friend moves to another city.; You finally ride your cycle without help.; A loud cracker bursts just behind you.; You have to sit next to someone you don't know.; Someone tears your notebook while playing.; You get a hug from a grandparent or an aunt you have not seen for a long time.; You forgot your homework at home.; You see a rainbow over the sea at Marina Beach.; Nobody picks you for the team.; You are lying under a fan on a quiet holiday afternoon.; You helped your neighbour carry her bags.; Someone laughs at your lunch.; The lights go out in a storm at night.; You see a friend fall and hurt her knee.; You hold a tiny new baby for the first time.; Your Pongal kolam wins the street prize.
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet:** Player name; Right feeling guesses; Kind ideas
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the cards. 2. Shuffle them face down. 3. Explain the rules.  
  *Parent says:* In this game you act a feeling without words. Then we guess the feeling and think of something kind to do.  
  *Child mode:* Act a feeling with no words. The others guess. Then think of a kind thing to do.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play 12 cards, taking turns. After each guess, the actor reads the card aloud and everyone says one kind thing to do. Keep score.  
  *Parent says:* Your turn. Read the card in your head, then show us the feeling.  
  *Child mode:* Read your card quietly. Show the feeling with your face and body. No words!
  - Note: When a guess is different from what the actor meant, ask both: 'Why did you think that?' Often both are fair. A wide-eyed face could be scared or surprised.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pick one card where the guess was wrong and talk about it.  
  *Parent says:* Which card was hardest to guess? Why was it hard?  
  *Child mode:* Which card was hardest to guess? Why do you think it was hard?
  - Check: You guessed your friend was angry, but your friend meant embarrassed. How can you be sure what a friend feels? → **Ask your friend how they feel**
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add up the points.  
  *Parent says:* Let's count the points. Who had the most kind ideas?  
  *Child mode:* Count your points. Who had the most kind ideas?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Face Test: which feelings can we read, and from which half of the face? (30 min)
- **question:** Which feelings are easy to read from a face, and which get mixed up? Do the eyes or the mouth tell us more?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which of the six feelings will be easiest to read? Which will be hardest? With the mouth hidden, will you still get them right? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The same face maker makes every face in all three rounds. Each face is held for a count of three, with no sounds and no words. The cards are shuffled before each round, so the guesser cannot know the order. The guesser never sees the card. Only one thing changes between rounds: how much of the face is hidden.
- **record:** Fill in the table with a tick or a cross for every face. Write what was guessed whenever it was wrong. Circle the two feelings that got mixed up most.
- **results table:** Feeling | Whole face: right? | Eyes only (mouth hidden): right? | Mouth only (eyes hidden): right? | If wrong, what was guessed?
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Write the six feelings on six cards: happy, sad, angry, scared, surprised, disgusted. 2. Draw the results table in the notebook. 3. Decide who is the face maker. A grown-up is best for the first go.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to test how well we really read faces. First, let's make six feeling cards and a table to write the results in.  
  *Child mode:* Write one feeling on each card. Then help make the table for your results.
  - Note: The face maker should be someone who is happy to pull faces. Before you start, make sure your child knows all six words. Disgusted means the feeling when you smell something rotten.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for a guess and a reason before you begin.  
  *Parent says:* Before we test anything, guess. Which feeling will be easiest to read? Which will be hardest? Will the eyes or the mouth tell you more? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Which feeling will be hardest? Will the eyes or the mouth help more?
  - Ask: Which do you think will be easiest, and why? (good: Happy, because a smile is easy to see.; Angry, because the eyebrows go down.)
  - Note: Write the guess at the top of the table. Don't say whether you agree.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 1, whole face. 1. Shuffle the cards in a bowl. 2. The face maker takes one without showing it. 3. They make that face for a count of three, with no sound. 4. The guesser names the feeling. 5. Write a tick or a cross. Do all six cards.  
  *Parent says:* Round one. You will see my whole face. I will make a face for a count of three. Then you tell me the feeling.  
  *Child mode:* Round one. Watch the whole face, then say the feeling you see.
  - Note: Don't tell your child whether they were right until the round ends. Then show each card. When a guess is wrong, write down what they guessed, for example 'said surprised, was scared'.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 2, eyes only. 1. Shuffle again. 2. The face maker holds the notebook across their nose and mouth, so only the eyes and eyebrows show. 3. Make each face again for a count of three. 4. Write the results in the 'eyes only' column.  
  *Parent says:* Now I will hide my mouth. You can only see my eyes and eyebrows. Do you think you will still get them right?  
  *Child mode:* Round two. The mouth is hidden. Only the eyes show. Can you still tell?
  - Note: The face maker should still make the whole face behind the notebook, mouth and all. Real faces move together.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 3, mouth only. 1. Shuffle again. 2. The face maker holds the notebook across their forehead and eyes, so only the nose and mouth show. 3. Make each face again. 4. Write the results in the 'mouth only' column.  
  *Parent says:* Last round. Now I will hide my eyes and eyebrows. Only my mouth will show.  
  *Child mode:* Round three. Now the eyes are hidden. Only the mouth shows. Guess again!
  - Note: If there is time, swap roles: your child makes the faces and you guess. Children are often surprised how hard it is to make a scared face that does not look surprised.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Count the ticks for each feeling. Find the two feelings that got mixed up most.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look at the table. Which feeling did you get right every time? Which ones got mixed up? Which half of the face helped most for each one?  
  *Child mode:* Count your ticks. Which feeling was easiest? Which two got mixed up? Was it the eyes or the mouth that helped?
  - Note: Most families find happy is easiest, especially from the mouth. Scared and surprised are often mixed up, and so are angry and disgusted. Eyes-only usually helps more for scared and angry. Mouth-only usually helps more for happy and disgusted. Your results may differ, and that is a real result too.
  - Check: In many homes, scared and surprised get mixed up. Why? → **Both feelings lift the eyebrows and open the eyes wide**
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they think, what they saw, and why. Then ask the face maker about one face that was guessed wrong.  
  *Parent says:* So what do you think? Tell me what you found, what you saw in the table, and why you think it happened. Now ask me about a face you got wrong.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. Say what your table shows. Say why. Then ask the face maker about one wrong guess.
  - Ask: What is the only way to be sure what feeling the face maker had in mind? (good: Ask them.; Look at the card, or ask.)
  - Note: A good answer has three parts. What they found: 'Happy is easy, but scared and surprised get mixed up.' What shows it: the ticks and crosses in the table. Why it happened: both faces lift the eyebrows. Let your child say it in their own words.
  - Think: If faces can be mixed up so easily, how do you know what a friend is feeling at school?
### e2 · Catch a Yawn: can watching someone yawn make you yawn? (20 min)
- **question:** Do you yawn more while watching someone yawn than while watching them smile?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will you yawn more when you watch someone yawn, or when you watch them smile? Or the same? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both rounds last 3 minutes, in the same room, with the same person to watch. The only change is what the person does: smile and blink, or yawn. Do the smiling round first, so a yawn from round two does not carry over. If you can, repeat the test on another day with the rounds the other way round.
- **record:** Write the yawns for each round as tally marks. Then write one sentence about what you found.
- **results table:** Round | What the face maker did | Yawns by the watcher | Did the watcher feel like yawning? (yes or no)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a guess and a reason. Sit facing each other. A third person can count, or the watcher counts their own yawns.  
  *Parent says:* Let's test whether yawns are catching. First, guess. Will you yawn more when I smile, or when I yawn? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make your guess, and say why you think so.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 1. Set a timer for 3 minutes. The face maker looks at the watcher and smiles or blinks now and then, about every 20 seconds. The watcher counts their own yawns.  
  *Parent says:* Round one. For three minutes I will just smile and blink. Watch my face. Count any yawns you do.  
  *Child mode:* Watch the face for 3 minutes. Count every yawn you do.
  - Note: Keep the room calm and don't talk about yawning during this round.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Round 2. Set the timer again. The face maker gives a big, slow yawn about every 30 seconds. The watcher counts their own yawns.  
  *Parent says:* Round two. Now I will yawn every so often. Keep watching my face and count your yawns.  
  *Child mode:* Watch again for 3 minutes. Count your yawns. Did you feel a yawn coming?
  - Note: Pretend yawns work, and they often turn into real ones. Many adults catch yawns. Children under about five rarely do, so a younger sibling may be a nice extra test.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the two rounds. Ask your child what they found, what the tally marks show, and why they think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Which round gave more yawns? What do you think is going on? Why might a yawn spread?  
  *Child mode:* Which round had more yawns? Why do you think yawns spread?
  - Note: Most people yawn more in round two, but not everyone, and that is a real result too. Even reading about yawns makes many people yawn. Scientists still argue about why yawns spread. One idea is that it helps a group stay alert together. Another links it to empathy.
  - Check: A child yawned 4 times while watching her mother yawn, and 0 times while watching her mother smile. What is the best conclusion? → **Yawns can spread from one person to another**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Swap roles if there is time. Then talk about smiles and laughs.  
  *Parent says:* Now you yawn and I'll watch. And what else spreads like a yawn? Have you noticed a laugh spreading?  
  *Child mode:* Now you yawn, and I'll watch. What else spreads like a yawn? Laughs? Smiles?
  - Note: Most people say no. A caught yawn comes by itself, the way your heart jumps at a loud noise. You do not choose it. That is one reason scientists think yawns link to how our brains copy each other.
  - Think: When you catch someone's yawn, do you decide to do it?

## Worksheets
### Face Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Your heart beats fast after a loud cracker bursts behind you. What made it speed up?  
   Options: You decided to make it beat faster / Adrenaline in your blood, released by fear / The noise hit your heart / You were running  
   **Answer:** Adrenaline in your blood, released by fear. *Why:* Fear makes two small glands release adrenaline, which speeds up the heart. It happens before you decide anything. The sound reaches your ears, not your heart.
2. (truefalse) A feeling happens only in your head.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A strong feeling changes the heart, the breath, the tummy and the face too. That is why you can feel butterflies in your tummy when you are nervous.
3. (fill) Understanding what someone else feels, and feeling some of it with them, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['empathy']. *Why:* Empathy starts with noticing someone's face and body, and then checking by asking.
4. (fill) A chemical messenger that makes your heart race when you are scared is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['adrenaline']. *Why:* Adrenaline is made by two small glands above the kidneys.
5. (match) Match each feeling to a face clue.  
   **Answer:** Angry → eyebrows pulled down and together; Surprised → eyebrows up, eyes wide, mouth open; Disgusted → nose wrinkled, upper lip lifted; Happy → cheeks lifted, mouth turned up; Sad → inner eyebrows up, mouth turned down. *Why:* Each feeling moves different face muscles. These are the most common clues, but faces vary from person to person.
6. (sort) Sort these into things that help your body and things that help your feelings. Some help both, so pick the main one.  
   **Answer:** drinking water → Mainly the body; talking to a friend → Mainly the feelings; eating a full lunch → Mainly the body; naming a big feeling → Mainly the feelings; washing hands → Mainly the body; a hug from someone you love → Mainly the feelings. *Why:* Water, food and clean hands mainly help the body. Talking, naming feelings and hugs mainly help the feelings. In fact each one helps both a little, because body and feelings affect each other.
7. (write) You see a friend frowning at her desk, and you think she is angry with you. What else could she be feeling? What should you do?  
   **Answer:** She could be worried, tired, unwell or concentrating hard. A frown fits many feelings. You should ask her kindly how she is.. *Why:* A strong answer gives at least two other feelings and says that asking is the way to be sure.
8. (draw) Draw two faces: a scared face and a surprised face. Show the difference in the eyebrows and mouth.  
   **Answer:** Both have raised eyebrows and wide eyes. The scared face has eyebrows pulled together and lips stretched back. The surprised face has a rounded, open mouth.. *Why:* The small differences are in how the eyebrows pull together and what the mouth does.
9. (write) When you can see only the eyes of a face, or only the mouth, which feelings are easier to read from the eyes, and which from the mouth? Why?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names a feeling for each, for example: 'The eyes help most for scared and angry. The mouth helps most for happy.' It says why: a happy mouth turns up, while fear and anger show in the eyes and eyebrows.. *Why:* Different feelings show in different parts of the face.
10. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Heart beats faster, breath gets quicker, hands sweat, hair grows longer.  
   **Answer:** hair grows longer. *Why:* A strong feeling like fear quickly speeds the heart, quickens the breath and makes the hands sweat. Hair grows slowly all the time, whatever you feel.
11. (mcq) Which of these shows respect?  
   Options: Asking 'Can I hug you?' and waiting for the answer / Hugging a cousin even when she says no / Laughing when a friend cries / Taking a friend's pencil without asking  
   **Answer:** Asking 'Can I hug you?' and waiting for the answer. *Why:* Respect means treating someone's body, feelings and choices as important. Asking first and listening to the answer does that.
12. (order) Put the steps for a big feeling in order.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Stopping gives the body time to calm a little. Naming the feeling often makes it smaller. Then it is easier to choose well.
13. (truefalse) Fear is a useless feeling that we should never have.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Fear keeps us safe. It makes us step back from a busy road or a hot stove. Every feeling has a job.
14. (truefalse) Crying is one way the body shows sadness or pain.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Every person has tear glands, and crying often brings comfort and help from others.
15. (fill) When a yawn spreads from one person to another, we say yawns are ____.  
   **Answer:** ['contagious']. *Why:* Contagious means spreading from one person to another. Laughs and smiles can be contagious too.
### Body and Feelings (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (perspective) A grandmother and her granddaughter disagree about anger.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, for example that naming a feeling often helps it settle, or that shouting in anger can hurt people. It also says what the other side has right: the grandmother is right that anger can hurt others, and the granddaughter is right that a hidden feeling is still there.. *Why:* Both have a point. Naming a feeling calmly is usually better than hiding it or exploding with it.
2. (write) In the Catch a Yawn test, a girl yawned more while watching her father yawn than while watching him smile. Her brother says, 'She was just tired.' How could she test whether he is right?  
   **Answer:** She could do the test again another day, with the yawning round first and the smiling round second. If she still yawns more when watching yawns, tiredness does not explain it.. *Why:* Changing the order is how scientists check that something else is not causing the result.
3. (label) The body outline has four blank lines, pointing to the fists, the chest, the legs and the tummy. Write the feeling that many people in the body-map study felt in each place: angry, scared, sad or nervous.  
   **Answer:** ['angry', 'scared', 'sad', 'nervous']. *Why:* In a body map study, many people coloured similar places for each feeling: fists and chest for anger, chest for fear, heavy legs for sadness, tummy for nervousness. Your own map may differ.
4. (fill) The Indian dance word for the feeling a performance brings out in the audience is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['rasa']. *Why:* The Natya Shastra describes the rasas. Dancers show them with the face, the eyes and the hands.
5. (write) A friend says, 'I can always tell what my friends feel just by looking at them.' What would you tell your friend?  
   **Answer:** Some feelings look alike, like scared and surprised. People also hide feelings or smile politely. So looking gives clues, but your friend should ask to be sure.. *Why:* A strong answer says faces give clues, not proof, and that asking is the way to know.
6. (match) Match each need to how much a child of 8 needs, or how often.  
   **Answer:** Sleep → about 9 to 12 hours a night; Active play → about an hour every day; Washing hands → before eating and after the toilet; Talking about feelings → whenever something feels big. *Why:* These are what children's doctors suggest. Sleep and play keep the body well and also make feelings easier to handle.
7. (mcq) After a bad night's sleep, a girl snapped at her brother over a small thing. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: She is an unkind person / Her brother deserved it / Too little sleep makes small things feel bigger and makes people cross more easily / Sleep has nothing to do with feelings  
   **Answer:** Too little sleep makes small things feel bigger and makes people cross more easily. *Why:* Body and feelings affect each other. Tiredness makes it harder to stay calm. It does not mean she is unkind.
8. (draw) Draw your calm corner plan. Label three things in it and say how each one helps.  
   **Answer:** Any plan that fits in a small space, with three labelled things, such as a cushion for comfort, a calm bottle to watch, and a picture card for slow breathing.. *Why:* A calm corner works best when the child chooses what helps them.
9. (order) A scientist wants to test whether people agree about what faces show. Put the steps of the test in a sensible order.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* First make the photos, then choose them, then test many people, then look at the results. Darwin did a test like this in 1868.
10. (sort) Sort these into kind and not kind.  
   **Answer:** sharing an umbrella at the bus stop → Kind; copying a friend's homework for her → Not kind; teaching a new girl a few Tamil words → Kind; laughing at someone's lunch → Not kind; asking a crying child what happened → Kind; telling a secret your friend asked you to keep → Not kind. *Why:* Kindness means doing real good for someone because you care how they feel. Copying homework for someone seems helpful, but it stops her learning.
11. (write) Your new neighbour cannot see well, and walks with a white stick. Write two things you could do that show respect. Then write one thing that would not.  
   **Answer:** Two respectful things: ask if she would like help, and wait for her answer. Talk to her directly, not only to the person with her. Not respectful: grabbing her arm without asking, or talking about her as if she is not there.. *Why:* A strong answer asks first, speaks to the person directly, and treats her choices as important.
12. (write) Why do you think fear makes your heart beat faster? How could that help an animal in danger?  
   **Answer:** A faster heart pumps more blood and oxygen to the muscles, so the body is ready to run away or fight. A deer that hears a tiger can run faster.. *Why:* The body gets ready to act before the mind has finished thinking.
13. (write) A classmate sits alone and looks sad. Write one thing you could say first, and one kind thing you could offer to do.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer starts with a question such as 'Are you all right?' and offers something real, such as sitting together or listening.. *Why:* Asking first checks the guess. Then the kind act fits what the classmate really needs.
14. (mcq) In the Face Test, why must the same person make every face in all three rounds?  
   Options: So only the hidden part of the face changes, and nothing else / So the guesser gets tired / So the faces look the same / So the guesser can guess the order  
   **Answer:** So only the hidden part of the face changes, and nothing else. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing at a time. If a different person made the faces, you would not know if the hidden part or the person made the difference.
15. (mcq) Rats make squeaks too high for us to hear when they are tickled, and they come back to the hand for more. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Rats are afraid of hands / Rats can feel something like joy / Rats notice nothing at all / Rats are hungry  
   **Answer:** Rats can feel something like joy. *Why:* Coming back for more tickling suggests the rats enjoy it. Cautious scientists add that we cannot ask a rat what it feels like.
16. (truefalse) A polite smile is always easy to spot, because it never crinkles the eyes.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Many people can crinkle their eyes on purpose, so even the eyes give a clue, not a proof.
### Thinking Like a Scientist About Feelings (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Darwin showed Duchenne's photographs of faces to visitors at his home. The visitors agreed on some faces but argued about others. Why might people disagree about what a face shows?  
   **Answer:** Some faces fit more than one feeling. Without knowing what is happening around a person, the face alone can be read in different ways. People also learn faces differently.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a face is a clue, and that context matters.
2. (perspective) Two scientists disagree about faces.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that the child's result supports both a little: happy was read right every time (Ekman), but scared and surprised got mixed up (Barrett). It uses the evidence and answers back from the other side.. *Why:* Real science often has evidence on both sides. The Fore people also mixed up fear and surprise.
3. (write) A company says its computer can tell if a child is paying attention by reading their face. Using what you learned, why might this be wrong sometimes?  
   **Answer:** Faces can be mixed up, people hide feelings, and the same face can mean different things. A thinking face can look like a cross or bored face. So the computer would only be guessing.. *Why:* A strong answer uses at least one fact from the unit.
4. (truefalse) Even reading about yawning can make some people yawn.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Scientists have found that reading about yawns, or even thinking about them, makes many people yawn.
5. (draw) Draw a comic of four boxes. In it, someone guesses a friend's feeling wrong from their face, then finds out by asking.  
   **Answer:** Four boxes: a face, a wrong guess, a question, and the real feeling.. *Why:* This shows the main idea of the unit: a face is a clue, and asking tells you for sure.
6. (write) A girl did the Kindness Week and felt happier after most kind acts. Her friend says this proves kindness makes everyone happier. What would you say to her friend?  
   **Answer:** One person's week is a clue, not a proof. Other things happen in a day too. To find out for many people, scientists test lots of people and compare.. *Why:* A strong answer says why one test is not enough and how scientists check.
7. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Yawns, laughs, smiles, freckles.  
   **Answer:** freckles. *Why:* Yawns, laughs and smiles can spread from person to person. Freckles do not.
8. (fill) A smile that lifts the cheeks and crinkles the corners of the eyes is called a ____ smile.  
   **Answer:** ['Duchenne']. *Why:* It is named after Guillaume Duchenne, who photographed face muscles in 1862.
9. (mcq) Why might a new child in class look cross on the first day?  
   Options: The child must be rude / The child does not like anyone / Feeling scared or nervous can make a face look tight and cross / Faces always show exactly what we feel  
   **Answer:** Feeling scared or nervous can make a face look tight and cross. *Why:* Fear and nervousness can tighten the face, so a nervous child can look cross. This is why asking matters.
10. (write) Is a calm corner more useful for the body or for the feelings? Explain your answer.  
   **Answer:** Both. It helps the feelings settle, and as feelings calm, the heart and breath slow down too. Body and feelings change together.. *Why:* A strong answer explains that body and feelings are linked.
11. (truefalse) Only humans have feelings.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Dogs, cats, crows, rats and many other animals show fear, joy and play. Rats even squeak with joy when tickled, too high for us to hear.
12. (match) Match each scientist to what they did.  
   **Answer:** Guillaume Duchenne → photographed face muscles moved by gentle electric currents; Charles Darwin → showed faces to visitors and compared people with animals; Paul Ekman → showed faces to the Fore people of Papua New Guinea; Charles Bell → taught that people have face muscles no animal has. *Why:* Each one added to what we know about faces, and Bell's idea was shown wrong by Darwin.
13. (write) Why is it better to ask a friend 'Are you all right?' than to say 'Why are you cross with me?'  
   **Answer:** The second question is a guess said as a fact, and the friend may not be cross at all. The first lets the friend say what they feel.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the face is only a clue, and that asking without blaming lets the friend answer honestly.
14. (write) Design a test to find out if people yawn when they can only hear a yawn, not see it. Say what you would change and what you would keep the same.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer plays a recorded or hidden yawn and a plain sound (such as a cough) for the same time to the same people, and counts yawns in each. Only the sound changes.. *Why:* It is a fair test: one thing changes, everything else stays the same.
15. (sort) Sort these into a clue about a feeling and the surest way to know it.

Asking and listening is how you know.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a feeling?  
   Options: Tall / Jealous / Hungry for rice / Brown  
   **Answer:** Jealous. *Why:* Jealous is a feeling. Tall and brown describe how something looks. Hunger is a body need, though it can make you grumpy.
2. (mcq) When you are scared, what happens to your heart?  
   Options: It stops / It beats slower / Nothing happens / It beats faster  
   **Answer:** It beats faster. *Why:* Fear releases adrenaline, which makes the heart beat faster so the body is ready to act.
3. (mcq) What is the surest way to know how a friend feels?  
   Options: Look at their face / Ask them kindly / Guess from their clothes / Ask someone else  
   **Answer:** Ask them kindly. *Why:* A face gives clues, but faces can be mixed up or hidden. Asking lets them tell you.
4. (mcq) How many hours of sleep does a child of 8 need each night?  
   Options: About 5 / About 9 to 12 / About 7 / About 15  
   **Answer:** About 9 to 12. *Why:* Children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night.
5. (mcq) Your little cousin says 'No, I don't want a hug.' What shows respect?  
   Options: Hug her anyway / Say 'OK' and give her a wave instead / Tell her she is rude / Laugh  
   **Answer:** Say 'OK' and give her a wave instead. *Why:* Respect means listening when someone says no about their own body.
6. (mcq) Many people feel happy when they share a treat with a friend. What does this show about happiness?  
   Options: Only getting things makes us happy / Sharing always makes people angry / Feelings never change / Happiness can come from giving, not only from getting  
   **Answer:** Happiness can come from giving, not only from getting. *Why:* Giving can make us feel good inside. Scientists found that even children under two looked happier giving a treat than getting one.
7. (mcq) How can you often tell a real, happy smile from a polite one?  
   Options: A real smile also crinkles the corners of the eyes / A real smile uses only the mouth / A real smile moves the ears / You cannot tell: they are the same  
   **Answer:** A real smile also crinkles the corners of the eyes. *Why:* A happy smile lifts the cheeks and crinkles the eye corners. A polite smile often uses only the mouth.
8. (mcq) Someone says animals do not have the face muscles people use to show feelings. How could you check?  
   Options: Ask a friend / Compare the faces and face muscles of people with apes, monkeys and dogs / Look at one photo of a person / Nobody can check  
   **Answer:** Compare the faces and face muscles of people with apes, monkeys and dogs. *Why:* Comparing is how you find out. Charles Darwin did this and found that people and other animals share face muscles and expressions.
9. (mcq) Your tummy flutters before a dance show. Where is this feeling happening?  
   Options: Only in your mind / Only in your feet / Nowhere / In your body as well as your mind  
   **Answer:** In your body as well as your mind. *Why:* Nervousness changes the body, including the tummy. A feeling happens in the whole body.
10. (mcq) Which helps a big angry feeling settle best?  
   Options: Stopping, breathing slowly and naming the feeling / Shouting louder / Hitting a pillow while thinking about the person / Hiding it forever  
   **Answer:** Stopping, breathing slowly and naming the feeling. *Why:* Stopping and naming the feeling helps it settle. A 2002 study found hitting a punching bag while angry made people more aggressive.
11. (mcq) In the Face Test, which feeling is usually easiest to read from the mouth alone?  
   Options: Happy / Scared / Surprised / Angry  
   **Answer:** Happy. *Why:* A happy mouth turns up, which is very easy to see. Scared, surprised and angry show more in the eyes and eyebrows.
12. (mcq) Your classmate uses a wheelchair. Which one shows respect?  
   Options: Push her chair without asking, so she gets there faster / Ask what help she wants, and wait for her answer / Make a joke about how she moves  
   **Answer:** Ask what help she wants, and wait for her answer. *Why:* Respect means asking first and listening to her choice. Pushing her chair without asking takes away her choice, and a joke about how she moves hurts her feelings.

## Applications
- Doctors and nurses use a chart of cartoon faces, from smiling to crying, so that young children can show how much something hurts.
- Kathakali and Bharatanatyam dancers train for years to show feelings with their eyes, eyebrows and hands.
- Animators draw eyebrows and eyes carefully so that cartoon characters' feelings are easy to read.
- Counsellors and teachers help children name feelings and find calm ways to handle them.
- Some companies make computers that try to read feelings from faces. Many scientists warn these often get it wrong, for the same reasons people do.

## Open questions
- **Why are yawns contagious?** Some scientists think it keeps a group alert together, others link it to empathy. The tests so far disagree.
- **Do faces mean the same thing everywhere?** Some studies find people everywhere match faces to feelings in the same way, others find big differences. Scientists still argue.
- **Why do people cry tears when they are sad or very happy?** Tears clean the eyes, but nobody knows for sure why feelings bring them. One idea is that tears ask others for help.
- **What is a feeling like for a crow, a dog or a plant?** Nobody yet knows which living beings feel, or how to find out what their feelings are like from the inside.

## Connected maths
- In the Face Test, a guesser saw each feeling 3 times. The guesser got 'happy' right 3 times, 'angry' 2 times, 'surprised' 2 times and 'scared' only 1 time. How many faces did the guesser get right altogether, out of these 12? Which feeling was hardest? **8 out of 12. Scared was hardest.**. 3 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 8. The guesser got 8 of the 12 faces right. Scared had the fewest right, only 1 of 3, because it was mixed up with surprised. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- A child of 8 goes to bed at 9 at night and wakes at 6:30 in the morning. How long does the child sleep? Is it enough? **9 and a half hours. Yes, it is enough.**. From 9 at night to 6 in the morning is 9 hours. 6 to 6:30 is another half hour. So 9 and a half hours. Children need about 9 to 12 hours, so this is enough. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In the Real Smile or Polite Smile test, one guesser got 8 of 10 smiles right. Another guesser got 5 of 10. Who probably read the eyes, and who was probably guessing? **The guesser with 8 probably read the eyes. A score of 5 is what guessing would give.**. With two choices, random guessing gives about 5 out of 10. So a score of 5 is what luck alone gives. A score of 8 is well above that, so that guesser was seeing a real clue. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L3`)
- In the Catch a Yawn test, four family members watched yawns for 3 minutes. They yawned 3, 0, 2 and 3 times. While watching smiles, they yawned 0, 0, 1 and 0 times. How many more yawns were there altogether when watching yawns? **7 more yawns.**. Watching yawns: 3 + 0 + 2 + 3 = 8. Watching smiles: 0 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 1. 8 minus 1 is 7. One person yawned 0 times in both rounds, so not everyone catches yawns. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
