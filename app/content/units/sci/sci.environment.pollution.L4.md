# Gone from sight: how dirty water hides, travels and builds up
`sci.environment.pollution.L4`

**Summary.** Five drops of colour turn a tumbler of water bright. Pour one spoonful into fresh water, and again, and again, and the colour fades until your eyes cannot find it. Is it gone? Your nose may say no. In this unit you thin out colour and scent step by step and test your eyes against your nose. Then you follow dirty water further. You see how oil spreads and how hard it is to clear, how a little pollution piles up inside fish and the animals that eat them, and how a sewage plant uses living microbes to clean water. You also find out why 'clean' always depends on what is being checked, and by whom.

**Intro.** *Two tumblers of water stand side by side. Both look exactly like water from the tap. Into one of them, someone stirred a spoonful of kewra essence. Can you tell which one, without tasting?* Look as hard as you like, and the two look the same. Now bring each tumbler to your nose. One smells of flowers and the other of nothing. Your eyes could not find the difference, but your nose could. So when a river, a pond or a well 'looks clear', what has been checked, and what has not?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that thinning dirty water out spreads the dirt through more water, but the amount of dirt stays the same.
1. I can name the main kinds of water pollution (bits that float or sink, dissolved things, living things, extra plant food) and say which tool can take out which.
2. I can run a blind test with a fair control, write down the results, and say how likely they were by chance alone.
3. I can explain how waste that microbes can eat uses up the oxygen that fish breathe.
4. I can explain how a pollutant that bodies cannot break down builds up along a food chain.
5. I can describe the stages of a sewage treatment plant and say what each stage takes out.
6. I can explain why 'clean water' depends on what is checked and who or what checks it, and why measuring beats looking.

## Terms
- **Dilution**: Mixing something into more water so that it is spread thinner. The same amount is there, but it is weaker. *e.g.* One spoon of coloured water stirred into a full tumbler of clear water.
- **Concentration**: How much of a substance is in a certain amount of water. A strong mix has a high concentration. A thin mix has a low one. *e.g.* Sweet tea has a higher concentration of sugar than tea with one pinch.
- **Parts per million**: A way to write a very small concentration. One part per million means one tiny part in every million parts of water. *e.g.* One drop of colour in a full bathtub of water is less than one part per million.
- **Sewage**: Used water from toilets, bathrooms and kitchens, with the waste it carries. *e.g.* The water that goes down the drain of a flat goes into a sewer pipe.
- **Effluent**: Liquid waste that flows out of a factory, a farm or a treatment plant. *e.g.* The water that a dyeing factory lets out after washing cloth.
- **Runoff**: Rain water that runs over the ground and carries things with it into drains, streams and lakes. *e.g.* After a storm, rain washes oil and dust from a road into the drain.
- **Nutrient**: A substance that living things use as food to grow. Too much of it in water can be a problem. *e.g.* The nitrogen in fertiliser feeds crops, and also feeds algae in a pond.
- **Algal bloom**: A sudden crowd of algae that turns the water green and thick. It happens when the water has a lot of nutrients. *e.g.* A pond covered in green scum in the summer.
- **Dissolved oxygen**: Oxygen gas mixed into water, in tiny bubbles too small to see. Fish and many other water animals breathe it through their gills. *e.g.* A cold glass of water grows bubbles as it warms, because dissolved air comes out.
- **Biodegradable**: Able to be eaten and broken down by microbes into simple things that soil and water can use. *e.g.* A banana peel rots in a few weeks, but a plastic cover stays for many years.
- **Bioaccumulation**: A substance building up inside a living body, because the body takes it in faster than it can get rid of it. *e.g.* Mercury builds up in the body of a big fish over her whole life.
- **Sewage treatment plant**: A place that cleans used water before it goes back to a river or a lake, or is used again. *e.g.* Many large apartment blocks have a small plant in the basement or at the back.
- **Indicator species**: A living being whose presence tells you how clean the water is, because they can live only in clean water, or only in dirty water. *e.g.* Mayfly nymphs live in clean streams. Red sludge worms live in very dirty ones.
- **Turbidity**: How cloudy water is, because of tiny bits floating in it. *e.g.* Water after rain in a river is muddy and has high turbidity.
- **Oil slick**: A thin layer of oil floating on the surface of water. *e.g.* A rainbow shine on a puddle in a car park.

## Explanations
### Dirty water: bits, dissolved things and living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Water pollution is anything added to water that harms the living beings who use it. It comes in three main kinds. Some are bits that float or sink, some are dissolved, and some are living, such as germs.

Bits are things you can pick out: plastic, leaves, mud and oil. They float on top or sink to the bottom. Dissolved things have spread out into single tiny pieces, so they cannot be seen or caught. Salt, sugar, dye and many chemicals are like this. Living pollution means germs from sewage, such as the bacteria that cause cholera and typhoid. Each kind needs a different way to clean it. A net takes out floating bits. A cloth takes out mud. Neither can take out salt.
- e.g. A plastic bag floating in a lake is a bit that floats. A net can lift it out.
- e.g. Mud in a monsoon river is a bit that sinks. If you leave the water still, it settles.
- e.g. Detergent in a drain is dissolved. A cloth cannot catch it.
- e.g. Germs from sewage are living. Chlorine or boiling kills them.
### Where dirty water comes from: one pipe or the whole land · *defined*
**Definition:** A point source is one place where pollution enters, such as a drain pipe. Runoff is pollution that arrives from the whole land at once, carried by rain.

A point source is easy to find and to stop. You can walk to the pipe and put a filter on it. Runoff is much harder. Rain washes oil from roads, fertiliser from fields, litter from streets and soil from building sites into drains. In India, the biggest single source of river pollution is sewage. The Central Pollution Control Board reports that less than half of the sewage made in Indian towns and cities is treated, and the rest flows on to rivers and lakes. Industry, farms and festivals add more.
- e.g. A factory pipe pouring dyed water into a stream is a point source.
- e.g. Rain washing fertiliser from many fields into a river is runoff.
- e.g. Thousands of idols dipped in a lake after a festival add paint and clay to the water at one time of year.
### Dilution: the amount stays, the strength falls · *defined*
**Definition:** Dilution means spreading a substance through more water. The concentration, which is the strength, goes down. The amount does not.

Stir one spoonful of coloured water into a tumbler of clear water. The mix has a pale colour. Take one spoonful of that and stir it into another clear tumbler, and the colour fades again. After a few steps your eyes see nothing. But the colour was never destroyed. Atoms and molecules are not used up when they mix. Each spoonful carries a little of the colour, and the rest stays behind in the earlier tumblers. Your nose usually keeps finding a scent for longer than your eyes keep finding a colour, but it gives out in the end too. Scientists write tiny strengths as parts per million.
- e.g. A spoonful of coloured water stirred into a tumbler of clear water makes a mix 41 times weaker than the spoonful.
- e.g. One drop of ink in a bucket vanishes to the eye, but a chemist's instrument can still find it.
- e.g. A chemist's instrument can find some chemicals at less than one part in a billion.
### Is the sea big enough to take our waste? · *debated*
For a long time, engineers said, 'The solution to pollution is dilution.' A river or the sea is huge, so waste poured in would thin out to nothing. Some of this is true. Small amounts of natural waste, such as leaves, are broken down by microbes in a river. But many things are not broken down, and they do not thin out to nothing. They are carried far away, they settle in mud, and they build up in living bodies. Scientists today still use dilution in planning, but they set strict limits on what can be let out.
- e.g. A small amount of kitchen waste in a fast, clean river is eaten by microbes within days.
- e.g. Mercury poured into Minamata Bay in Japan did not thin out to nothing. It built up in fish and then in people.
- e.g. Plastic waste thrown into rivers in one country is found on beaches far away.
- **Old engineering rule (mid 1900s):** Rivers and seas are so large that waste put into them is thinned to harmless levels. Cities can pour sewage and factory waste into them.
- **Modern scientists:** Thinning lowers the strength, but not the amount. Many pollutants do not break down, settle in mud and build up in animals. Waste must be treated before it is let out.
- **Water engineers today:** Mixing is still part of safe design. A treated stream is let into a river at a rate that the river can mix and clean. The key is to measure, and not to guess from how the water looks.
### Dissolved things: too small for any cloth · *defined*
**Definition:** Dissolved means spread out into single atoms, ions or molecules, so small that no cloth or sand can catch them.

A grain of mud is made of billions of atoms and can be seen with a magnifying glass. When salt dissolves, the salt breaks into single ions. An ion is an atom, or a small group of atoms, with an electric charge. Water holds each one apart. A single ion is thousands of times narrower than a grain of fine mud. A filter has gaps that are far too big to stop it. That is why sand and cloth clean muddy water but leave salty water as salty. Cleaning dissolved pollution needs other methods, such as charcoal that holds chemicals on its surface, or reverse osmosis, which pushes water through a skin with the tiniest holes.
- e.g. Salty water poured through a cloth stays salty.
- e.g. Charcoal in a water filter takes out some dissolved chemicals and smells.
- e.g. A reverse osmosis purifier removes most dissolved salts, but also wastes a lot of water.
### Food in the water uses up the oxygen · *defined*
**Definition:** When waste that microbes can eat gets into water, the microbes multiply and use up the dissolved oxygen. Fish and other animals can then no longer breathe.

Kitchen waste, sewage and dead plants are food for microbes. When a lot of food reaches a pond, bacteria multiply and eat the food. They breathe the oxygen dissolved in the water as they do. Fresh oxygen from the air mixes in only slowly. If the bacteria use oxygen faster than fresh oxygen arrives, the water runs short. Fish crowd to the surface, gasp, and then die. So the harm is not poison. It is that the waste fed a huge crowd of microbes, and they all breathe. Scientists measure this as BOD, the oxygen the microbes need to eat the waste.
- e.g. Fish floating dead in a lake the morning after a heavy sewage flow.
- e.g. A pond where leaves from many trees pile up in summer, and the fish come to the top at dawn.
- e.g. A scientist's meter that shows 2 milligrams of oxygen in a litre, too low for most fish.
### Too much plant food: algal blooms · *defined*
**Definition:** Nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus feed algae. When too much reaches a lake, the algae grow into a thick green crowd called an algal bloom.

Fertiliser, detergent and sewage all carry nitrogen and phosphorus. These are the same foods that make crops grow. In a lake they make algae grow too. The algae cover the surface and block the sunlight from plants below. When the algae die, microbes eat them, and use up the oxygen, as in the last card. So a lake can turn green first and then run short of oxygen. Some blooms also make poisons. In Bengaluru, lakes such as Bellandur have been covered with white foam and floating weed, which people link to detergents and sewage.
- e.g. A farm pond that turns green a week after fertiliser is spread nearby.
- e.g. Foam on a polluted lake, made partly of detergent.
- e.g. Lilies or water hyacinth covering a lake so thickly that boats cannot pass.
### Building up in bodies: from plankton to people · *defined*
**Definition:** Bioaccumulation is a pollutant building up inside a living body over time. When it passes up a food chain and grows stronger at each step, it is called biomagnification.

Some pollutants are not broken down or passed out of the body. Methylmercury is one. Tiny plankton take it in from the water. A small fish eats thousands of plankton and keeps all the mercury in them. A bigger fish eats many small fish and keeps theirs. A person or a heron at the end of the chain eats the biggest fish and gets the most. The water itself may have almost none. In the fish at the top there can be many thousands of times more. This is why pollution that looks tiny in a river can still harm the animals at the top of the food chain.
- e.g. Mercury in large fish such as swordfish and shark, which some countries warn pregnant women not to eat.
- e.g. DDT, a pesticide, built up in birds at the top of food chains and thinned their eggshells. Rachel Carson warned about it in Silent Spring in 1962.
- e.g. The Minamata story: cats, then people, fell ill from fish.
### Why some pollution goes away and some does not · *defined*
**Definition:** Biodegradable waste is eaten by microbes and turned into simple things. An element, such as lead or mercury, cannot be broken down by anyone. It can only move, or be locked away.

Microbes are very good cooks. They can eat sewage, vegetable waste, cooking oil and even some oil spills. These are made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen joined together in ways microbes have met for millions of years. But a mercury atom is an element. Nothing in a living body can turn it into something else. It can sit in mud, be washed to the sea or be swallowed by a fish, but the mercury stays mercury. Some plastics sit in between. Microbes have only just begun to meet them, and most have no way to eat them yet.
- e.g. A banana peel is food for microbes and rots away in weeks.
- e.g. Lead from old petrol, once in the air, is still found in soil and in ice from long ago.
- e.g. Most plastic bags last for many years, breaking into smaller pieces and not into soil.
### Oil on water: contain it, soak it, skim it · *defined*
**Definition:** Oil is lighter than water and does not mix with it, so it spreads into a thin floating layer. Because it floats on top, it can be held in with a floating barrier, skimmed off, or soaked up.

A drop of oil on water spreads out wider and wider. The thinner it gets, the harder it is to collect. So the first job in a spill is to keep it from spreading. A boom is a long floating barrier, with a part above the water and a skirt below it. Then boats skim the oil from the top, or workers soak it up with materials that take in oil but not water. Detergent does not remove oil. It breaks the oil into tiny drops that mix through the water. Experts sometimes use special chemicals called dispersants to do this on purpose, and scientists argue about when that is wise.
- e.g. In January 2017, two ships collided off Ennore near Chennai and oil washed on to the beach. Many volunteers helped to scoop it up.
- e.g. Floating booms around a ship that is being loaded with oil.
- e.g. Mats of hair or sponge-like material used to soak up a spill.
### How a sewage treatment plant cleans water · *defined*
**Definition:** A sewage treatment plant cleans used water in stages. It takes out big bits, then lets solids settle, then lets microbes eat the waste, and last kills germs.

First, screens catch big things such as plastic and rags. Second, the water stands still in a tank and heavy bits settle as sludge. Third, air is bubbled through the water and microbes that breathe air eat the waste in the water. This is the stage that does most of the cleaning, and it is alive. Fourth, the cleaned water is disinfected with chlorine or ultraviolet light. The clean water can be used to flush toilets or water gardens, or it can be let into a river. The sludge is dried and used for compost. A plant cannot take out everything, and it cannot take out most dissolved chemicals from factories.
- e.g. Many large apartment blocks in Indian cities have a small treatment plant at the back, and use its water for gardens.
- e.g. In the East Kolkata Wetlands, a Ramsar site, the city's sewage runs through shallow fish ponds, where algae and microbes clean it.
- e.g. Reed beds, which are shallow ponds planted with reeds, clean small amounts of water in some villages and schools.
### How we check water: tests and living signs · *defined*
**Definition:** No single test says whether water is clean. Each test checks one thing. Scientists use many tests, and also living beings that show the health of the water.

A turbidity test checks how cloudy the water is. A pH test checks how sour or soapy it is. A dissolved oxygen meter checks how much air the fish have. A coliform test checks for the bacteria found in human and animal waste, which means germs may be present. The Central Pollution Control Board gives rivers a best-use class, from A, which is drinking after disinfection, to E, which is only for irrigation and cooling. So 'clean' always means clean for a use. Streams also have living signs. Mayfly nymphs need clean, oxygen-rich water. Red sludge worms live in very dirty mud.
- e.g. A river stretch can pass the test for bathing and fail the test for drinking.
- e.g. Finding mayfly nymphs under the stones of a hill stream means the water is rich in oxygen.
- e.g. A coliform test on a village well after a flood.
### Microplastics: tiny bits, everywhere · *idea*
**Definition:** Microplastics are pieces of plastic smaller than five millimetres. Some are made that small, and most come from bigger plastic that has broken up.

A plastic bottle left in the sun and rain slowly cracks and breaks into smaller and smaller bits. Washing clothes made of polyester or nylon also sends tiny fibres into the drain. Scientists have found microplastics in the deepest ocean trench, in Himalayan snow, in tap water and in fish. In 2022 a team in the Netherlands reported plastic particles in the blood of most of the 22 people they tested. What this does to a body is still being studied. Nobody yet knows for sure. This is an idea we are still testing, and not a finished answer.
- e.g. Tiny fibres in the water from a washing machine.
- e.g. Small bits of a bottle found in a river after a flood.
- e.g. Microplastics have been found in sea salt, in some bottled water and in fish.

## Mostly agreed
- Thinning something out in water lowers how strong it is, but not how much of it there is. *How we know:* Matter is not used up when it mixes. A chemist can still find the dye from one drop in a bathtub, using an instrument.
- Untreated sewage is the biggest source of pollution in India's rivers. *How we know:* The Central Pollution Control Board's river monitoring reports name sewage as the main source, and find that much of the sewage from towns and cities is not treated.
- Waste that microbes can eat uses up the oxygen dissolved in water, and fish die when it runs low. *How we know:* Dissolved oxygen meters show low readings in polluted water, and fish kills follow.
- Some pollutants build up along a food chain, so animals at the top carry far more than the water does. *How we know:* At Minamata in Japan, fish held far more mercury than the bay water. People and cats who ate the fish fell ill. Researchers at Kumamoto University traced the cause to mercury in 1959.
- A sewage treatment plant takes out most solids, much of the microbe food and most germs. It takes out few of the dissolved chemicals from factories. *How we know:* Plants measure the water before and after each stage, and report what is taken out.
- Plastic breaks into smaller pieces, but does not rot away within a person's lifetime. *How we know:* Plastic pieces have been found in the deepest ocean trenches, in mountain snow and in human blood.

## How we found out
**Dr Hajime Hosokawa and researchers at Kumamoto University, with the writer Michiko Ishimure, who told the patients' stories, 1956 (the illness was reported), 1959 (the cat test and the mercury finding), 1968 (the cause was named), 1969 (Ishimure's book).** In the 1950s, in the fishing town of Minamata in Japan, cats began to stagger, spin and fall into the sea. Then people fell ill, with shaking hands, blurred sight and trouble walking. Neighbours feared it was catching and avoided the families. The Chisso chemical factory poured its waste into the bay. Like many people then, its managers believed the sea was so big that waste would thin out to nothing. In 1959 Dr Hajime Hosokawa, who worked at the factory's own hospital, fed the factory's waste water to a cat. The cat fell sick in the same way, and the company made him stop his tests. Researchers at Kumamoto University found the cause was mercury, in a form called methylmercury, which shellfish and fish had taken in and kept. It had built up in the bodies of the cats and people who ate them. The factory stopped the process in 1968, and the government named the waste as the cause. In 1969 the writer Michiko Ishimure told the patients' stories in a book.  
*What people believed before:* Many believed the sea would thin the factory's waste to nothing, and that the illness was an infection. In fact the mercury did not thin out. Shellfish and fish took it in, kept it, and passed it up the food chain, so the fish held far more than the water around them.

## Challenging perspectives
- **If water looks clear, it is clean.** (Many people, and sometimes even people who run water supplies.; *rejected*)  
  For: Muddy and green water are often dirty, and clear water is often fine to drink.  
  Against: Salt, dye, petrol, many germs and chemicals such as arsenic and fluoride dissolve in water and leave it clear. Only a test can tell.
- **A river or the sea is so big that it can take any amount of waste.** (Many engineers in the 1900s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rivers do clean themselves a little. Microbes eat natural waste, and the flowing water brings in fresh oxygen.  
  Against: Some wastes do not break down, and they build up in mud and in animals. Too much waste even of the kind microbes can eat uses up all the oxygen.
- **Boiling makes any water safe to drink.** (Many families, and some health posters.; *rejected*)  
  For: Boiling kills germs, such as the ones that cause cholera and typhoid.  
  Against: Boiling does not remove dissolved chemicals such as pesticides, fluoride or arsenic. As the water boils away, the chemicals left behind get stronger.
- **Dispersants, chemicals that break oil into droplets, are a good way to deal with a big oil spill at sea.** (Some spill-response agencies, and some scientists.; *debated*)  
  For: The tiny droplets are eaten faster by microbes, and the oil stays off beaches and birds' feathers.  
  Against: The oil is still in the water, and may harm fish and corals below. Scientists are still studying how much harm the dispersants add.
- **A river can be a person in law, with rights of her own.** (In 2017, the Whanganui River in New Zealand was given legal personhood. In the same year a High Court in Uttarakhand declared the Ganga and the Yamuna to be living entities.; *debated*)  
  For: A river cannot speak for herself, and today no one is made to answer for what is done to her. A guardian could speak for the river.  
  Against: It is not clear who would be responsible, and who would pay if the river were harmed. The Supreme Court of India stopped the Uttarakhand order later in 2017.

## Roots
We have thinned colour and scent into water, and followed dirty water into fish, rivers and sewage plants. Now we ask what this tells us about who is doing the checking, and about what 'away' really means.

**Deep truth.** In the tumbler test, the colour vanished from the water after a few steps. The smell lasted one tumbler longer. So 'clean' is not only about the water, but also about who is checking and what they check with. Water that passes one check can fail another.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk as if water were clean or dirty in itself, the way a stone is hard or soft. But 'clean' is a verdict that someone reaches with one check, such as looking, sniffing or reading a meter.  
*Evidence:* Your eyes lost the colour in a tumbler where your nose could still find the scent, and a chemist's instrument goes on finding things long after both give up. If your own results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Your body is mostly water, and that water has been rain, river and cloud before it reached you. What goes into a river can come round to you.
- *what is life:* Every living being checks water in their own way. A fish uses gills, a mayfly lives or dies by it, and a person uses eyes and nose. Living beings are some of the best water meters there are.
- *what is this world:* Water carries salt, soil, seeds and sewage from high places to low places. 'Away' only ever means somewhere else downstream.

A river looks like one thing, but it is water going round and round: sea, cloud, rain, river, sea. It carries the mountain's soil, the field's plant food, the town's sewage and the fish's bodies. Whatever one person puts in, a person further down drinks, and a fish further on keeps. The water is one water, and it shows up as every river, tank and tumbler. Clean and dirty are two ways that this one water meets different beings.

- **Rig Veda 1.23.22:** A hymn asks the Waters to carry away whatever is unclean in the speaker. (The hymn trusts water to carry things away. Science says that carrying away means carrying somewhere else, where someone else lives.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 8:** The highest good is like water. Water does good to all things and does not compete. She rests in the low places that others avoid. (Water settles in low places, so the lowest village on a river meets everything that all the villages above it put in.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water and bring the water back the next morning. The salt cannot be seen, but each sip from the top, middle and bottom tastes of salt. (In the tumbler test, the colour left the eyes but the scent stayed for the nose. Something unseen was still in every sip.)
- Science (settled): Matter is not made or lost when it mixes. Thinning something out spreads its atoms through more water, but every atom is still there.
- Science (open): Microplastics have been found in human blood. What they do to a body over a lifetime is not yet known.
- Other view, Many water engineers and regulators: 'Clean' has a plain meaning: below the safe limit for each named substance, set from health studies. That limit does not depend on who is checking. A look or a sniff is a poor guide, and the measurement is what counts.
- Practice, One glass, three ways of checking (3 min): Pour a glass of clean drinking water and sit with it. Look at it for ten seconds and say what you find. Now smell it and say what you find. Now hold it to the light and tilt it slowly. Ask what your eyes, nose and hands cannot tell you about this water. Ask where this water was last year.
- Ask: If your eyes say a glass of water is clean and your nose says it is not, which one is right?
- Ask: A fish, a dog and a lab machine each check the same pond. Are they checking the same water?
- Ask: When we send water 'away', where is away, and who lives there?

## Story: The Well You Could See Through
*The story is made up, and so are the people and the village. The facts are true. Petrol floats on water, but a little of it dissolves, and a leaking underground tank at a petrol bunk can pollute a nearby well. People can smell petrol in water in tiny amounts, and a lab measures the exact amount.*

Thamarai lived in a village between Madurai and Theni, in a house with a red-oxide floor and a well in the back yard. The well was older than her Paati. In all those years it had never run dry.

In the summer when Thamarai turned nine, the water began to smell. It was a faint smell, like a scooter that has just been switched off. She noticed it first when she filled the pot for rice. She sniffed the pot. She sniffed the tumbler. She even sniffed her own hands, in case the smell was theirs. It was in the water.

'Paati,' she said, 'the well water smells of petrol.'

Paati took the lamp and leaned over the wall of the well. The water below was green-black and still. She could see the old bricks going down, and the pale shape of a lost pot lid on the bottom. 'Kanna,' she said, 'I can see right to the bottom. Dirty water is brown. This water is clean. Your nose is confused by the heat.'

Thamarai did not argue. But she did not stop sniffing either. A year ago a petrol bunk had opened at the junction on the highway, with two bright pumps and a flag. The bunk stood uphill from their well. Thamarai remembered that, because her uncle had said the road was good for business.

The week before, in school, Gomathi Miss had made the class thin out coloured, scented water, one spoonful at a time. Thamarai remembered the third tumbler. It looked just like plain water, but it still smelled of vanilla. 'Your eyes give up first,' Gomathi Miss had said. 'Your nose keeps going a little longer. But only a measuring instrument keeps going all the way.'

That evening Thamarai tried something. She borrowed three steel tumblers that looked exactly alike. She filled two from the tap at the Panchayat tank, which came from a different source. She filled one from the well. Then she asked her little cousin Karthik to stand behind the door, shuffle the tumblers, and roll a die to decide where the well water would go.

'Paati,' she said, 'close your eyes. One of these is from the well. Sniff, and point.' Paati sighed and did it, to prove Thamarai wrong. She sniffed the first tumbler. She sniffed the second. She frowned at the third. Then she pointed straight at the right one.

'Again,' said Thamarai. Karthik shuffled. Paati pointed. Right. 'Again.' Right once more. Paati opened her eyes, and for a while she said nothing. 'Three out of three,' said Thamarai quietly. 'If you were only guessing, Paati, you would get all three right just one time in twenty-seven.'

The next morning they went to see Pushpam Akka, the Panchayat president. She did not laugh. She phoned the water-testing lab in the district town. Two days later a young scientist called Hameeda came in a white van, with a box of clean glass bottles and a pair of gloves. She filled one bottle from the well and one from the Panchayat tank, and sealed them both.

Ten days later the report came. Hameeda stood in the Panchayat office with the paper in her hand. 'There is petrol in your well,' she said. 'Not a lot. The water would look perfectly clear. But it is in there, and it is enough that nobody should drink it for years.' She looked at Thamarai. 'Your nose found it before my machine did. But a nose cannot tell how much, and some dangerous things have no smell at all. So we measure.'

The old underground tank at the petrol bunk had rusted through. It had been leaking slowly for months. Petrol floats on water, but a little of it dissolves, and a slow leak has all the time in the world. The owner, Mr Sivakumar, dug up the tank and paid for a tanker to bring water to the village until the well could be pumped clean. It took six months.

Thamarai still smells the well every morning. 'The eyes are good at finding what you can see,' she told Karthik. 'But clear only means the eyes found nothing. It does not mean there is nothing.'

And Paati, who had looked into that well for sixty years and trusted her eyes, now sniffs the water before she pours it for the rice. 'Not because my eyes are old,' she says. 'Because they are only one of the ways to look.'

**For the parent:** Clear only means the eyes found nothing. It does not mean there is nothing.

- Ask: How could you find out by testing, and not by arguing, whether a well has a smell?
- Ask: A nose found the petrol and a machine measured it. What can each one do that the other cannot?
- Ask: Who should check a village's water, and how often?

### g1 · Net, Cloth or Neither? Can you clean it out? (20 min)
- **rules:** Net or skimmer means a net, a strainer or a skimmer can lift it out. Settle, then cloth means it sinks when the water is still, and a cloth catches what is left. Neither works means it is dissolved, too small, or not a thing at all, so a net or a cloth cannot take it out.; One player is the reader. The reader takes the top card and reads the front aloud. The reader does not show the back.; Every other player puts one token face down. Then everyone turns the tokens over together.; The reader reads the back. Each player whose token matches the first words of the back scores one point.; The reader's role passes round after each card. The winner is the player with the most points when the cards run out.; Playing alone: draw a card, put down a token, then turn the card over and read the back. Try to score 18 out of 24.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: something in dirty water on the front, and the answer with the reason on the back:** A plastic bag floating on a pond. (back: Net or skimmer. It floats and it is big, so a net lifts it out.); Mud washed into a stream by monsoon rain. (back: Settle, then cloth. Mud grains sink if the water stands still, and a cloth catches the grains that are left.); Salt dissolved in a tumbler of water. (back: Neither works. Dissolved salt is split into single tiny pieces. No net or cloth can hold them. Evaporation or special filters are needed.); Coconut shells and flower garlands left floating on a lake after a festival. (back: Net or skimmer. They float and they are big, so a net or a boat with a skimmer can lift them out.); The brown colour that has dissolved in a cup of tea. (back: Neither works. The tea leaves can be strained out, but the colour is dissolved and passes through any strainer.); Tea leaves floating in a cup of tea. (back: Net or skimmer. A tea strainer is a tiny net. It catches the big bits.); Fine silt from a building site that makes rainwater cloudy. (back: Settle, then cloth. Silt sinks slowly when the water is still. A cloth then catches what is left.); Cooking oil in a thin layer on a bowl of water. (back: Net or skimmer. Oil floats. A spoon, a skimmer or a pad that soaks up oil can lift it, though a thin film is hard to get.); Fertiliser dissolved in the water of a farm pond. (back: Neither works. Dissolved plant food is too small for any cloth. It has to be kept out of the water in the first place.); Detergent dissolved in the water from a washing machine. (back: Neither works. The foam can be skimmed off, but the detergent stays dissolved in the water.); Dead leaves floating on a stream. (back: Net or skimmer. They float and they are big, so a net lifts them out.); Turmeric powder stirred into a glass of water so that it turns yellow and cloudy. (back: Settle, then cloth. The grains are tiny and sink slowly. Left alone they settle, and a cloth or paper filter catches the rest.); A pesticide dissolved in runoff from a field. (back: Neither works. A dissolved chemical goes through a net and a cloth. Charcoal or special treatment is needed.); Dye dissolved in the waste water from a cloth-dyeing factory. (back: Neither works. Dissolved dye is too small to catch. The factory has to treat the water before it goes out.); Pieces of thermocol floating on a lake. (back: Net or skimmer. They float and are big, so a net lifts them out.); Germs from sewage swimming in a pond. (back: Neither works. The gaps in a cloth are far wider than a germ. Chlorine, ultraviolet light or boiling kills germs.); Sand stirred up by a flood in a river. (back: Settle, then cloth. Sand sinks within a minute when the water is still, and a cloth catches any that is left.); Hot water from a factory, warm enough to harm fish. (back: Neither works. Heat is not a bit that can be caught. The water has to cool down before it goes out.); Used engine oil from a garage, floating as a rainbow shine on a puddle. (back: Net or skimmer. Oil floats, so it can be skimmed or soaked up from the top.); Rice husk and straw floating on a canal. (back: Net or skimmer. They float and they are big, so a net lifts them out.); Arsenic dissolved in well water in parts of the Ganga plains. (back: Neither works. It is dissolved, and the water looks clear and has no smell. Special filters, or another water source, are needed.); Chalk dust shaken into a bucket of water. (back: Settle, then cloth. The dust sinks slowly. A cloth or filter paper catches what is left once it settles.); Plastic bottles and rubbish stuck at a drain grill. (back: Net or skimmer. They are big and they float or stick, so a net or a rake lifts them out.); Sugar dissolved in sweet water from a sweet-making shop. (back: Neither works. Dissolved sugar goes straight through any cloth. It is also food for microbes, which can use up the oxygen in the water.)
- **Printable tokens: 12 verdict tokens: four sets of three:** Net or skimmer; Settle, then cloth; Neither works; Net or skimmer; Settle, then cloth; Neither works; Net or skimmer; Settle, then cloth; Neither works; Net or skimmer; Settle, then cloth; Neither works
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the 12 tokens. Shuffle the cards and put them in a pile. Give each player one token of each kind.  
  *Parent says:* These cards show things in dirty water. Each player gets three tokens. We guess whether a net, a cloth, or neither can take it out.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Shuffle the cards. Take three tokens.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first six cards. After each card, read the back aloud. Ask the question below after the third card.  
  *Parent says:* The reader reads the front. Everyone else picks a token. Turn the tokens over together. Now the reader reads the back. Who scored?  
  *Child mode:* The reader reads a card. Pick a token. Turn it over. Check the back.
  - Ask: Why do you think a net or a cloth could not catch that one? (good: It is dissolved.; It is too small.; It is a germ, which is alive.; It floats, so a cloth is not needed.)
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the next twelve cards. Keep score. After the sixth card of this round, ask the question below.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. Keep your own score. Is there a pattern in what a net can catch, and what it cannot?  
  *Child mode:* Keep playing. Keep your score. Is there a pattern?
  - Ask: What do the Neither works cards have in common? (good: They are dissolved.; They are too small to see.; Most of them are clear.)
  - Note: Most Neither works cards are dissolved things, germs, or heat. The cards that a net can catch float, and the cards that need a cloth sink. The clear point is that dissolved pollution cannot be seen and cannot be caught.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the last six cards. Add up the scores. Ask your child which kinds of pollution are the hardest to clean, and which kinds are the easiest.  
  *Parent says:* Play the last six cards and count up your points. Which kind of dirt is the hardest to clean up? Which is the easiest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Play the last cards. Count your points. Which dirt is hardest to clean?
  - Note: Cleaning is hardest for the things that cannot be seen. That is why the best answer is not to put them in the water at all.
  - Think: Why is it better to keep dissolved things out of the water than to clean them out afterwards?

## Experiments
### e1 · Gone from Sight: where do the colour and the smell stop? (36 min)
- **question:** If we thin coloured, scented water again and again, in which tumbler does the colour stop showing to the eye, and in which does the smell stop showing to the nose? Is the colour gone when we can no longer see it?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. In which tumbler will the colour vanish? In which will the smell vanish? Which goes first? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every tumbler holds the same 200 millilitres of clean water, and every step uses one level teaspoon. Tumbler 0 is the control: no colour and no essence. Each tumbler is looked at in the same light, on the same white paper. In the sniff test, a die picks where the odd cup goes, the child's eyes are shut, and each tumbler is tried three times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Do not drink or taste anything in the tumblers. Food essence is strong, so keep it away from eyes and from small children. Wash hands afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw the five tumblers in a row and colour each one as you saw it. Mark the tumbler where the colour stopped and the tumbler where the smell stopped.
- **results table:** Tumbler | How much weaker than tumbler 1 | Can you see colour? (yes or no) | Sniff trial 1 (right or wrong) | Trial 2 | Trial 3
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the five numbered tumblers, the colour and the essence. Explain the plan. Write down your child's guess and the reason for it.  
  *Parent says:* We will make coloured, scented water, and then thin it out again and again. In which tumbler will the colour vanish? In which will the smell vanish? Which goes first, and why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will thin out coloured, scented water again and again. Where will the colour vanish? Where will the smell vanish? Which goes first, and why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one goes first? (good: Eyes need a strong colour to see it.; Smells travel in the air, so the nose finds them easily.; I do not know, so let us test it.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Wait for a place and a reason. Write both at the top of the table.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Label five tumblers 0 to 4 with a marker or a sticker. Pour 200 millilitres of clean water into each. Leave tumbler 0 plain. Add 5 drops of food colour and 1 teaspoon of essence to tumbler 1. Stir well.  
  *Parent says:* Pour clean water into all five tumblers, 200 millilitres each. Tumbler 0 stays plain. It is our control, the water we compare with. Into tumbler 1 go five drops of colour and one teaspoon of essence. Stir it well.  
  *Child mode:* Pour 200 millilitres of water into each tumbler. Leave tumbler 0 plain. Put the colour and essence into tumbler 1. Stir.
  - Note: Any food essence works, for example vanilla, rose or kewra. A fresh bottle is stronger than an old one. Level the teaspoon with a knife. If you have no food colour, use two tablespoons of strong tea that has cooled.
  - Check: Why does tumbler 0 get no colour and no essence? → **So we have plain water to compare with**
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take one level teaspoon from tumbler 1 and stir it into tumbler 2. Rinse the spoon. Take one teaspoon from tumbler 2 into tumbler 3. Stir and rinse. Take one teaspoon from tumbler 3 into tumbler 4. Stir and rinse.  
  *Parent says:* Now we thin it out. One spoonful from tumbler 1 goes into tumbler 2. Stir. Rinse the spoon. Then one spoonful from 2 goes into 3, and one from 3 goes into 4.  
  *Child mode:* Take one spoon from tumbler 1. Stir it into tumbler 2. Rinse the spoon. Do the same from 2 to 3, then 3 to 4.
  - Note: Rinse the spoon in the bowl, never in the tumblers. A level spoon holds 5 millilitres. It lands in 200 millilitres, so the tumbler then holds 205 millilitres. That makes each tumbler 41 times weaker than the one before.
  - Check: One spoonful of tumbler 1 goes into 200 millilitres of clean water. How much weaker is tumbler 2 than tumbler 1? → **41 times weaker**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the tumblers in a row on white paper, in daylight. Look at each from the side and from above. Compare each with tumbler 0. Write yes or no for colour in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at each tumbler next to tumbler 0. Is there any colour at all? Say yes or no, and I will write it down. Which is the first tumbler with no colour?  
  *Child mode:* Line the tumblers up on white paper. Compare each with tumbler 0. Is there colour? Write yes or no.
  - Ask: Look at the first tumbler with no colour. Is the colour really gone? (good: It looks gone, but I am not sure.; It is just too thin for me to see.; I think it is still in there.)
  - Note: Do not hurry. Hold tumbler 0 right beside each tumbler. Some children say 'no colour' as soon as the colour is very pale. Write what they see, even if it is not what they guessed.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set out three identical small cups. Pour 2 tablespoons from tumbler 0 into two of them. Pour 2 tablespoons from tumbler 3 into the third. Roll a die to pick the place of the odd cup. A 1 or 2 means left, 3 or 4 means middle, and 5 or 6 means right. Your child shuts their eyes, sniffs all three and points to the one that smells. Write right or wrong. Do three trials, then repeat with tumbler 4.  
  *Parent says:* Close your eyes. I will put three cups in front of you. Two are plain water and one is from tumbler 3. Sniff each one. Point to the one that smells. Do not tell me why until the end.  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Sniff the three cups. Point to the one that smells. Then we do it again.
  - Ask: How sure are you? Did you smell it, or guess? (good: I smelled something faint.; I guessed.; One cup was different, but I cannot say how.)
  - Note: Do not look at the child, and give no hints with your face. Roll the die again for each trial, so the odd cup moves around. Rinse and dry the cups between trials. If even tumbler 2 has no smell, your essence is weak. Write that down and compare tumbler 2 with tumbler 0 instead.
  - Check: Three cups, and only one has the scent. If a child just guesses, how often will they point to the right cup? → **1 time in 3**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I thought. What I saw and smelled. Why I think it happened. Then read the parent note together.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see and smell? Why do you think the colour and the smell stopped where they did?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What did you think? What did you see and smell? Why did it happen?
  - Note: A strong answer says that the colour was too thin for the eyes by tumbler 3 or 4, while the nose could still find the scent. So tumbler 3 was not plain water. After the child has said this, add that a chemist's instrument can go on finding things long after the nose gives up. Neither eyes nor nose are measuring tools.
  - Check: Suppose a tumbler of thinned scented water looks like plain water, but a nose picks it out three times out of three. What does this show? → **The scent is still there. The eyes stopped finding it before the nose did.**
  - Think: Tumbler 3 looks like plain water. Is it plain water?
- **s7 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask where the five drops of colour are now. Then tip the tumblers into the sink and wash up.  
  *Parent says:* We began with five drops of colour. Where are they now?  
  *Child mode:* Where are the five drops of colour now?
  - Ask: Where are the five drops of colour now? (good: Spread out through all the tumblers.; Most of them are still in tumbler 1.; They are still somewhere, just too thin to see.)
  - Note: Most of the colour is still in tumbler 1, because each spoonful took only one forty-first of it. The rest is spread out, thinner at each step. Nothing was destroyed. It was only spread out. You can say this after your child has answered.
### e2 · The Overnight Collector: can a paper strip find what your eyes cannot? (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** A tumbler of water looks almost clear. If a paper strip hangs in it overnight, does the strip collect the hidden colour near its top? What about a tumbler where you saw no colour at all?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. In which tumbler will the strip show colour in the morning? In which will it show none? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The three tumblers each hold 200 millilitres. The strips are the same paper, the same size, and dip the same 2 centimetres. One tumbler holds plain water, as the control. The tumbler marked 'tint' is the last one in which you could see a faint colour. The tumbler marked 'none' is the first one in which you saw no colour. All three stand in the same place overnight.
- **safety:** Do not drink the water. Make the tumblers again if you did the first test more than a day ago.
- **record:** Write what each strip looks like in the morning, and measure how high any colour has climbed. Draw the three strips side by side.
- **results table:** Tumbler | What it held | Colour on the strip? (none, faint or clear) | Height of the colour from the water (cm)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess. Make the three tumblers if needed. Hang the strips and leave them overnight in a breezy spot. (15 min); day 2: Take out the strips. Compare them. Measure where any colour has climbed. Say what you found and why. (10 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three tumblers and the paper strips. Explain that the strips will hang in the water overnight. Write down your child's guess and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* Here are three tumblers, and each gets a paper strip. One tumbler has plain water, one has a very faint tint, and one looks like plain water. In which will the strip show colour tomorrow? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three tumblers get a paper strip each. In which will the strip show colour tomorrow? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that strip will show colour? (good: Water climbs up the paper and leaves the colour behind.; The paper drinks the colour.; I am not sure, so let us test it.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write it down. Use the tumblers from the Gone from Sight test, or make a new row of five and use tumbler 0, the last tint and the first one with no colour.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut three strips, 2 centimetres wide and 15 centimetres long. Tape the top of each strip to a pencil. Rest each pencil across a tumbler so that the strip dips 2 centimetres in the water. Mark the water level with the marker. Write the labels plain, tint and none on the tumblers.  
  *Parent says:* Tape each strip to a pencil. Lay the pencil across the tumbler. The strip should just dip into the water, about two centimetres. Label the tumblers plain, tint and none.  
  *Child mode:* Tape a strip to a pencil. Lay the pencil across a tumbler. Dip the strip 2 centimetres. Label the tumblers.
  - Note: The strip must hang free and not touch the glass. If it touches, the water can creep down the glass instead. Make sure all three strips are cut from the same sheet of paper.
  - Check: Why do all three strips dip the same 2 centimetres? → **So the only difference is the water in the tumbler**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three tumblers on a plate in a breezy place, such as by a window or in a gentle fan's breeze. Leave them overnight. Do not move them.  
  *Parent says:* Put the tumblers somewhere with moving air, by a window or in the breeze of a slow fan. Leave them overnight. We will look in the morning.  
  *Child mode:* Put the tumblers in a breezy spot. Leave them overnight. Do not touch them.
  - Note: Moving air makes the water dry off the strip faster. As water dries off the top of the strip, more climbs up to replace it and brings its colour along. The colour stays when the water leaves.
  - Wait: Leave it overnight, for at least 8 hours.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take out the strips and lay them side by side on a white plate. Look for colour near the top of each one. Write none, faint or clear. Measure the height of any colour from the water line. Look at each tumbler again beside the plain one.  
  *Parent says:* Lay the three strips side by side. Is there colour on any of them? Where is it? Write none, faint or clear for each one.  
  *Child mode:* Lay the strips side by side. Is there colour? Where? Write none, faint or clear.
  - Ask: Which strip has the most colour? Why do you think that is? (good: The one from the tint tumbler.; The paper collected it as the water dried.; The strip from the plain water has none.)
  - Note: Food colour often climbs as a band, and some colours split into two bands. The strip from the 'none' tumbler may show a very faint colour or nothing. Both are good results. Write what you really see.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they found and why. Then read the note together.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you find on the strips? Why do you think the colour ended up there?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you found and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that water climbed the strip and dried off near the top, and that the colour stayed behind and piled up there. The colour in the tumbler was too thin to see, but the strip collected it from a lot of water. After they say this, add that scientists often use mussels as living collectors for the same reason.
  - Think: Mussels filter a lot of water and keep what they cannot break down. How is a mussel like your strip?
### e3 · Oil Spill Race: which tool clears the most oil? (33 min)
- **question:** Which of five tools clears the most oil from a bowl of water in two minutes: a spoon, a paper towel, cotton wool, a tuft of hair or a drop of dishwash liquid? Does any of them leave the water truly oil-free?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which tool will clear the oil best, and which will do worst? Will any tool leave the water oil-free? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Six bowls each hold 300 millilitres of water and 1 tablespoon of the same oil. One bowl is left alone, as the control. Each tool works for exactly two minutes by the timer, and the same person works every tool. Afterwards a strip of brown paper is dipped in each bowl and left to dry. Oil leaves a see-through spot on paper, and plain water does not.
- **safety:** Oil on the floor is slippery, so work on a tray over newspaper. Never pour oily water down the sink, because oil blocks pipes and is hard to clean. Pour it into an old container. Wipe the bowls with paper and put the paper in the dustbin. Keep dishwash liquid away from eyes, and wash hands after.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Score the shine by looking at each bowl from a low angle, with light behind it. Compare it with the control bowl, which scores 5.
- **results table:** Bowl | Tool | Shine left after 2 minutes (0 none, 5 as much as the control) | Oil spot on the paper? (yes or no)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the six bowls and the five tools. Explain that each tool gets two minutes. Write down your child's guess and the reason for it.  
  *Parent says:* We will spill oil on six bowls of water, and then clear it with five different tools. One bowl is left alone. Which tool will clear the oil best? Which will do worst? Will any tool leave the water oil-free? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will clear oil from water with five tools. Which works best? Which works worst? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that tool will work best? (good: It soaks up the oil.; It lifts the oil off the top.; Soap washes oil away.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect dishwash liquid to win, since it cleans greasy plates. Write the guess and the reason down.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Number the bowls 1 to 6. Pour 300 millilitres of water into each. Add 1 tablespoon of oil to each. Label bowl 6 control. Match the tools to the bowls: 1 spoon, 2 paper towel, 3 cotton wool, 4 hair, 5 dishwash liquid.  
  *Parent says:* Pour water into all six bowls. Add a tablespoon of oil to each. Watch how the oil spreads. Bowl 6 is the control, so we will not touch it.  
  *Child mode:* Pour water into six bowls. Add a spoon of oil to each. Watch how it spreads. Bowl 6 is the control.
  - Ask: What does the oil do when it hits the water? (good: It floats.; It spreads out into a round patch.; It does not mix.)
  - Note: Pour the oil slowly onto the middle of the water. Oil floats because it is lighter than water, and it does not mix with water. Over a minute it spreads into a thin round patch.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start the timer for bowl 1. Skim with the spoon for exactly two minutes. Stop. Then do bowl 2 with the paper towel, dabbing and replacing the squares. Do bowl 3 with cotton wool and bowl 4 with the tuft of hair. For bowl 5, add 3 drops of dishwash liquid and stir once gently. Leave bowl 6 alone.  
  *Parent says:* Time two minutes for each bowl. Bowl 1, the spoon. Bowl 2, paper towel. Bowl 3, cotton wool. Bowl 4, hair. Bowl 5, three drops of dishwash liquid and one gentle stir. Do not touch bowl 6.  
  *Child mode:* Two minutes per bowl. Use the tool on its bowl. Do not touch bowl 6.
  - Ask: What do you notice about the oil in bowl 5 after the dishwash liquid? (good: It breaks into lots of little drops.; It moved to the sides.; The water looks cloudy.)
  - Note: Use the same effort for each tool. Dishwash liquid usually pushes the oil to the edges first, and then breaks it into small drops that spread through the water. Squeeze the paper towel and cotton wool over the spare container, not over the bowls.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look across each bowl from a low angle, with a window behind it. Score the shine from 0 to 5. The control bowl scores 5. Write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look across each bowl from a low angle. How much shine is left on the water? Give it a score from 0 to 5. The control bowl is 5.  
  *Child mode:* Look across each bowl. How much shine is left? Score 0 to 5.
  - Note: Scores are a judgement, so ask the same person to score every bowl. A thin rainbow film still counts as shine. A bowl with tiny drops all over scores more than a bowl with a clean surface.
  - Check: Why do we leave bowl 6 untouched? → **It shows how much shine there is with no tool at all**
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Dip one brown paper strip in each bowl for one second. Lay the strips on a plate and label them. Leave them to dry for five minutes. Then hold each strip up to the light and look for a see-through spot.  
  *Parent says:* Dip a paper strip into each bowl, then lay it down to dry. Oil makes paper see-through. Water does not. Let us see which bowls still have oil in them.  
  *Child mode:* Dip a paper strip in each bowl. Let it dry. Look for see-through spots.
  - Ask: A bowl looks clear, but its strip has a see-through spot. What does that tell us? (good: There is still oil in the water.; Our eyes cannot find it all.; The tool did not get all the oil.)
  - Note: Even a very thin film will leave a faint spot. Most bowls will have a spot. That is the finding, not a failure. Bowl 5 often shows a spot because the oil is still there, mixed into the water as tiny drops.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then read the note together.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think the tools worked the way they did?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says which tool left the least shine, and that the strips showed oil was left in every bowl, or in most. After the child has said this, explain that skimming and soaking take oil out. Dishwash liquid does not. It splits oil into tiny drops that spread through the water.
  - Think: Dishwash liquid made the surface look cleaner. Did it take the oil out of the water?
- **s7 (2 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Pour the oily water into the old container. Wipe the bowls with paper. Put the paper in the dustbin. Wash hands.  
  *Parent says:* Pour the oily water into the container, not down the sink. Wipe the bowls with paper. Put the paper in the dustbin.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the oily water into the container. Wipe the bowls with paper. Wash your hands.
  - Note: Close the container and put it out with the dry waste, or give it to a collector of used cooking oil if one comes to your area.

  *Child mode:* Which tumbler showed the beam? Why? What can a torch not find?
  - Note: A strong answer says the beam showed up in the milk because milk is made of tiny bits floating in the water, and that it did not show up in the salt water because salt is dissolved. So the torch finds floating bits, but not dissolved things. After they say this, add that a torch beam is one more check, and like the eyes it misses some things.
  - Think: Water from a pond looks clear, and the beam shows nothing. Is the water clean?
### a2 · Pass-It-On Seeds: how a little becomes a lot up a food chain (20 min)
- **safety:** Keep the seeds away from small children and from mouths and noses. Do not eat the seeds after the game.
- **record:** Write how many seeds were in one plankton square, in one small fish bowl, in one big fish bowl and on the person's plate. Then write how many times more the person's plate holds than one plankton.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a grid of 30 squares on paper. Put one black seed in each square. Say that each square is one tiny plankton. Each seed is a tiny bit of pollution that the plankton took from the water. Ask for a guess.  
  *Parent says:* Here are thirty tiny plankton, and each one carries a very small bit of pollution. A small fish eats five plankton. A big fish eats three small fish. A person eats two big fish. Who will have the most pollution in their body? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Each square is a plankton. Each seed is a bit of pollution. Who ends up with the most? Why?
  - Ask: Who do you think will have the most pollution in their body? Why? (good: The person, because they eat the most fish.; The big fish.; They will all have the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write it down.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Line up six small bowls. Each bowl is a small fish. Move the seeds from five squares into each bowl. Count the seeds in one bowl and write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Six small fish. Each eats five plankton. Move the seeds from five squares into one bowl. Do it six times. How many seeds does one small fish hold?  
  *Child mode:* Six small fish bowls. Move five seeds into each. How many seeds in one bowl?
  - Note: Each small fish bowl holds 5 seeds. In real life, a small fish eats thousands of plankton, so the numbers are far larger. The seeds are only a way to see how it works.

  *Child mode:* Two big fish bowls. Tip three small bowls into each. How many seeds?
  - Note: Each big fish bowl holds 15 seeds. The big fish cannot get rid of the pollution, so the pollution stays and adds up.
  - Check: A big fish eats three small fish, and each small fish holds 5 seeds. How many seeds will the big fish hold? → **15 seeds**

  *Child mode:* Tip both big bowls onto the plate. Count the seeds. How many times more than one square?
  - Note: The plate holds 30 seeds, which is 30 times as many as in one plankton square. In the real world, the pollutant in the water may be too thin to measure, and the fish at the top can still carry thousands of times more.
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what would change if the fish could pass the pollution out of their bodies. Ask what a person could do to take in less.  
  *Parent says:* What would change if the fish could get rid of the pollution? Why is it a problem when the pollution is not broken down?  
  *Child mode:* What if fish could get rid of the pollution? What changes?
  - Note: Pollution builds up when a body takes it in faster than it can get rid of it. Mercury leaves a body very slowly, so it adds up over a whole life. In this game every seed stays. Real animals do pass out some pollutants, and some pollutants go much faster than others.
  - Think: Which would hold more pollution, a small fish or a big fish? Which would you eat more often?

## Worksheets
### Clear or Clean? (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) You stir one spoonful of red water into a full tumbler of clean water. What has happened to the amount of red stuff?  
   Options: It is all still there, spread through more water / Half of it has disappeared / It has all gone  
   **Answer:** It is all still there, spread through more water. *Why:* Mixing spreads the red stuff through more water, so each sip is paler. But nothing is destroyed when things mix. The amount stays the same, and only the strength falls.
2. (mcq) After four steps of thinning, the colour is too faint to see. Which sentence is right?  
   Options: The colour is gone for good / The colour is still there, but too thin for our eyes / The water has made new colour  
   **Answer:** The colour is still there, but too thin for our eyes. *Why:* Each step spread the colour through more water. The colour was too thin to show up to the eye, but it was still in the water, and a nose or an instrument may find what an eye cannot.
3. (truefalse) If water looks clear, there is nothing in it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Salt, dye, petrol, germs and many chemicals can be in clear water. Looking is only one way of checking.
4. (fill) In the tumbler test, each step made the mix about ____ times weaker.  
   **Answer:** ['41', 'forty-one', 'forty one']. *Why:* A spoonful of 5 millilitres ended up in 205 millilitres of water. 205 divided by 5 is 41.
5. (match) Match each kind of dirt in water to the tool that can take it out.  
   **Answer:** A floating plastic bag → a net; Mud that has stirred up in still water → letting it settle, then a cloth; Salt dissolved in water → none of the simple tools: it needs special filters or evaporation. *Why:* Things that float can be lifted out. Things that sink settle, and a cloth catches the rest. Dissolved things are too small for any net or cloth.
6. (sort) Sort each thing by how it behaves in water.  
   **Answer:** a plastic bag → Floats; mud → Sinks; salt → Dissolves; thermocol → Floats; sand → Sinks; sugar → Dissolves. *Why:* Light things float and heavy grains sink. Salt and sugar break into single tiny pieces and spread through the water.
7. (mcq) A thick cloth is held over a glass and salty water is poured through it. Is the water in the glass still salty?  
   Options: Yes, because salt is dissolved in single tiny pieces that pass through / No, because the cloth catches salt / No, because cloth turns salt into water  
   **Answer:** Yes, because salt is dissolved in single tiny pieces that pass through. *Why:* A cloth has gaps that are far wider than a single piece of dissolved salt. So the salt goes through with the water. A cloth can only catch bits that are bigger than its gaps.
8. (count) A child sniffs three cups, with only one scented, and just guesses each time. She does 30 trials. About how many will she get right by luck alone?  
   **Answer:** 10. *Why:* With three cups, a guess is right one time in three. One third of 30 is 10. If she gets many more right than 10, she is not just guessing.
9. (order) Put these steps of a fair blind sniff test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Pour plain water in two cups and scented water in one', 'Roll a die to decide where the odd cup goes', 'The child shuts their eyes and sniffs all three', 'The child points, and the grown-up writes right or wrong', 'Repeat the test three times']. *Why:* The die stops the grown-up from choosing the place by habit. Shut eyes stop the child from seeing a difference. Repeating the test shows whether the result was luck.
10. (truefalse) In a fair blind test, the person who sniffs may look at the cups first.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. If the person can look, they might see a difference, such as a trace of colour or the way the cups are placed. Shut eyes make sure only the nose is being tested.
11. (write) A friend says: 'The river near our house looks clear, so it must be clean.' What would you tell your friend, using what you know about water?  
   **Answer:** Looking only checks for things the eye can see. Dissolved things like dye, chemicals and some germs can be in clear water. Colour can also get too thin to see. To know if water is clean, it must be tested for the things we care about, and clean also depends on what it is for.. *Why:* A strong answer says that clear does not mean clean, names something that hides in clear water, and says that testing is needed.
12. (label) Here is a drawing of a pond with too much waste in it. Label the three parts: the thick green layer, the crowd of tiny living beings feeding on waste, and the fish gasping.  
   **Answer:** ['Thick green layer on the surface: algae', 'Tiny living beings around floating waste: bacteria', 'Fish near the surface with open mouths: fish gasping for oxygen']. *Why:* Plant food grows the algae. When the waste and dead algae feed bacteria, the bacteria use up the oxygen, and the fish go up to find air.
13. (mcq) Why do fish gasp at the surface of a pond that has a lot of rotting waste in it?  
   Options: The microbes eating the waste have used up the oxygen in the water / The waste is poisonous gas / The fish want to eat the waste  
   **Answer:** The microbes eating the waste have used up the oxygen in the water. *Why:* Microbes eating the waste breathe in oxygen, so there is less left for the fish. At the surface a little oxygen comes in from the air.
14. (draw) Draw a food chain with plankton, a small fish, a big fish and a heron. Show a little pollution in the plankton, and draw how much builds up in each animal after that.  
   **Answer:** Four animals in a row, with a few dots in the plankton and more and more dots in each one that comes after, ending with the most in the heron.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the dots growing along the chain, because each animal keeps the pollution of everything they eat.
15. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Salt, sugar, mud, dye. Think about which can be caught by a cloth.  
   **Answer:** Mud. Salt, sugar and dye all dissolve in water and pass through a cloth. Mud does not dissolve, and a cloth can catch it.. *Why:* Dissolved things are too small to be caught. Mud grains are bigger than the gaps in a cloth, so it is held back.
16. (perspective) A factory lets its treated waste water into a big river. Its manager says the river is so big that it will thin out any waste.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view, gives a reason, and says what measurement would help, such as testing the water above and below the pipe, or testing the fish.. *Why:* This question has no single right answer. A strong answer weighs the claim and asks for evidence, rather than trusting what the water looks like.
17. (mcq) A river is marked 'best for outdoor bathing'. What does that tell you about drinking it straight from the river?  
   Options: Nothing: bathing water can still need cleaning before it is safe to drink / It is also safe to drink / It is not safe to bathe in  
   **Answer:** Nothing: bathing water can still need cleaning before it is safe to drink. *Why:* Each use has its own tests and limits. Water can be fine for bathing and still need treatment before drinking. 'Clean' means clean for a use.
### Follow the Pollution (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A heavy shower washes leaves, fertiliser and cow dung into a village pond. A week later the pond is thick and green. What happened first?  
   Options: The plant food in the runoff made algae grow thickly / The cow dung turned into algae / The rain made the water green  
   **Answer:** The plant food in the runoff made algae grow thickly. *Why:* Fertiliser and dung carry nitrogen and phosphorus, which feed algae. The algae multiply quickly and turn the pond green.
2. (truefalse) In a pond with a thick algal bloom, the oxygen can be lowest just before dawn.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. At night the algae and the microbes use oxygen and no sunlight is making more. By dawn there can be very little left for the fish.
3. (fill) When a pollutant that is not broken down builds up inside living bodies, it is called bio____.  
   **Answer:** ['accumulation', 'bioaccumulation']. *Why:* Accumulation means piling up. The pollutant is taken in faster than the body can get rid of it, so it adds up.
4. (mcq) Health warnings tell some people to eat large sea fish, such as shark, less often than small fish. What is the reason?  
   Options: Large fish eat many fish and so keep more of the mercury in them / Large fish taste bad / Small fish have no mercury at all  
   **Answer:** Large fish eat many fish and so keep more of the mercury in them. *Why:* Mercury is not passed out of a body quickly. A large fish eats many smaller fish over many years and keeps what is in them, so it holds the most.
5. (count) A plankton holds 1 unit of a pollutant. A small fish eats 20 plankton. A big fish eats 10 small fish. How many units does the big fish hold, if nothing is passed out?  
   **Answer:** 200. *Why:* A small fish holds 20 units. A big fish eats 10 of them, so the big fish holds 10 times 20, which is 200 units.
6. (match) Match each stage of a sewage treatment plant to what it takes out.  
   **Answer:** Screens → rags and plastic; Settling tank → heavy bits that sink as sludge; Tank with air and microbes → waste that microbes can eat; Chlorine or ultraviolet light → germs. *Why:* Each stage takes out a different kind of dirt. Screens catch big things, settling takes the heavy bits, microbes eat the waste that is dissolved or very fine, and the last stage kills germs.
7. (order) Put the stages of a sewage treatment plant in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Screens catch rags and plastic', 'Heavy bits settle as sludge', 'Air and microbes eat the waste', 'Chlorine or ultraviolet light kills germs']. *Why:* Big things come out first so they do not block anything. Then the heavy bits settle. Microbes do most of the cleaning, and the germs are killed last.
8. (mcq) In the third stage of a sewage plant, air is bubbled through the tank. Why?  
   Options: The microbes that clean the water need oxygen to breathe / The air makes the water colder / The air pushes the sludge out  
   **Answer:** The microbes that clean the water need oxygen to breathe. *Why:* The microbes that eat the waste breathe oxygen. Without bubbles, the oxygen would run out and they could not do the cleaning.
9. (sort) Sort each method by whether it takes out bits or kills germs.  
   **Answer:** a net → Takes out bits; a cloth → Takes out bits; letting mud settle → Takes out bits; boiling → Kills germs; chlorine → Kills germs; ultraviolet light → Kills germs. *Why:* Nets, cloths and settling catch things that are big enough to hold back. Heat, chlorine and ultraviolet light harm germs, which are alive.
10. (truefalse) Boiling water takes out dissolved pesticides.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Boiling kills germs, but dissolved chemicals stay in the water. As water boils away, the chemicals left behind get stronger.
11. (write) A torch beam shows up in water with a drop of milk, but not in water with dissolved salt. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Milk is made of tiny floating bits that scatter the light, so the beam shows. Dissolved salt is in single tiny pieces that scatter almost no light, so the beam is not seen. A torch can find floating bits but not dissolved things.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the light bounces off floating bits, that dissolved things are too small to do this, and that the torch is another check that misses some things.
12. (write) A factory pours a litre of dye into a big river. A kilometre downstream the water looks clear. Is the dye gone? Explain.  
   **Answer:** No. The dye has been spread through more water, so it is too thin to see, but it has not been destroyed. The amount is the same. Some of it may settle in mud, be taken in by living things, or flow on to the sea.. *Why:* A strong answer says that thinning lowers the strength but not the amount, and says where the dye might be now.
13. (draw) Draw a floating oil boom around a spill. Show the part above the water, the skirt under the water and the oil inside.  
   **Answer:** A ring on the water with a float above the surface and a skirt hanging down below it, and oil inside the ring.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the skirt reaching below the surface, since oil can slip under a barrier that only floats on top.
14. (perspective) A town wants to let its treated sewage into a big lake. It says the lake will mix and thin the water.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view, gives a reason, and names a measurement such as dissolved oxygen, nutrients or germs.. *Why:* This question has no single right answer. A strong answer asks for measurements before deciding, and thinks about those who live in the lake.
15. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Lead, mercury, banana peel, arsenic.  
   **Answer:** Banana peel. The others are elements, which nothing alive can break down. A banana peel is eaten by microbes and rots away.. *Why:* Lead, mercury and arsenic are elements. They can move or be locked away, but they stay what they are. Microbes can digest a banana peel.
16. (mcq) Which waste would use up the most oxygen in a pond?  
   Options: A bucket of leftover dal and rice / A bucket of clean sand / A bucket of small stones  
   **Answer:** A bucket of leftover dal and rice. *Why:* Dal and rice are food for microbes. As the microbes eat it, they breathe in the oxygen. Sand and stones are not food, so they use none.
### Who Decides What Is Clean? (challenge) (challenge, 17 questions)
1. (write) A lab tests your well and finds nothing wrong. Your nose still smells petrol. What would you do next, and why?  
   **Answer:** Test again with a different lab or a different test for petrol. Take a fresh sample in a clean glass bottle and keep it cold. Tell the village leader. A test only finds what it is looking for, so a test for one thing can miss another.. *Why:* A strong answer treats the nose as a reason to test more, and says that each test checks only certain things.
2. (mcq) What is the best reason to measure water with an instrument, as well as looking and smelling?  
   Options: A measurement gives a number that different people can compare / Instruments are always right / Looking never finds anything  
   **Answer:** A measurement gives a number that different people can compare. *Why:* Eyes and noses differ from person to person. A number from a good instrument can be compared by anyone, and checked against a safe limit.
3. (truefalse) A water test that finds no germs proves that the water has no chemicals in it.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A germ test finds signs of germs only. Chemicals need their own tests.
4. (match) Match each check to what it can find.  
   **Answer:** The eyes → mud and colour in a thick layer; The nose → some smells at very low amounts; A dissolved oxygen meter → how much air the fish have; A coliform test → signs of germs from sewage. *Why:* Each check sees one part of the water. None of them sees all of it.
5. (perspective) After an oil spill at sea, some agencies spray dispersants, chemicals that break the oil into tiny droplets.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view, gives a reason and names evidence, such as how the droplets affect fish, or how fast microbes eat them.. *Why:* This question has no single right answer. A strong answer weighs both the clean beach and the harm in the water.
6. (write) The East Kolkata Wetlands clean a city's sewage in shallow fish ponds. Name two kinds of living beings that help, and say what each one does.  
   **Answer:** Microbes eat the waste in the water and use oxygen. Algae grow on the nutrients and give out oxygen in the sunlight. Fish eat the algae and other small creatures, and are taken to market. Plants and reeds also take up nutrients.. *Why:* A strong answer names two living things and links what each one does to the cleaning of the water.
7. (mcq) Why does a lake with a heavy algal bloom often lose its fish a week or two later?  
   Options: The dead algae feed microbes, which use up the oxygen / The algae eat the fish / The algae make the water too cold  
   **Answer:** The dead algae feed microbes, which use up the oxygen. *Why:* When the algae die, microbes eat them and breathe in the oxygen. With less oxygen left, the fish cannot breathe.
8. (count) Each step of a thinning test makes the mix 41 times weaker. After three steps, how many times weaker is the last tumbler than the first? Use 41 times 41 is 1,681.  
   **Answer:** 68921. *Why:* After two steps the mix is 1,681 times weaker. One more step multiplies by 41 again. 1,681 times 41 is 68,921.
9. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Plastic bag, thermocol, rice husk, salt.  
   **Answer:** Salt. It dissolves and cannot be netted. The other three float, and a net can lift them out.. *Why:* Floating things can be taken out with a net. Dissolved things cannot.
10. (draw) Draw a river from the hills to the sea. Mark one point source of pollution with a P and one place where runoff comes in with an R.  
   **Answer:** A river drawn from the hills to the sea, with one pipe marked P from a factory or a town, and many thin streams marked R coming off fields or roads.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows a point source as one pipe or drain, and runoff as many streams across the land.
11. (mcq) If the sea has a little mercury, which fish should people eat less often: a tiny sardine, or a large shark?  
   Options: A large shark, because it has eaten many fish and kept their mercury / A tiny sardine, because it is smaller / Both hold the same amount  
   **Answer:** A large shark, because it has eaten many fish and kept their mercury. *Why:* Mercury builds up along a food chain. A large fish at the top holds far more than a small one near the bottom.
12. (truefalse) A fish in water that has only a tiny amount of mercury can still hold a lot of it in its body.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A fish takes in a little with every meal and cannot get rid of it quickly, so over her life it adds up.
13. (fill) Fish in a polluted pond gasp at the surface because the water has too little dissolved ____.  
   **Answer:** ['oxygen']. *Why:* Fish breathe the oxygen that is dissolved in water. Microbes eating waste can use it up.
14. (order) Put the response to an oil spill in the best order.  
   **Answer:** ['Float a boom round the spill', 'Skim or soak up the oil', 'Clean the shore and check the water']. *Why:* A boom first, so the oil stops spreading. The thicker the oil, the easier it is to remove. Cleaning the shore and checking the water comes last.
15. (label) Here is a drawing of an oil boom. Label the three parts: the float above the water, the skirt below the water and the oil.  
   **Answer:** ['The part that sits above the water: the float', 'The strip that hangs below the water: the skirt', 'The layer on the water inside the ring: the oil']. *Why:* The float keeps the boom on the surface, and the skirt stops oil slipping underneath.
16. (write) Write a short notice for your building or village that says why 'it looks clear' is not enough to say that water is safe.  
   **Answer:** Water that looks clear can still carry things we cannot see, such as dissolved chemicals and germs. Our eyes lose a colour before our nose loses a smell, and an instrument finds even less. So water must be tested for the things that matter, and tested again from time to time.. *Why:* A strong answer says clear does not mean clean, gives one example of something hidden, and asks for testing.
17. (mcq) Kavya gets 2 of 3 trials right in a three-cup sniff test. What is the best conclusion?  
   Options: It could easily be luck, so repeat with more trials / She definitely smelled it / She definitely did not smell it  
   **Answer:** It could easily be luck, so repeat with more trials. *Why:* Getting 2 or more of 3 right by pure guessing happens about 1 time in 4. A better test has more trials.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Sneha stirs one spoonful of red water into a full tumbler of clean water and stirs it well. What is true about the red stuff?  
   Options: It is all still there, spread through more water / Half of it disappears / It all disappears  
   **Answer:** It is all still there, spread through more water. *Why:* Mixing spreads the red stuff out, but it does not destroy it. The strength falls and the amount stays the same.
2. (mcq) A cup of water looks exactly like tap water, but a nose can pick it out. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: Something is in the water that the eye cannot see but the nose can find / The nose is always more correct than the eye / The cup is empty  
   **Answer:** Something is in the water that the eye cannot see but the nose can find. *Why:* Both senses check different things. The nose found something in the water that was too thin for the eye to see.
3. (mcq) Which tool could lift a floating plastic bag off a pond?  
   Options: A net / A cloth / A tumbler  
   **Answer:** A net. *Why:* A plastic bag floats and is big, so a net can lift it out.
4. (mcq) Salty water is poured through a thick cloth. Why does it come out just as salty?  
   Options: Dissolved salt is in single tiny pieces that pass through the gaps / The cloth adds salt / Salt cannot be seen  
   **Answer:** Dissolved salt is in single tiny pieces that pass through the gaps. *Why:* The gaps in a cloth are much bigger than the pieces of dissolved salt. So the salt goes through with the water.
5. (mcq) Farhan sniffs three cups with one scented cup, and points to the right one three times in a row. What is the best conclusion?  
   Options: He probably smelled it, because guessing would rarely give three in a row / He must have looked / It proves he has a very good nose  
   **Answer:** He probably smelled it, because guessing would rarely give three in a row. *Why:* Guessing right three times in a row happens only 1 time in 27. So it is likely he smelled it, though one test cannot prove more than that.
6. (mcq) Why can too much kitchen waste in a pond kill fish?  
   Options: Microbes eating the waste use up the oxygen / The waste is a poison / The waste makes the water too hot  
   **Answer:** Microbes eating the waste use up the oxygen. *Why:* Microbes that eat the waste breathe in the oxygen dissolved in the water. If it runs low, fish cannot breathe.
7. (mcq) Fertiliser washes from a field into a pond. What happens to the algae in the pond?  
   Options: They grow thickly, because the fertiliser is food for them / They die, because fertiliser is poison for them / Nothing changes  
   **Answer:** They grow thickly, because the fertiliser is food for them. *Why:* Fertiliser holds nitrogen and phosphorus, which feed plants and algae. Algae can then cover the pond.
8. (mcq) A river holds a tiny trace of mercury. Plankton, small fish, big fish and a heron live there. Which one holds the most mercury?  
   Options: The heron / The plankton / They all hold the same  
   **Answer:** The heron. *Why:* Mercury is kept in the body and passed up the food chain. The heron at the top eats the most fish, so she holds the most.
9. (mcq) What does the settling tank in a sewage plant do?  
   Options: Lets heavy bits sink to the bottom as sludge / Kills the germs / Lets microbes breathe  
   **Answer:** Lets heavy bits sink to the bottom as sludge. *Why:* The water stands still, and heavy bits sink. Killing germs happens in a later stage.
10. (truefalse) Boiling water kills the germs in it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Heat kills most germs. But boiling does not take out dissolved chemicals.
11. (mcq) River A is marked 'best for outdoor bathing' and river B is marked 'best for drinking after treatment'. What does this show?  
   Options: Clean always means clean for a use / River B is dirtier / Rivers cannot be tested  
   **Answer:** Clean always means clean for a use. *Why:* Each use has its own tests and limits. So the same water can pass for one use and fail for another.
12. (mcq) A well's water looks clear and smells fine. How can you tell whether it is safe to drink?  
   Options: Have it tested for the things that matter / Taste a big sip / Look at it again in the sun  
   **Answer:** Have it tested for the things that matter. *Why:* Looking, smelling and tasting find only some things. A lab can measure the others.

## Applications
- Water-supply boards and village panchayats test wells and taps for germs, chemicals and oxygen, rather than trusting how the water looks.
- Sewage treatment plants in apartment blocks and towns clean used water so it can be reused for gardens or flushing.
- Pollution control boards give rivers a best-use class, and check whether each stretch is clean enough for its use.
- Floating booms and skimmers hold and clear oil after a ship accident.
- Factories have effluent treatment plants that clean their waste water before it leaves.
- Health warnings about mercury in large fish, such as shark and swordfish.
- Farmers who use less fertiliser, or spread it in the right amount and at the right time, keep it from washing into ponds and rivers.
- Scientists use mussels and oysters as living collectors, because they filter lots of water and keep what they cannot break down.
- Clay idols and natural colours at festivals, so that less paint and plaster go into lakes and rivers.
- Citizens who test their local pond or lake and share the results, so that the people in charge can see the problem.

## Open questions
- **What do microplastics do to a body over a whole lifetime?** They have been found in blood and in many organs, but it takes many years and many people to find out what harm they do. The studies so far are early.
- **Can microbes be found or made that eat plastic fast enough to help clean the world?** In 2016, Japanese scientists reported a bacterium that digests one common plastic, PET, but very slowly. Whether this can be made fast and safe at a large scale is not known.
- **Is there any amount of a harmful chemical in water that is completely safe?** For some chemicals, harm has been found even at low amounts. For others, scientists have not found a level below which there is no harm at all.
- **How much can a river take before she can no longer clean herself?** Each river is different. It depends on her flow, her temperature, her microbes and the season, and measuring all of it is hard.
- **How do dispersants change the long-term harm of an oil spill?** Studies after big spills disagree. Some find that oil breaks down faster. Others find that fish and corals take in more of it.
- **How should we measure the health of a river: by chemicals, or by the fish, birds and plants who live there too?** Both give part of the picture. Scientists and communities still argue about which counts more, and whose measures should decide.

## Connected maths
- Each step in the tumbler test makes the mix 41 times weaker. How many times weaker than tumbler 1 is tumbler 3? **1,681 times weaker.**. Tumbler 2 is 41 times weaker than tumbler 1. Tumbler 3 is 41 times weaker again, so it is 41 times 41. 40 times 41 is 1,640, and one more 41 makes 1,681. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- Zainab sniffs three cups and just guesses. She does three trials. How many different ways can her three guesses go, if each trial has three cups to pick from? How many of them are all right? **27 ways, and only 1 of them is all right.**. The first guess can go 3 ways, the second 3 ways and the third 3 ways. 3 times 3 times 3 is 27. Only one of the 27 has all three right. So by luck alone she gets all three right 1 time in 27. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
- A level teaspoon holds 5 millilitres. How many teaspoons of water are there in 205 millilitres? **41 teaspoons.**. 205 divided by 5 is 41. So one teaspoon is one forty-first of the mix. That is why each step made the mix 41 times weaker. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- In an imagined class result, the shine left on the bowls after two minutes was: control 5, spoon 2, paper towel 3, cotton wool 2, hair 3 and dishwash liquid 1. Which tool had the lowest score? Does that mean its water was the cleanest? **Dishwash liquid had the lowest score, 1. It does not mean the cleanest water.**. The bar for dishwash liquid is the shortest. But the paper strip test showed oil spots in the bowls, and dishwash liquid breaks oil into tiny drops that stay in the water. So a low shine score alone does not show clean water. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- In Pass-It-On Seeds, a small fish eats 5 plankton, a big fish eats 3 small fish and a person eats 2 big fish. Each plankton holds 1 seed. How many seeds does the person hold, and how many times more is that than one plankton? **30 seeds, which is 30 times as many as one plankton.**. A small fish holds 5 seeds. A big fish holds 3 times 5, which is 15. A person holds 2 times 15, which is 30. One plankton holds 1, so the person holds 30 times as much. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- A town makes 20 million litres of sewage a day. Its plant can treat 12 million litres. This is an imagined town. What fraction of the sewage is not treated? **2 out of 5, or two fifths.**. 20 million take away 12 million is 8 million litres not treated. 8 out of 20 is the same as 2 out of 5. In many Indian towns, less than half of the sewage is treated. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A village's well is polluted, so a tanker brings 6,000 litres of water for 900 rupees. The village uses 18,000 litres a day. What does the water cost for one day? What for 30 days? **2,700 rupees a day, and 81,000 rupees for 30 days.**. 18,000 litres is 3 tankers, because 18,000 divided by 6,000 is 3. 3 times 900 is 2,700 rupees a day. 30 times 2,700 is 81,000 rupees. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L4`)
- In an imagined pond, the oxygen readings were 8 milligrams per litre at 6 pm, 6 at 10 pm, 3 at 2 am and 2 at 6 am. Fish need at least 4. Between which two readings did the oxygen fall below 4? About when did it reach 4? **Between 10 pm and 2 am, at about 12:40 am.**. At 10 pm it was 6 and at 2 am it was 3. It fell 3 in 4 hours, which is three quarters of a unit each hour. To fall from 6 to 4 takes 2 divided by three quarters, which is about 2 hours 40 minutes. 10 pm plus 2 hours 40 minutes is about 12:40 am. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In an oil spill test, 1 tablespoon of oil is 15 millilitres, and a bowl holds 300 millilitres of water. What fraction of the liquid in the bowl is oil, in its simplest form? **One twenty-first.**. The total liquid is 300 plus 15, which is 315 millilitres. 15 out of 315 is the same as 1 out of 21, because 315 divided by 15 is 21. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
