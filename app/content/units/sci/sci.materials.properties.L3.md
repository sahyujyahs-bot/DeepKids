# Properties of materials: what a material is like, and how to test it
`sci.materials.properties.L3`

**Summary.** A steel spoon feels colder than a wooden spoon, yet a thermometer shows they are at the same temperature. So touching is a start, but testing tells you more. A property is what a material is like, such as hard, flexible, shiny or waterproof. You find a property by looking, touching, listening and testing, and you can measure many of them as a number. Every material has many properties, and the job decides which ones matter. Some properties change with the conditions: damp paper is much weaker than dry paper. Properties come from a material's atoms and how they are joined.

**Intro.** *Try this. Pick up a steel spoon in one hand and a wooden spoon in the other. They have been in the same room all day. Which one feels colder? Now guess what a thermometer would say.* Here is a puzzle from the past. About three hundred years ago, in Bengal, weavers made a cotton cloth so fine that people called it woven air. Stories say a whole sari could be pulled through a finger ring. The spinners found that their thread held best in the damp early morning. In the dry heat of noon, it kept snapping. Was the cotton bad? Were the spinners tired? Or does the strength of a thread change with the air? In this unit you will find out, by testing paper and thread at home.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a property is, and name at least ten properties of materials.
1. I can tell an object from the material it is made of, and say which properties belong to the material.
2. I can compare two materials in one property, and put several in order from most to least.
3. I can measure a property with a simple test, and write the result as a number with a unit.
4. I can explain that no property is good or bad on its own, because the job decides which one matters.
5. I can give examples of properties that change with thickness, wetness or heat.
6. I can say where properties come from: atoms and how they are joined. And I can name something scientists still cannot predict.

## Terms
- **Material**: A material is what a thing is made of. Steel, wood, glass, cotton and clay are materials. *e.g.* A spoon is an object. Steel is the material it is made of.
- **Object**: An object is one thing with a shape and a use, like a spoon, a tumbler or a bangle. *e.g.* A steel tumbler and a steel spoon are two objects. They are made of the same material.
- **Property**: A property is a word that tells what a material is like. You find it out by looking, touching, listening or testing. *e.g.* Hard, shiny and heavy are all properties of steel.
- **Hard**: Hard means hard to scratch or dent. *e.g.* A steel nail is hard. A bar of soap is not.
- **Soft**: Soft means easy to scratch, dent or squash. *e.g.* Chalk is soft. Your fingernail can scratch it.
- **Rigid**: Rigid means stiff. A rigid thing keeps its shape and does not bend easily. *e.g.* A stone step and a thick wooden plank are rigid.
- **Flexible**: Flexible means it bends easily and does not break. *e.g.* A cotton cloth, a rubber band and a plastic ruler are flexible.
- **Elastic**: Elastic means it gets longer or bends when you pull it, and springs back when you let go. *e.g.* A rubber band and a hair tie are elastic.
- **Strong and weak**: A strong material can hold a big pull or push without breaking. A weak material breaks with a small one. *e.g.* A steel wire rope on a crane is strong. A single sewing thread is weak.
- **Brittle**: Brittle means it snaps or shatters instead of bending. *e.g.* A glass bangle, a dry biscuit and a stick of chalk are brittle.
- **Texture**: Texture is how the surface of a material feels. It can be rough, smooth, bumpy, silky or fluffy. *e.g.* Sandpaper is rough. A polished marble floor is smooth.
- **Shiny and dull**: A shiny surface bounces light back like a mirror. A dull surface does not. *e.g.* A steel tumbler is shiny. A clay brick is dull.
- **Transparent**: You can see clearly through a transparent material. *e.g.* Window glass and clean water are transparent.
- **Translucent**: Light comes through a translucent material, but you cannot see clearly through it. *e.g.* Butter paper and a frosted bathroom window are translucent.
- **Opaque**: No light comes through an opaque material. *e.g.* A steel plate and a wooden door are opaque.
- **Waterproof**: A waterproof material does not let water in or through. *e.g.* A plastic raincoat and a rubber boot are waterproof.
- **Absorbent**: An absorbent material soaks up liquid into its tiny gaps. *e.g.* A cotton towel, a sponge and a paper towel are absorbent.
- **Conductor of heat**: A conductor of heat lets heat flow through it quickly. *e.g.* A steel spoon in hot tea gets hot at the handle very soon.
- **Insulator of heat**: An insulator of heat lets heat flow through it slowly. *e.g.* Wood, plastic and wool are insulators. That is why a dosa tawa or frying pan often has a wooden or plastic handle.
- **Float and sink**: A thing floats if it stays on top of water. It sinks if it goes to the bottom. Things that are less dense than water float. Dense means how much matter is packed into each bit of space. *e.g.* A cork floats. A steel nail sinks.
- **Measure**: To measure is to find out how much, using a number and a unit. *e.g.* The card bent down by 4 centimetres. The number is 4. The unit is centimetres.
- **Scale**: A scale puts things in order, from least to most, so we can compare them. *e.g.* The Mohs scale puts minerals in order from 1, the softest, to 10, the hardest.
- **Fibre**: A fibre is a thin strand, like a hair. Cotton, wool, paper and wood are all made of fibres. *e.g.* A cotton thread is many tiny fibres twisted together.
- **Atom**: Atoms are the tiny building blocks of every material. They are far too small to see. *e.g.* A single grain of sand has far more atoms in it than there are people on Earth.

## Explanations
### What a property is · *defined*
**Definition:** A property is a word that tells what a material is like.

You can find out a property in four ways. You can look, touch, listen or test. Some you see at once, like shiny or see-through. Others need a test, like strong or waterproof. A property belongs to the material, not to one object. A steel spoon and a steel tumbler have different shapes. But both are hard and shiny, because both are steel. Another property is whether a magnet attracts it. The unit Magnetic materials is all about that.
- e.g. Look: a steel spoon is shiny and a brick is dull.
- e.g. Touch: sandpaper is rough and marble is smooth.
- e.g. Listen: a steel spoon rings when tapped and a wooden spoon makes a dull knock.
- e.g. Test: drip water on a plastic bag and on a cotton cloth, and see which one soaks it up.
### One material, many properties, and the job decides · *defined*
**Definition:** Every material has many properties at the same time. The job decides which of them matter.

Steel is hard, strong, shiny, heavy and stiff. It also conducts heat well, so it feels cold to touch. For a bridge, strength matters most. For a cooking pot, conducting heat matters most. No property is good or bad by itself. Toilet paper is weak when wet, and that is useful. It falls apart in water, so it does not block the pipes. Cotton is absorbent, which is right for a towel but wrong for a raincoat.
- e.g. A window needs glass to be see-through. It does not matter that glass breaks easily, as long as it is not hit.
- e.g. A towel needs cotton to be absorbent. A raincoat needs plastic to be waterproof.
- e.g. A cricket bat needs wood that is strong but a little springy.
### Hard and soft: the scratch ladder · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is hard if it is hard to scratch. If material A scratches material B, then A is harder than B.

You can put any materials in order of hardness with one simple test. Try to scratch each with the others. In 1812 a German mineral expert called Friedrich Mohs did this with ten minerals. He put talc at 1, the softest, and diamond at 10, the hardest. Now anyone can place a new mineral on the ladder. A fingernail is about 2 and a half. A steel nail is about 4 to 5. Ruby and sapphire are 9. Diamond is far harder than 9, not just one step more. The numbers only give the order. Scratching is only one way to test hardness, and the full scratch test is in the unit Sorting materials.
- e.g. Your fingernail scratches soap and chalk, so it is harder than both.
- e.g. A diamond scratches everything, so it sits at the top of the ladder.
- e.g. Ruby and sapphire are the same mineral, called corundum. Only the colour is different.
### Rigid, flexible, elastic and brittle · *defined*
**Definition:** A flexible material bends easily. An elastic material springs back to its shape after bending or stretching.

Flexible and elastic are not the same. A rubber band is both. It stretches, then springs back. Aluminium foil is flexible but not elastic. It bends easily and stays bent. A thick steel ruler is rigid, but if you bend it a little, it springs back. Bend it too far and it stays bent. Bend a paper clip back and forth many times, and it gets warm and then breaks. Your bending put energy into the metal, and the energy became heat. Dry spaghetti is brittle. It almost never snaps into just two pieces, and scientists only worked out why in 2005.
- e.g. Clay and dough are flexible but not elastic. They keep any new shape.
- e.g. A bamboo pole bends in the wind and springs back, so it can be used for scaffolding.
- e.g. A glass bangle does not bend at all. It breaks. That is called brittle.
### Strong is not the same as hard · *defined*
**Definition:** A strong material holds a big pull or push without breaking. Hard means hard to scratch. They are different properties.

Glass is hard. A nail cannot scratch it easily. But glass is not strong against a knock. It shatters, because it is brittle. A cotton rope is soft, but it is strong enough to pull a full bucket up from a well. Strength also depends on size. A thick thread holds more than a thin thread of the same cotton. So when we compare strength, we cut the strips to the same width and length. Steel is both hard and strong, and that is why it is used so much.
- e.g. A glass tumbler is hard but breaks when dropped.
- e.g. A jute rope is soft but strong.
- e.g. A chalk stick is soft and weak.
### Light through things: transparent, translucent, opaque · *defined*
**Definition:** A transparent material lets light through so you can see clearly. A translucent one lets light through but blurred. An opaque one lets no light through.

When light meets a material, three things can happen. It can go straight through. It can be scattered, which means it bounces off in all directions. Or it can be soaked up. Clear glass lets nearly all light go straight through. Butter paper scatters it, so you see a glow but no picture. A steel plate soaks up or bounces back all of it. Thickness matters too. One sheet of butter paper is translucent. A tall stack of sheets is opaque. Even clear sea water is dark by the time you go down about a thousand metres.
- e.g. A frosted bathroom window lets in light but hides you.
- e.g. Morning mist is translucent when thin and almost opaque when thick.
- e.g. A stone jaali screen lets in light and air but keeps a room private.
### Inside the material: why glass is clear and metal is shiny · *defined*
**Definition:** How a material treats light depends on how its electrons behave.

Electrons are tiny charged specks inside every atom. In glass, the electrons are held tightly. Light of the colours we can see does not have the right energy to move them. So the light passes through, and glass is clear. In a metal, some electrons are not tied to any one atom. They move freely among all the atoms. When light arrives, these free electrons shake and send the light straight back. That is why metals look shiny. The same free electrons carry heat quickly, which is why metals feel cold. They also hold all the atoms together like a shared glue. So the atoms can slide past each other without coming apart, and metals can be hammered into shape. This is a simple picture. The full story needs quantum physics.
- e.g. A polished steel tumbler acts like a poor mirror.
- e.g. A brass puja bell rings for a long time and can be hammered into a bowl.
- e.g. Gold leaf can be beaten so thin that light shines through it. The light that comes through looks green.
### Waterproof and absorbent · *defined*
**Definition:** An absorbent material soaks liquid into its tiny gaps. A waterproof material keeps liquid out.

Cotton, paper and sponge are full of tiny gaps between fibres. Water is pulled into the gaps and climbs up through them, even against gravity. This climbing is called wicking. A plastic bag has no gaps, so water stays on top. Some leaves are waterproof in a special way. A colocasia leaf, called arbi in many kitchens, is covered in tiny wax bumps. Water cannot spread out on it, so it rolls into a ball and runs off. Scientists copied this idea to make paint and cloth that stay dry.
- e.g. A diya wick draws oil up by wicking.
- e.g. A clay water pot (a kooja or matka) is a little absorbent. Water seeps to the outside and cools the pot as it dries.
- e.g. A lotus leaf and a colocasia leaf keep dust off because rolling drops carry it away.
### Surface tension: why water forms round drops · *defined*
**Definition:** Surface tension is a pull between the particles at the top of water. It makes the surface act like a stretchy skin.

Look at a drop on a leaf. Its surface holds together like a thin skin. That is why a needle can float on still water, and why a pond insect can walk on the water. About 135 years ago, a woman called Agnes Pockels, who lived in Germany, measured this skin in her kitchen. Women in Germany were not allowed to study at university then. She used a tin tray, a balance and a small button. She found that a drop of oil or soap on the water changes its skin. In 1891 she wrote to Lord Rayleigh, a famous British physicist. He helped her get her work printed in the journal Nature. Later, the American scientist Irving Langmuir built on her kind of tray, and he won a Nobel Prize in 1932. She did not. The unit Testing materials tells more of her story.
- e.g. A water strider insect stands on a pond without sinking.
- e.g. A drop of dish soap makes the skin weaker, so pepper sprinkled on the water rushes away from it.
- e.g. Soap lets water soak into cloth, because it weakens the skin.
### Conductors and insulators of heat · *defined*
**Definition:** A conductor of heat lets heat flow through quickly. An insulator lets it flow slowly.

Heat always flows from the warmer thing to the cooler thing. In a room, a steel spoon and a wooden spoon are the same temperature. But the steel spoon feels colder. Your hand is warmer than the spoon. Steel carries the heat away from your hand very fast. Wood carries it away slowly. So your skin tells you about how fast heat leaves, and not about the real temperature. Metals are good conductors. Wood, plastic, wool, cloth and air are good insulators. Diamond conducts heat even better than copper. On the Moon, a probe on India's Chandrayaan-3 lander found the top of the soil was about 70 degrees Celsius. Just eight centimetres down, it was about 10 degrees below zero. Moon soil is a very good insulator. The unit Using materials has a hot spoon race to test conductors.
- e.g. A tawa or frying pan has a wooden or plastic handle to stop heat reaching your hand.
- e.g. Woollen clothes keep you warm because they trap air, and air is a poor conductor.
- e.g. A steel plate left in the freezer feels much colder than a plastic one.
### Float or sink · *defined*
**Definition:** A thing floats on water if it is less dense than water. Dense means how much matter is packed into each bit of space.

A big ship floats, but a small stone sinks. So weight is not the answer. What matters is how tightly the matter is packed. A stone is packed tight, so it is denser than water. A ship is mostly air inside. Counting the steel and the air together, it is less dense than water. That is why a lump of aluminium foil sinks, but the same foil folded into a boat floats. The material is the same. The object is different. Ice floats too, which is unusual. Most solids sink in their own liquid, but ice floats on water. Lakes freeze from the top, and fish survive underneath.
- e.g. A coconut floats and a stone of the same size sinks.
- e.g. A steel ship floats, because its hollow shape holds air.
- e.g. A lump of plasticine sinks, but a plasticine boat floats.
### From words to numbers: measuring a property · *defined*
**Definition:** To measure a property is to give it a number and a unit. Then anyone can repeat the test and get the same answer.

Words like 'harder' or 'more flexible' are a start. But numbers let us say how much, and let other people check. More than four thousand years ago, Indus Valley traders used neat stone cubes as weights, in a regular system. A buyer and a seller in two different towns could trust them. A good measure uses the same size of sample, the same method, and the same unit each time. We repeat it three times, because one try can be a fluke. If the three numbers are close, we trust them. We call that reliable.
- e.g. How far a card droops, in centimetres.
- e.g. How many cups of water a hanging paper strip can hold before it tears.
- e.g. How many layers of paper it takes to block a torch.
### Same atoms, different properties: diamond and pencil lead · *defined*
**Definition:** A material's properties come from which atoms it has and how those atoms are joined together.

A diamond and the black lead of a pencil are both made only of carbon atoms. Yet diamond is the hardest natural material. Graphite, the pencil lead, is so soft that it rubs off on paper. In a diamond, every atom is joined strongly to four neighbours, in all directions, like a locked climbing frame. In graphite, the atoms lie in flat sheets. Within a sheet the joins are strong, but the sheets slide over each other like cards in a pack. In 1913, a father and son used X-rays to find the pattern inside a diamond. They were William Henry Bragg and William Lawrence Bragg. Later, Kathleen Lonsdale used X-rays to study how atoms are arranged in many crystals, including diamond. In 1945 she became one of the first two women elected to the Royal Society.
- e.g. Press your pencil lightly and the sheets slide off onto paper. That is how a pencil writes.
- e.g. A diamond can cut glass because it is the hardest natural material.
- e.g. A single sheet of graphite one atom thick is called graphene. It was first peeled off with sticky tape in 2004.
### Why a rubber band stretches and springs back · *defined*
**Definition:** A rubber band is made of very long, tangled chains of atoms. They uncoil when pulled and tangle up again when let go.

Rubber comes from the milky sap of the rubber tree. The sap is made of very long chains of atoms, like a bowl of cooked noodles. When you pull the band, the noodles straighten out. When you let go, they tangle up again, because a tangle is the more likely arrangement. Here is a strange fact. Stretch a thick rubber band fast, then touch it to your lip. It feels warm. Let it go quickly and it feels cool. Stretching and tangling change how the atoms jiggle, and that changes the heat.
- e.g. Rubber bands, balloons and the straps of rubber chappals are all elastic.
- e.g. A hair tie stretches and springs back many times, until the chains wear out.
- e.g. A balloon feels slightly warm just after you stretch it, if you hold it to your lip.
### Properties change: thickness, wetness and heat · *defined*
**Definition:** A property is not always the same. It can change when the thickness, the wetness or the temperature changes.

One sheet of butter paper is translucent. A tall stack is opaque. A strip of toilet paper can hold up a bag with a few cups of water in it. When its middle is damp, the same strip tears with almost no water in the bag. A cotton thread does not get weaker when damp. Careful tests show it gets a little stronger. Warm wax is soft and cold wax is hard. A blacksmith heats iron until it glows, and then it is soft enough to hammer. This is why a good scientist always writes down how the test was done. 'This thread held 11 shells' means little. 'This thread, as long as my forearm, in the damp morning air, held 11 shells' means a lot.
- e.g. Wet paper is much weaker than dry paper.
- e.g. Chocolate is hard in the fridge and soft in your hand.
- e.g. Glass is hard and brittle at room temperature, but a glassblower shapes it when it glows.
### Which is the strongest material? · *debated*
Scientists agree on how to measure strength. They do not agree on the winner, because 'strongest' can mean many things. Strongest for its weight? Strongest for its thickness? Strongest that anyone has actually touched? Different answers give different winners. Three of the best claims are very different from each other.
- e.g. Steel holds up bridges and buildings.
- e.g. A spider's dragline silk is thinner than a hair and tough enough to stop a flying insect.
- e.g. Graphene is one atom thick.
- **Engineers:** For building things, the best material is the one that does the job cheaply and safely. So steel and concrete still win.
- **Biologists and material scientists studying spider silk:** Weight for weight, spider silk is among the toughest fibres known. A spider makes this silk at room temperature from a fly's meal. People still cannot copy it exactly.
- **Physicists who measured graphene in 2008:** They poked a single sheet of graphene with a tiny diamond tip. They found it was the strongest material ever measured, for its thickness. Sheets this perfect are very hard to make large.
- **Astrophysicists using computer models, in 2018:** Deep inside a dead star there may be a material called nuclear pasta. Their simulations say it could be billions of times stronger than steel. Nobody has ever touched it, and nobody can.
### Materials that grow: bone, wood and shell · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things make their own materials, with properties that engineers admire.

Your bones are a material your body builds and repairs. Bone is made of a hard mineral, which resists squashing, and a flexible protein called collagen, which stops it snapping. Together they are tough and light. The outside of your teeth, called enamel, is the hardest material your body makes. Wood is made of long thin cells with strong walls, like bundles of straws. The inside of a sea shell, called mother-of-pearl, is made of tiny chalky tiles stuck together with a stretchy glue. It is much harder to crack than the plain chalk it is made from. The unit What is it made of? shows where materials come from.
- e.g. Bengal craftspeople have carved bangles from conch shell for centuries.
- e.g. A bamboo stem is hollow and strong because of its fibres.
- e.g. A hen's eggshell is thin but hard to crush if you press the ends.

## Mostly agreed
- Every material has properties that we can find out by looking, touching, listening and testing. *How we know:* Craftspeople have done this for thousands of years. Laboratories today get the same answers with machines. Anyone can repeat a scratch test or a bend test and get the same order.
- A property can change with thickness, wetness and temperature, so a test must say how it was done. *How we know:* One sheet of butter paper lets light through and a tall stack does not. Cotton thread is stronger when damp than when dry. Textile scientists have measured this for a hundred years.
- Hard, strong, stiff and tough are different properties, and one does not tell you the others. *How we know:* Glass is hard but breaks when dropped. A rope is soft but strong. Engineers measure each one with its own test.
- Properties come from the atoms a material has and how they are joined. *How we know:* In 1913 the Braggs used X-rays to see the pattern of atoms in a diamond. Diamond and pencil lead are both carbon, and the pattern is the only difference.
- Measuring with numbers and units lets different people compare and trust each other's results. *How we know:* The Indus Valley traders used standard stone weights more than four thousand years ago. Every science laboratory today does the same with standard units.

## How we found out
**Antoine Lavoisier, Smithson Tennant, and William Henry Bragg with his son William Lawrence Bragg, 1772, 1797 and 1913.** For thousands of years, people thought a diamond could never be destroyed. Its old Greek name, adamas, means 'unconquerable'. Nobody thought it had anything to do with black charcoal or pencil lead. In 1772 the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier heated a diamond with sunlight, focused by a giant lens. The diamond burned away. It made the same gas that burning charcoal makes, which we now call carbon dioxide. In 1797 the English chemist Smithson Tennant went further. He burned diamond and charcoal of the same weight. Both made the same amount of that gas. So a diamond is pure carbon, just like charcoal. But then why is one clear and hard, and the other black and soft? In 1913 William Henry Bragg and his son William Lawrence Bragg shone X-rays through a diamond. The pattern showed each carbon atom joined tightly to four neighbours. The atoms in diamond and pencil lead are the same. Only the way they are joined is different.  
*What people believed before:* Diamond was thought to be a special stone that could never be destroyed, with nothing in common with black charcoal. Lavoisier and Tennant burned diamond and found it makes the same gas as charcoal, so it is carbon too.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Metal is colder than wood, even in the same room.** (Almost everybody, because it feels true every time we touch a metal gate on a winter morning.; *rejected*)  
  For: A steel spoon feels much colder than a wooden one when you pick it up.  
  Against: A thermometer reads the same for both. Metal feels colder only because it pulls heat out of your hand faster. Your skin is a poor thermometer. It tells you how fast heat is leaving, not what the temperature is.
- **Hard things are strong things.** (A common idea, and very easy to believe because steel is both.; *rejected*)  
  For: The hardest materials we know, like diamond, are used for cutting and drilling. Hard things feel solid and strong.  
  Against: Glass is hard but shatters when dropped. A diamond can crack if it is hit on the right side. A cotton rope is soft but can pull a full bucket up from a well. Hard means hard to scratch. Strong means hard to break by pulling or pushing.
- **Heavy things sink and light things float.** (Most young children, and many grown-ups when they are not thinking.; *rejected*)  
  For: A stone sinks and a feather floats. A coin sinks and a leaf floats.  
  Against: A huge steel ship weighs thousands of tonnes and floats. A tiny pebble sinks. A big log floats and a small nail sinks. What matters is how tightly the matter is packed into the space, not how heavy a thing is.
- **A material always has the same property, wherever and whenever you test it.** (A natural way of thinking, which we all use when we say 'steel is strong'.; *rejected*)  
  For: Steel is nearly always strong. Glass nearly always breaks. These words are useful.  
  Against: Properties change with thickness, wetness and heat. Damp paper is much weaker than dry paper, and a damp cotton thread is a little stronger than a dry one. Hot iron is soft. A thin sheet of any material is more flexible than a thick one. So a good scientist says how the test was done.
- **A property belongs to a thing all by itself, whether or not anything is touching it.** (Most of us, when we say 'this stone is hard'. Some thinkers, like the Buddhist teacher Nagarjuna (about the 2nd century CE), looked at it differently.; *debated*)  
  For: A stone seems hard even in a dark cupboard where nobody is testing it. Its atoms are joined the same way whether anyone scratches it or not.  
  Against: 'Hard' means 'harder than something else'. 'Cold' means 'takes heat from a warm hand'. A cotton thread holds more in damp air than in dry air. A property shows up in a meeting between two things. Nagarjuna taught that nothing exists all alone, and everything depends on other things. Most scientists would add that the stone has a real structure that decides what happens in every meeting.
- **Water always makes things weaker.** (A guess many people make, because wet paper and wet cardboard fall apart.; *rejected*)  
  For: Wet paper tears. Wet cardboard goes soft. Wet mud is weak.  
  Against: Wet cotton thread is stronger than dry. Textile scientists have measured this. Damp sand is firmer than dry sand. Egyptians poured water on sand to slide heavy stone on sledges. Water can help or harm, depending on the material.
- **A wonder material like graphene will soon replace the old ones.** (Many news stories and some companies since 2004, when graphene was first made.; *debated*)  
  For: Graphene is very thin, very strong, flexible and nearly clear. It carries heat and electricity very well.  
  Against: It is hard and costly to make big, perfect sheets. Steel, wood and glass are cheap, strong enough and easy to make. In twenty years graphene has found some uses, but most of the early promises have not come true yet. Scientists still debate how big its future will be.

## Roots
We have been testing what materials are like, and found that some properties change with water and heat. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A dry strip of toilet paper can hold up a bag with water in it. Wet its middle with a fingertip, and the same strip tears with almost no water in the bag. Damp cotton thread holds as much, or a little more. So to know whether paper or thread will hold, you must also know whether it will get wet.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'paper is strong enough' or 'cotton is strong' as if strength were part of the material's name. But the same strip goes from strong to weak with a few drops of water, so a property needs a 'when' and a 'where'.  
*Evidence:* In the Dry or Damp test, a dry toilet paper strip held a bag with one or more cups of water, and a damp one tore with almost none. Cotton thread held about as much, or a little more, when damp.

- *who am i:* Your body is made of materials too. Your skin stretches easily when it is damp, and can crack when it is very dry.
- *what is life:* Life makes its own materials. Your body grows bone, hair and the clear lens of your eye, and mends them.
- *what is this world:* The world is full of materials whose properties change with heat, cold and water. A path that is firm today can be mud after rain.

Diamond and pencil lead are both only carbon atoms, joined in two different patterns. Paper and cotton thread are both made mostly of cellulose, the substance of plant fibres. Yet water weakens paper and not thread, because their fibres are held together in different ways. So the many materials around us come from a few kinds of atom, arranged and joined in many ways.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** Uddalaka gives his son Shvetaketu a lump of salt and tells him to put it in water. In the morning the salt cannot be seen. But when Shvetaketu tastes the water from the top, the middle or the bottom, it is salty everywhere. Uddalaka says the finest part of everything is like this: unseen, yet everywhere. (You cannot see the atoms in a steel spoon or a strip of paper. But every property you tested, hard, shiny or strong, comes from them and how they are joined.)
- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika, chapter 24, verse 18:** Nagarjuna, a Buddhist teacher of about the 2nd century CE, taught that nothing exists all alone. Everything is what it is because of other things. (The strength you measured depended on other things: the thread on the damp air, the paper on whether it was dry. Hard means harder than something else.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding, on interbeing:** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you see a cloud in it. Without the cloud there would be no rain, no tree and no paper. - Science (settled): Every property of a material comes from the atoms it has and how they are joined. Diamond and pencil lead are both only carbon.
- Science (settled): Paper is made of plant fibres that stick to each other where they touch. Water gets between the fibres and loosens these joins, so wet paper tears easily. Makers add special chemicals to kitchen towels so that they stay strong when wet.
- Science (debated): Philosophers still argue whether a stone is hard when nobody is testing it. Some say it has the property all by itself. Others say it only has the power to show it when it meets something.

## Story: The Thread That Held at Dawn
*Real: the muslin of Bengal and its names Abrawan, Shabnam and Baft Hawa. Real: the best spinning was done in the damp of early morning and evening, near rivers. Real: cotton thread is a little stronger when damp than when dry. Real: cowrie shells were used as small money in Bengal for centuries. Imagined: Shefali, her aunt Pishi, the village and the merchant's agent, the shell-counting test and its numbers.*

About three hundred years ago, a village stood on a wide brown river in Bengal, not far from a town called Sonargaon. The people there made a cloth so fine that merchants carried it across the sea. Its names were poems. One was Abrawan, flowing water. One was Shabnam, evening dew. One was Baft Hawa, woven air.

Shefali was ten. She lived with her aunt, her Pishi, in a house with walls of woven bamboo. Pishi was the best spinner in the village. She could pull a bundle of cotton into thread so thin that you only saw it when the light fell sideways. Shefali's jobs were small ones. She cleaned the cotton, she fetched water, and she kept quiet while her aunt worked.

One morning, a merchant's agent came walking up from the river. He wore a clean white cloth and carried a thin silver ring. 'The Nawab's household wants one piece,' he said. 'It must be the finest ever made. When it is finished, I must be able to pull all of it through this ring. In one breath.' He set the ring down on the mat. 'Do this, and you will be paid in silver. Fail, and the order goes to the next village.' Everyone looked at Pishi.

Pishi began at once. In the first grey light, the thread came out like a hair of mist and held. Shefali watched it with her breath held too. But as the sun climbed, something went wrong. The thread began to snap. It snapped on the spindle. It snapped on the loom. Pishi tied it, spun again, and it snapped again. By noon, the mat was covered in little broken ends, like white hairs.

The village had opinions. 'The cotton is bad,' said the neighbour. 'Her hands are tired,' said another. 'The river is angry,' said a third. Pishi said nothing. But Shefali saw her hands shake, and she knew they were not tired hands. They were the same hands that had spun the dawn thread.

That night, Shefali could not sleep. She thought about it the way you pull at a knot. If the cotton was bad, it would have been bad at dawn. If the hands were tired, they would have been tired at dawn. Same cotton. Same hands. So something else had changed between the morning and the noon. What?

She did not know. But she knew one way to find out. Her mother was a trader, and she had taught her to count with cowrie shells. People in Bengal used the little white shells as money. Shefali kept a basket of them, all picked out to be the same size.

Before sunrise, she got up. She cut a piece of thread as long as her forearm. She tied a small cup made of leaf to the bottom, and she hung the thread from a bamboo pole. Then she dropped shells into the cup, one at a time, and counted out loud. At ten shells, the thread shivered. At thirteen, it was still there. At fourteen, it snapped. She wrote the number in the dust. Then she did it with a second thread, and a third. Fourteen, thirteen, fourteen.

At noon, she did it all again, with threads from the same ball of cotton, each cut to the same forearm's length. The first one snapped at eleven shells. The second, at twelve. The third, at eleven. Every dawn thread had held more than every noon thread.

Shefali ran to Pishi. 'Count with me,' she said. 'The thread is stronger at dawn.' Pishi had never counted a thread before. She watched the white shells fall, one by one, and her face slowly changed. 'It is the air,' said Shefali. 'In the morning the air by the river is damp. At noon it is dry. Maybe the thread needs the damp.' She ran a wet finger along a noon thread and tested it again. It held until fourteen.

Pishi was quiet for a long time. Then she said, 'My teacher used to say that the thread loves the river. I thought she was speaking poetry.' She looked at the numbers in the dust. 'Perhaps she was speaking the truth in the only words she had.'

After that, the village changed its hours. They spun before sunrise, and again in the evening when the mist rose off the river. At noon, they rested. The weavers set their looms close to the water and kept little bowls beside them, to touch the thread with damp fingers. Pishi spun the finest thread of her life. And every evening, she let Shefali test a thread from each new spool with her shells.

Weeks later, the cloth was finished. The agent held it up, and it was so fine that he could see his own hand through it, as if through mist. When it was spread on the grass, the grass showed through. He lifted a corner and drew all of it through the silver ring in one breath. The ring did not even touch it. He paid in silver, and the cloth went away down the river and across the sea.

Nobody wrote down Pishi's name, or Shefali's. The cloth got a name, and a price, and an owner far away. But in the village the new hours stayed. The mothers said to their daughters, 'Spin while the air is wet.' They did not say why, because by then nobody remembered who had counted.

That kind of muslin is no longer made. The last masters grew old, the special cotton nearly vanished, and the world changed. Today, weavers and scientists are trying to bring it back, and the first thing they have to learn is what Shefali learned. A thread has a strength. But the strength depends on the air it lives in.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a property is not a fixed fact. The strength of cotton thread changes with the dampness of the air. The grown-ups guessed, and blamed the cotton, the hands and the river. Shefali counted. She used the same length, the same shells and the same method every time. So the numbers could be compared. Only then could the real cause show up.

- Ask: In the story, the thread snapped at noon but not at dawn. What did Shefali think had changed?
- Ask: How did Shefali make her test fair? Why did she cut every thread to the same length?
- Ask: Why did she count shells, and not just say 'this one is stronger'?
- Ask: Everyone in the village had a guess. Why was a number better than a guess?
- Ask: Is the thread's strength in the thread, in the air, or in both together? What do you think?
- Ask: Nobody wrote down Shefali's name. Who does a small job at your home that makes everything else work?

Pour with the same cup, one cupful at a time. Make every damp strip damp in the same way, right across its middle with a wet fingertip. Test each one twice.
- **safety:** Keep the plastic bag away from babies and toddlers.
- **record:** Fill in the table. In the last column, write weaker, stronger or about the same.
- **results table:** Material | Dry: cups held | Damp: cups held | What changed?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the toilet paper, the newspaper and the thread. 2. For each one, ask your child to guess: will the damp one hold more water, less, or the same? 3. Ask why.

  *Child mode:* Look at the three materials. Will the damp one hold more, less or the same? Why?
  - Ask: Why did you guess that for the thread? (good: Wet paper tears, so wet thread might break too.; Wet cloth feels heavy and tough.; I am not sure. We have to test it.)
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put two chairs back to back, a little apart. Lay the broom handle across their backs. 2. Wrap the top of a dry toilet paper strip once round the handle, and tape it to itself. 3.   *Child mode:* Hang the strip from the stick. Hang the bag from the pencil. Put the bucket below.
  - Note: For the thread, fold one piece in half. Put the pencil through the loop at the bottom. Tie the two loose ends round the broom handle with a double knot. Two strands make the thread strong enough to hold at least a cup. If the tape slips instead of the strip tearing, do not count that try. Tape it more firmly and start again.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fill the cup to the brim and pour it gently into the bag. Count out loud. 2. Keep adding one cup at a time. 3. When the strip tears, write down how many cups were in the bag. 4. Empty the bag and test the second dry strip. 5. Then test the dry newspaper and the dry thread the same way, twice each.

  *Child mode:* Pour one cup at a time and count. Write the number when it tears.
  - Note: If a strip or thread holds 10 cups and is still there, write 'more than 10' and stop, because the bag may get too full. Pouring with a jerk can tear a strip early. Pour slowly, every time.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hang a new dry strip with the empty bag, as before. 2. Dip a fingertip in the bowl of water. 3. Dab the middle of the strip, right across from edge to edge. Stop when a band about 3 centimetres tall (the whole width of the newspaper strip) looks dark and damp. For the thread, run a wet finger along the middle of both strands. 4. Wait one minute. 5.   - Note: Make the damp band go right across the strip. If one edge stays dry, the dry part still holds the bag. If a damp toilet paper strip tears before any water goes in, write 0. That is a real result. We hang the strip first and dampen it after, because a damp strip is hard to hang without tearing.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child fill in the last column, then compare the changes.  
  *Parent says:* For each material, look at dry and damp. Write weaker, stronger or about the same. Which one changed the most?  
  *Child mode:* Look at dry and damp for each one. Write weaker, stronger or the same.
  - Note: Your numbers depend on your paper, your thread and your cup, so they will not match anyone else's. With a cup of about 200 millilitres, expect roughly this. Dry toilet paper holds about 1 to 6 cups, and dry newspaper about 2 to 8. Both damp papers usually tear before the second cup, often before the first. Paper is held together by joins where its fibres touch, and water loosens these joins. The doubled thread usually holds about 2 to 7 cups, dry or damp. Textile scientists find damp cotton is about one tenth to one fifth stronger. A change that small may not show when you count whole cups, so 'about the same' is an honest result.
  - Check: A newspaper strip held 6 cups of water when dry and 1 cup when damp. What can you say? → **Water made the newspaper much weaker**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: water makes paper much weaker, but not cotton thread. What I saw: the dry toilet paper held 4 cups, and the damp one tore with none. The thread held 5 cups dry and 5 damp. Why: paper is held together where its fibres touch, and water loosens those joins. Thread is held together by its twist. Damp cotton fibres swell a little and grip each other harder, so the thread does not get weaker.
  - Think: Damp cotton thread did not get weaker. Why might this matter to someone who spins very fine cotton thread?
### e4 · The Vanishing Drop: how fast does it soak in? (25 min)
- **question:** How many seconds does one drop of water take to vanish on each surface? Which surfaces never let it in?
- **predict:** Line up the six surfaces. Put the one you think soaks up a drop fastest first, and the one that never soaks it up last. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, make every drop the same size, using the same dropper or the same spoon tip. Put one drop on every piece at the same time, and time all six together. Do three rounds, each time on a fresh spot. Stop a round at 120 seconds if a drop is still there.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart. Draw one bar for each surface. Make each bar as tall as the middle value in seconds.
- **results table:** Surface | Try 1: seconds | Try 2: seconds | Try 3: seconds | Middle value (seconds)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the six pieces. 2. Ask your child to put them in a line, fastest-soaking first.  
  *Parent says:* Which surface will soak up a drop the fastest? Which will never soak it up? Put them in a line, fastest first. Why that order?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the six surfaces. Put them in a line. Fastest to soak first. This is your guess.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the six pieces flat on the tray, a few centimetres apart. 2. Fill the dropper with water. 3. Put one drop on each piece, one after the other, quickly. 4. Start the timer when the last drop lands. 5. Look from the side. When a drop's shiny top is gone, write the seconds beside that piece.  
  *Parent says:* Put one drop on each surface, then start the timer. Watch from the side. Stop when a shiny drop has gone into the material, and write down the seconds.  
  *Child mode:* Put one drop on each piece. Start the timer. Write the seconds when each shiny drop is gone.
  - Note: If the drop is still sitting there after 120 seconds, stop and write 120. That is a result, and it means the surface is waterproof. Use a fresh spot for each try.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do two more rounds. Put each new drop on a fresh spot of the same piece. Write down the seconds each time.  
  *Parent says:* Do two more rounds, six drops each time. Write the seconds. For the plastic, write 120 if the drop is still there.  
  *Child mode:* Do two more rounds. Write the seconds. If a drop is still there after 120, write 120.
  - Note: To find the middle value, put the three numbers in order and take the middle one. If one try is very different, check that the drop was the same size.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the middle values, with one bar for each surface.  
  *Parent says:* Each surface gets one bar. A short bar means the drop vanished fast. A tall bar means it took a long time or never went in.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each surface. A short bar means the drop soaked in fast.
  - Check: In the vanishing drop chart, a very tall bar means the surface is… → **Waterproof or nearly so**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill a plate with water. Shake pepper onto it. Dip a finger in dish soap and touch the middle. Also look at a drop on a colocasia leaf, if you have one.  
  *Parent says:* Pepper floats on the water. Now touch the middle with a soapy finger. What happens? The surface of water acts like a thin, stretchy skin. Soap weakens it.  
  *Child mode:* Sprinkle pepper on a plate of water. Touch the middle with a soapy finger. Watch the pepper!
  - Note: The pepper rushes to the edge. The skin of water, called surface tension, is weaker where the soap touches. The stronger skin around pulls the pepper away. The same skin is why a drop is round on the colocasia leaf. The leaf is covered with tiny wax bumps, so the water cannot spread. Agnes Pockels, a woman in Germany, studied this skin in her kitchen.
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: paper towel is the most absorbent and plastic is waterproof. What I saw: the drop vanished in 2 seconds on paper towel. It was still there after 120 seconds on plastic. Why: the paper towel has tiny gaps that pull water in, and plastic has no gaps.
### e5 · Layers to Darkness: transparent, translucent or opaque? (20 min)
- **question:** How many layers of each material does it take to stop a torch's light?
- **predict:** Guess how many layers of butter paper it will take before the light disappears from the wall. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use the same torch, the same wall distance and the same dim room each time. Add one layer at a time. Count the layers when the light spot on the wall just disappears.
- **safety:** Do not shine the torch into anyone's eyes.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart. Draw one bar for each material. Make each bar as tall as the number of layers.
- **results table:** Material | Transparent, translucent or opaque (1 layer) | Layers until the light disappears
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dim the room. 2. Shine the torch on the wall. 3. Hold each thing in front of the torch. Try the cup, the butter paper, the cloth, the steel plate and the cardboard. 4. Sort each one: transparent, translucent or opaque.  
  *Parent says:* Hold each thing in front of the torch. Transparent means a clear bright spot. Translucent means a blurry glow. Opaque means just a shadow.  
  *Child mode:* Hold each thing in front of the torch. Is the spot on the wall clear, blurry or gone? Sort them.
  - Note: Good answers: the cup is transparent. The butter paper and thin cloth are translucent. The steel plate and cardboard are opaque.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold one sheet of butter paper flat against the front of the torch. 2. Look at the glow on the wall. 3. Add a second sheet. 4. Keep adding, one at a time, counting, until you cannot see any glow on the wall.  
  *Parent says:* Add one sheet at a time and watch the glow. Count the sheets. Stop when the light spot is just gone.  
  *Child mode:* Add one sheet at a time. Count them. Stop when the light is gone.
  - Ask: What do you notice as you add more layers? (good: The light gets dimmer.; The glow gets smaller.; It goes from translucent to opaque.)
  - Note: If you use up all your sheets and a glow still shows, write 'more than' and the number of sheets. That is a real result. A bright torch may need many sheets.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Repeat with tissue paper, thin cloth and newspaper. Write down the number of layers for each.  
  *Parent says:* Now do the same with tissue paper, cloth and newspaper. Which one blocks the light with the fewest layers?  
  *Child mode:* Do the same with tissue paper, cloth and newspaper. Count the layers each time.
- **s4 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart of the layers for each material.  
  *Parent says:* Each material gets a bar. A taller bar means it took more layers to block the light.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each material. A taller bar means more layers.
  - Check: One sheet of butter paper is translucent. A tall stack is opaque. What does this show? → **Translucent or opaque can depend on thickness**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: more layers make a material more opaque. What I saw: one butter paper let a glow through, but 5 layers stopped it. Why: each layer scatters and soaks up some light, so the more layers there are, the less gets through.

## Worksheets
### Property Detective (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 3, says four things about her steel lunch box. Which one is about the box, and not about the steel?  
   Options: It is shaped like a rectangle / It is shiny / It keeps water out / It rings when I tap it  
   **Answer:** It is shaped like a rectangle. *Why:* Shape belongs to the object. The same steel could be made into a round tumbler or a flat spoon. Shiny, waterproof and ringing stay true of steel in any shape, so they are properties of the material. A strong answer says why: change the shape and these three stay the same.
2. (sort) Which words tell you what a material is like? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** Bends easily → Tells what the material is like; Soaks up water → Tells what the material is like; Easy to scratch → Tells what the material is like; Lets light through → Tells what the material is like; Costs 50 rupees → Does not; Bought in Kochi → Does not; My favourite → Does not. *Why:* A property is something you can find out by looking, touching or testing the material itself. Price, where it was bought and whether you like it tell you about you or the shop, not about the material.
3. (match) Match each property to a simple test.  
   **Answer:** Hard → Try to scratch it; Flexible → Try to bend it; Waterproof → Drip water on it; Transparent → Look through it; Strong → Hang weights from it. *Why:* Each property has a test that shows it. Hard is about scratching. Flexible is about bending. Waterproof is about water staying out. Transparent is about seeing through. Strong is about how much weight it holds before breaking.
4. (fill) A rubber band stretches when you pull it and goes back to its old shape when you let go. This property is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['elastic']. *Why:* Elastic means it springs back after being stretched or bent. A paper clip that stays bent is not elastic.
5. (truefalse) A coir rope is soft, so it must be weak.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A coir rope is soft to touch, yet it can lift a full bucket from a well. Soft is about scratching or squashing. Strong is about how much weight it holds before it breaks. They are different properties, so one does not tell you the other.
6. (mcq) A girl leaves a steel spoon and a wooden spoon standing in a pot of hot rasam. After two minutes she touches the top of each handle. What will she feel?  
   Options: The steel handle is hot, the wooden one is only warm / Both are equally hot / The wooden handle is hotter / Both are still cold  
   **Answer:** The steel handle is hot, the wooden one is only warm. *Why:* Steel is a conductor of heat: heat travels through it quickly, all the way up the handle. Wood is an insulator: heat moves through it slowly. A strong answer names the property and says what it does to the heat.
7. (order) Put these in order, from the one that lets MOST light through to the one that lets LEAST through: a wooden door, a clear glass window, a thin cotton dupatta held up to the window, a folded bedsheet.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Clear glass is transparent: you see clearly through it. A thin dupatta is translucent: light gets through, but blurred. A folded bedsheet lets only a little light through, because there are more layers. A wooden door is opaque: no light at all.
8. (write) A bath towel and an umbrella are both made of cloth. The towel soaks up water. The umbrella keeps water out. How can two cloths behave in opposite ways? Use what you saw in the vanishing drop test.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the two cloths are different materials, or are made differently. Towel cotton has loose loops with many tiny gaps, so water creeps in and the drop vanishes fast. Umbrella cloth is woven very tightly and often coated with plastic, so the drop sits on top, like on the plastic bag.. *Why:* The word 'cloth' does not tell you the properties. What the fibres are and how tightly they are packed decide whether water gets in.
9. (mcq) Divya, a girl in Class 3, says, 'My pencil is made of wood. So wood is one of its properties.' What is her mistake?  
   Options: Wood is the material. A property would be a word like light or easy to sharpen / There is no mistake / Pencils are not made of anything / Wood is the object  
   **Answer:** Wood is the material. A property would be a word like light or easy to sharpen. *Why:* The material is what a thing is made of. A property says what that material is like. Divya mixed up the two. Picking 'wood is the object' mixes it up the other way, because the pencil is the object.
10. (draw) Draw one object from your kitchen. Write its material next to it. Then label three properties of that material, and how you found each one out.  
   **Answer:** Any kitchen object with its correct material and three true properties. For example, a steel tumbler: shiny (I see my face in it), hard (my fingernail cannot scratch it), waterproof (water stays inside).. *Why:* This checks that the child can tell object, material and property apart, and that each property comes from looking, touching or testing.
11. (perspective) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3, needs a new water bottle for school. Three people in her family give advice.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks one bottle and names the properties that matter for a child at school: strong and not brittle, light, transparent, or no taste. It then admits a fair point from another side, for example that glass is transparent but brittle, or that steel is strong but heavier and opaque.. *Why:* No material wins on every property. The job, and who does it, decides which properties matter most. Here a dropped bottle is a real risk, so many children choose steel, but there is no single right answer.
12. (mcq) You push three strips over the edge of a table, the same length each: a steel ruler, a plastic ruler and a strip of cotton cloth. Which tip will droop the most?  
   Options: The cloth / The steel ruler / The plastic ruler / All the same, because they are the same length  
   **Answer:** The cloth. *Why:* Cloth is the most flexible, so it hangs straight down. The steel ruler is the most rigid and hardly droops. Picking 'all the same' forgets that the material changed, and the material decides how flexible the strip is.
13. (truefalse) Big, heavy things always sink in water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A big wooden log floats, and a tiny steel pin sinks. What matters is how tightly packed the material is compared with water, not how big or heavy the object is. Many people believe this, so it is worth testing with things from the kitchen.
14. (write) In the snap, bend or spring back test, you pushed things past their limit. Pick one thing that snapped, one that stayed bent and one that sprang back. Name the property each one showed.  
   **Answer:** For example: the chalk snapped, so it is brittle. The paper clip stayed bent, so it is flexible but not elastic. The plastic ruler sprang back, so it is elastic. A strong answer uses the child's own results.. *Why:* Brittle, flexible and elastic are three different ways a material can answer a push. The child's own test is the evidence.
15. (fill) A sponge soaks up water into its tiny holes. A material that soaks up water is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['absorbent']. *Why:* Absorbent means it takes water into its gaps. The opposite is waterproof.
16. (mcq) A girl wants a cover for her notebook that will not be spoiled when she walks home in the rain. Which property matters most?  
   Options: Waterproof / Absorbent / Transparent / Brittle  
   **Answer:** Waterproof. *Why:* The job is to keep rain off the pages, so the cover must keep water out. An absorbent cover would soak up the rain and wet the book.
### Measure and Reason (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (order) Read the clues. A steel key scratches a copper coin. The coin scratches a wooden pencil. The pencil's wood makes a groove in candle wax. Put them in order from SOFTEST to HARDEST.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* If one thing scratches another, the first is harder. So wax is below wood, wood is below copper, and copper is below steel. Putting things in order from clues like these is how the Mohs scale of hardness was built.
2. (mcq) A pressure cooker has a steel body and a black plastic handle. Why did the makers use two different materials?  
   Options: Each part has a different job: the steel carries heat to the food, the plastic keeps heat away from your hand / Plastic is stronger than steel / Steel is too costly for a handle / The plastic handle only makes the cooker look nice  
   **Answer:** Each part has a different job: the steel carries heat to the food, the plastic keeps heat away from your hand. *Why:* The body must carry heat from the flame to the food, so it needs a conductor of heat. The handle must stop heat reaching your hand, so it needs an insulator. One object can use several materials, each chosen for the property its part needs. Picking 'stronger' misses the point: the handle's job is about heat.
3. (write) A girl rolls a sheet of kitchen foil into a tight ball and drops it into a bucket of water. It sinks. Then she flattens the same foil into a little boat. What do you predict will happen, and why?  
   **Answer:** The boat floats. A strong answer says the material is the same, but the shape has changed. The boat is hollow and full of air, so all together it is less tightly packed than the water it pushes aside. The ball is solid foil, which is more tightly packed than water.. *Why:* Sinking and floating depend on the object's shape as well as its material. This is why a steel ship floats.
4. (mcq) A strip held 6, 5 and 6 cups of water in three tries. Another strip held 2, 7 and 4. Which result can you trust more?  
   Options: The first, because the three numbers are close / The second, because it reached 7 / Neither, because tests are never reliable / They are equally reliable  
   **Answer:** The first, because the three numbers are close. *Why:* When repeated tries give close numbers, the result is reliable. When they are far apart, something changed between tries, such as the method. Picking the second because of the biggest number would be trusting the luckiest try.
5. (mcq) Look at the table. Foil and cloth both drooped 10 centimetres, which is the whole way down. How could you find out which of the two is more flexible?  
   Options: Make both strips stick out only 5 centimetres, so neither hangs straight down / Use a longer strip of cloth / Measure them again in the same way / You cannot ever find out  
   **Answer:** Make both strips stick out only 5 centimetres, so neither hangs straight down. *Why:* When both strips hang straight down, the test has reached its limit and cannot tell them apart. A shorter overhang makes a gentler test. Measuring again in the same way gives the same tie. Changing only one strip's length is not fair.
6. (match) Match each material to what is happening inside it.  
   **Answer:** Diamond → Each atom is locked to four neighbours; Graphite → Atoms lie in flat sheets that slide; Metal → Some electrons roam between the atoms; Rubber → Long tangled chains that straighten when pulled. *Why:* These pictures explain properties. Locked atoms make diamond hard. Sliding sheets make graphite soft. Roaming electrons make metals shiny and good at carrying heat. Tangled chains make rubber stretchy.
7. (sort) Sort these into conductors and insulators of heat.  
   **Answer:** Base of a steel pan → Conductor of heat; Wooden spoon handle → Insulator of heat; Woollen shawl → Insulator of heat; Aluminium foil → Conductor of heat; Plastic tiffin lid → Insulator of heat; Copper vessel → Conductor of heat. *Why:* Metals carry heat quickly. Wood, wool and plastic carry it slowly. A woollen shawl keeps you warm because it traps air, and air is a poor conductor. It does not make heat.
8. (mcq) You want to know whether a rubber band or a thread stretches more. Which is the fairest test?  
   Options: Start both at the same length and hang the same weight. Measure the stretch in centimetres / Pull the thread harder than the rubber band / Start with a long thread and a short rubber band / Pull both and see which feels stretchier  
   **Answer:** Start both at the same length and hang the same weight. Measure the stretch in centimetres. *Why:* A fair test changes only the material. The starting length and the weight must be the same, and the stretch measured with a ruler. Picking different lengths or pulls changes more than one thing.
9. (truefalse) On the Mohs scale, diamond at 10 is exactly 10 times as hard as talc at 1.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The numbers only give the order. Diamond is far harder than the next mineral, corundum at 9, not just a little harder. A ladder tells you who is above whom, not by how much.
10. (fill) Water climbing up through the tiny gaps in a paper towel is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['wicking']. *Why:* Wicking is water creeping up through tiny gaps, even upwards against gravity. It is how a diya wick lifts oil and how a towel soaks up a spill.
11. (write) Name one property that matters most for school shoes, and one for a school bag. Explain your choices.  
   **Answer:** For shoes, grip or strong and flexible, because shoes must bend with the foot and not slip. For a bag, strong and waterproof, because it holds heavy books and must survive rain. Any sensible choice with a reason is right.. *Why:* The job decides which property matters. A good answer says what the object must do, then names the property that does it.
12. (draw) Draw a torch beam meeting three materials: clear glass, butter paper and a steel plate. Show what the light does in each.  
   **Answer:** Clear glass: the beam goes straight through. Butter paper: the beam goes in but spreads out in all directions. Steel plate: the beam stops or bounces back.. *Why:* Transparent materials let the light pass straight. Translucent ones scatter it. Opaque ones soak it up or bounce it back.
13. (perspective) Is glass a strong material or a weak one? Three people disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and uses the right words: strong (holds a big load), brittle (snaps instead of bending). It may say that Sumathi and Ananya are both right about different things: glass holds a steady load well but cannot take a sudden knock. The scientist's view joins the two.. *Why:* Strong and brittle are different properties. However strong a material is, a small crack can grow and break it. That is why the answer depends on what the glass has to do.
14. (mcq) Nisha, a girl in Class 4, tests a dry newspaper strip at noon and a damp cotton thread at dawn. The thread holds more. She says, 'Damp makes things stronger.' What is wrong with her test?  
   Options: She changed two things at once: the material and the dampness / Nothing, it is a fair test / She should have tested at night / Newspaper and thread cannot be tested  
   **Answer:** She changed two things at once: the material and the dampness. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. Nisha changed the material and the dampness together, so she cannot tell which one made the difference. To test dampness, she must test the same material dry and damp.
15. (write) Look at your own dry or damp results. Which material lost the most strength when it was damp? Use your numbers to show it. Then say why water did that.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names the material, gives the dry and damp numbers and the difference. For example, newspaper held 6 cups dry and 1 cup damp, so it lost 5 cups. It explains that water gets between the paper fibres and loosens the joins that hold them together.. *Why:* Evidence means your own numbers. A reason says what happens inside the material.
16. (truefalse) A strip of kitchen foil folded into three layers bends more easily than a single layer of the same foil.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Three layers are thicker, so the strip is stiffer and droops less. The material is the same, but its thickness changes how flexible it is.
### Think Like a Materials Scientist (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) It is easy to measure how long a rope is. Why is it harder to measure how hard a stone is?  
   **Answer:** Length has one clear way to measure it, with a ruler. 'Hard' could mean hard to scratch, hard to dent or hard to break, and these tests give different orders. So scientists use several hardness scales.. *Why:* Hardness is really a family of properties. Scientists must say which test they used before they give a number.
2. (mcq) Why do metals feel cold and look shiny?  
   Options: Some of their electrons roam freely, carrying heat and bouncing light / Metals are always colder than other things / Metal atoms are bigger / Metals have no atoms in them  
   **Answer:** Some of their electrons roam freely, carrying heat and bouncing light. *Why:* In a metal, some electrons are not tied to one atom. They carry heat away from your hand quickly, so metal feels cold. They also bounce light back, which makes metals shiny. Picking 'always colder' is the idea this unit tested and rejected.
3. (write) Invent a test for a property we have not tested, such as how sticky a tape is. Say what you would measure, what you would keep the same, and the unit.  
   **Answer:** For stickiness: stick the same length of tape to the same smooth table. Hang a small bag from the free end. Pour in water one spoon at a time until the tape peels off. Keep the tape length, the table and the way I press it the same. The unit is spoons of water.. *Why:* A good test measures one thing, keeps everything else the same, and gives a number with a unit.
4. (sort) Which property matters most for each job?  
   **Answer:** A bus window → See-through; A rope for lifting a bucket from a well → Strong; A cloth for wiping a spill → Absorbent; A tea cosy → Insulator of heat; A rubber band → Elastic. *Why:* The job decides the property. The window must let you see out. The rope must not snap. The cloth must soak up water. The tea cosy must keep heat in. The band must stretch and spring back.
5. (sort) What happens to each material when it gets damp?  
   **Answer:** Newspaper → Weaker; Toilet paper → Weaker; Plastic carry bag → About the same; Cotton thread → A little stronger. *Why:* Paper loses strength because water loosens the joins between its fibres. Plastic does not take in water, so it stays about the same. Cotton fibres hold together a little better when damp, so cotton gets a little stronger. That is why the spinners in the story worked in the damp morning air.
6. (mcq) A probe on the Moon measured the soil. It was about 70 degrees Celsius on top. It was about 10 degrees below zero just eight centimetres down. What does this show about Moon soil?  
   Options: It is a very good insulator of heat / It is a very good conductor of heat / It is a transparent material / It is hard to scratch  
   **Answer:** It is a very good insulator of heat. *Why:* If heat flowed easily through it, the top and the deeper layer would soon be about the same temperature. A huge difference in a few centimetres means heat flows very slowly. That is an insulator. Picking 'conductor' reverses it.
7. (truefalse) A hard material can never be flexible.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A steel spring is hard to scratch, and it is also elastic. A thin plastic ruler is fairly hard and flexible. Hard means hard to scratch. Flexible means it bends without breaking. They are different properties, so a material can have any mix.
8. (mcq) A scientist says, 'Cotton thread is strong.' What should she add to be more accurate?  
   Options: How long and how thick the thread was, and how damp the air was / How old the scientist is / What colour the thread is / Nothing, because it is always true  
   **Answer:** How long and how thick the thread was, and how damp the air was. *Why:* A thread's strength depends on its thickness and on the dampness of the air. The same words 'cotton thread' could describe very different results. Picking 'nothing' is the idea that properties never change, which the story showed is wrong.
9. (draw) Draw a zoom-in: a cotton thread, then one fibre, then a pattern of atoms joined together. Label the three pictures.  
   **Answer:** A thread made of many fibres twisted together. One fibre as a long thin strand. At the smallest scale, tiny balls joined in chains.. *Why:* Properties come from small things joined in patterns. Each zoom-in shows a smaller building block.
10. (write) Invent a material for the roof of an auto-rickshaw. List three properties it must have. For each one, say how you would test it at home.  
   **Answer:** For example: waterproof (drip water on it and check the underside after five minutes), strong (hang a bag from a strip and add water cup by cup until it tears), and an insulator of heat (put two pieces in the sun and feel the underside after ten minutes). Light and flexible are good answers too. Any three properties that the job needs, each with a fair test, are right.. *Why:* Inventing a material means knowing which properties the job needs, and being able to test for them.
11. (perspective) Graphene is a sheet of carbon only one atom thick. It is the strongest material ever measured. People disagree about what it means for the future.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the unit's idea that the job decides which properties matter. It weighs strength against cost, how easy the material is to make, and how well we know it. It admits a fair point from another side, for example that the strongest material is not always the best choice.. *Why:* This is a real debate. Graphene was first made in 2004, and it is used in a few products, but steel, concrete and plastic still do most jobs. Being the strongest is only one property.
12. (mcq) Ghee is solid on a cold winter morning in Delhi. It is runny on a hot afternoon in Chennai. What does this show?  
   Options: A property can change with temperature / It is two different materials / Ghee in Chennai is not real ghee / Ghee has no properties  
   **Answer:** A property can change with temperature. *Why:* The ghee is the same material. Heat changes whether it is solid or runny. This is why a test must say the temperature, just as it must say how damp or thick the material was.
13. (write) What if every material in your home were waterproof, even towels, paper and clay pots? Name two things that would stop working, and explain why.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names jobs that need absorbent materials and says why. For example, a towel could not dry you. Paper could not take ink. A clay pot, a matka, keeps water cool because a little water soaks through its walls and dries away outside. A waterproof pot could not do that.. *Why:* No property is good or bad by itself. Being waterproof helps an umbrella, but many jobs need the opposite.
14. (mcq) A boy in your class says, 'Rubber is a good material and glass is a bad one.' Which reply is best?  
   Options: Each is good for some jobs: rubber for a tyre, glass for spectacles / The boy is right, because glass breaks / The boy is wrong, because glass is always better / Materials cannot be compared  
   **Answer:** Each is good for some jobs: rubber for a tyre, glass for spectacles. *Why:* A material is good or bad only for a job. Glass is transparent, so it is right for spectacles, where rubber would be useless. Rubber is elastic and tough, so it is right for a tyre.
15. (fill) Hard means hard to scratch. A material that takes a knock without cracking, like leather or rubber, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['tough']. *Why:* Tough means it soaks up a knock without cracking. Glass is hard but not tough. Leather is soft but tough. Scientists test hard, strong, stiff and tough separately, because one does not tell you the others.
16. (write) Look back at every test you did in this unit. Which result surprised you most? Write what you guessed, what really happened, and what you think now.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer names a real test, gives the guess and the result honestly, and says what changed in the child's thinking. For example: 'I guessed the damp thread would break first. It held more cups of water. Now I think water weakens paper but helps cotton.'. *Why:* Changing your mind because of evidence is what scientists do. A guess that turns out wrong is useful, because it shows you what you did not know.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A girl writes four words about her steel tumbler. Which one is NOT a property of steel?  
   Options: Bought in Madurai / Shiny / Hard / Waterproof  
   **Answer:** Bought in Madurai. *Why:* Where something was bought tells you about the shop, not the material. Shiny, hard and waterproof tell what steel is like, so they are properties. Picking 'hard' may come from thinking a property must be something you see.
2. (mcq) A steel spoon and a steel tumbler are different objects. What is the same about them?  
   Options: The material, so the properties / The shape / The use / The size  
   **Answer:** The material, so the properties. *Why:* Both are steel, so both are hard, shiny and cold to touch. Picking shape, use or size mixes up the object and the material. Those are properties of the object, not of the steel.
3. (mcq) A fingernail scratches chalk. A steel nail scratches a fingernail. Which is hardest?  
   Options: The steel nail / The fingernail / The chalk  
   **Answer:** The steel nail. *Why:* If A scratches B, then A is harder than B. The nail scratches the fingernail, and the fingernail scratches the chalk. So the nail is hardest and the chalk is softest. Picking the chalk reverses the clue.
4. (mcq) Strip A drooped 2 centimetres in the droop test. Strip B drooped 9 centimetres. Which is more flexible?  
   Options: Strip B / Strip A / They are the same  
   **Answer:** Strip B. *Why:* A big drop means the strip bent a lot, so B is more flexible. A drooped only a little, so it is more rigid. Picking A is the common mistake of thinking a smaller number means more flexible.
5. (mcq) In the dry or damp test, a dry toilet paper strip held 2 cups of water and a dry newspaper strip held 6. How many times as much did the newspaper hold?  
   Options: 3 times / 4 times / 6 times / 2 times  
   **Answer:** 3 times. *Why:* 6 divided by 2 is 3. So the newspaper held 3 times as much. 'How many times as much' asks how many 2s make 6. 2 + 2 + 2 = 6, so the answer is 3. Picking 2 times or 4 times is a guess without working it out.
6. (mcq) Glass is the right material for a window and the wrong one for a bridge. Why?  
   Options: The job decides which properties matter / Glass is a bad material / Bridges are not made of anything / Glass is too hard  
   **Answer:** The job decides which properties matter. *Why:* A window needs to be transparent. A bridge needs to be strong. Glass is transparent but brittle. No property is good or bad by itself. Picking 'glass is a bad material' misses that glass is perfect for its own job.
7. (mcq) In the story, Shefali cut every thread to the same length and used shells of the same size. Why?  
   Options: So that only the dampness changed, and the numbers could be compared / So that the shells would not fall out / So that the thread would look neat / Because the village told her to  
   **Answer:** So that only the dampness changed, and the numbers could be compared. *Why:* A fair test changes one thing. Same length and same shells meant that any difference in the count came from the dampness. 'Neat' and 'the village' are not reasons that make numbers comparable.
8. (mcq) A paper clip stays bent. A plastic ruler springs back. A dry spaghetti stick snaps. Which one is brittle?  
   Options: The spaghetti / The paper clip / The ruler / All three  
   **Answer:** The spaghetti. *Why:* Brittle means it snaps or shatters instead of bending. The spaghetti snaps. The paper clip bends but stays bent, and the ruler springs back, which is called elastic. Picking the paper clip mixes up 'bends and stays bent' with 'breaks'.
9. (mcq) A drop on paper towel vanished in 2 seconds. A drop on a plastic bag was still there after 2 minutes. What does this show about the bag?  
   Options: It is waterproof / It is very absorbent / It is very heavy / The test did not work  
   **Answer:** It is waterproof. *Why:* A drop that stays on top means the water cannot get in. That is waterproof. Picking 'very absorbent' reads the result backwards. Picking 'the test did not work' is a common reaction to a zero. But a drop that stays is a real answer.
10. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still not fully understand?  
   Options: What exactly glass is, and why a liquid turns into glass / That a steel nail is harder than chalk / That wet cotton is stronger than dry / That rubber stretches  
   **Answer:** What exactly glass is, and why a liquid turns into glass. *Why:* Glass is an open puzzle. Its atoms are jumbled like a liquid's but stuck like a solid's. A famous physicist called it one of the deepest unsolved problems of solid matter. The other three have been tested again and again.
11. (mcq) Toilet paper falls apart when it gets wet. Is that always a bad property?  
   Options: No, it depends on the job / Yes, weak is always bad / Yes, because paper should be strong / No, because toilet paper is not a material  
   **Answer:** No, it depends on the job. *Why:* Toilet paper that falls apart in water does not block the pipes, so for its job that is useful. Weak is only bad when the job needs strong. Picking 'always bad' treats a property as good or bad on its own.

## Applications
- Kitchens use steel for pans, because steel carries heat. They use wood or plastic for handles, which do not.
- Raincoats and umbrellas are plastic or coated cloth, because they must be waterproof.
- Towels and dishcloths are cotton, because cotton is absorbent.
- Windows are glass, because glass is transparent. Bathroom windows are frosted, because translucent glass lets in light but hides us.
- Bridges and railway lines use steel, because steel is both strong and hard.
- Cricket bats are made of willow wood, which is strong but a little springy and does not split.
- Wires are copper or aluminium, because metals carry electricity and heat well. Their covering is plastic, which does not.
- Jewellers use a heat probe to tell a diamond from glass, because a diamond carries heat much faster.
- Builders in hot, dry places make thick walls of clay or stone. Heat takes many hours to pass through them, so rooms stay cool in the day.
- Engineers choose bamboo for scaffolding in India because it is strong and flexible and cheap.
- Mattresses and pillows use foam or cotton, because soft, springy materials are comfortable.
- Safety helmets and shoes use hard shells and soft padding, because each material does a different job.
- Geologists test rocks with a scratch test and a hand lens. They use the Mohs scale in the field.
- Space agencies, including ISRO, choose materials for rockets and landers that stay strong in heat, cold and vibration.
- Doctors use materials in the body, like steel and plastic joints and dental fillings. These must be strong, safe and not rust.

## Open questions
- **What exactly is glass? Is it a solid, a very slow liquid, or something else?** Glass has atoms jumbled like a liquid's but stuck in place like a solid's. In 1995 the physicist Philip Anderson wrote that glass may be the deepest unsolved problem in solid physics. Scientists still argue about why a liquid turns into glass when it cools. One old idea is wrong, though: glass does not slowly flow at room temperature. Old windows that are thicker at the bottom were made that way.
- **Can we work out a material's properties before we make it?** In 2023, a computer program from Google DeepMind predicted 2.2 million new crystals, and 380,000 looked stable. Some chemists said many were not really new or useful. Nobody knows yet how many will turn out to be good materials.
- **Is there a material harder than diamond?** Computer models say a rare form of diamond with a different atom pattern, called lonsdaleite, could be harder. It is named after Kathleen Lonsdale. In 2014 other scientists said that natural samples are not a separate material. Whether a pure one can be made is still argued.
- **How does a spider spin silk so tough, from water and a fly's meal, at room temperature?** Scientists have copied the chemical recipe. But nobody has made fibre as good as the real thing in large amounts without a spider.
- **What is the strongest material that can possibly exist?** Graphene is the strongest ever measured. A computer model says nuclear pasta, inside a neutron star, could be far stronger. Nobody knows whether anything stronger could exist, or could ever be made.
- **Can we make materials that mend themselves, like skin?** Some plastics and concretes in laboratories can close small cracks. None yet matches living things, which heal again and again for decades.
- **Why is hardness so hard to define?** Scratching, denting and bouncing back are different tests, and they do not always give the same order. Scientists use several hardness scales. There is still no single number that means 'hard'.
- **What properties are there that we have not yet thought to test?** Strength, hardness and shine were noticed long ago. Others, like how a material behaves at one atom thick, were only found when new tools arrived. We cannot know what we have not yet looked for.

## Connected maths
- In the droop test, a thin card strip dropped 3 centimetres. A paper strip dropped 9 centimetres. How many times as far did the paper drop? **3 times.**. The card dropped 3 centimetres and the paper dropped 9. 9 divided by 3 is 3. So the paper dropped 3 times as far. You can check: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the vanishing drop test, one drop took 2 seconds on paper towel and 6 on cotton cloth. It took 25 on newspaper and 70 on printer paper. Draw a bar chart. Then say which surface is the third most absorbent. **Newspaper is the third most absorbent. Here is the bar chart.**. Draw one bar for each, as tall as its number of seconds. A shorter time means a more absorbent surface. Put the times in order, from shortest to longest: 2, 6, 25, 70. The shortest is the paper towel. The next is the cotton. The third is the newspaper, at 25 seconds. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- In the dry or damp test, a dry newspaper strip held 8 cups of water. A damp strip of the same newspaper held only 1 cup. Is 1 cup more or less than a quarter of 8 cups? **Less than a quarter. A quarter of 8 cups is 2 cups, and the damp strip held only 1.**. A quarter of 8 is 8 divided by 4, which is 2. You can check: 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8. The damp strip held 1 cup, which is less than 2. In fact 1 cup is half of a quarter, or one eighth, of 8 cups. So the damp strip held 7 cups fewer than the dry strip. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)
- A family tests 12 things in the Kitchen Float Lab. 7 floated. How many sank? Of the 7 that floated, 3 were wooden. How many floaters were not wooden? **5 sank, and 4 floaters were not wooden.**. 12 things in all, and 7 floated. 12 take away 7 leaves 5 that sank. Of the 7 floaters, 3 were wooden. 7 take away 3 leaves 4 floaters that were not wooden. You can check: 7 + 5 = 12, and 3 + 4 = 7. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- Look at the ruler in the picture. It shows how far a paper strip dropped in the droop test. Each small mark is one centimetre. What is the reading? **7 centimetres.**. The tip of the strip reaches the mark after 6 and before 8. It sits on the mark between them, which is 7. So the strip dropped 7 centimetres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L3`)
- In the Property Trumps project, the most flexible strip dropped 10 centimetres. The paper strip dropped 8 centimetres. The best one gets a score of 5. What flexible score out of 5 does the paper get? **4.**. Multiply the paper's drop by 5. 8 times 5 is 40. Then divide by the biggest drop: 40 divided by 10 is 4. So the paper scores 4 out of 5. You can check: 10 times 5 ÷ 10 is 5 for the best strip. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
