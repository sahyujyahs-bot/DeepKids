# Growing old: how a body changes from baby to elder
`sci.body.growing-old.L3`

**Summary.** A grandmother holds her phone far from her face to read it, while a child reads it close up. Every person goes through the same stages: baby, child, teenager, adult and older adult. As the years pass, hair turns grey, skin springs back more slowly, and the lens in the eye gets stiffer. Your body makes millions of new cells every second, but some parts, like the middle of the eye lens, are never replaced. Scientists still argue about why bodies age at all.

**Intro.** *Look at the back of your hand. Now look at the back of the hand of the oldest person in your home. What is different? What is the same?* Every grandparent you know was once a baby. They had no teeth. They could not walk. Their head was huge compared to their body. One day you will be as old as them. Your hair may turn white, and your skin may fold into soft lines. Why does a body change like this? And how much of you is new, and how much is as old as you are?

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the stages of a human life in order: baby, child, teenager, adult and older adult.
1. I can describe how a body changes at each stage, using examples like teeth, height, hair, skin and eyes.
2. I can run a fair test that compares people of different ages, and show the results in a table and a bar chart.
3. I can explain that the body is always making new cells, but some cells stay with us all our lives.
4. I can say what gets harder and what can get better as people grow old, and how we can help elders.
5. I can say what scientists agree about ageing, and what they still argue about.
6. I can talk gently about the end of a life as part of every life cycle.
7. I can explain big ideas about ageing: tiny caps on our genes, a grandmother idea, and animals that barely seem to age.

## Terms
- **Life cycle**: All the stages a living thing goes through, from the start of their life to the end. *e.g.* A mango tree starts as a seed, grows, makes mangoes with new seeds, grows old and one day dies.
- **Life stage**: One part of a life cycle, when the body is a certain way. *e.g.* Being a baby is one life stage. Being a grandparent is often another.
- **Infant**: A baby in their first year of life. *e.g.* Your baby cousin who cannot walk yet is an infant.
- **Teenager**: A person aged about 13 to 19. Their body grows fast and changes into a grown-up shape. *e.g.* An older cousin whose voice is getting deeper is a teenager.
- **Adult**: A person whose body has finished growing taller. In India, a person is called an adult in law from age 18. *e.g.* Your parents and teachers are adults.
- **Older adult or elder**: A person in the later part of life, often over 60. Many elders are grandparents. *e.g.* A grandmother who has lived 70 years is an older adult.
- **Ageing**: The slow changes a body goes through as the years pass. *e.g.* Grey hair and reading glasses are signs of ageing.
- **Cell**: The tiny living building block that bodies are made of. Most cells are too small to see without a microscope. *e.g.* Your skin, blood and bones are all made of cells.
- **Collagen**: A strong, stretchy material in skin that makes it springy. The body makes less of it as we get older. *e.g.* A young child's skin springs back fast when pinched, because it has lots of collagen.
- **Pigment**: A material that gives something its colour. The pigment in hair and skin is called melanin. *e.g.* Black hair has lots of dark pigment. White hair has almost none.
- **Lens**: The clear, bendy part inside your eye. It changes shape so that things look sharp. *e.g.* When you read a book up close, your lens gets fatter to make the words sharp.
- **Near point**: The closest distance at which you can still see something sharply. *e.g.* A child can read a page 10 cm from the nose. Many grandparents need it 50 cm away.
- **Lifespan**: How long a living thing lives, from birth to death. *e.g.* The longest checked human lifespan is 122 years.
- **Life expectancy**: How many years, on average, people born in a certain place and time are expected to live. *e.g.* In India today, life expectancy is about 70 years.
- **Cell renewal**: The body making new cells to replace old or worn-out ones. *e.g.* The outer layer of your skin is replaced about every month.
- **Death**: When a living thing's body stops working for good. It is the last part of every life cycle. *e.g.* Old leaves fall from a tree and become soil. A very old pet may die peacefully.

## Explanations
### A life has stages · *defined*
**Definition:** Every human life goes through the same stages in the same order: baby, child, teenager, adult and older adult.

Nobody skips a stage. Everybody you know is in one of them right now. The stages do not have sharp edges. Nobody wakes up one morning and is suddenly a teenager. Bodies change slowly, a little every day. Each stage also has its own jobs. Babies learn to walk and talk. Children learn and play. Adults often work and care for others. Elders often teach, remember and help raise the young.
- e.g. Your grandmother was a baby, then a girl, then a young woman, then a mother, and now a grandmother.
- e.g. A newborn baby needs others for everything. An 8-year-old can dress and feed themselves.
- e.g. A mango tree and a dog also have life stages, but they move through them at different speeds.
### A baby's body is different · *defined*
**Definition:** A baby's body is not a small adult body. It has different proportions, softer bones and it grows faster than at any other time of life.

A newborn is about 50 centimetres long. By their first birthday it is about 75 centimetres. That is the fastest growing a person ever does. A baby's head is about one quarter of their whole length. In an adult, the head is only about one eighth. A baby's skull has soft spots, where the bones have not joined yet. This lets the skull grow with the brain. A baby is born with about 300 bones and soft parts that will become bone. Many of them join together as the child grows. An adult has 206 bones.
- e.g. A baby's legs are short compared to their body. In an adult, the legs are about half of the whole height.
- e.g. Baby teeth are already hiding inside a baby's gums at birth.
- e.g. A newborn's soft spot on top of the head closes by about age two.
### Childhood and the teenage growth spurt · *defined*
**Definition:** In childhood the body grows steadily, about 5 to 6 centimetres a year. In the teenage years it grows fast again, then stops growing taller.

Between about age 6 and 12, milk teeth fall out and adult teeth grow in. Bones get longer at their ends, in places called growth plates. In the teenage years there is a growth spurt, a time of very fast growing. Bodies also slowly change into a grown-up shape. Then the growth plates close and turn to solid bone. After that, a person does not grow taller any more. This usually happens by the late teens or early twenties.
- e.g. An older cousin may grow 8 or 10 centimetres in one year as a teenager.
- e.g. Losing a milk tooth at 6 or 7 is a sign the body is moving on to the next stage.
- e.g. Your feet often grow fast before the rest of you does, so shoes stop fitting first.
### The grown-up years · *defined*
**Definition:** Adults have stopped growing taller. Their bodies are usually at their strongest in their twenties and thirties, and then begin to change slowly.

In the twenties and thirties, muscles, bones, heart and lungs are usually at their strongest. Bones are at their thickest at about age 30. From around age 40, small changes begin. Many people notice they need to hold small print further away. A few grey hairs may appear. Muscles slowly shrink if they are not used. These changes are slow and different for every person. Exercise, good food, sleep and friends make a big difference at every age.
- e.g. Many top runners and footballers are in their twenties.
- e.g. Your parent may have started wearing reading glasses at about 40 or 45.
- e.g. A farmer of 50 who works in the fields every day can be stronger than an office worker of 30 who sits all day.
### Why hair turns grey · *defined*
**Definition:** Hair turns grey and white when the cells at the root of each hair stop making colour, called pigment.

Each hair grows from a tiny pocket in the skin. In that pocket are pigment cells. They put colour into the hair as it grows. As we get older, these cells slowly run out. The new hair grows without colour, so it is white. A head with some dark hairs and some white hairs looks grey from far away. A hair that has already grown cannot change colour. Only new hair grows in white. Your genes decide a lot about when this starts. Some people go grey at 30, others at 70.
- e.g. A grandparent's 'grey' hair is really a mix of white and dark hairs.
- e.g. Eyebrows and beards can turn grey too, because they are also hair.
- e.g. Dogs often get white hairs around the nose when they are old.
### Why skin wrinkles · *defined*
**Definition:** Skin wrinkles because, over many years, it gets thinner, drier and less springy.

Inside your skin are stretchy threads called collagen and elastin. They work like the springs in a mattress. They make skin bounce back when you pinch it. Most elastin is made while you are young, and the body makes very little later. So many of the springy threads in an elder's skin were made when the elder was young. As people get older, the body also makes less collagen. Skin makes less oil, so it gets drier. Strong sunlight over many years breaks the threads faster. So older skin folds and springs back slowly. The lines often form where we smile and frown the most.
- e.g. Skin on the back of a young child's hand snaps back fast when pinched. An elder's skin flattens slowly.
- e.g. Farmers who work in strong sun often get wrinkles earlier.
- e.g. The lines at the corners of a grandmother's eyes show where she has laughed most.
### Why older eyes hold books far away · *defined*
**Definition:** As people get older, the lens inside the eye gets stiffer. A stiff lens cannot focus on things that are close, so small print looks blurry.

To see something close, a tiny muscle lets the lens in your eye get fatter and rounder. Then the close thing looks sharp. A child's lens is soft, like jelly, and changes shape easily. The lens never throws away old cells. All through life it adds new layers around the old ones, so it gets thicker and stiffer every year. By about age 45, most people cannot see small print sharply at a normal reading distance. So they hold the newspaper further away. Reading glasses do the extra focusing for them. Far-away things usually still look sharp. Doctors call this change presbyopia, from a Greek word for an old man.
- e.g. A child can read a book held 10 centimetres from the nose.
- e.g. A grandmother may hold her phone at arm's length to read a message.
- e.g. Reading glasses are often sold at chemist shops in steps like +1, +2 and +3.
### Ears, bones, muscles and joints · *defined*
**Definition:** As years pass, ears hear high sounds less well, bones get thinner, muscles get smaller and joints get stiffer. Using the body slows these changes down.

Deep inside the ear are tiny hair cells that pick up sounds. The ones for very high sounds wear out first. So many older people hear high voices and whistles less well. Bones slowly lose some of their hard minerals, so they break more easily in a fall. Muscles get smaller if they are not used. Joints, like knees, can get stiff and sore. Muscles can grow stronger at any age. In 1990, scientists in Boston gave strength exercises to people aged about 90. In eight weeks, their muscles got much stronger.
- e.g. A grandparent may ask you to repeat what you said, especially in a noisy room.
- e.g. Older people often walk carefully on wet floors, because a fall can break a bone.
- e.g. Many elders in Indian parks do yoga and walk every morning to keep strong.
### What keeps growing as we get older · *idea*
Not everything gets weaker with age. Some things keep growing. Scientists have tested thousands of people of all ages on puzzles and words. Quick puzzles were done fastest by people in their late teens and twenties. But the number of words people knew kept rising until their sixties and seventies. Elders also know a lot from experience, like how to calm a crying baby or what to do in a flood. Some studies find that many older people feel calmer and happier than middle-aged people. Other studies disagree, so this part is still an idea.
- e.g. Your grandparent may know the names of plants, songs and festivals that nobody else in the family remembers.
- e.g. In 2018, Karthyayani Amma of Kerala took a literacy exam at age 96 and scored 98 out of 100.
- e.g. An old carpenter can often tell good wood by touch, faster than any young worker.
- **Many researchers:** Knowledge, words and some kinds of wisdom keep growing well into old age, even while speed slows down.
- **Other researchers:** How happy and calm people feel in old age differs hugely by country, health, money and friends. We cannot say all elders feel happier.
### Your body is always rebuilding itself · *defined*
**Definition:** Your body is made of about 37 trillion cells. Every second, it makes millions of new ones to replace old ones.

Scientists estimate your body makes about 4 million new cells every second. Most are blood cells. A red blood cell lasts about four months. The outer layer of your skin is replaced about every month. The lining of your stomach and gut is replaced every few days. Your whole skeleton is slowly rebuilt over about ten years. But not every cell is replaced. Most cells in the thinking part of your brain stay with you all your life. The cells in the middle of your eye lens were made before you were born. So some parts of you are a few days old, and some are older than you are.
- e.g. The dust in your home is partly old skin flakes, from everyone who lives there.
- e.g. A cut on your knee heals because new skin cells grow to fill the gap.
- e.g. Scientists found how old brain cells are by measuring a kind of carbon left in the air by bomb tests in the 1950s and 1960s.
### Why do bodies age at all? · *debated*
Scientists know a lot about how bodies age. They still argue about why. Here are the main ideas. Many scientists think more than one of them is true at once.
- e.g. Many of your cells can only split to make new cells a certain number of times. In 1961, Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead found human cells in a dish stopped after about 40 to 60 splits.
- e.g. Elizabeth Blackburn and Carol Greider found telomerase, a substance that rebuilds the caps on the ends of genes. They shared a Nobel Prize in 2009 with Jack Szostak.
- e.g. A mouse lives about 2 years. A human can live 80 or more. Their cells are very alike, so scientists think the timetable is set partly by genes.
- **Wear and repair:** Every day, tiny bits of damage happen inside cells. The body repairs most of it. Over many years, a little damage builds up faster than it is fixed.
- **The tiny caps:** Your genes are stored on long threads. Each thread has a cap at each end, like the plastic tip on a shoelace. In many cells the caps get a little shorter every time the cell splits. When they get too short, the cell stops splitting.
- **Nature's timetable:** Some scientists, like Peter Medawar in 1952 and George Williams in 1957, argued this. Nature mostly shapes how well bodies work while they are young and can have children. So little pushes bodies to stay young for ever.
### The end of a life · *defined*
**Definition:** Death is when a body stops working for good. It is the last stage of every life cycle, for plants, animals and people.

Most people die when they are very old, after a long life. A very old body becomes tired, and its parts slowly stop working. Sometimes people die younger, because of an illness or an accident. That is rare, and doctors work hard to stop it. When a person has died, their pain, cold and hunger are over. It is natural to feel very sad when someone dies. That sadness is a sign of love. People remember those who have died through stories, photos, food they liked and festivals. Different families believe different things about what happens after death. You can ask your family what they believe.
- e.g. Dry leaves fall from a tree and turn into soil that feeds new plants.
- e.g. Many Indian families remember their elders on special days, by cooking their favourite food.
- e.g. The atoms in a body that has died go back into the soil and air, and become part of new living things.
### Why do humans live long after they stop having babies? The grandmother idea · *debated*
Most female animals can have babies until near the end of their lives. Women stop having babies at about 50, and then often live for 20 or 30 more years. A few kinds of whales do the same. Why? Scientists argue about this.
- e.g. The Hadza people in Tanzania are hunters and gatherers. Grandmothers there dig up many tubers, a kind of root, to feed their grandchildren.
- e.g. Killer whales are big dolphins. Old grandmother killer whales often lead their family to salmon, especially when food is hard to find.
- e.g. In many Indian homes, grandparents cook, tell stories and look after children while parents work.
- **Kristen Hawkes and her team (1998):** Grandmothers helped feed and care for their grandchildren. So families with long-lived grandmothers had more children who survived. Over many generations, humans came to live longer.
- **Other scientists:** Long life may have come for other reasons, like hunting, sharing skills or bigger brains. The grandmother effect may be real but too small to explain everything.
### People are living longer than ever before · *defined*
**Definition:** Life expectancy is how long people are expected to live on average. In India it has more than doubled in about 70 years.

In 1951, a baby born in India could expect to live about 32 years on average. Today it is about 70 years. That does not mean people were old at 32. Back then, many babies and children died of illnesses like cholera and measles. Those early deaths pulled the average down. Clean water, vaccines, better food and doctors changed that. The longest life ever checked with proper records belonged to Jeanne Calment of France. She lived to 122 years, from 1875 to 1997.
- e.g. Your great-grandparents may have had brothers or sisters who died as babies. Ask your family.
- e.g. Vaccines given to babies at Indian health centres protect them from illnesses that once killed many.
- e.g. There are now more people over 60 in India than ever before.
### Can ageing be slowed? The frontier · *idea*
Some living things barely seem to age. Scientists study them to learn how ageing works. Nobody yet knows how to stop human ageing, and some think we never will.
- e.g. In 1993, Cynthia Kenyon changed one gene in tiny worms. The worms lived about twice as long.
- e.g. Tiny water animals called hydras showed no signs of ageing in a four-year study by Daniel Martínez in 1998.
- e.g. Greenland sharks swim in icy seas. Scientists in 2016 found one that was at least 272 years old.
- **Some scientists:** Ageing is a set of changes we can learn to slow, like slowing an illness. One day people may stay healthy much longer.
- **Other scientists:** Human bodies may have a natural limit of about 120 years. Helping people stay healthy and active until the end matters more than living for ever.

## Mostly agreed
- Every human goes through the same life stages, in the same order. *How we know:* Doctors have measured and recorded millions of babies, children and adults all over the world. Everyone grows and changes in the same order, though at slightly different speeds.
- Babies and young children grow faster than at any other time. *How we know:* Height charts used in every Indian health centre show a baby grows about 25 centimetres in their first year, but a child grows only about 5 to 6 centimetres a year.
- Grey hair, wrinkles and needing reading glasses are normal parts of ageing. *How we know:* These changes are seen in people everywhere. Scientists can see the causes under microscopes: fewer pigment cells in hair roots, fewer springy threads in skin and stiffer eye lenses.
- The body keeps making new cells all through life. *How we know:* Scientists can count and date cells. Red blood cells last about four months, and the outer skin is replaced about every month.
- Exercise, good food, sleep and friends help people stay healthy at every age. *How we know:* Large studies that follow thousands of people for many years find that active people with friends usually stay stronger and healthier for longer.
- People in India and the world live much longer now than 100 years ago. *How we know:* Birth and death records show life expectancy in India rose from about 32 years in 1951 to about 70 years today.

## How we found out
**Alexis Carrel, a French surgeon, and Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead, two American scientists, 1912 to 1961.** In 1912, the French surgeon Alexis Carrel began growing cells from a chicken embryo's heart in a glass dish. He fed them a juice made from crushed chicken embryos. His lab said the cells kept splitting and growing for 34 years, longer than a chicken lives. So most scientists came to believe that cells, kept in a dish, could live for ever. Ageing, they thought, came from the body around the cells, not from the cells. In 1961, Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead in Philadelphia grew human cells. Their cells always slowed and stopped after about 40 to 60 splits. Many scientists thought they had made a mistake. So they mixed old cells from a man with young cells from a woman, in the same dish. The old male cells stopped first, while the young female cells kept splitting. So the dish was not to blame. The cells were counting their own splits. Many scientists now think fresh living cells came into Carrel's dishes with the embryo juice.  
*What people believed before:* Cells kept in a dish can split for ever, so ageing comes only from the body around them. Hayflick and Moorhead showed that human cells stop after about 40 to 60 splits, even in a dish.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Old people cannot learn new things.** (A common belief. Many people think this without checking.; *rejected*)  
  For: Learning very fast things, like a new video game, often does get slower with age.  
  Against: Brains keep making new connections all life long. In 2018, Karthyayani Amma of Kerala passed a literacy exam at 96. Man Kaur of Chandigarh started running races in her nineties and won medals.
- **Every cell in your body is replaced every seven years.** (A popular saying, repeated in many books and websites.; *rejected*)  
  For: Many cells really are replaced, and some, like skin and blood, are replaced much faster than seven years.  
  Against: There is no seven-year rule. Gut cells are replaced in days. Most brain cells and the cells in the middle of your eye lens are never replaced. Scientists checked this by measuring the age of cells in 2005.
- **Worry and fright can turn hair grey.** (An old saying in many countries. Stories tell of people going grey overnight after a shock.; *debated*)  
  For: In 2020, a team led by Ya-Chieh Hsu found that great stress in mice used up the cells that make hair colour. The mice went grey.  
  Against: Hair that has already grown cannot change colour overnight. Stress may make new hair grow in grey sooner, but genes and age matter most. More studies in humans are needed.
- **Ageing is an illness that doctors could one day cure.** (Some scientists who study ageing, and some companies that sell anti-ageing products.; *debated*)  
  For: Scientists have made worms, flies and mice live much longer by changing genes or food. Some animals barely seem to age.  
  Against: Many scientists say ageing is not one illness but many slow changes at once. No product has been shown to stop ageing in humans. The oldest people still die at around 110 to 120.
- **Adult brains never make any new brain cells.** (Most scientists believed this for much of the 1900s.; *debated*)  
  For: Most brain cells we have as adults were made before we were born or as babies.  
  Against: Studies since the 1990s found new brain cells being made in one part of the adult brain, used for memory. Scientists still argue how many are made in old age.
- **Elders are a burden, and young people do all the useful work.** (An attitude some people have, though few say it aloud.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some elders need a lot of care, especially when they are ill.  
  Against: Elders care for grandchildren, cook, teach, keep family memories and give advice. The grandmother idea says long-lived grandmothers helped human children survive. In killer whales, old grandmothers lead their families to food.

## Roots
We have been learning how a body changes over a whole life, from baby to elder. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your skin and blood are replaced again and again, but your eye lens never throws a cell away. It keeps adding new layers around the old ones, so it grows thicker and stiffer every year. That is why the near point test found older people holding print further away. So some ageing comes from what a body keeps, like the old cells in the lens.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of ageing as a body wearing out, like an old chappal. The lens shows another way: it gets older because it keeps every cell it has ever made.  
*Evidence:* In the near point test, a child reads tiny print at about 8 centimetres, while many people over 45 need 30 centimetres or more. The cells in the middle of the lens were made before birth, and the lens keeps growing all life.

- *who am i:* Your size, skin and hair all change over your life. Some parts of you, like the middle of your eye lens, have been with you since before you were born.
- *what is life:* A living body is always making new cells and clearing away old ones, from birth to the end of life.
- *what is this world:* Seeds, trees, rivers and people all change through stages. Nothing in the world stays exactly the same.

A body is always being taken apart and built again. Food brings in new atoms, and old cells are broken down and carried away. Skin, blood and gut are renewed again and again, while the eye lens and most brain cells stay. When a body dies, its atoms return to the soil and air and become part of plants and other living things. So the atoms in a grandmother and in her grandchild came from the same soil, water and air, and will go back to them.

- **Bhagavad Gita 2.13:** The one who lives in the body passes through childhood, youth and old age. In the same way, it passes on to another body. The wise are not confused by this. (The Gita says the body changes at every stage, but the one living in it stays the same. Science shows the body really does change, cell by cell.)
- **Heraclitus, the Greek thinker, as quoted in Plato's Cratylus 402a:** You cannot step into the same river twice. The water is always new, yet we still call it the same river. (Your body is like the river. Its cells are always being replaced, but we still call it you.)
- **Ecclesiastes 3:1–2, in the Hebrew Bible:** There is a time for everything. A time to be born and a time to die. A time to plant and a time to harvest. (Every life has its stages, in order, and each stage has its own work: babies learn to walk, elders teach and remember.)
- Science (settled): Your body makes millions of new cells every second, mostly blood cells. Yet most brain cells and the cells at the centre of your eye lens stay with you all your life.
- Science (settled): When a body dies, its atoms are not lost. They return to the soil, water and air, and become part of other living things.
- Other view, The Buddha, and many neuroscientists today: The Buddha taught anatta, that there is no fixed self going through the stages. There is only the changing itself, moment to moment. Many neuroscientists also think 'you' is a pattern your brain makes, which changes as the brain changes.
- Practice, Hands across time (3 min): Let's put our hands flat on the table, side by side. Look at your hand. Now look at mine. Close your eyes and breathe in slowly. Picture your hand as a tiny baby's hand, curled up. Breathe out. Now picture it as an old person's hand, with soft lines and spots. Breathe in. It is the same hand. Open your eyes. Your hand will change for the rest of your life. Will the one looking at it change too?
- Ask: If all your cells change, what is it that stays 'you'?
- Ask: Is the baby in your old photo you, or someone else?
- Ask: What do you think you will know when you are 80, that you do not know now?
- Ask: When an old tree falls and becomes soil for new trees, has the tree ended, or changed?

## Deep and big questions
- *big:* Your grandparent was once a baby. Are the baby and the grandparent the same person? What stayed the same? (hint: Think about what changed: size, skin, hair, cells. Now think about what might not have changed.)
- *deep:* If most of your cells are replaced, why do you still remember things from when you were four? (hint: Most cells in the thinking part of your brain stay with you all your life.)
- *big:* Would you want to live for ever? What would be good about it, and what might be hard? (hint: Think about friends, the Earth, new babies being born, and what makes days special.)
- *deep:* Why does a mouse live about 2 years, and a human about 80? (hint: Their cells are very alike. So what might be different? Think about genes, size and danger.)
- *big:* What can an elder do better than you? What can you do better than an elder? (hint: Think about eyes, ears and legs. Then think about stories, skills and patience.)
- *big:* How should a family, a town or a country take care of its elders? (hint: Think about ramps, big-print signs, seats on buses, and who visits them.)
- *big:* If you could look at your own hands at 80, what do you think they will look like? What will they know how to do? (hint: Hands that have cooked, written or played music for 70 years learn a lot.)
- *deep:* Is ageing something that happens to us, or something we are doing all the time? (hint: Remember that your body makes about 4 million new cells every second.)
- *deep:* When someone dies, where do the atoms of their body go? (hint: Think of a fallen leaf turning into soil, and a new plant growing from it.)
- *big:* Why do we feel sad when someone we love dies? What does the sadness tell us? (hint: We do not miss people we never cared about.)

## Story: The Needle Who Knew Two Hands
*Chikankari, the white-thread embroidery of Lucknow, is real, and so are the lanes of Chowk where it is sewn. Rehana Khala, Zubair and the needle are made up.*

I am a needle. I am thin and silver, as long as your little finger. I have lived for sixty years in the cloth pincushion of Rehana Khala, in a narrow lane in the old part of Lucknow.

When Rehana first held me, she was eight years old. Her fingers were small and quick. Her eyes were sharp as a sparrow's. She held the cloth so close to her face that her nose almost touched it. She threaded me in one try, every time, and laughed at the grown-ups who squinted.

Her teacher was an old woman called Bano Apa. She held the cloth far away, at the end of her long arms. 'My arms have grown too short,' she used to say. Rehana thought it was a joke. She did not understand it. Not then.

Rehana grew. She grew tall, and her plaits grew long. Her fingers got long and strong. Together we made a hundred kurtas, then a thousand. We made white flowers on white cloth, so fine that you had to tilt it to the light to see them. That is chikankari. It is a slow, patient kind of sewing.

The years went by like threads through cloth. Her black hair turned to salt and pepper, then to salt. Little lines grew at the corners of her eyes, in the places where she smiled. And slowly, so slowly that she did not notice at first, she began to hold the cloth further away.

One morning, she held me up to the window. She licked the end of the thread. She poked at my eye. She missed. She tried again. She missed again. My eye is the tiny hole at my top, and it had not changed at all. It was her eyes that had changed.

'My arms have grown too short,' she said, and this time she did not laugh.

That was the year Zubair came. He was eight, and he lived two doors down. After school he sat on her step, swinging his legs, and watched. One day she held me out to him. 'Can you?' she asked. He held me close to his nose, the way she once had, and threaded me in one try.

After that he came every day. He threaded six needles for her, one after another, and stuck them in her pincushion like little flags. In return, she taught him stitches. The shadow stitch. The tiny knot called phanda, round as a mustard seed.

'Why can't you see the hole any more?' he asked her once.

Rehana tapped the corner of her eye. 'There is a little window in here,' she said, 'that bends to look at near things. When you are young, it is soft, like jelly. Mine has gone stiff, like an old rubber band. Far away, I see the kites over the river. Near, everything is fog.'

Zubair looked at his own hands. 'Will mine go stiff too?'

'If you are lucky,' she said, 'and live long enough.'

Then came the night of the wedding dupatta. It was the hottest week of the summer. A bride's family had ordered a long white dupatta, covered in flowers, for the morning. There were still three flowers to finish. Zubair had threaded a whole row of needles and was sitting with Rehana by the lamp.

Then the lights went out. The fan creaked to a stop. The whole lane went black. Somewhere a dog barked. Zubair's mother called from the next house that the power would not be back till morning.

Zubair held the cloth up to his face. He could see nothing at all. Not the flowers. Not the cloth. Not me. 'It's ruined,' he whispered. 'We can't see.'

In the dark, Rehana laughed. It was a soft, surprised laugh. 'Now,' she said, 'we have the same eyes.'

She took the cloth from him. She did not hold it near, and she did not hold it far. She laid it on her knee and closed her eyes. Her fingertips moved across it like an ant across a leaf. They found the edge of the last flower. They counted the threads, one, two, three. Then I felt her hand pick me up and push me through, exactly where I needed to go.

For sixty years her eyes had been changing. But all that time her fingers had been learning. They had sewn so many flowers that they could feel where the next stitch belonged. Her eyes had grown older. Her hands had grown wiser.

'Give me your hand,' she said to Zubair. She put his fingers on the cloth and her own fingers over his. 'Feel the bump? That is the last knot. Now one thread over. There.' She guided his hand. Together they made one phanda, then another, in the dark.

They sewed like that all night, two hands on one needle. A small soft hand, and a big wrinkled one. I could not tell where one ended and the other began.

When the sky over the river turned grey, the lights flickered back on. Zubair blinked at the cloth. All three flowers were done. Some of the knots were tiny and perfect. A few were a little lumpy. Those were his. Rehana said those were her favourites.

I am still a needle. I have been held by a girl who became an old woman. Now I am held by a boy who will one day be an old man. Some days, when he is sewing on her step, I think it is only one hand that holds me. It is just very long, and it reaches all the way across the years.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Rehana cannot thread the needle because the lens in her eye has stiffened with age. This is presbyopia, and nearly everyone gets it by their mid-forties. The lens never sheds old cells. It keeps adding layers, so it gets thicker and stiffer. Zubair's young lens is soft, so he sees near things sharply. But ageing is not only losing things. While her lens stiffened, her hands were learning, and sixty years of practice let her sew by touch. In the dark, eyes stop mattering and the old woman's skill becomes the key.

- Ask: In the story, why could Rehana Khala not thread the needle any more? Was something wrong with the needle?
- Ask: Why could Zubair thread it in one try?
- Ask: When the lights went out, why did Rehana say they now had 'the same eyes'?
- Ask: What had Rehana's hands been learning while her eyes were getting older?
- Ask: At the end, the needle says it feels held by 'one hand' that reaches across the years. What do you think it means?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Snap-Back Skin Test (20 min)
- **question:** Does skin spring back more slowly as people get older?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Whose skin will go flat fastest after a gentle pinch? Line up the people you will test, from the one you think is fastest to the one you think is slowest. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, keep everything the same except the person's age. Pinch the same place, the back of the hand. Pinch for the same time, 3 seconds. Keep the hand flat on the table. Test each person 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Pinch very gently, only the loose skin on the back of the hand. Always ask first. Stop if anyone says it hurts. Never laugh at anyone's skin.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart. Draw one bar for each person, in order of age. Make each bar as tall as that person's middle value.
- **results table:** Person | Age (years) | Try 1: seconds to go flat | Try 2: seconds | Try 3: seconds | Middle value (seconds)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess, and to line up the people from fastest to slowest.  
  *Parent says:* We will gently pinch the skin on the back of a hand, let go, and count until it goes flat. Whose skin do you think will go flat fastest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will pinch skin gently and see how fast it goes flat. Guess whose skin is fastest. Line everyone up, fastest to slowest. Say why.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show how to pinch gently on your own hand first.  
  *Parent says:* Let's practise on my hand first. Rest it flat. Pinch only the loose skin, softly, like picking up a feather. Hold it while we count to three. Then let go.  
  *Child mode:* Practise on your own hand. Rest it flat. Pinch the loose skin very softly. Count to three. Let go.
  - Note: Show that it does not hurt. Ask each person's permission before you pinch them. This teaches that we ask before touching anyone's body.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Test each person 3 times on the back of the hand. 2. Time from letting go until the skin is flat. 3. Write each time in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Ready, pinch, one, two, three, let go. Watch the little ridge of skin. Say stop when it is flat.  
  *Child mode:* Ask first. Pinch for three seconds. Let go. Count until the skin is flat. Write the number. Do it 3 times.
  - Note: Young skin goes flat almost at once, so write 'less than 1 second'. Skin of people over 60 can stay up for a few seconds. Between a child and a parent of 35 there may be almost no difference, so test an elder if you can, such as a grandparent or an older neighbour. Drinking little water can also slow it down, so it is not a perfect test.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each person, find the middle value of the three tries.  
  *Parent says:* Put the three numbers in order, smallest to biggest. The one in the middle is the middle value. It is a fair number to use, because it ignores one very fast or very slow try.  
  *Child mode:* Put the 3 numbers in order. The middle one is the one we use.
  - Check: Arjun, a boy in Class 3, did the skin test with his Ammamma, his mother's mother. Her three tries took 2, 5 and 3 seconds. What is the middle value? → **3 seconds**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: older skin springs back more slowly. What I saw: my skin went flat in less than 1 second, but Ammamma's took 3 seconds. Why: older skin has fewer springy threads inside.
### e2 · How Close Can You Read? (20 min)
- **question:** Does the closest distance at which people can read clearly change with age?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Who in your family can read tiny print closest to their nose? Who will need it furthest away? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use the same card with the same tiny print for everyone. Test in the same bright room. Everyone uses both eyes. Someone who wears reading glasses only for reading takes them off. Someone who wears glasses all day for seeing far keeps them on. Test each person 3 times, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Never touch the eye with the card or ruler. Hold the end of the tape against the cheekbone, not the eye.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar chart, one bar for each person in order of age.
- **results table:** Person | Age (years) | Try 1: closest sharp distance (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Middle value (cm)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to guess who can read tiny print closest.  
  *Parent says:* Some grown-ups hold a phone far away to read it. Let's measure how close each person in our family can hold tiny print and still read it. Who do you think can hold it closest? Who will need it furthest away? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Some grown-ups hold a phone far away to read it. Guess who can read tiny print closest to their nose. Say why.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. The person holds the card at arm's length. 2. They bring it slowly towards their face. 3. When the letters blur, they move it back a little until it is just sharp. 4. Measure from cheekbone to card. 5. Repeat 3 times for each person.  
  *Parent says:* Bring it closer, slowly. Tell me the moment the letters go fuzzy. Now move it back just until they are sharp. Hold still while I measure.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the card far away. Bring it closer slowly. Stop when the letters go fuzzy. Move it back until sharp. Measure.
  - Note: Children often reach 7 to 10 centimetres. People over 45 without reading glasses often need 30 centimetres or more. If an elder cannot see the print sharply even at arm's length, write 'more than arm's length'. A short-sighted person without their glasses can read very close at any age. That is why they keep their glasses on. If nobody at home is over 40, ask a grandparent, a relative or a neighbour to be tested.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the bars together.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the bars. What happens to the distance as people get older?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your bars. What happens as people get older?
  - Check: In the near point test, a grandmother of 58 needed the card 60 centimetres away. A child of 8 could read it at 8 centimetres. Why? → **The grandmother's eye lens has got stiffer, so it cannot focus on near things**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: older people need to hold small print further away. What I saw: I could read at 8 centimetres, but Paati needed 60 centimetres. Why: the lens in her eye has got stiff, so it cannot focus up close.
### e3 · The High Sound Test (20 min)
- **question:** Can younger people hear higher sounds than older people?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Who will hear the highest sound? Will anyone hear sounds the others cannot? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, play every sound from the same speaker, at the same quiet volume, from the same distance. Everyone sits in the same quiet room. Each person raises a hand when they hear a sound, without looking at the screen.
- **safety:** A grown-up controls the volume. Keep it quiet, never loud. Never use earphones for this test. Stop at once if a sound feels uncomfortable.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a tick for each sound a person heard. Then colour in a bar for each person up to their highest sound.
- **results table:** Person | Age (years) | Heard 4,000 (yes or no) | Heard 8,000 | Heard 12,000 | Heard 15,000 | Highest sound heard
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open an online tone generator. 2. Set the volume low. 3. Set it to 4,000 hertz, a medium-high beep.  
  *Parent says:* Sounds can be low, like a drum, or high, like a whistle. How high a sound is, is measured in hertz. Let's see who can hear the highest sounds.  
  *Child mode:* Fetch a grown-up for this test. Sounds can be low or high. We will play higher and higher beeps.
  - Note: Hertz means how many times a second the air vibrates, or shakes back and forth. 4,000 hertz is a sharp beep. Young children can often hear up to about 17,000 hertz or more. Many older people hear only up to 8,000 to 10,000.
- **s2 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Play 4,000, then 8,000, then 12,000, then 15,000 hertz for 3 seconds each. Keep the volume the same. Write a tick for everyone who raises a hand.  
  *Parent says:* Eyes closed. Raise your hand if you hear anything.  
  *Child mode:* Close your eyes. Raise your hand when you hear a beep. Write a tick for each person.
  - Note: Some phone speakers cannot play the highest sounds. If nobody hears 15,000, that may be the phone, not the ears.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together.  
  *Parent says:* Who heard the highest sound? Why do you think older ears miss them?  
  *Child mode:* Who heard the highest beep? Why might older ears miss it?
  - Check: Priya, a girl in Class 3, played beeps to her family. She heard a beep of 15,000 hertz. Her grandfather did not. What is the best reason? → **The tiny hair cells for high sounds wear out first as we age**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think? What did you see? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: young people can hear higher sounds. What I saw: I heard 15,000 hertz but Thatha only heard up to 8,000. Why: the hair cells for high sounds in his ears have worn out over the years.

## Worksheets
### The Stages of a Life (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (order) Put these people in order of their life stage, from the earliest stage to the latest: Paati, who is 72. Baby Nila, who is 6 months old. Arjun, who is 15. Kavya, who is 8. Amma, who is 38.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Every person goes through the same stages in the same order: baby, child, teenager, adult, older adult. Nobody skips one. A strong answer also names the stage of each person.
2. (mcq) Kavya is 8. She thinks children her age grow faster than anyone. A baby grows from about 50 cm to about 75 cm in their first year. An 8-year-old grows about 6 cm in a year. Who grows fastest?  
   Options: The baby, by about 25 cm in one year / Kavya, because she is older and bigger / They grow at the same speed / Nobody grows after they are born  
   **Answer:** The baby, by about 25 cm in one year. *Why:* 75 take away 50 is 25 cm in one year. That is about four times what an 8-year-old grows. The first year is the fastest growing of a whole life. Kavya's guess is a common one, because children can see themselves growing out of clothes.
3. (mcq) A baby's head is about one quarter of their length. A grown-up's head is only about one eighth. What does this tell you?  
   Options: After birth, the arms, legs and body grow much more than the head / A grown-up's head shrinks / Babies have bigger heads than grown-ups / Heads stop growing at birth  
   **Answer:** After birth, the arms, legs and body grow much more than the head. *Why:* The head is already big at birth, because the brain grows early. Then the legs and body do most of the growing. So the head becomes a smaller part of the whole. A grown-up's head is still bigger than a baby's, so 'bigger heads' is wrong.
4. (mcq) Lakshmi Paati's hair has turned grey. Her granddaughter Meenakshi says, 'Paati's black hairs turned white.' What really happened?  
   Options: Her black hairs stayed black. The cells at the roots stopped making colour, so new hairs grew in white. / Her black hairs slowly turned white from the tip down / Dust collected on her hair / The sun bleached every hair  
   **Answer:** Her black hairs stayed black. The cells at the roots stopped making colour, so new hairs grew in white.. *Why:* A hair that has already grown cannot change colour. Pigment cells at the root put colour into each new hair. As they run out, new hairs grow white. White hairs mixed with dark ones look grey from far away.
5. (write) Think of your snap-back skin test, where you gently pinched the back of a hand. Whose skin took longest to go flat? Use the springy threads inside skin to explain why.  
   **Answer:** For example: Paati's skin took about 3 seconds. Mine went flat in less than 1 second. Skin has springy threads called collagen and elastin. Older skin has fewer of them, so it springs back more slowly.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the child's own numbers, puts the people in order of age, and explains the result with the springy threads, not with 'old skin is tired'.
6. (fill) The clear, bendy part inside your eye changes shape to make close things look sharp. It is called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lens', 'eye lens']. *Why:* The lens gets fatter and rounder to focus on near things. A child's lens is soft like jelly. An older lens is stiffer.
7. (mcq) Before the near point test, Divya made a guess. She thought her mother, who is 46, would read the tiny card closer than Divya could. 'Grown-ups read more, so their eyes are better at it,' she said. What did the test most likely show?  
   Options: Divya read it closer. Her mother had to hold it further away, because her lens is stiffer. / Her mother read it closer, because she reads more / They both read it at exactly the same distance / Neither could read it at all  
   **Answer:** Divya read it closer. Her mother had to hold it further away, because her lens is stiffer.. *Why:* Reading a lot does not keep the lens soft. The lens adds new layers all through life and gets stiffer. By about 45, most people must hold small print further away. A strong reason uses the lens, not practice.
8. (match) Match each life stage to something that often happens in it.  
   **Answer:** Baby → learns to walk; Child → milk teeth fall out; Teenager → growth spurt; Adult → bones at their strongest; Older adult → hair turns white. *Why:* Each stage has its own changes. Babies learn to walk, children lose milk teeth, teenagers grow fast, adults are strongest, and elders' hair often turns white.
9. (sort) Sort these. Do they usually get HARDER with age, or can they get BETTER?  
   **Answer:** reading tiny print → Often gets harder; knowing many words → Can get better; hearing very high sounds → Often gets harder; telling family stories → Can get better; running fast → Often gets harder; knowing what to do in a crisis → Can get better. *Why:* Bodies slow down with age, but knowledge, words and experience can keep growing all life.
10. (mcq) Your body makes millions of new cells every second. Which part is NOT replaced during your life?  
   Options: The outer layer of skin / Red blood cells / The cells in the middle of the eye lens / The lining of the gut  
   **Answer:** The cells in the middle of the eye lens. *Why:* The cells in the middle of your eye lens were made before you were born, and they stay all your life. Skin, blood and gut lining are replaced again and again. Picking 'skin' usually means it has been pictured as fixed, but the outer skin is replaced about every month.
11. (truefalse) Muscles cannot get stronger after age 60.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Many people believe this. But in 1990, people aged about 90 got much stronger in eight weeks of strength exercise. Muscles respond to exercise at any age.
12. (truefalse) Grown-ups stop getting taller because they stop eating as much as children.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bones grow longer at their ends, in places called growth plates. In the late teens the growth plates close and turn to solid bone. After that, no amount of food makes a person taller.
13. (draw) Draw the same person at three ages: 5, 35 and 75. Show and label at least three body changes.  
   **Answer:** A small child with a big head and milk teeth. A tall adult. An older person with grey hair, some wrinkles and reading glasses.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows changes in size, in how big the head is compared to the body, and in hair, skin and eyes, each one labelled.
14. (write) Your Paati's knees are stiff, and small print looks blurry to her. Name two ways you could help her at home. For each one, say which body change it helps with.  
   **Answer:** For example: I can read the small print on medicine boxes for her, because her eye lens is stiff. I can put a rubber mat in the bathroom, because stiff knees and thinner bones make a fall dangerous. I ask her first if she wants the help.. *Why:* A strong answer links each kind of help to a real body change, and remembers to ask first. Elders are capable people.
15. (perspective) Three people talk about growing old. Is it mostly losing things, or mostly gaining things?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and backs it with evidence, such as the near point test, the skin test, or the fact that people keep learning words into their seventies. It then admits a fair point from another side.. *Why:* Ageing really has both sides. Some things get harder, like reading tiny print. Some can get better, like knowledge and experience. There is no single right answer.
16. (fill) The last stage of every life cycle, for plants, animals and people, is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['death', 'dying']. *Why:* Every living being is born, grows, and one day dies. It is a natural part of every life cycle. It is fine to feel sad about it.

   **Answer:** The hair cells for very high sounds wear out first with age. *Why:* Deep inside the ear, tiny hair cells pick up sounds. The ones for the highest sounds wear out first. So most older people stop hearing very high beeps, even when they listen hard.

5. (truefalse) Every cell in your body is replaced exactly every seven years.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. This is a popular saying, but there is no seven-year rule. Gut cells are replaced in days, and outer skin in about a month. Most brain cells and the cells in the middle of the eye lens are never replaced.

   **Answer:** Many babies and children died of illnesses, which pulled the average down. *Why:* An average counts every life, including the many babies who died of illnesses like cholera and measles. Many people who lived through childhood reached 60 or 70. 'Everyone was old at 32' is a common mistake about averages.

   **Answer:** Bones lose some hard minerals with age, so they break more easily. *Why:* Older bones slowly lose some of their hard minerals, so the same fall can break them. That is why a rubber mat and a handrail in the bathroom help elders.
9. (perspective) Can worry turn hair grey? Three people give different answers.

### The Big Questions of Ageing (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (write) Your body makes about 4 million new cells every second. Is it still the same body it was last year? Give reasons.  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer. For example: Much of it is new, because skin, blood and gut cells have been replaced. But my eye lens cells and most brain cells are the same. My memories carry on too. So it is the same body, but rebuilt.. *Why:* This is a real puzzle that thinkers have argued about for thousands of years. A strong answer uses facts about which cells are replaced and which stay.
2. (perspective) Scientists still argue about why bodies age at all. Here are three of their main ideas.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks an idea and gives a reason, such as 'the lens keeps old cells, so damage can build up in them'. It then says why another idea could also be true. Many scientists think more than one is true at once.. *Why:* This is a real, open question in science. The three ideas do not cancel each other out. Each explains some of what we see.
3. (write) A mouse lives about 2 years. A human lives about 80. Their cells are very alike. Suggest one reason for the difference.  
   **Answer:** For example: Mice are eaten by cats and owls so young that nature never shaped them to live long. Humans are protected and cared for, so a long life helped. Or their genes may set a different timetable.. *Why:* This is an open question. Scientists point to genes, body size, and how dangerous an animal's life is. A strong answer gives a reason that could be tested.
4. (perspective) Will doctors one day cure ageing? Three people disagree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a view and uses evidence, such as the worm study or the many different changes in the unit (lens, skin, bones, hearing). It then answers back fairly from another view.. *Why:* This is debated. Scientists have made worms, flies and mice live longer. No product has been shown to stop ageing in humans. Whether we should want to is also a real question.
5. (mcq) Killer whales are big dolphins. Old grandmother killer whales often lead their families to salmon, mostly in years when food is hard to find. Which idea does this support?  
   Options: Long life after having babies can help a family survive / Old animals are always weaker than young ones / Whales do not age / Salmon prefer grandmothers  
   **Answer:** Long life after having babies can help a family survive. *Why:* The grandmothers remember where food was in past hard years. Their families do better with them. This supports the grandmother idea: that long-lived grandmothers help their grandchildren survive.
6. (write) Remember the snap-back skin test? The result can change if someone has drunk very little water. Name two ways you would make the test fairer.  
   **Answer:** Ask everyone to drink a glass of water an hour before. Test everyone at the same time of day, in the same room. Pinch the same place for the same 3 seconds.. *Why:* Good scientists look for other things that could change the result, and keep them the same. A strong answer names at least two.
7. (mcq) Each time a cell splits, the caps on the ends of their gene threads get a little shorter. Skin cells split again and again all life. The cells in the middle of the eye lens hardly split at all. After 60 years, whose caps would you expect to be shorter?  
   Options: The skin cells' caps / The lens cells' caps / Both exactly the same / Neither has caps  
   **Answer:** The skin cells' caps. *Why:* The caps shorten with each split. Skin cells split many more times, so their caps shorten more. The lens shows that ageing is not only about splitting. It ages by keeping its old cells.
8. (write) When a body dies, their atoms go back into the soil, water and air. Where might those atoms go next?  
   **Answer:** Into a plant, which a cow or a person might eat. Into a tree. Into a river. Into a new living being.. *Why:* Atoms are never lost. They move through soil, plants and animals again and again. A strong answer follows the atoms for at least two steps.
9. (mcq) Hydras are tiny water animals. In a 1998 study, hydras showed no signs of ageing for four years. What does this tell scientists?  
   Options: Ageing is not the same for every living being / Hydras live for ever, for sure / All animals live four years / Hydras are not alive  
   **Answer:** Ageing is not the same for every living being. *Why:* Hydras seem to keep renewing their bodies. This shows ageing is not fixed for all life. Scientists still do not know if hydras truly never age.
10. (draw) Design a bus stop sign that an elder with a stiff eye lens can read from the bench. Label two choices you made, and which body change each one helps with.  
   **Answer:** For example: big, dark letters on a light background, for older eyes. A picture of the bus number, so it can be read from far away.    **Answer:** Any honest answer. For example: We feel sad because we loved them and miss them. My family lights a lamp and cooks their favourite food on their day.. *Why:* Grief is a sign of love. Families and cultures have many ways of remembering. Every honest answer is a good one.
12. (sort) Sort these claims about ageing.  
   **Answer:** Bodies go through stages in the same order → Scientists agree; Exercise helps at any age → Scientists agree; Why bodies age at all → Scientists still argue; Whether ageing can be cured → Scientists still argue; Grey hair comes from fewer pigment cells → Scientists agree; How many new brain cells old brains make → Scientists still argue. *Why:* Science is sure about many facts of ageing, but its deepest causes are still open.
13. (mcq) Here are made-up near point results: 8 cm at age 8, 10 cm at 20, 25 cm at 45, 60 cm at 65. Meera, a girl in Class 4, says, 'So the lens only starts to stiffen at 45.' What is wrong with her idea?  
   Options: The distance already grew from 8 to 10 cm by age 20. The lens stiffens all life, slowly at first. / Nothing. The lens is soft until 45. / The lens gets softer after 45 / The numbers show nothing about the lens  
   **Answer:** The distance already grew from 8 to 10 cm by age 20. The lens stiffens all life, slowly at first.. *Why:* The near point moves out a little every year. The change is small at first, so people notice it only in their forties, when small print starts to blur at a normal reading distance.
14. (mcq) Scientists tested thousands of people. Quick puzzles were done fastest by people in their twenties. But people kept learning more words into their sixties and seventies. A company needs someone to help new workers with hard problems. Kavya, a girl of 9, says, 'Hire only young people.' What does the evidence say?  
   Options: An older worker's knowledge and experience may help more with hard problems / Only young people can solve problems / Old people know fewer words / Age makes no difference to anything  
   **Answer:** An older worker's knowledge and experience may help more with hard problems. *Why:* Different abilities peak at different ages. Speed on quick puzzles is highest when young. Knowledge, words and experience keep growing. A strong team often needs both.
15. (truefalse) Animals with bigger bodies always live longer than animals with smaller bodies.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It is often true, but not always. Some bats, as small as mice, live for over 40 years, while mice live about 2. So body size is not the whole story.
16. (draw) Draw a graph of a person's height from birth to age 90. Show where it rises fast, where it stops, and where it drops a little in old age.  
   **Answer:** A steep rise in the first year, a slower rise in childhood, a fast rise in the teenage growth spurt, then flat from the late teens. Near the end it dips a little, because the spine gets a little shorter in old age.. *Why:* A strong graph shows the fast first year, the growth spurt, the flat grown-up years and a small drop in old age.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Arjun is 15, and he is growing very fast. Which life stage is he in, and which stage comes next?  
   Options: Teenager, then adult / Child, then teenager / Adult, then older adult / Baby, then child  
   **Answer:** Teenager, then adult. *Why:* A person aged about 13 to 19 is a teenager, and the teenage years have a growth spurt. Next comes the adult stage, when growing taller stops.
2. (mcq) A newborn baby's skull has soft spots, where the bones have not joined yet. Why is this useful?  
   Options: The skull can grow as the baby's brain grows / It makes the head lighter / It lets the baby hear better / It is a sign the baby is ill  
   **Answer:** The skull can grow as the baby's brain grows. *Why:* A baby's brain grows very fast. The soft spots let the skull grow with it. Later the bones join together.
3. (mcq) Meenakshi's mother is 45. She has started holding her phone far away to read messages. What would help her read up close?  
   Options: Reading glasses, which do the extra bending her stiff lens cannot do / Longer arms / A brighter phone only / Closing one eye  
   **Answer:** Reading glasses, which do the extra bending her stiff lens cannot do. *Why:* Her lens has got stiffer, so it cannot bulge enough to focus on near things. Reading glasses add the extra bending. This happens to almost everyone by their mid-forties.
4. (mcq) Kavya's Paati often misses what little children say. But she hears Thatha's deep voice easily. Why?  
   Options: Children's voices are high, and the hair cells for high sounds wear out first / Children speak too fast / Paati likes Thatha more / Deep voices are always louder  
   **Answer:** Children's voices are high, and the hair cells for high sounds wear out first. *Why:* Tiny hair cells deep in the ear pick up sounds. The ones for high sounds wear out first with age. So high voices get harder to hear before deep ones do.
5. (mcq) Meera, a girl in Class 3, gently pinched the skin on the back of her own hand. It went flat in less than 1 second. Her grandmother's skin took 3 seconds. Why?  
   Options: Older skin has fewer springy threads inside / The grandmother was pinched harder / Children's skin has no threads in it / The grandmother's hand was moving  
   **Answer:** Older skin has fewer springy threads inside. *Why:* Collagen and elastin threads make skin springy, and older skin has fewer. In a fair test, everyone is pinched the same way, so the pinch was not the cause.
6. (mcq) In the skin test, you pinch the back of a hand and count how long the skin takes to go flat. To make it fair, what must stay the same for everyone?  
   Options: The place pinched and how long it is pinched / The person's age / Nothing, it does not matter / The colour of their clothes  
   **Answer:** The place pinched and how long it is pinched. *Why:* In a fair test you change only the thing you are testing, here the age. Everything else, like the place and time of the pinch, stays the same.
7. (mcq) Your body makes about 4 million new cells every second. So why don't you keep getting bigger and bigger?  
   Options: Old cells die and are cleared away while new ones are made / The new cells are invisible / New cells are made only while you sleep / You do get bigger every second, for ever  
   **Answer:** Old cells die and are cleared away while new ones are made. *Why:* Most new cells replace old ones that are worn out. Red blood cells last about four months, and skin cells about a month. So the body is rebuilt without growing.
8. (mcq) Lakshmi Paati is 75. She can't run fast any more. But she finishes the newspaper word puzzle quicker than anyone in the house. What does this show?  
   Options: Some abilities slow down with age, while knowing words can keep growing / Running and puzzles are the same skill / Paati is not really old / Word puzzles get easier every year  
   **Answer:** Some abilities slow down with age, while knowing words can keep growing. *Why:* Speed and strength often fall with age. Knowledge and words can keep growing into the seventies. Ageing is not only losing.
9. (mcq) What is the best way to help an elder who finds a task hard?  
   Options: Ask if they would like help, then help in the way they want / Do everything for them without asking / Laugh, so they feel better / Tell them they are too old  
   **Answer:** Ask if they would like help, then help in the way they want. *Why:* Elders are capable people. Good help starts by asking. Doing everything without asking can make someone feel they are not trusted.
10. (mcq) Revathi's Paati is 68. She wants to get up from a chair more easily. What is most likely to help?  
   Options: Standing up and sitting down slowly, a few times every day, to build leg muscles / Staying sitting as much as she can, to rest her legs / Nothing can help after 60 / Only eating more sweets  
   **Answer:** Standing up and sitting down slowly, a few times every day, to build leg muscles. *Why:* Muscles grow stronger with exercise at any age. In 1990, people aged about 90 got much stronger in eight weeks. Resting all the time makes muscles shrink.
11. (mcq) A shop sells a cream that says it 'stops ageing'. What does the evidence say?  
   Options: No product has been shown to stop ageing in humans. Scientists still argue whether it can even be slowed. / The cream will keep you young for ever / Creams make cells stop being replaced / Scientists all agree ageing is cured  
   **Answer:** No product has been shown to stop ageing in humans. Scientists still argue whether it can even be slowed.. *Why:* Scientists have made worms and mice live longer in the lab. But nothing has been shown to stop human ageing. Healthy food, sleep, exercise and friends are what the evidence supports.
12. (mcq) Why did life expectancy in India rise from about 32 years in 1951 to about 70 today?  
   Options: Clean water, vaccines, better food and doctors saved many babies and children / People started having more birthdays / Bodies became made of different cells / The weather got cooler  
   **Answer:** Clean water, vaccines, better food and doctors saved many babies and children. *Why:* Most of the change came from far fewer babies and children dying of illnesses. Picking 'more birthdays' mixes up counting years with the reasons people live longer.
13. (mcq) What happens at the end of every life cycle?  
   Options: The living being dies, and their atoms return to the soil, water and air / They start again as a baby the next day / They turn into a different animal / Nothing ever dies  
   **Answer:** The living being dies, and their atoms return to the soil, water and air. *Why:* Death is the last stage of every life cycle, for plants, animals and people. The atoms of the body are not lost but become part of other living beings. Families have many different beliefs about what happens after death.
14. (mcq) Telomeres are tiny caps on the ends of the threads that hold our genes. What happens to them in many cells?  
   Options: They get shorter each time the cell splits / They grow longer every birthday / They turn into hair / They never change  
   **Answer:** They get shorter each time the cell splits. *Why:* In many cells the caps shorten with each split. When they get too short, the cell stops splitting. Elizabeth Blackburn and Carol Greider found the substance that can rebuild them.
15. (mcq) Kristen Hawkes, an American scientist, watched Hadza grandmothers in Tanzania dig up food for their grandchildren. Which idea did this lead to?  
   Options: The grandmother idea: grandmothers who help feed grandchildren may have helped humans evolve long lives / Grandmothers never get old / Only humans have grandmothers / Children do not need food from grown-ups  
   **Answer:** The grandmother idea: grandmothers who help feed grandchildren may have helped humans evolve long lives. *Why:* Hawkes and her team suggested in 1998 that long-lived grandmothers helped more children survive. It is debated, and other scientists suggest other reasons too.

## Applications
- Doctors and health workers check babies' height and weight on growth charts to see they are growing well.
- Eye doctors test the near point and prescribe reading glasses for people over 40.
- Hearing tests use high and low beeps, just like the high sound test, to find hearing loss early.
- Builders add ramps, handrails and non-slip tiles to homes, stations and temples so elders can move safely.
- Phones and tablets have big-text and loud settings for older eyes and ears.
- Bus and train seats are kept for senior citizens, because standing in a moving vehicle can cause falls.
- Doctors called geriatricians specialise in caring for elders.
- Physiotherapists teach elders strength and balance exercises to prevent falls.
- Scientists study tiny worms, flies and hydras to learn how ageing works.
- Vaccines given to babies are one big reason people now live longer.
- Sunscreen, hats and shade slow down skin wrinkling caused by strong sun.
- Pension schemes and senior citizen discounts help elders who no longer earn a salary.
- Day care centres for elders give company, games and exercise, which help body and mind.

## Open questions
- **Why do bodies age at all?** Scientists have several ideas: damage building up, shortening caps on genes, and nature's timetable. They do not agree which matters most.
- **Is there a limit to how long a human can live?** The oldest checked person lived to 122. Some scientists think about 120 is a natural limit. Others think there is no fixed limit.
- **How do some animals, like hydras, seem not to age?** Hydras keep making new cells for their whole bodies. Scientists are still finding out how, and whether they truly never age.
- **How many new brain cells does an older brain make?** Some studies find many new brain cells in elders, and others find almost none. Scientists are still checking how to count them properly.
- **Can ageing be slowed in humans?** It has been slowed in worms, flies and mice. Nobody has shown it safely in people yet.
- **Why do some people go grey at 30 and others at 70?** Genes play a big part, and stress may too. Scientists are still finding out exactly how.
- **If almost all your cells change, what makes you the same person?** This is a question for science and philosophy. Some say memory, some say the pattern of your body, and some say awareness itself. Nobody has a final answer.

## Connected maths
- In the near point test, a child read tiny print at 10 cm. A grandmother needed it 60 cm away. How many times further away did the grandmother need it? **6 times.**. The grandmother needed 60 cm. The child needed 10 cm. 60 divided by 10 is 6. You can check: 10 + 10 + 10 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 60. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Riya, a girl in Class 3, did the skin test with her Dadi, her father's mother. Dadi's three tries took 4 seconds, 2 seconds and 3 seconds. What is the middle value? **3 seconds.**. Put the numbers in order, smallest first: 2, 3, 4. The number in the middle is 3. So the middle value is 3 seconds. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)
- A newborn is about 50 cm long. By its first birthday it is about 75 cm. Later, a child grows about 5 cm a year. How many years does a child take to grow as much as a baby grows in their first year? **5 years.**. The baby grew 75 minus 50, which is 25 cm, in one year. A child grows 5 cm a year. 25 divided by 5 is 5. So a child takes 5 years to grow what a baby grows in one. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L3`)
- In 1951, life expectancy in India was about 32 years. Today it is about 70 years. How many more years can a baby born today expect to live? **38 more years.**. 70 minus 32. First 70 minus 30 is 40. Then 40 minus 2 is 38. So about 38 more years. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- In your interview, the elder is 68 years old and you are 8. How many years ago was the elder your age? If this year is 2026, what year was that? **60 years ago, in 1966.**. 68 minus 8 is 60. So the elder was 8 years old 60 years ago. 2026 minus 60 is 1966. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- A red blood cell lasts about 4 months. A year has 12 months. About how many times are your red blood cells replaced in one year? **About 3 times.**. 12 divided by 4 is 3. So your red blood cells are replaced about 3 times a year. You can check: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
