# Ionic, covalent and metallic bonds: why salt shatters, wax melts and metal bends
`sci.chemistry.bonds.L4`

**Summary.** A paper clip bends, but a salt crystal shatters. What is different inside them? In this lesson you will test three solids by pressing, wetting and heating, build a row model to see why they behave differently, and check when salt carries electricity.

**Intro.** *Sodium is a soft metal that can catch fire in water. Chlorine is a poisonous green gas. Joined together, they are the salt in your sambar. How can joining change two things so much?* A bond is the way atoms hold on to each other. The kind of bond decides how a material behaves. In this lesson you press a salt crystal and a ball of foil, and see one break and one bend. You heat salt, wax and foil over a candle with a grown-up. You build a tester from a battery and two pencils. With it you find out when salt carries electricity. By the end you can say what happened to the sodium and the chlorine.

## Terms
- **Electron**: A very tiny particle with a minus electric charge. Electrons move around the centre of every atom. *e.g.* The outer electrons of an atom are the ones that make bonds.
- **Chemical bond**: The way atoms hold on to each other. Every bond comes from plus charges and minus charges attracting each other. *e.g.* Bonds hold the atoms of a salt crystal, a drop of water and a steel spoon together.
- **Metal and non-metal**: Metals are elements such as iron, copper, sodium and gold. Their atoms hold their outer electrons weakly. Non-metals are elements such as oxygen, chlorine and carbon. Their atoms hold electrons strongly. *e.g.* Sodium is a metal. Chlorine is a non-metal.
- **Ion**: An atom that has lost or gained electrons, so it has an electric charge. Losing electrons makes a plus ion. Gaining electrons makes a minus ion. *e.g.* A sodium atom that gives away one electron becomes a sodium ion with a plus charge.
- **Ionic bond**: The attraction between plus ions and minus ions. It forms when metal atoms give electrons to non-metal atoms. *e.g.* Table salt is sodium ions and chloride ions held by ionic bonds.
- **Lattice**: A regular pattern of particles that repeats in every direction through a crystal. *e.g.* In a salt lattice, every sodium ion has six chloride ions around it.
- **Covalent bond**: A bond made when two atoms share a pair of electrons. It forms between non-metal atoms. *e.g.* In water, the oxygen atom shares electrons with two hydrogen atoms.
- **Molecule**: A group of atoms joined by covalent bonds. It is the smallest piece of a substance such as water or sugar. *e.g.* A water molecule is one oxygen atom joined to two hydrogen atoms.
- **Metallic bond**: The bond in a metal. All the atoms share their outer electrons, and these electrons move freely among them. *e.g.* In a copper wire, the free electrons are shared by every copper atom in the wire.
- **Brittle**: Breaking suddenly into pieces when pressed or hit, without bending first. *e.g.* A salt crystal and a piece of chalk are brittle.
- **Malleable**: Able to be pressed or beaten into a new shape without breaking. *e.g.* Silver is so malleable that it is beaten into the thin leaf put on sweets.
- **Conductor**: A material that lets electricity pass through it. *e.g.* Copper, steel and salt water are conductors.
- **Insulator**: A material that does not let electricity pass through it. *e.g.* Candle wax, dry sugar and dry salt are insulators.
- **Electrolyte**: A substance that carries electricity when it is dissolved or melted, because its ions can move. *e.g.* The salts in ORS are electrolytes.
- **Melting point**: The temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. *e.g.* Ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius. Salt melts at about 800 degrees Celsius.
- **Alloy**: A metal made by mixing a metal with one or more other elements. *e.g.* Steel is an alloy of iron. Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc.

## Explanations
### One attraction makes every bond · *defined*
**Definition:** A chemical bond is the way atoms hold on to each other. Every bond comes from the attraction between plus charges and minus charges.

Every atom has a centre called the nucleus, which has a plus charge. Around it move electrons, which have a minus charge. Plus and minus attract each other. An electron is attracted to its own nucleus. If another atom comes close, the electron is attracted to that nucleus too. That double attraction is what holds atoms together. Only the outer electrons take part, because they are the furthest from their own nucleus. There are three main things the outer electrons can do. One atom can give electrons to another. That makes an ionic bond. Two atoms can share electrons between them. That makes a covalent bond. A whole crowd of atoms can share their electrons together. That makes a metallic bond. Salt, water and copper are held in these three ways.
- e.g. Salt is held by ionic bonds.
- e.g. Water, wax and sugar are held by covalent bonds.
- e.g. Copper, aluminium and steel are held by metallic bonds.
### Ionic bonds: one atom gives, another takes · *defined*
**Definition:** An ion is an atom with an electric charge, because it has lost or gained electrons. An ionic bond is the attraction between plus ions and minus ions.

A sodium atom holds its one outer electron weakly. A chlorine atom attracts one more electron strongly. When they meet, the electron moves from the sodium to the chlorine. The sodium now has one electron too few, so it has a plus charge. The chlorine has one too many, so it has a minus charge. Both are now ions. The chlorine ion is called chloride. A sodium ion is not like a sodium atom. The atom reacts fiercely because it gives its electron away so easily. The ion has already given it away, so it does not react in that way. This is how a fiery metal and a poisonous gas become table salt. The ions do not pair off. Each plus ion attracts every minus ion near it. So they pack into a pattern called a lattice, plus next to minus in every direction. In salt, every sodium ion has six chloride ions around it. Every chloride ion has six sodium ions around it. There is no salt molecule. The formula NaCl only says there is one sodium ion for every chloride ion.
- e.g. Table salt is sodium ions and chloride ions.
- e.g. Cooking soda is made of sodium ions and other ions. Toothpaste often has sodium fluoride, which is made of ions too.
- e.g. A sea shell is mostly calcium carbonate, which is held together by ionic bonds.
### Why salt is hard, brittle and hard to melt · *defined*
**Definition:** An ionic solid is hard and brittle, and it melts only at a very high temperature. This is because every ion is held by strong attractions on all sides.

In Three Solids, Three Tests, the salt cracked under the spoon. It did not melt over the candle, and it dissolved in water. To melt salt, the ions must break free of all their neighbours. That needs about 800 degrees Celsius. A spoon held over a candle does not get that hot. Salt is hard for the same reason, because every ion is held tightly by its neighbours. Salt is brittle because of the way its ions sit in a regular pattern. Press hard, and one layer of ions slips one place sideways. Now plus ions sit next to plus ions, and minus next to minus. Like charges repel each other. So the two layers push apart, and the crystal splits along a flat face. Water takes salt apart more gently. Water molecules attract the ions and pull them out of the lattice one by one.
- e.g. A crystal of rock salt breaks into pieces with flat faces.
- e.g. Salt stays solid on a hot dosa tawa.
- e.g. Factories melt salt and pass electricity through it to make sodium metal.
### Covalent bonds: two atoms share · *defined*
**Definition:** A covalent bond is a pair of electrons shared by two atoms. Atoms joined by covalent bonds make a molecule.

Non-metal atoms all attract electrons strongly, so neither atom can take an electron from the other. They share. In a water molecule, the oxygen atom shares a pair of electrons with each of two hydrogen atoms. The shared pair is attracted by both atoms, and that holds them together. Covalent bonds inside a molecule are very strong. But one molecule attracts the next molecule only weakly. Melting does not break any covalent bonds. It only lets whole molecules slide past each other. So most substances made of molecules melt easily. Ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius, and candle wax at about 60. In Three Solids, Three Tests, the wax melted within seconds. Molecules have no charge, and their electrons stay in their bonds. So wax and sugar do not carry electricity. A few covalent solids are not made of small molecules. In a diamond, every carbon atom is bonded to four others, all through the stone. To break or melt a diamond, covalent bonds themselves must break. That is why diamond is the hardest natural material.
- e.g. Water, sugar, cooking oil, wax and plastic are made of covalent molecules.
- e.g. The oxygen and nitrogen in air are molecules of two atoms each.
- e.g. Diamond and the quartz in sand are covalent all the way through.
### Metallic bonds: all the atoms share · *defined*
**Definition:** In a metallic bond, every atom gives its outer electrons to a pool shared by the whole piece of metal. The electrons move freely, and they hold all the atoms together.

Metal atoms hold their outer electrons weakly. In a piece of metal, these electrons leave their own atoms. They move freely among all the atoms. What is left of each atom has a plus charge. The plus atoms are held together by the minus electrons moving between them. This explains what metals do. A metal bends because its atoms can slide to new places and the electrons still hold them. So a ball of foil flattens and a paper clip bends. A metal carries electricity because its free electrons can flow along it. A metal shines because the free electrons send light back from its surface. Free electrons carry heat too, so a steel spoon in hot rasam soon feels hot. Metals melt at very different temperatures. Mercury is a liquid at room temperature. Aluminium melts at 660 degrees Celsius, and iron at about 1,540. An alloy is a metal mixed with other elements. Atoms of a different size make it harder for the layers to slide. So steel is harder than pure iron.
- e.g. Gold is beaten into leaf only a few hundred atoms thick.
- e.g. Copper is pulled into wire for every house.
- e.g. Bronze idols and bell-metal urulis are alloys of copper and tin.
### When salt carries electricity, and when it does not · *defined*
**Definition:** Electricity passes through a material only if it has charged particles that are free to move. In a metal these are electrons. In salt water they are ions.

In Dry Salt, Wet Salt, the steel spoon carried electricity and the candle did not. Dry salt did not carry it. Wet salt did. Dry salt is full of charged ions, but each one is held in place by its neighbours. Charges that cannot move cannot carry electricity. Water pulls the ions out of the lattice. Now they can drift through the water. The plus sodium ions drift towards the minus wire. The minus chloride ions drift towards the plus wire. That moving charge is the current. Sugar dissolves as whole molecules with no charge, so sugar water carries hardly any electricity. Pure water carries hardly any either. Tap water carries a little, because it always has some salts dissolved in it. Melting frees ions too. In 1807 Humphry Davy melted a white solid called potash and passed electricity through it. Shining drops of a new metal, potassium, appeared. A substance that carries electricity through its moving ions is called an electrolyte. Your nerves and muscles work by moving sodium and potassium ions through water.
- e.g. Salt water carries electricity, and dry salt does not.
- e.g. ORS puts back the electrolytes that a sick child loses.
- e.g. The liquid inside a phone battery carries lithium ions from one side to the other.
### Which bond is it? The kinds of atoms, and four tests · *defined*
**Definition:** The kinds of atoms in a substance tell you which bond to expect. Tests then check it.

Start with the atoms. Metal atoms with non-metal atoms usually make ionic bonds. Non-metal atoms alone make covalent bonds. Metal atoms alone make metallic bonds. Then test. An ionic solid is brittle, and it does not melt on a kitchen flame. It often dissolves in water, and then the water carries electricity. A solid made of covalent molecules is soft or brittle, and it melts easily. It does not carry electricity, dry or dissolved. A metal bends or flattens, and it carries electricity as a dry solid. No single test is enough. Sugar is brittle like salt, and it dissolves like salt. Only the electricity test tells them apart. There are exceptions. Pencil lead is graphite, which is covalent, yet it carries electricity. Each carbon atom in graphite has one electron that is free to move. Lemon juice and vinegar are made of covalent molecules. In water some of these molecules split into ions, so they carry some electricity.
- e.g. Sodium fluoride is a metal with a non-metal, so it is ionic.
- e.g. Carbon dioxide is two non-metals, so it is covalent.
- e.g. Brass is copper and zinc, two metals, so it is metallic.
### Why water dissolves salt but not wax · *defined*
**Definition:** The electrons in a water molecule are not shared equally. This gives each water molecule a slightly minus end and a slightly plus end, which attract ions.

In a water molecule, the oxygen atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly than the hydrogen atoms do. So the electrons spend more time near the oxygen. The oxygen end of the molecule is slightly minus. The hydrogen ends are slightly plus. When salt meets water, the oxygen ends turn towards the plus sodium ions. The hydrogen ends turn towards the minus chloride ions. Many water molecules together attract an ion more strongly than the lattice holds it. The ion comes away with a coat of water molecules around it. Wax molecules share their electrons almost equally, so they have no charged ends. Water molecules attract each other far more than they attract wax. So wax stays out of the water. In Three Solids, Three Tests, the salt dissolved and the wax floated unchanged.
- e.g. Salt and sugar dissolve in water.
- e.g. Oil, ghee and wax do not dissolve in water.
- e.g. Water runs off a waxy lotus leaf in round drops.
### Most real bonds are a mixture · *defined*
**Definition:** Ionic, covalent and metallic are three pure types. Most real bonds lie somewhere between them.

In 1932 the American chemist Linus Pauling gave each element a number. The number says how strongly its atoms attract shared electrons. If two atoms have the same number, they share equally. That is a pure covalent bond, as in oxygen gas. If the numbers differ a little, the sharing is unequal, as in water. If they differ a lot, one atom takes the electron almost fully, as in salt. So there is no sharp line between sharing and giving. Even in salt, the electron is not given completely. Later chemists drew a triangle with the three pure types at its corners. Salt sits near the ionic corner, and copper near the metallic corner. Most substances sit somewhere inside the triangle.
- e.g. In oxygen gas, the two atoms share their electrons equally.
- e.g. In water, the oxygen atom pulls the shared electrons harder than the hydrogen atoms do.
- e.g. In salt, the sodium atom gives its electron almost completely to the chlorine atom.
### Bonds can change under pressure: hydrogen inside Jupiter · *idea*
**Definition:** Hydrogen on Earth is a gas of covalent molecules. Scientists think that deep inside Jupiter, it is squeezed into a liquid metal.

The kind of bond is not fixed for ever by the kind of atom. It also depends on how hard the atoms are pressed together. In 1935 Eugene Wigner and Hillard Huntington worked out that hydrogen should become a metal under great pressure. The atoms would be pushed so close that their electrons would be shared by all. In 1996 a team in California squeezed liquid hydrogen with a shock for a tiny moment. It carried electricity like a metal. Deep inside Jupiter the pressure is millions of times greater than our air pressure. Scientists think most of Jupiter's hydrogen is a liquid metal. Currents in it would explain Jupiter's very strong magnetism. Nobody has been there to check. In 2017 two scientists at Harvard reported solid metallic hydrogen made in a laboratory. Other teams doubt the result, and it has not been confirmed.
- e.g. Hydrogen gas in a balloon is made of molecules of two atoms.
- e.g. Jupiter has the strongest magnetism of any planet in our Sun's family.
- e.g. Earth's own magnetism comes from liquid iron moving in its core.
### Is a bond a thing? · *debated*
**Definition:** Chemists draw a bond as a line between two atoms. They disagree about whether that line stands for a real object.

Nobody has ever picked up a bond. What exists for certain is the nuclei and the electrons. A bond is the place where electrons gather between two nuclei, and this makes the atoms steadier together than apart. People argue about whether that place should be called a thing.
- e.g. A chemist draws a water molecule as an H, a line, an O, another line, and a second H. Each line stands for one bond.
- e.g. In 2009 a microscope picture of a molecule showed bright lines where chemists draw bonds.
- **Many working chemists:** Bonds are real. They have a length and a strength that can be measured. In 2009 Leo Gross and his team in Zurich pictured a single molecule with a very fine microscope. The picture showed lines where the bonds were drawn.
- **Some chemists and physicists who work with quantum theory:** Only nuclei and electrons are real. The electrons spread through the whole molecule as a cloud. A bond is our name for one part of that cloud. The lines in the picture show where electrons are thick, which fits both views.

## Mostly agreed
- Salt has no molecules. Its ions sit in turns in a lattice, and each has six neighbours of the other kind. *How we know:* In 1913 Lawrence Bragg worked this out from the pattern that X-rays make when they pass through a salt crystal. Every later measurement has agreed.
- Melted or dissolved salts carry electricity, because their ions move. *How we know:* In 1807 Humphry Davy passed electricity through melted potash and got potassium metal. In 1834 Michael Faraday began to call the moving particles ions. In the 1880s Svante Arrhenius showed that the ions are already present in salt water before any current flows.
- The electricity in a metal is carried by electrons that are free to move. *How we know:* In 1916 Richard Tolman and Dale Stewart spun a coil of wire very fast and stopped it suddenly. A brief current flowed, because the free electrons kept moving for a moment. Its direction showed the moving charges were minus.
- A covalent bond is a pair of electrons shared by two atoms. *How we know:* Gilbert Lewis proposed this in 1916. In 1927 Walter Heitler and Fritz London calculated the bond in a hydrogen molecule from quantum theory. Their answer came close to the measured strength.
- Covalent bonds hold atoms at fixed distances and angles. *How we know:* In 1929 Kathleen Lonsdale used X-rays to show that a ring of six carbon atoms is flat. She measured the distances between the atoms. Dorothy Hodgkin later mapped penicillin and insulin atom by atom in the same way.
- There is no sharp line between sharing an electron and giving it. *How we know:* In 1932 Linus Pauling measured bond strengths and gave each element a number for how strongly it attracts electrons. Bonds between elements with different numbers come out part shared and part given.

## How we found out
**William Bragg and his son Lawrence Bragg in England, and the chemist Henry Armstrong, who objected, The Braggs began in 1912. Lawrence Bragg published the structure of salt in 1913. They shared the Nobel Prize in 1915. Armstrong's letter appeared in 1927..** Before 1913, chemists were sure that salt was made of molecules. Each molecule was thought to be one sodium atom joined to one chlorine atom. In 1912 William Bragg and his son Lawrence, in England, began to shine X-rays through crystals. The rays bounced off the layers of atoms and made a pattern of spots on a photographic plate. From the spots, the two men could work out where the atoms sat. In 1913 Lawrence, aged 23, worked out the pattern of salt. No sodium atom was paired with one chlorine atom. Instead, sodium and chlorine sat in turns, like the squares of a chessboard. Each had six of the other kind around it. Many chemists disliked this. In 1927 the well-known chemist Henry Armstrong wrote to the journal Nature. He wrote against the chessboard idea. But every later measurement agreed with the Braggs. In 1915 father and son shared the Nobel Prize in Physics. Lawrence was 25, and nobody younger has won that prize.  
*What people believed before:* That salt is made of separate molecules, each with one sodium atom and one chlorine atom. X-ray patterns showed a lattice in which every ion has six equal neighbours and no partner of its own.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Salt is made of salt molecules, each with one sodium atom and one chlorine atom.** (Most chemists before 1913, and Henry Armstrong as late as 1927.; *rejected*)  
  For: The formula NaCl looks like a molecule. Water and sugar really are made of molecules.  
  Against: X-rays show that each ion has six equal neighbours and no partner of its own. The formula NaCl only tells us the ratio, which is one sodium ion for every chloride ion.
- **Salt water has no ions until an electric current makes them.** (Michael Faraday and most chemists before the 1880s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Ions were first noticed only when a current was flowing.  
  Against: Svante Arrhenius showed in the 1880s that dissolved salt is already split into ions. Salt lowers the freezing point of water about twice as much as the same number of sugar molecules. That fits two particles for every lesson of salt.
- **Pure water carries electricity well.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: People get electric shocks with wet hands, so water seems to carry electricity.  
  Against: Pure water carries very little. The salts dissolved in tap water, sweat and sea water carry it. Since nearly all water around us has salts, wet hands near a socket are truly dangerous.
- **Ionic, covalent and metallic are three separate kinds of bond.** (Many school books.; *debated*)  
  For: The three types explain the tests in this lesson well.  
  Against: Linus Pauling showed in 1932 that sharing shades into giving with no sharp line. Graphite is covalent and still carries electricity. The three types are corners, and most bonds lie between them.
- **Every atom bonds until it has eight outer electrons.** (A rule of thumb from Gilbert Lewis and Irving Langmuir, used in most school books.; *debated*)  
  For: The rule gives the right formula for salt, water and many other compounds.  
  Against: Hydrogen stops at two. Many atoms, such as sulphur and iron, often break the rule. Chemists use it as a quick guide and check it against measurements.
- **Hydrogen, the lightest gas, becomes a metal if it is squeezed hard enough.** (Eugene Wigner and Hillard Huntington in 1935.; *debated*)  
  For: Liquid hydrogen squeezed by a shock carried electricity like a metal in 1996. Jupiter's strong magnetism fits a deep layer of metallic hydrogen.  
  Against: Solid metallic hydrogen was reported in 2017, but other teams doubt it and it has not been confirmed. The pressure needed is very hard to reach and to measure.

## Roots
In this lesson you found three ways that atoms hold on to each other. Here we ask what salt, water and metal tell us about what a thing is.

**Deep truth.** In Dry Salt, Wet Salt, dry salt carried no electricity. The same salt carried it once water was added, and water alone carried almost none. So the charged ions were in the dry salt all along, each held in place by its neighbours. Whether salt carries electricity depends on whether its ions are free to move.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort materials into conductors and insulators, as if each label were fixed inside the material. Salt is an insulator when dry and a conductor when wet. The label describes how the particles are held at that moment.  
*Evidence:* In Dry Salt, Wet Salt, the same two teaspoons of salt gave no bubbles dry and many bubbles wet. Plain water and wet sugar gave almost none. In Slide a Row, every ion in the coin model was held on all sides.

- *who am i:* Your nerves and muscles work by ions moving through water. Sodium ions from the salt you eat are part of how you move your hand.
- *what is life:* A living body uses covalent bonds and ionic bonds together. Long covalent chains make up its flesh. Ionic crystals make up bones and shells. Free ions carry messages along the nerves.
- *what is this world:* A salt pan, a river and a copper mine are all built from atoms. Each atom has a plus centre with minus electrons around it. They are very different because the outer electrons sit in different places.

Every bond in this lesson is the same attraction, between plus charges and minus electrons. Salt, water and copper differ only in where the outer electrons end up. They are given, or shared by two atoms, or shared by all. From that one attraction come hardness, melting, shine and the signals in your nerves. Many sages taught that the many things of the world come from one source. The science here says something close and plain: thousands of materials come from one attraction, arranged in different ways.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** “लवणमेतदुदकेऽवधायाथ मा प्रातरुपसीदथा इति / तं होवाच । यद्दोषा लवणमुदकेऽवाधा अङ्ग तदाहरेति / तद्धावमृश्य न विवेद / मध्यादाचामेति । कथमिति । लवणमिति / तं होवाचात्र वाव किल तत्सोम्य न निभालयसेऽत्रैव किलेति” Uddalaka asks his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the salt cannot be found, yet every sip of the water is salty. (The salt has not gone. Its ions have left the lattice and spread through the water. The Upanishad uses this as a picture of the self present in everything, which science does not test.)
- **Nagarjuna, Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18:** “यः प्रतीत्यसमुत्पादः शून्यतां तां प्रचक्ष्महे / सा प्रज्ञप्तिरुपादाय प्रतिपत् सैव मध्यमा” Whatever arises in dependence on other things has no fixed nature of its own. (Salt does not carry electricity by its own nature, and water hardly does. Together they do. What salt can do depends on what it is with.)
- **The Jain teaching of syadvada, set out in Mallisena's Syadvadamanjari (1292):** A statement about a thing is true 'in some respect'. From one side a thing is so, and from another side it is otherwise. (Is salt a conductor? When wet, yes. When dry, no. The Jain teachers asked us to state the condition along with the claim, as a careful scientist does.)
- Science (settled): Every chemical bond comes from one force, the electric attraction between nuclei and electrons. Quantum theory has been used to calculate bonds since 1927.
- Science (debated): Chemists and philosophers argue about whether a bond is a real object. The other view is that it is a useful drawing of where electrons are.
- Other view, Many chemists and physicists: Words such as 'gives', 'shares' and 'holds on' are only figures of speech. Charges attract each other by the electric force, and electrons settle where the energy is lowest. On this view, wet salt carrying electricity is an ordinary fact of physics. It says nothing deeper about what a thing is.
- Practice, One grain on the tongue (3 min): Hold a steel spoon in one hand and one grain of salt in the other. Feel how hard each one is. Now put the grain on your tongue and close your eyes. Notice the moment the taste begins. The ions are leaving the crystal and moving into the water of your mouth. Sit quietly for five breaths while the taste spreads.
- Ask: In Dry Salt, Wet Salt, the same salt was an insulator and then a conductor. Which one is salt really?
- Ask: In a grain of salt, no ion belongs to just one partner. In a copper wire, the electrons are shared among all the atoms. So where does one piece of salt or copper end?
- Ask: The sodium in salt was once a metal that burns in water. Is it still the same thing now?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Sodium metal burns in water, but the sodium ions in salt do not burn. Are they the same thing? (hint: A sodium ion has the same nucleus as a sodium atom. The only difference is one electron that is missing. So what is it that makes a thing what it is?)
- *deep:* Dry salt does not carry electricity, and wet salt does. Is carrying electricity a fact about salt, or about salt and water together? (hint: Think about what you found in Dry Salt, Wet Salt. Could you call salt a conductor, an insulator, or neither?)
- *deep:* In a copper wire, the free electrons belong to no single atom. So is the wire many separate atoms, or one thing? (hint: In a salt crystal, no ion belongs to just one partner either. So where would you say one piece ends and the next begins?)
- *deep:* Nobody has picked up a bond. Is a bond a thing, or a name for what electrons do? (hint: A wave on the sea can be seen and measured. Is a wave a thing?)
- *big:* Your nerves send messages by moving ions through water. Where did the ions in your body come from? (hint: Think about salt, food, the sea and the rocks that rivers wear down.)
- *big:* Lawrence Bragg was 23, and a famous older chemist argued against his idea. How should people decide whom to trust? (hint: Think about what each of them could show, and whether other people could repeat it.)

## Story: The Bell That Sang at Dawn
*Poongodi, Aachi, Kathir and Mumtaz are made up. These facts are real. Vedaranyam is a town on the coast of Tamil Nadu where people make salt from sea water. Sea wind leaves a thin crust of salt on things near the shore. Sea salt takes in water from damp air. A musical doorbell starts playing when a very small current passes between the two contacts of its button. Such bells can ring by themselves in rain or mist.*

The bell sang at ten to six in the morning, and nobody was at the gate.

Poongodi heard it from her mat. It was the new bell that her aunt had brought from Nagapattinam. It ran on two pencil cells, and it played a tune like a film song. Its button was screwed to the gatepost, facing the sea road.

Aachi was already up. She was drawing the Margazhi kolam by the front step. She looked at the empty gate, and went on drawing.

"That is the third morning," said Poongodi.

"It is the sea," said Aachi. "She was ringing bells here before there were bells."

Aachi had worked for forty years in the salt pans of Vedaranyam. She said things like that, and she did not explain them. Poongodi was nine, and she liked things explained.

So she kept watch. At noon the gatepost was hot and dry, and the bell was silent. Her little cousin Kathir pressed the button, and it sang. He let go, and it stopped. Nothing was wrong with the bell. At four it was silent. At night she fell asleep still listening.

At ten to six the next morning it sang again. This time Poongodi reached the gate before the tune ended.

Nobody was there. A thick white mist lay over the road. She could not see the neem tree on the far side. The gatepost was wet, as if someone had washed it. She put her finger on the wet wood, and then on her tongue.

It tasted of salt.

She looked closely at the button. Its plastic cover had a crack across it. Around the crack, and over the two small screws, lay a thin white crust. She knew that crust. It was on the window bars and on the cycle. It was on Aachi's spectacles every evening. The sea wind carried salt in, and left it on everything.

"It is the salt," she told Aachi. "The salt joins the two screws, just as the button does."

"The salt is there at noon also," said Aachi. She did not look up from her kolam. "At noon your bell is quiet."

That was true, and it was annoying. The crust sat on the button all day long. The bell sang only at dawn.

Poongodi thought about it through breakfast. She thought about the wet gatepost. She thought about Aachi's salt, which lay in dry white heaps beside the pans. Aachi always said that a heap was finished, and the wet pan was still at work.

At noon she went back to the gate with Kathir. The crust was dry and bright in the sun. The bell was silent.

"Watch," she said. She put her mouth close to the button, as she did to clean her spectacles. She breathed out, long and slow. "Haaaa."

For a moment nothing happened. Then the bell began to sing.

Kathir jumped. "You did not touch it!"

"I only made it wet." The tune ran on. Then the sun dried the crust, and the tune stopped in the middle.

She dipped one finger in the water pot and let a single drop fall on the crack. The bell sang again. This time it would not stop until Kathir wiped the button with his shirt.

"Dry salt sits still," said Poongodi slowly. "Wet salt lets the bell's electricity cross. The mist comes at dawn and wets the crust. That is all."

Aachi had come to the gate with her kolam powder still on her fingers. "So I was wrong?" she asked.

Poongodi thought about it. "No. The sea sends the salt, and the sea sends the mist. It is the sea. You only left out the middle."

Aachi laughed until she had to hold the gatepost.

They mended it together. Poongodi took the two cells out first. Aachi poured drinking water over the button until no white was left. They let it dry in the sun for an hour. Then Poongodi lit the stub of a candle and let the wax drip over the crack and over both screws. The wax set hard and smooth.

"Why wax?" asked Kathir.

"Water runs off wax. And wax lets nothing cross it, wet or dry."

The next dawn the mist came in as thick as before. The gatepost dripped. Poongodi lay on her mat with her eyes open, and the bell did not sing.

At half past six it sang.

She ran to the gate. Mumtaz, who sold fish from a basket on her head, stood there with her thumb on the button.

"Your bell works," said Mumtaz.

"Now it does," said Poongodi. "Now it only sings for people."

**For the parent:** The salt crust lay on the button all day, but the bell sang only when the mist wetted it. Dry salt does not carry electricity, because its charged ions are held in place. Wet salt carries it, because the ions can move through the water.

- Ask: The salt crust was on the button at noon and at dawn. What was different at dawn?
- Ask: A drop of water on a salt crust lets electricity cross. What do you think the water does to the salt?
- Ask: Why did candle wax keep the bell quiet in the mist, when salt did not?

## Experiments
### e1 · Three Solids, Three Tests: press, water and heat (25 min)
- **question:** Salt, candle wax and aluminium foil are all solids. What does each one do when it is pressed, put in water and heated over a candle?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which solid will break when pressed? Which will dissolve in water? Which will melt over a candle within one minute? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use pieces of about the same size, each as big as a pea. The same person presses every piece, as hard as they can, with the same spoon. Use the same amount of water and stir each tumbler 30 times. Hold the spoon at the same height over the same flame. Heat each solid for a slow count of 60. Only the solid changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights the candle and holds the spoon over the flame. Tie back long hair. Heat only about ten grains of salt, and keep faces away, because a hot salt grain can crack and jump. The spoon stays hot for some minutes, so put it on the steel plate to cool. Do not taste anything after it has been heated.
- **record:** Draw a table with three rows: salt, wax and foil. Make three columns: pressed, in water and heated. Write what you saw in each box.
- **results table:** The solid | Pressed with a spoon | Stirred in water | Heated for a count of 60
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the salt, the wax pieces and the foil balls on the plate. Ask for the three guesses and the reasons. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Here are salt, wax and foil. We will press each one, put each one in water, and heat each one. What do you think each will do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the salt, wax and foil. Guess what each will do. Say why.
  - Ask: Which one do you think will be the hardest to melt, and why? (good: The foil, because it is metal.; The salt, because it never melts in cooking.; Any guess with a reason.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Many children expect the metal to be the only one that will not melt.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* First draw the results table described under Record. Put the salt crystal on the plate and cover it with the folded paper. Your child presses hard with the back of a spoon. Lift the paper and look. Do the same with the wax and the foil ball.  
  *Parent says:* Press as hard as you can, then lift the paper. What happened to the salt? Now press the wax. Now press the foil. Write what you see each time.  
  *Child mode:* Cover the salt. Press hard with the spoon. Look. Then press the wax and the foil.
  - Note: The paper stops pieces flying. A salt crystal cracks with a small snap into pieces with flat sides. With fine salt, press a pinch between two spoons and listen for the crunch.
  - Check: In Three Solids, Three Tests, why does the same person press every solid? → **So each solid gets the same push**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put a quarter teaspoon of salt in the first tumbler. Put a piece of wax in the second and a foil ball in the third. Your child stirs each one 30 times and writes what they see.  
  *Parent says:* Stir each tumbler 30 times. Count aloud. Which solid can you still see? Which one has gone into the water?  
  *Child mode:* Put each solid in water. Stir 30 times. Look closely at each tumbler.
  - Note: The salt dissolves. The wax floats and the foil ball floats or sinks, and neither changes.
- **s4 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Light the candle. Put about ten grains of salt in the heating spoon. Hold the spoon with the cloth, just above the tip of the flame. Count slowly to 60 together. Then heat a fresh foil ball the same way. Heat the wax last.  
  *Parent says:* I will hold the spoon. Stand one step back and count slowly to 60 with me. Watch the solid. Tell me the moment anything changes.  
  *Child mode:* Stand one step back. Count to 60. Watch the solid in the spoon.
  - Note: Salt and foil stay solid. A grain of salt may crack and jump. Wax melts to a clear liquid within about 15 seconds, so heat it last to keep the spoon clean. Soot on the spoon wipes off when it is cool.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Let us look at our table together. Did any two solids behave the same way in all three tests?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Salt and foil both stayed solid over the flame. What test tells them apart? (good: Pressing. The salt cracked and the foil only flattened.; Water. The salt dissolved and the foil did not.)
  - Ask: What might be different about the way the atoms hold on in each solid? (good: In wax the pieces let go of each other easily.; In salt they hold tightly but cannot slide.; In foil they hold and can still slide.)
  - Note: The reason: the three solids are held in three ways. Salt is plus and minus ions in a fixed pattern. They hold tightly, so salt does not melt. If a layer slips, like charges meet and push apart, so salt cracks. Wax is molecules that hold each other weakly, so it squashes and melts. Foil is metal atoms that share their outer electrons. The atoms can slide and still be held, so foil flattens.
  - Think: Sugar also cracks when pressed and dissolves in water. What test might tell sugar from salt?
### e2 · Dry Salt, Wet Salt: when does salt carry electricity? (30 min)
- **question:** Which of these carry electricity? Test a steel spoon, a candle, dry sugar, dry salt, plain water, wet sugar and wet salt.
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which things will make the tester bubble? Will dry salt and wet salt give the same result? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same tester for every test, and do not move the pencils. Hold the two probes one finger-width apart each time. Count slowly to 20 for every test. Use 2 teaspoons of each powder, and add 1 teaspoon of the same water to each. Wipe both probes dry with a clean cloth after every test. Test the salt last, so that no salt reaches the sugar.
- **safety:** Use only a 9-volt battery. Never use a wall socket or a charger. Do not let the bare ends of the red and black wires touch each other, because the battery gets hot. Do the wet salt test only once, for a count of 20, in a room with a window open. Do not taste the powders after the test. Wash the saucers afterwards.
- **record:** Draw a table with seven rows: steel spoon, candle, dry sugar, dry salt, plain water, wet sugar and wet salt. Beside each, write many, few or none.
- **results table:** What we tested | Bubbles at the pencil tips: many, few or none
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the spoon, the candle, the sugar, the salt and the water. Ask which will carry electricity, and why. Write the guesses down.  
  *Parent says:* We will test seven things. Which do you think will carry electricity? Will dry salt and wet salt be the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the things on the table. Guess which ones carry electricity.
  - Ask: Why do you think wet salt will or will not carry electricity? (good: Water carries electricity.; Salt is not a metal, so it will not.; Any guess with a reason.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say that the water does the carrying.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Push the two pencils through the cardboard, 2 centimetres apart. Rest the cardboard on the tumbler of soda water, with the lower tips under water. Twist the bare end of the red wire round the top tip of one pencil. Wrap one end of the foil ribbon tightly round the top tip of the other pencil. Hold it in place with the clothes clip or tape. Now touch the free end of the foil to the bare end of the black wire.  
  *Parent says:* The black wire and the loose end of the foil are our two probes. Touch them together and watch the pencil tips in the water. What do you see? Now pull them apart.  
  *Child mode:* Touch the two probes together. Watch the pencil tips. Then pull the probes apart.
  - Note: Fine bubbles should stream from both lower tips within a few seconds, and stop when the probes part. If not, press the wire and the foil harder against the pencil tips. The soda water is only part of the tester. It stays the same all through.
  - Check: In the bubble tester, what do bubbles at the pencil tips tell you? → **Electricity is passing between the probes**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child holds both probes on the steel spoon, one finger-width apart, for a slow count of 20. Write many, few or none. Do the same on the side of the candle.  
  *Parent says:* Hold both probes on the spoon. Do not let them touch each other. Count to 20 and watch the pencil tips. Now try the candle.  
  *Child mode:* Hold both probes on the spoon. Count to 20. Then test the candle.
  - Note: The spoon gives many bubbles. The candle gives none.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make a heap of 2 teaspoons of sugar on one dry saucer and 2 teaspoons of salt on another. Push both probes into the sugar, one finger-width apart, and count to 20. Wipe the probes. Test the dry salt. Wipe the probes. Then pour 1 teaspoon of water on the third saucer and test the small pool.  
  *Parent says:* Push the probes into the dry sugar. Count to 20. Now wipe them and try the dry salt. Then try the small pool of plain water. Write each result.  
  *Child mode:* Test the dry sugar. Wipe the probes. Test the dry salt. Then test plain water.
  - Note: The saucers must not be metal, because a metal plate would carry the electricity itself. Dry sugar and dry salt give none. Plain water gives none, or a very few slow bubbles, because tap water has a little salt in it. On a damp monsoon day, salt takes in water from the air and may give a few bubbles. Warm it in a dry pan for a minute first.
- **s5 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Add 1 teaspoon of water to the sugar heap and stir with a matchstick. Test it for a count of 20. Wipe the probes. Add 1 teaspoon of water to the salt heap and stir with a fresh matchstick. Test it for a count of 20.  
  *Parent says:* Now we wet the sugar and test it. Then we wet the salt and test it. Watch the pencil tips closely each time. Which one changed?  
  *Child mode:* Wet the sugar and test it. Wipe the probes. Wet the salt and test it.
  - Note: Wet sugar gives the same as plain water. Wet salt gives many bubbles at once, and it may fizz at the probes too. The salt paste may turn grey or green where the probes react with it. Throw the paste away and wash the saucer.
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Let us look at our table together. What did the water change about the salt?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Plain water gave almost no bubbles, and wet sugar gave almost none. So what carried the electricity in the wet salt? (good: Something from the salt.; The salt, once it was in the water.; Charged particles from the salt.)
  - Ask: The spoon carried electricity dry. Why might a metal need no water? (good: Something in the metal can already move.; Its electrons are free.)
  - Note: The reason: electricity passes only where charged particles can move. Salt is made of charged particles called ions. In dry salt each ion is held in place by its neighbours, so nothing can move. Water pulls the ions apart, and they drift through the water and carry the electricity. Sugar is made of molecules with no charge, so wet sugar carries almost none. In the steel spoon, electrons are free to move all the time.
  - Think: A doorbell button near the sea has a crust of dry salt on it. What might happen on a misty morning?

## Worksheets
### Three ways to hold on (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is an ion?  
   Options: An atom with an electric charge / A very small molecule / A kind of metal / An atom with no electrons  
   **Answer:** An atom with an electric charge. *Why:* An ion is an atom that has lost or gained electrons, so its plus and minus charges no longer balance.
2. (mcq) Which bond forms when a metal atom gives electrons to a non-metal atom?  
   Options: Ionic / Covalent / Metallic / No bond forms  
   **Answer:** Ionic. *Why:* Giving an electron makes a plus ion and a minus ion. Their attraction is an ionic bond.
3. (fill) A bond made when two atoms share a pair of electrons is called a ____ bond.  
   **Answer:** ['covalent']. *Why:* Both atoms attract the shared pair, and that holds them together. Non-metal atoms bond in this way.
4. (truefalse) A grain of salt is made of salt molecules.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Salt is a lattice of ions. Each ion has six neighbours of the other kind, and no partner of its own.
5. (match) Match each bond to what the outer electrons do.  
   **Answer:** Ionic → Move from one atom to another; Covalent → Are shared by two atoms; Metallic → Are shared by all the atoms. *Why:* The three bonds differ in where the outer electrons end up: given, shared by two, or shared by all.
6. (sort) Sort these materials by the kind of bond that holds them.  
   **Answer:** table salt → Ionic; cooking soda → Ionic; sugar → Covalent; water → Covalent; steel spoon → Metallic; aluminium foil → Metallic. *Why:* A metal with a non-metal is ionic. Non-metals alone are covalent. Metals alone are metallic.
7. (mcq) Why does a metal bend without breaking?  
   Options: Its atoms slide and still hold / Its atoms are soft / It has no bonds / Its ions repel each other  
   **Answer:** Its atoms slide and still hold. *Why:* The free electrons hold the atoms wherever they slide to. If you chose soft atoms, remember that the atoms do not change. Only their places change.
8. (truefalse) Dry salt carries electricity.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The ions in dry salt are held in place. Charges must be free to move before they can carry electricity.
9. (mcq) Candle wax melts in seconds over a flame. Why?  
   Options: Its molecules hold each other weakly / Its covalent bonds break / It is made of ions / It has free electrons  
   **Answer:** Its molecules hold each other weakly. *Why:* Melting only lets whole molecules slide past each other. The covalent bonds inside each wax molecule stay unbroken.
10. (fill) A material that can be beaten into a new shape without breaking is ____.  
   **Answer:** ['malleable']. *Why:* Metals are malleable because their atoms can slide to new places and still be held by the free electrons.
11. (order) Put these solids in order of melting point, lowest first.  
   **Answer:** ['Ice', 'Candle wax', 'Aluminium', 'Salt']. *Why:* Ice melts at 0 degrees Celsius and wax at about 60. Aluminium melts at 660 and salt at about 800. The two solids made of molecules melt most easily.
12. (mcq) What carries electricity through a copper wire?  
   Options: Free electrons / Moving ions / Water molecules / Copper molecules  
   **Answer:** Free electrons. *Why:* In a metal the outer electrons are free to flow. Ions carry electricity in salt water, and copper has no molecules.
13. (write) In Three Solids, Three Tests, salt cracked and foil flattened. Explain why in two sentences.  
   **Answer:** In salt, a slipped layer brings like charges together, and they push the crystal apart. In foil, the atoms slide and the free electrons still hold them.. *Why:* A strong answer says what happens when a layer slips in each solid. It names like charges for salt and free electrons for metal.
14. (write) In Dry Salt, Wet Salt, dry salt gave no bubbles and wet salt gave many. What did the water do?  
   **Answer:** The water pulled the ions out of the lattice. Then the ions could move through the water and carry the electricity.. *Why:* A strong answer says the ions were already in the dry salt. The water freed them. It did not carry the electricity itself.
15. (draw) Draw a flat piece of a salt crystal as a pattern of plus and minus ions. Show eight of each.  
   **Answer:** Four rows of four circles. Plus and minus take turns along every row and down every column. So each ion has only the other kind beside it.. *Why:* A strong drawing does not show any ion paired up with just one neighbour. Every plus ion touches only minus ions along its sides.
### Why each solid behaves as it does (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Sodium atoms react fiercely. Sodium ions in salt do not. Why?  
   Options: The ion has already given its electron / The ion is much bigger / Salt holds no sodium / The ion is frozen  
   **Answer:** The ion has already given its electron. *Why:* A sodium atom reacts by giving away its outer electron. The ion has no such electron left to give, so it does not react in that way.
2. (mcq) In a salt crystal, how many chloride ions surround each sodium ion?  
   Options: Six / One / Two / Eight  
   **Answer:** Six. *Why:* Each sodium ion has four chloride ions beside it in its own layer. It has one more above and one more below, which makes six. If you chose one, remember that no ion in salt has just one partner.
3. (truefalse) Melting ice breaks the covalent bonds inside water molecules.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Melting only lets whole water molecules slide past each other. Each molecule is still one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms.

4. (mcq) Which of these is made of molecules?  
   Options: Sugar / Table salt / Copper / Brass  
   **Answer:** Sugar. *Why:* Sugar is carbon, hydrogen and oxygen joined by covalent bonds. Salt is a lattice of ions, and copper and brass are metals.
5. (mcq) Why does a metal shine and carry electricity?  
   Options: It has free electrons / It has ions in a lattice / It is made of molecules / It is very heavy  
   **Answer:** It has free electrons. *Why:* Free electrons flow as a current, and they send light back from the surface.
6. (truefalse) Salt melts on a kitchen flame.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Salt melts at about 800 degrees Celsius, because every ion is held strongly on all sides.
7. (mcq) When does salt carry electricity?  
   Options: When it is dissolved or melted / Only when it is dry / Never / Only when it is cold  
   **Answer:** When it is dissolved or melted. *Why:* Dissolving and melting both free the ions. Free ions can move, and moving charges carry electricity.
8. (mcq) A solid flattens when pressed and carries electricity when dry. What is it?  
   Options: A metal / An ionic solid / A solid made of molecules / Sugar  
   **Answer:** A metal. *Why:* Only a metal does both. An ionic solid cracks and carries electricity only when wet or melted.
9. (mcq) Magnesium is a metal and oxygen is a non-metal. Which bond joins them?  
   Options: Ionic / Covalent / Metallic / No bond forms  
   **Answer:** Ionic. *Why:* The metal atom gives electrons to the non-metal atom. Magnesium oxide is made of ions.
10. (mcq) What kind of evidence showed that salt has no molecules?  
   Options: The pattern X-rays make in a crystal / The taste of salt / The weight of salt / The colour of salt  
   **Answer:** The pattern X-rays make in a crystal. *Why:* X-rays bounce off the layers of ions and make a pattern of spots. The pattern shows every ion with six equal neighbours.
11. (truefalse) Most real bonds are partly shared and partly given.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Pure sharing and full giving are the two ends of a line, and most bonds lie between them.
12. (mcq) Why does water dissolve salt?  
   Options: Its molecules attract the ions / It melts the salt / It is heavier than salt / It breaks the atoms apart  
   **Answer:** Its molecules attract the ions. *Why:* Each water molecule has a slightly plus end and a slightly minus end. Many of them together pull an ion out of the lattice.

## Applications
- A tank-full alarm has two metal probes near the top of a water tank. When the water reaches them, the ions in the water carry a small current and a buzzer sounds.
- Electricity companies near the sea wash salt off the insulators on their power lines. Damp salt would let electricity leak across them.
- A phone battery works by moving lithium ions through a liquid from one side to the other. The electrons go round through the phone.
- ORS holds sodium and potassium salts. In water they split into ions, which the body needs for nerves and muscles to work.
- Copper and aluminium are pulled into wires, because metals stretch without breaking. Their free electrons carry the current.
- Dorothy Hodgkin used X-rays to find where every atom sits in penicillin and in insulin. Knowing the shape of a medicine's molecule helps chemists make it.

## Spotted around us
- White heaps of salt beside salt pans by the sea. The sun dries sea water, and the ions pack back into crystals.
- Silver leaf on a kaju katli or a barfi. Silver is beaten so thin that a breath can lift it.
- A bronze idol or a bell-metal pot. Both are alloys of copper and tin.
- The copper bottom of a steel cooking pot. The free electrons in copper spread heat quickly.
- Table salt that has gone damp and lumpy in the monsoon. Sea salt takes in water from the air.
- A crust of white on a tap or a steel bucket. These are ionic compounds left behind when hard water dries.

## Open questions
- **Is a chemical bond a real object, or a useful way of drawing where electrons are?** Bond lengths and strengths can be measured, and microscopes show lines between atoms. But quantum theory describes only nuclei and electrons, and chemists disagree about what to call real.
- **Can solid metallic hydrogen be made and kept?** It was reported in 2017, but other teams doubt it and it has not been confirmed. The pressure needed is close to the limit of what diamond tools can stand.
- **What is the hydrogen deep inside Jupiter and Saturn like?** Models say it is a liquid metal. No probe has gone that deep, and laboratories can hold such pressures only for a moment.
- **Can we predict how a new material will behave just from its atoms?** For simple crystals, computers do this well. For large molecules and mixtures, the calculations are still too big, and surprises are common.
- **Can a metal be made that carries electricity with no loss at room temperature?** Some materials do this when very cold or under huge pressure. Scientists do not fully understand why, so they cannot yet design one for everyday use.

## Warm-up
- What is an ion? → An atom with an electric charge, because it has lost or gained electrons.
- Name the three kinds of bond. → Ionic, covalent and metallic.
- What do the outer electrons do in each kind of bond? → In ionic bonds they are given. In covalent bonds two atoms share them. In metallic bonds all the atoms share them.
- Why does salt crack when it is pressed? → When a layer of ions slips sideways, plus ions end up next to plus ions. Minus ions end up next to minus ions. Like charges push each other apart, so the crystal cracks.
- Why does a metal bend without breaking? → Its atoms can slide past each other to new places. The free electrons still hold the atoms together, so the metal does not break.
- Why does wax melt so easily? → Wax is made of molecules that attract each other only weakly, so they slide apart easily when heated.
- When does salt carry electricity? → When it is dissolved or melted, because then its ions can move.
- A compound has a metal and a non-metal. Which bond do you expect? → Ionic.
