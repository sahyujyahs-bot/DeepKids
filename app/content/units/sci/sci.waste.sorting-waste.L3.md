# Sorting waste: why we separate it the moment we throw it
`sci.waste.sorting-waste.L3`

**Summary.** A dry newspaper can be sold, pulped and made into new paper. Drop it in a bin with wet sambar and peels, and within minutes it is soaked, stained and tears in your hand. No washing can make it good paper again. That is why we sort waste the moment we throw it: wet waste in one bin, dry waste in another, and dangerous things like batteries, tube lights and broken glass in a box of their own. You will soak waste in two jars, race to sort waste before and after it is mixed, and set up a sorting station at home.

**Intro.** *Take one sheet of newspaper and tear it in half. Dip one half in a bowl of water for a minute. Now pull each half gently from both ends. Which one tears first?* Every home in India throws out two very different kinds of waste. Wet waste is food and other things that rot, like peels, leftover rice and used tea leaves. Dry waste is things that do not rot quickly, like paper, plastic, metal and glass. Dry waste that is clean can be sold and made into new things. But when the two kinds go into one bag, the wet soaks into the dry. Can we ever separate them again afterwards? The two-jar test will show you.

## Terms
- **Waste**: Anything we have finished using and throw out. *e.g.* Onion peels, an empty toothpaste tube and yesterday's newspaper are all waste.
- **Wet waste**: Waste that rots, because tiny living things can feed on it. Most of it is food. *e.g.* Vegetable peels, leftover rice, used tea leaves, eggshells and old flowers.
- **Dry waste**: Waste that does not rot quickly, like paper, plastic, metal, glass and cloth. When clean, much of it can be recycled. *e.g.* A rinsed milk packet, a cardboard box, a steel lid, a glass jar and an old cotton rag.
- **Household hazardous waste**: Waste from home that can hurt people, animals or the soil if it is thrown out with the rest. Hazardous means dangerous. *e.g.* Old batteries, broken tube lights, broken glass, used blades, old medicines and paint tins.
- **Sorting waste**: Putting each kind of waste in its own bin or bag. People also call this segregating waste. *e.g.* Peels go in the green bin, the milk packet goes in the blue bin, and the battery goes in the danger box.
- **Sorting at source**: Sorting waste in the place where it is made, like your kitchen, before anything is mixed. *e.g.* Sorting in the kitchen is sorting at source. Sorting at a garbage dump is not.
- **Mixed waste**: Wet, dry and dangerous waste thrown together in one bag or bin. *e.g.* One plastic bag holding sambar, newspaper, a battery and a broken glass.
- **Recycle**: To break used paper, plastic, metal or glass down into raw material and make new things from it. *e.g.* Old newspapers are soaked into pulp at a paper mill and made into new paper.
- **Contaminate**: To make something dirty or unsafe by mixing something else into it. *e.g.* Sambar soaking into newspaper contaminates the paper, so it can no longer be recycled.
- **Absorb**: To soak up a liquid into the inside of something. *e.g.* Paper, cardboard and cotton absorb sambar. A plastic packet does not, so it can be rinsed.
- **Landfill**: A place where a city dumps its waste in a huge heap and covers it with soil. *e.g.* Brahmapuram near Kochi and Deonar in Mumbai are big dumping grounds.
- **Waste picker**: A person who collects and sorts waste and sells what can be recycled. Many are women. *e.g.* In Kerala, women of the Haritha Karma Sena collect sorted dry waste from homes.
- **Mercury**: A silvery metal that is liquid at room temperature. It is poisonous to people and animals. *e.g.* Tube lights and the curly CFL bulbs hold a tiny amount of mercury. This is why they go in the danger box.
- **Breed**: To have young. Mosquitoes breed by laying eggs in still water. *e.g.* Rain water caught in a thrown-away coconut shell or cup is a place where mosquitoes breed.

## Explanations
### Three kinds of household waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Household waste is sorted into three kinds: wet waste, which rots; dry waste, which does not rot quickly; and household hazardous waste, which is dangerous.

Wet waste is mostly food: peels, leftovers, tea leaves, eggshells and old flowers. Tiny living things like bacteria and fungi feed on wet waste. They turn it into compost. Dry waste is paper, plastic, metal, glass, rubber and cloth. When it is clean and dry, much of it can be recycled. Hazardous waste can hurt people or the soil, like batteries, tube lights, medicines, blades and broken glass. India's Solid Waste Management Rules of 2016 ask every home to keep these three kinds apart. Many cities use a green bin for wet waste and a blue bin for dry waste.
- e.g. Wet: a banana peel, leftover rice, used coffee powder from the filter.
- e.g. Dry: a rinsed milk packet, a cardboard box, a steel lid.
- e.g. Hazardous: an old battery, a broken tube light, a strip of expired tablets.
### Why mixing spoils dry waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Paper, cardboard and cloth absorb liquids from wet waste. Once soaked and stained, they cannot be recycled into good new material.

Paper is made of tiny plant fibres pressed together. Liquid gets in between the fibres and loosens them, so wet paper tears easily. Oil and food stuck in paper cannot be washed out at the paper mill, and they spoil the new paper. So the mill does not take it. Plastic, metal and glass do not absorb liquid. Their surface gets dirty, but they can be rinsed. So mixing hurts paper and cloth most. Mixed waste also smells, draws flies and rats, and is unsafe for the people who sort it.
- e.g. A dry newspaper can be sold to the kabadiwala. A newspaper soaked in sambar cannot.
- e.g. A pizza box with cheese stuck on it is not taken for recycling. A clean cardboard box is.
- e.g. A milk packet smeared with curd can be rinsed and dried, and then it is dry waste again.
### Mixing is quick, unmixing is slow · *defined*
**Definition:** Things mix by themselves the moment they touch. Separating them again always takes work and time, and sometimes it cannot be done.

Drop a newspaper into sambar and the sambar soaks in without anyone doing anything. Getting the sambar out again is not possible. Even things that do not soak in get stuck together. Tea leaves cling to paper, and broken glass hides in peels. So a heap of mixed waste takes many hours of hand sorting, and much of it is never sorted at all. Scientists see this everywhere in nature: sugar spreads through tea by itself, but never gathers back into a lump. It is always easier to keep things apart than to pull them apart.
- e.g. A drop of ink spreads through a glass of water by itself. Nobody can pull it back into a drop.
- e.g. In the sorting race, sorting 30 things into bins as you throw them is quick. Sorting the same 30 after they are mixed with wet tea leaves takes much longer.
- e.g. Waste pickers at dumping grounds spend their whole day pulling dry waste out of mixed heaps.
### What happens to sorted waste and to mixed waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Sorted wet waste can become compost or cooking gas, and sorted dry waste can be recycled. Mixed waste mostly ends up on a dump.

Sorted wet waste can go to a compost pot at home, or to a city compost yard or biogas plant. Bacteria make biogas from wet waste. Biogas is a gas that can be used for cooking. Sorted dry waste goes to a kabadiwala or a dry waste collection centre. There, people sort it again into paper, hard plastic, soft plastic, metal and glass, and sell each kind. Mixed waste is hard to use. Most of it is dumped on a landfill. In a landfill, the wet waste rots without air and makes methane, a gas that warms the Earth and catches fire easily. In March 2023, the dumping ground at Brahmapuram near Kochi caught fire, and smoke covered parts of the city for nearly two weeks.
- e.g. Peels from a sorted green bin become compost for a balcony plant.
- e.g. Clean newspapers from a sorted blue bin are sold by weight to the kabadiwala.
- e.g. A bag of mixed waste goes to the dumping ground, where it can rot, smell and burn.
### Dangerous waste needs its own box · *defined*
**Definition:** Household hazardous waste is kept apart, wrapped and marked, because it can cut, poison or burn the people and animals who touch it later.

Broken glass and used blades cut the hands of the people who sort waste, so wrap them in thick newspaper and write 'broken glass' on it. Tube lights and curly CFL bulbs hold a tiny amount of mercury, a poisonous metal, so do not break them. Phone and laptop batteries can catch fire when they are crushed in a garbage truck. Old medicines should never be flushed or thrown loose, because they can harm animals that eat them and get into water. Used diapers and sanitary pads are wrapped in newspaper and marked, so that nobody opens them. Keep a small box for dangerous waste, and ask your city or a shop that sells batteries or bulbs where it can be taken.
- e.g. A broken glass tumbler wrapped in newspaper and marked 'broken glass'.
- e.g. Old batteries kept in a jar until there are enough to take to a collection point.
- e.g. A box of expired tablets kept aside, not thrown in the bin.
### How waste makes a home or lane unsafe · *defined*
**Definition:** Open waste draws flies, rats, crows and stray animals, and thrown-away containers catch rainwater where mosquitoes breed.

Food in an open bin smells, and flies, cockroaches and rats come to feed. Flies walk on the waste and then on our food, carrying germs. Animals tear open garbage bags on the road and scatter the waste further. Cows and dogs may swallow plastic bags with food in them. After rain, a thrown-away cup, tyre or coconut shell holds still water. Mosquitoes breed in it, and some of them spread dengue. Plastic bags block drains, so water floods the street. A bin with a lid, waste taken out every day, and nothing left where water can collect keep a home and a lane safe.
- e.g. A coconut shell left on a terrace after rain can hold mosquito young within a week.
- e.g. A bin with a lid keeps flies and ants out of the kitchen.
- e.g. A drain blocked by plastic bags floods the road after a heavy shower.
### The people who handle our waste · *defined*
**Definition:** Collectors, waste pickers, kabadiwalas and sanitation workers carry, sort and sell our waste. Sorting at home makes their work cleaner, safer and better paid.

Every bag a home throws out is touched again by somebody's hands. In many cities, waste pickers sort dry waste and sell it, and many of them are women. In Kerala, women of the Haritha Karma Sena go from home to home collecting sorted dry waste. In Pune, waste pickers formed a group called SWaCH to collect waste from homes. When waste arrives sorted, clean paper and plastic sell for more, and nobody is cut by hidden glass. For a long time in India, cleaning other people's waste was pushed onto certain castes. Taking charge of our own waste is also about fairness.
- e.g. A collector can sell a bundle of clean, dry newspaper. A soaked bundle is worth nothing.
- e.g. A waste picker sorting a mixed bag can be cut by a blade hidden inside.
- e.g. A thank-you and a sorted bag make a waste collector's day easier.
### Whether every home should sort, or machines should do it · *debated*
Nearly everyone agrees that sorted waste is easier to use. People disagree about who should do the sorting.
- e.g. Bengaluru and Indore ask every home to hand over waste sorted.
- e.g. Some cities run sorting plants that pull out metal with magnets.
- e.g. Even a sorting plant cannot take sambar back out of a newspaper.
- **Most waste experts, and India's 2016 rules:** Sort at source. Mixing happens in seconds and cannot be undone, so the person holding the waste is the best one to sort it. It costs almost nothing at home.
- **Some engineers and city planners:** Families make mistakes and many will never sort. Big sorting plants with belts, magnets, blowers and trained workers can separate dry waste on a large scale.
- **Waste picker groups:** Homes should sort, and the waste should go to the waste pickers who already know the work, not to big plants that take away their living.

## Mostly agreed
- Clean, dry paper, plastic, metal and glass can be recycled. Paper soaked or stained with food usually cannot. *How we know:* Paper mills reject paper with food and oil on it, because these spoil the new paper. You can feel the difference yourself: soaked newspaper tears with a gentle pull.
- Sorting waste at home, before it is mixed, is the easiest and cheapest way to sort it. *How we know:* Cities that collect waste already sorted, like Indore and Mysuru, recover far more compost and recyclable material. Sorting a mixed heap by hand is slow, dirty and dangerous.
- Wet waste dumped in heaps makes methane, a gas that warms the Earth and catches fire. *How we know:* Gas is measured coming out of landfills all over the world. Dumping grounds like Deonar in Mumbai and Brahmapuram near Kochi have had large fires.
- Still water in thrown-away containers lets mosquitoes breed, and some of them spread dengue. *How we know:* Health workers find mosquito young in coconut shells, cups, tyres and pots after rain. Clearing these is one of the main ways cities fight dengue.
- Poisonous waste does not become safe by being spread out in water. *How we know:* At Minamata in Japan, mercury poured into the sea built up in fish and made people and cats very ill. This is why mercury and other dangerous waste are kept apart.

## How we found out
**Hajime Hosokawa, a doctor, the research team at Kumamoto University, and the writer Michiko Ishimure, who told the families' story, 1956 to 1968.** In the 1950s, people living around Minamata Bay in Japan began to fall ill. Their hands shook, they stumbled, and some could not speak clearly. Cats in fishing families twitched and fell, and birds dropped from the sky. A chemical factory had been pouring its waste water into the bay since the 1930s. Many people believed the sea was so big that any waste poured in would spread out and become harmless. In 1956, Hajime Hosokawa, a doctor at the factory's own hospital, reported the illness to health officials. In 1959, scientists at Kumamoto University found the cause: a poisonous form of mercury from the factory. Tiny living things in the water had taken it up. Small fish ate them, and big fish ate many small fish, so the mercury built up instead of spreading out. People and cats who ate the fish were poisoned. That same year, Hosokawa fed the factory's waste water to cats, and one became ill in the same way. The company kept his result quiet. The government only accepted the cause in 1968. Writers like Michiko Ishimure helped the world hear the families' story.  
*What people believed before:* That poisonous waste poured into the huge sea would spread out and become harmless. Instead, the mercury built up in fish, and the people and cats who ate them were poisoned.

## Challenging perspectives
- **There is no point sorting at home, because the truck mixes it all again.** (Many people living in cities; *rejected*)  
  For: In some places this really happened. Workers tipped sorted bags into one truck, and people stopped bothering.  
  Against: Most cities now collect wet and dry waste in separate parts of the truck or on separate days. Where they don't, the fix is to complain, not to stop sorting. Once mixed at home, the dry waste is already spoiled.
- **Waste-to-energy plants can burn mixed waste, so we don't need to sort.** (Some companies and city officials; *debated*)  
  For: Burning waste in a closed plant can make electricity. Burning also shrinks the heap.  
  Against: Indian waste is about half wet food, which is full of water and burns badly. Several such plants in India have struggled and been blamed for smoke. Burning also destroys paper and plastic that could have been recycled.
- **Composting is only for houses with gardens.** (Many families in flats; *rejected*)  
  For: A compost heap in a garden is the usual picture of composting.  
  Against: Wet waste can be composted in clay pots stacked on a balcony, or in a closed bucket with holes. Many apartment buildings in Bengaluru and Chennai run their own compost units.
- **Poisonous waste poured into the sea is safe, because the sea is so big.** (Many factories and officials until the 1960s; *rejected*)  
  For: A small amount of poison spread through a huge amount of water is very weak.  
  Against: Tiny living things in the water take up the mercury. Small fish eat them, and big fish eat many small fish, so the mercury builds up. At Minamata, people and cats who ate the fish became very ill.
- **Plastic is always the worst kind of waste.** (Many people and many posters; *debated*)  
  For: Plastic lasts for a very long time, spreads into seas and soil, and animals swallow it.  
  Against: Some dangerous waste, like mercury, batteries and medicines, is far more harmful in small amounts. And wet waste, when mixed and dumped, makes methane and fires. Each kind of waste has its own harm.

## Roots
We have been learning to sort waste, and finding out that mixing is quick and unmixing is slow. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the mixed jar, sambar soaked into the newspaper by itself within minutes. Once soaked, the paper tore when lifted and could not be sold. Mixing took no effort, but no rinsing could undo it. So the easiest moment to sort waste is the moment you throw it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of sorting as a job for someone later: the truck, the waste picker, the plant. But mixing starts the moment things touch, and undoing it is slow, dirty work. So sorting belongs to the person holding the waste.  
*Evidence:* In the two-jar test, the dry newspaper stayed whole while the one beside the sambar soaked and tore. In the sorting race, sorting the mixed heap took several times longer than sorting as you threw. Waste pickers at dumping grounds spend whole days pulling dry waste out of mixed heaps.

- *who am i:* You are the person holding the waste at the one moment when sorting is easy. That makes you the first part of the city's cleaning.
- *what is life:* Living things in nature mostly keep their leftovers where others can use them. A fallen leaf feeds worms and fungi right where it falls.
- *what is this world:* A world where things mix by themselves and only work can pull them apart. Every clean street is kept clean by someone's work.

In nature, one living thing's leftovers become another's food, because they stay where others can reach them. A leaf falls and is eaten by worms and fungi, and the soil they make feeds the tree. People's waste breaks this circle when it is mixed. A peel stuck in plastic cannot feed the soil, and paper soaked in curry cannot become paper again. Sorting puts each thing back where it can be used.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 63:** Deal with a hard thing while it is still easy. Handle a great thing while it is still small. (Sorting one peel at the bin takes a second. Sorting a mountain of mixed waste takes years, and may never be done.)
- **Tirukkural, verse 298:** Water makes the outside clean. Truthfulness makes the inside clean. (In the two-jar test, water rinsed the milk packet clean, but it could not clean the soaked paper. Water cleans only the outside of things.)
- **M. K. Gandhi, An Autobiography (The Story of My Experiments with Truth), 1927:** Gandhi insisted that everyone in his home and ashram clean up their own waste, including the toilets. In India this work was often forced onto people of certain castes. (Taking charge of our own waste means not leaving the dirty and dangerous part to someone else.)
- Science (settled): Things that are mixed tend to stay mixed, and separating them takes energy. Physicists measure how mixed-up things are with a quantity called entropy, and left to itself it does not go down.
- Science (settled): Some poisons, like mercury, build up as they pass from small living things to the animals that eat them. Big fish can hold far more mercury than the water around them.
- Other view, Some engineers and economists: Asking every family to sort is unreliable, and families make many mistakes. It is better to collect waste and sort it in big plants with machines and trained, well-paid workers. This is an honest view, and some cities do both.
- Practice, The bin pause (2 min): Pick up one thing you are about to throw away. Stand by the bins and hold it still. Breathe in slowly, and breathe out. Say out loud what it is made of. Now think of the next person who will touch it. Picture their hands. Then put it in the bin where it belongs, gently.
- Ask: If nobody ever saw where their waste went, would they still sort it?
- Ask: Sugar spreads through tea by itself but never gathers back. Is there anything that unmixes by itself?
- Ask: Does a crow sorting through a garbage heap see waste, or food?
- Ask: Lissy chechi carried the danger of the hidden glass, not the person who threw it. Is that fair?

## Story: Lissy Chechi Says No
*The Brahmapuram fire is real. In March 2023 the dumping ground at Brahmapuram, near Kochi, burned for nearly two weeks, and smoke covered parts of the city. The Haritha Karma Sena is real too: groups of women in Kerala's towns and villages who collect sorted dry waste from homes. Nila, her uncle Babu, Lissy chechi and what they say are made up.*

The smoke came on a Thursday. Nila woke up in her flat in Kochi and the sky outside the window was grey, though there were no clouds. The air smelled like burnt rubber. Her mother shut all the windows and stuffed a towel under the balcony door.

'The Brahmapuram dump is on fire,' her mother said, reading her phone. 'They say it may burn for days.'

Nila's uncle Babu was staying with them that week. He had come from the Gulf, where he worked, and he liked to cook. That evening he made fish curry and appam for everyone. Afterwards he cleared up. He scraped the plates into a big plastic bag. In went the fish bones and the leftover curry. In went the newspaper he had used to drain the fried fish, the empty milk packets, a broken glass and the curry powder box. Last of all, he dropped in the old torch battery from the drawer. He tied the bag in a knot and set it by the front door.

'Babu Mama,' said Nila. 'Lissy chechi won't take that.'

'Who is Lissy chechi?' he asked.

'She collects our waste. You'll see.'

Lissy chechi came the next morning in her green uniform, with a face mask over her nose because of the smoke. She worked for the Haritha Karma Sena, a group of women who collected dry waste from every flat in the building. She looked at the knotted bag by the door. She lifted it a little, and something dripped onto the floor.

'Sorry, mone,' she said to Uncle Babu, kindly but firmly. 'I can't take this one. It's mixed.'

Uncle Babu laughed. 'It's all rubbish, chechi. What does it matter how it's packed?'

Lissy chechi put down her sack. 'Can I show you?' she asked. She opened the bag on an old sheet of newspaper on the landing, with her gloves on. Nila crouched beside her.

Lissy chechi picked up the newspaper that had drained the fish. It was orange and soaked through. When she lifted it by one corner, it tore and fell back into the curry. 'Dry, this paper is money,' she said. 'The kabadi shop buys it by the kilo, and the mill makes new paper from it. Soaked in curry, it is nothing. Nobody can wash curry out of paper.'

Next she picked up a milk packet. It was smeared with curry, but when Nila rinsed it under the tap on the landing, it came clean. 'This one can be saved,' said Lissy chechi. 'Plastic doesn't drink the curry. Paper does.'

Then she stopped. Very carefully, she pulled out the pieces of the broken glass, one by one, and laid them on the paper. 'If I had put my hand in without looking,' she said quietly. Uncle Babu stopped smiling.

Last, she held up the torch battery. 'And this should never go in any bag. Batteries can leak or catch fire. Keep them in a jar until we take them to the collection point.'

Uncle Babu looked at the heap on the landing. 'I'm sorry, chechi,' he said. 'It took me one minute to put all that in one bag.'

'That's the trouble,' said Lissy chechi. 'It takes one minute to mix. It takes a person all day to pull a mixed heap apart, and most of it never gets pulled apart at all.' She pointed through the stairwell window, at the grey sky. 'Where do you think the mixed bags go?'

Nila looked at the smoke. 'To Brahmapuram,' she said.

'A mountain of mixed bags,' said Lissy chechi. 'The food rots inside it and makes a gas that burns. The plastic and paper are stuck in the rotting food, so nobody can take them out. And then one day it catches fire.'

That afternoon, Nila set up the kitchen. She put a steel bucket with a lid under the sink for wet waste and wrote WET on it. She put a cardboard box beside it for dry waste and wrote DRY. She found an old biscuit tin for the danger box and drew a red cross on the lid. She wrapped the broken glass in three sheets of newspaper and wrote BROKEN GLASS on it in big letters. Then she taught Uncle Babu. 'Fish bones, wet. Milk packet, rinse it, then dry. Newspaper, dry, but only if it's clean. Battery, the tin.'

Uncle Babu tried it all week. On Wednesday he forgot and put a tea bag in the dry box. Nila fished it out with a spoon. One tea bag had made a brown stain on a whole stack of newspapers.

'One second of carelessness,' said Uncle Babu, looking at the stain. 'And no way to undo it.'

On Friday, Lissy chechi came again. Uncle Babu was waiting at the door with a bag of clean, dry newspapers, a bundle of rinsed milk packets and a cardboard box folded flat. 'Sorted, chechi,' he said. 'And this time, I sorted it myself.'

Lissy chechi weighed the paper on her hanging scale and smiled. 'Now this,' she said, 'is worth something.' The rain came that night and the smoke over Kochi began to clear. In the kitchen, the green bucket was full of peels for the compost pot, and the blue box was full of things that would be made new.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn comes when Lissy chechi says it takes one minute to mix and all day to pull apart. Uncle Babu thinks waste is one thing, 'all rubbish'. Lissy chechi shows him that what was dry paper and money became curry-soaked rubbish only because it was put in the same bag. The tea bag on Wednesday makes the same point in a small way: mixing happens instantly and cannot be undone. The story also shows who really carries the risk of mixed waste: the hidden glass would have cut Lissy chechi, not Uncle Babu.

- Ask: Uncle Babu said, 'It's all rubbish. What does it matter how it's packed?' What did Lissy chechi show him?
- Ask: Why could the milk packet be saved, but not the newspaper?
- Ask: Who would have been hurt by the broken glass in the mixed bag?
- Ask: How was the fire at the dump connected to bags like Uncle Babu's?
- Ask: One tea bag stained a whole stack of newspapers. What does that tell you about where sorting should happen?

### g1 · Three Bins Snap (15 min)
- **rules:** Lay the three mats in the middle, where everyone can reach them.; Shuffle the cards and put them face down in a pile.; One player turns over the top card and reads it aloud.; Everyone races to slap the right mat. ## Experiments
### e1 · The Two-Jar Test: what does wet waste do to dry waste? (35 min, 2 days)
- **question:** If dry waste is thrown in with wet waste for just 15 minutes, can the dry waste still be sold for recycling? Which kinds survive, and which are spoiled?
- **predict:** Look at the newspaper, the biscuit-box piece and the milk packet. Which of them do you think will still be good enough for the kabadiwala after sitting in sambar? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both jars get exactly the same dry things, cut to the same size, and wait for the same time. Only one thing is different: one jar also gets wet waste. So any difference must come from the wet waste.
- **safety:** Use a spoon or tongs, or wear gloves, to take things out of the mixed jar. Wash hands with soap afterwards. If you keep the mixed jar overnight, a grown-up opens it near a window and nobody sniffs it closely.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Stick the dry newspaper strip and a scrap of the soaked one side by side in your notebook once both are dry, and write one sentence about each.
- **results table:** Thing | My guess: still sellable? | Dry jar: clean and whole? | Mixed jar: clean and whole? | Mixed jar, after rinsing: clean? | Could the kabadiwala take it?
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess, set up both jars, shake them gently and wait 15 minutes. Then do the lift test, the pull test and the rinse test, and fill in the table. (30 min); day 2: If you kept the closed jars overnight, a grown-up opens each one near a window. Compare the smell, then empty the mixed jar into the wet bin. (5 min)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the dry things in two matching sets on a plate. 2. Ask your child to guess which ones will still be sellable after sitting in sambar.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to put these in two jars. One jar gets sambar and peels too. Which of these will still be good enough to sell? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the newspaper, the box piece and the milk packet. Which will still be good after sitting in sambar? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that one will survive? (good: Plastic doesn't soak things up.; Paper gets soggy.; You can wash plastic.; Cardboard is thicker than newspaper.)
  - Note: Write the guesses in the table before you start. Don't explain anything yet. Many children guess that everything can just be washed afterwards.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label one jar DRY ONLY and the other MIXED. 2. Put one newspaper strip, one box piece and one milk packet strip in each jar. 3. Add the sambar, rice and peel to the MIXED jar only.  
  *Parent says:* Both jars get the same dry things. Only the MIXED jar gets wet waste. Why do we keep everything else the same?  
  *Child mode:* Put the same three things in each jar. Now add sambar, rice and a peel to the MIXED jar only.
  - Ask: Why does the DRY ONLY jar matter? (good: So we can compare.; It shows what the things are like with no wet waste.; Then we know the sambar made the difference.)
  - Note: The DRY ONLY jar is the control. It shows what happens with nothing changed, so any difference in the MIXED jar comes from the wet waste.
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Close both lids. 2. Tip each jar gently from side to side five times, like a bag being carried. 3. Set a timer for 15 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* A garbage bag gets swung and bumped on its way down the stairs. Tipping the jars does the same. Now we wait 15 minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Close both jars. Tip each one gently five times. Now wait 15 minutes.
  - Note: Use the wait to play a round of the Three Bins Snap game, or to start setting up the sorting station. You can peek through the glass: the newspaper usually darkens within a few minutes.
  - Wait: Wait 15 minutes.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. With tongs or a spoon, lift each newspaper strip out by one end. 2. Hold each by both ends and pull gently.  
  *Parent says:* Lift the dry strip first, then the strip from the MIXED jar. Now pull each one very gently from both ends. What happens?  
  *Child mode:* Lift out both newspaper strips. Pull each one gently from both ends. What happens?
  - Ask: What did you feel when you pulled the soaked strip? (good: It tore straight away.; It was floppy and broke.; The dry one held, the wet one ripped.)
  - Note: The soaked strip usually tears with a very gentle pull, or even when lifted. The dry strip holds. If the soaked strip holds, pull a little harder on both: the soaked one gives way first.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take out the box piece and the milk packet strip from the MIXED jar. 2. Rinse both under the tap for ten seconds. 3. Compare them with the ones from the DRY ONLY jar.  
  *Parent says:* Let's try to wash them. Rinse each one for ten seconds. Which one comes clean? Which one stays stained?  
  *Child mode:* Rinse the box piece and the milk packet for ten seconds. Which comes clean?
  - Note: The milk packet almost always rinses clean, because plastic does not absorb liquid. The box piece stays stained and goes soft, because cardboard is made of paper fibres that soak up the sambar.
  - Check: In the Two-Jar Test, the milk packet from the MIXED jar came clean after rinsing, but the newspaper did not. Why? → **Paper soaks up liquid between its fibres, but plastic does not**
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill in the table together. For each thing, decide: could the kabadiwala take it?  
  *Parent says:* Now let's fill in the table. Which things could we still sell? Which are spoiled for good?  
  *Child mode:* Fill in the table. Which things could you still sell? Which are spoiled?
- **s7 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for a claim, the evidence and the reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what you found. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why does it happen?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you found. Say what you saw. Then say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Could we ever get the sambar back out of the newspaper? (good: No, it is inside the paper.; Washing makes the paper fall apart.; No, so we should keep them apart from the start.)
  - Note: A strong answer: 'Wet waste spoils paper and cardboard but not plastic. The newspaper tore and the box piece stayed stained, but the milk packet rinsed clean. Paper soaks up liquid. Once it is in, you can't get it out, so we must keep wet and dry apart from the start.'
  - Think: It took one second to mix them. How long would it take to unmix them?
- **s8 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep both jars closed overnight, if you like. 2. Next day, a grown-up opens each jar near a window. 3. Compare the smell from a little distance. 4. Empty the MIXED jar into the wet bin and wash both jars.  
  *Parent says:* Let's open the jars near the window. Don't put your nose close. Just notice the smell from here. Which jar smells?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to open the jars by the window. Don't sniff closely. Which jar smells?
  - Note: In a warm room the MIXED jar smells sour after a day, and the newspaper may show spots of mould. The DRY ONLY jar smells of nothing. This is why a bag of mixed waste smells and draws flies, while a box of dry waste does not.
  - Wait: Do this the next day.
### e2 · The Sorting Race: sort as you throw, or sort later? (30 min)
- **question:** How much longer does it take to sort waste after it has been mixed, compared with sorting each thing as you throw it?
- **predict:** Guess how many seconds each round will take. Will the mixed round take a little longer, twice as long, or much longer? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same 30 things, the same three bowls and the same person sorting in both rounds. Only one thing changes: whether the things are mixed first. Do each round twice and take the faster time, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Wear gloves or use a spoon for the mixed round. Wash hands with soap afterwards.
- **record:** Write your times in the table. Then draw two bars, one for each round, as tall as the faster time.
- **results table:** Round | My guess (seconds) | Try 1 (seconds) | Try 2 (seconds) | Faster try (seconds) | Mistakes found afterwards
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show the 30 things and the three labelled bowls. 2. Ask for a guess for each round, and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* First you will sort these one at a time, as I hand them to you. Then we mix them all and you sort them again. How long will each round take? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how long each round will take. Will the mixed round be slower? Why?
  - Ask: Why might the mixed round be slower? (good: You have to search for each thing.; Tea leaves stick to the paper.; Things get stuck together.)
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Start the timer. 2. Hand your child the 30 things one by one, in any order. 3. Stop the timer when the last one is in a bowl. 4. Do it once more and write both times.  
  *Parent says:* Ready? I'll hand you one thing at a time. Drop it in the right bowl as fast as you can.  
  *Child mode:* Take each thing and drop it in the right bowl. Go fast!
  - Note: Round 1 usually takes under a minute. This is what sorting at the bin feels like.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tip everything into the big bowl. 2. Add one spoon of water. 3. Stir well with a spoon for ten seconds.  
  *Parent says:* Now we mix it all, the way a single dustbin does. A spoon of water is like the juice from leftovers.  
  *Child mode:* Tip it all in the big bowl. Add a spoon of water. Stir.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child puts on gloves. 2. Start the timer. 3. They sort the mixed bowl into the three bowls, picking tea leaves off the paper too. 4. Stop when everything is sorted. 5. Do it once more if they want to.  
  *Parent says:* Now sort the mixed bowl into the three bowls. Tea leaves stuck to paper count as wet. Pick them off first.  
  *Child mode:* Put on gloves. Sort the mixed bowl into the three bowls. Pick off stuck tea leaves.
  - Note: The mixed round usually takes several times longer. Used tea leaves stick to damp paper and are fiddly to pick off. Some scraps of paper will be too soggy to save. That is a real result too.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Check each bowl for mistakes and count them. 2. Fill in the table. 3. Draw the two bars.  
  *Parent says:* Let's check the bowls. Did anything end up in the wrong place? How many times longer did the mixed round take?  
  *Child mode:* Check each bowl. Count the mistakes. Which round was slower? By how much?
  - Check: In the Sorting Race, Round 2 took much longer than Round 1. What made it slower? → **The things were mixed and stuck together, so each had to be found and pulled apart**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what this means for the dustbin at home.  
  *Parent says:* If a whole city's waste comes mixed, how long would sorting take? Who would do it?  
  *Child mode:* Think of a whole city's waste, all mixed. Who would sort it? How long would it take?
  - Ask: What is the quickest way to get waste sorted? (good: Sort it when you throw it.; Have two bins at home.; Never mix it in the first place.)
### e3 · Lid or No Lid: who finds the open bin? (20 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Does a lid really keep ants, flies and smells away from wet waste?
- **predict:** Which box do you think ants or flies will find first? Will they reach the closed box at all? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Both boxes get the same waste and stand side by side in the same spot for the same time. The only difference is the lid.
- **safety:** Wash hands after touching the peels. Throw both peels into the wet bin when you finish.
- **record:** Write your counts in the table at each check. Draw both boxes on day 2.
- **results table:** Time | Open box: ants | Open box: flies | Closed box: ants | Closed box: flies | Smell near each box
- **schedule:** day 1: Set up both boxes side by side. Check and count after 1 hour and after 4 hours. (15 min); day 2: Check and count the next morning. Open the closed box and look inside. Empty both into the wet bin. (5 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show both boxes and ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Both boxes get a sweet peel. One has a lid. Who will find the peels? Will anyone get into the closed box? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Which box will ants or flies find? Can they get in the closed one? Why?
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one peel in each box. 2. Close one box tightly. 3. Put both side by side where you have seen ants.  
  *Parent says:* We put them right next to each other. Then the place is the same for both, and only the lid is different.  
  *Child mode:* Put a peel in each box. Close one. Put them side by side.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Check after 1 hour and after 4 hours. Count the ants and flies on or around each box.  
  *Parent says:* Let's look. How many ants? Any flies? Where are they going?  
  *Child mode:* Count the ants and flies at each box. Write the numbers.
  - Note: In a warm Indian kitchen, ants usually find the open peel within a few hours. Ants follow smell trails, and they lay new trails for other ants. If no ants come, look for tiny fruit flies by the next morning. If nothing at all comes, leave the boxes one more day.
  - Wait: Check after 1 hour and after 4 hours.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Next morning, count again. 2. Open the closed box and look inside. 3. Empty both peels into the wet bin and wash the boxes.  
  *Parent says:* What did the lid do? Now think of the dustbin under our sink.  
  *Child mode:* Count again. Open the closed box. Did anyone get in?
  - Check: In the Lid or No Lid test, ants came to the open box but not to the closed one. What does that tell us about a kitchen bin? → **A lid keeps the smell in, so ants and flies don't find the waste or get to it**
  - Wait: Do this the next morning.

## Applications
- Cities like Indore and Mysuru collect waste already sorted, and turn wet waste into compost and dry waste into recycled material.
- Dry waste collection centres, many run by waste pickers, sort paper, plastic, metal and glass by kind and sell them to recyclers.
- Biogas plants use sorted wet waste to make cooking gas.
- Apartment buildings compost their own wet waste and use it on their gardens and potted plants.
- Hospitals sort their waste into coloured bins, so sharp and infected things are handled safely.
- Festivals and weddings that plan their bins send far less to dumping grounds.
- Mosquito control: clearing thrown-away containers that hold water is one of the main ways cities fight dengue.
- Battery and bulb collection points keep mercury and other harmful metals out of soil and water.

## Open questions
- **What is the best way to get every family in a city to sort their waste?** Cities have tried fines, rewards, school campaigns, and refusing to collect mixed bags. What works in one city often fails in another, and people still argue about why.
- **Should cities sort waste at home, in big machine plants, or both?** Machines are getting better at sorting dry waste, but they are costly and can take work away from waste pickers. No one agrees on the best mix.
- **How harmful are the tiny amounts of mercury and other metals from old batteries and bulbs that reach dumps?** Scientists can measure them in soil and water near some dumps, but it is hard to say how much harm they do over many years.
- **Can we make packets that are easy to sort and recycle, like chips packets?** Many packets are made of several layers stuck together, like plastic and metal foil. Pulling the layers apart is hard, and researchers are still working on it.

- In Sorting Week, Kavya made these tallies of mistakes: Monday 8, Tuesday 6, Wednesday 6, Thursday 4, Friday 3, Saturday 2, Sunday 1. Did the mistakes go down? Each day had the same or fewer mistakes than the day before, so they went down. Half of 8 is 4. 4 is not below 4, so Thursday doesn't count. Friday's 3 is below 4. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- A collector buys clean, dry newspaper for 12 rupees a kilogram. A family has 5 kilograms of clean newspaper and 2 kilograms that got soaked in curry. How much money does the family get? **60 rupees.**. Only the clean newspaper can be sold. 5 kilograms times 12 rupees is 60 rupees. The 2 soaked kilograms are worth nothing, so they would have earned 24 more rupees if they had been kept dry. (queues `math.money.money-in-life.L3`)
- A building has 48 flats. A waste collector can collect from 8 flats in 10 minutes if the waste is sorted. How long does she take for the whole building? **60 minutes, which is 1 hour.**. 48 flats in groups of 8 makes 6 groups, because 6 times 8 is 48. Each group takes 10 minutes. 6 times 10 is 60 minutes. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the Lid or No Lid test, Divya counted 14 ants at the open box after 4 hours and 0 at the closed box. The next morning there were 23 at the open box. How many more ants came overnight? **9 more ants.**. 23 take away 14 is 9. You can check: 14 + 9 = 23. More ants came because ants that find food lay a smell trail that leads other ants to it. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
