# Simple circuits: why a bulb lights only in a complete loop
`sci.electricity.simple-circuits.L3`

**Summary.** A torch bulb lights only when electricity can flow from a cell, through the bulb and all the way back to the cell. This complete loop is called a circuit. A gap anywhere in it, and the bulb goes out at once. Metals let electricity through, so they are called conductors. Plastic, rubber, wood, paper and glass do not, so they are called insulators. A wall socket in India is 230 volts and can kill, so we experiment only with small 1.5 volt cells. Inside the wire, tiny particles called electrons are pushed round the loop by the cell, and they come back to it.

**Intro.** *Try this with a grown-up. Stand a torch bulb on top of an AA cell. It does not light. Now add one wire, in the right place, and it shines. Why does the bulb need that one extra wire?* Electricity must go out of the cell, through the bulb, and come back to the cell. It needs a full loop. In this unit you build loops with cells, wires, foil and paper clips. You find out which things let electricity through and which stop it. You make your own switch. You learn why the sockets on the wall are never for experiments. And you find out what is really moving inside a wire.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name things at home that use electricity, and say which ones use mains electricity from the wall and which use cells.
1. I can say the safety rules for electricity and the reason for each one: never touch sockets, plugs or bare wires, never with wet hands, and only grown-ups plug things in.
2. I can build a complete circuit with a cell, wires and a bulb, and trace with my finger the path the electricity takes.
3. I can explain why a bulb goes out when the circuit is broken anywhere, and find the break in a circuit that does not work.
4. I can test materials with a circuit tester and sort them into conductors and insulators, and say that most conductors are metals.
5. I can make a switch from a paper clip and explain how it opens and closes a circuit.
6. I can tell how Galvani and Volta argued about frog legs, and how Volta's experiments led to the first battery.

## Terms
- **Electricity**: A kind of energy that flows along wires and makes things work, such as lights, fans and phones. *e.g.* When you switch on a ceiling fan, electricity flows through it and makes it turn.
- **Cell**: A small store of energy that pushes electricity round a circuit. People often call it a battery. *e.g.* An AA cell in a TV remote. It is 1.5 volts.
- **Battery**: Two or more cells joined together. In everyday talk, people also call a single cell a battery. *e.g.* A TV remote with two AA cells side by side is using a battery of two cells.
- **Terminal**: One of the two ends of a cell where wires are joined. One is marked plus (+) and the other minus (−). *e.g.* The small bump on top of an AA cell is its plus terminal. The flat bottom is its minus terminal.
- **Circuit**: A complete loop that electricity can flow all the way round, from the cell and back to the cell. *e.g.* In a torch, electricity flows from the cells, through a metal strip, through the bulb and back to the cells.
- **Complete circuit**: A circuit with no gaps. A bulb lights only in a complete circuit. *e.g.* When the wire touches both the cell and the bulb, the loop is complete and the bulb lights.
- **Broken circuit**: A circuit with a gap somewhere. Electricity cannot cross the gap, so nothing works. It is also called an open circuit. *e.g.* If a wire slips off the cell, the bulb goes dark.
- **Bulb**: A small glass lamp with a very thin wire inside, called a filament. The filament gets so hot that it glows. *e.g.* An old torch bulb has a tiny coiled filament you can see through the glass.
- **Switch**: A part that opens and closes a gap in a circuit, so you can turn things off and on. *e.g.* The light switch on your wall opens and closes a gap in the wire to the light.
- **Conductor**: A material that lets electricity flow through it easily. Most conductors are metals. *e.g.* Copper wire, a steel spoon and aluminium foil are all conductors.
- **Insulator**: A material that does not let electricity flow through it. Plastic, rubber, wood, glass, paper and cloth are insulators. *e.g.* The plastic around a fan wire is an insulator. It keeps the electricity inside the copper.
- **Mains electricity**: The electricity that comes to wall sockets from power stations. In India it is 230 volts. It can kill. *e.g.* The fridge, the mixer and the iron are plugged into the mains.
- **Volt**: The unit for how hard a cell or supply pushes electricity. The short form is V. *e.g.* An AA cell is 1.5 volts. A wall socket in India is 230 volts, about 150 times as much.
- **Electron**: A tiny particle found in every atom. It has an electric charge. In metals, some electrons can move about freely. *e.g.* When a bulb lights, electrons are moving through the wire and the filament.
- **Short circuit**: A path where electricity goes straight from one end of a cell to the other, with nothing like a bulb on the way. The wire gets hot and the cell runs down fast. *e.g.* Never join the two ends of a cell with just a wire or a strip of foil.
- **Electric shock**: The harm done when electricity flows through a person's body. Mains electricity can burn and can stop the heart. *e.g.* Touching a bare wire that is plugged in can give an electric shock.

## Explanations
### Where we use electricity · *defined*
**Definition:** Electricity is a kind of energy. Electrical appliances use it to make light, sound, heat, cold and movement.

Look around any home in India and you find electricity at work. Tube lights and LED bulbs make light. A TV and a doorbell make sound. An iron, a geyser and an induction stove make heat. A fridge makes things cold. A ceiling fan and a mixer-grinder make movement. Some appliances do two jobs. A TV makes light and sound. A fan makes movement, and the moving air cools you. When the power goes off, all of these stop at once. Only the things with their own cells or batteries keep working, such as a torch, a phone or a wall clock.
- e.g. A mixer-grinder turns electricity into movement, and its loud whirr is sound.
- e.g. A phone runs on its own battery, so it keeps working in a power cut until the battery runs down.
- e.g. An inverter keeps a few lights and fans on in a power cut, using big batteries charged earlier.
### Mains electricity and cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Mains electricity is made in power stations and comes to our homes along wires. In India it is 230 volts. A cell is a small, portable store of energy, usually 1.5 volts.

Power stations make electricity from burning coal, falling water, wind, sunlight or the heat of splitting atoms. Thick wires on tall towers carry it across the country to every town. Smaller wires bring it into your home, to the switches and sockets in the wall. This is mains electricity. It pushes very hard, at 230 volts. That is strong enough to push electricity through a person's body, and it can kill. A cell works differently. Chemicals inside it react and push electricity round a circuit. An AA cell pushes at only 1.5 volts. It is too weak to push enough electricity through your dry skin for you to notice anything. That is why all the experiments in this unit use AA cells, and never the wall socket.
- e.g. The Shivanasamudra power station on the Kaveri river in Karnataka began making electricity from falling water in 1902.
- e.g. A wall clock uses one AA cell for many months.
- e.g. A big solar panel on a roof makes electricity from sunlight.
### Staying safe with electricity · *defined*
**Definition:** Mains electricity can push electricity through your body and give you an electric shock, which can kill. Water and damaged wires make shocks more likely.

Your body is mostly water with salts dissolved in it, and salty water lets electricity through. Dry skin slows electricity down a lot. Wet skin slows it much less. That is why you must never touch a switch, a plug or an appliance with wet hands. The plastic on a wire is an insulator that keeps the electricity inside the copper. If the plastic is cracked or burnt, the bare copper can give a shock, so stay away and tell a grown-up. Never push anything into a socket. Never pull a plug out by its wire. Never fly a kite near overhead power lines. Only grown-ups plug in, unplug and move mains appliances. Cells need care too. Never open, bite or burn a cell. Keep small round button cells away from babies, because a swallowed button cell can badly burn the food pipe in as little as two hours.
- e.g. A grown-up switches off the socket before pulling out the plug.
- e.g. In many homes, the geyser switch is outside the bathroom, away from water.
- e.g. If a kite gets stuck on a power line, leave it there and tell a grown-up.
### A complete circuit · *defined*
**Definition:** A circuit is a complete loop that electricity can flow round, from one terminal of the cell, through the parts of the circuit, and back to the other terminal.

A cell has two terminals, plus and minus. Electricity flows only when there is a path from one terminal to the other. A bulb has two contacts too. One is the metal tip at its bottom. The other is the metal side around its screw. The thin filament inside joins them. So the loop goes like this. From the plus terminal, along a wire, into the bulb's tip, through the filament, out of the bulb's side, along a second wire, and back to the minus terminal. If you stand a bulb on top of a cell, only the tip touches. The bulb's side has no path back to the bottom of the cell, so it stays dark. Add one wire from the bulb's side to the bottom of the cell, and the loop is complete.
- e.g. In a torch, a spring and metal strips join the cells, the switch and the bulb into one loop.
- e.g. A bulb holder has two screws. One joins to the bulb's tip and one to its side.
- e.g. A bulb lights whichever way round the cell is joined. An LED lights only one way round.
### Why a broken circuit stops everything · *defined*
**Definition:** If there is a gap anywhere in a circuit, the electricity stops flowing everywhere in the circuit at once.

It does not matter where the gap is. Lift the wire off the cell, or off the bulb, or cut it in the middle, and the bulb goes dark at once. This is because electricity is not like water poured into a bucket. It does not fill the bulb and stay there. It flows round and round the loop, back to the cell, for as long as the loop is whole. When the loop is broken, the flow stops everywhere together, like a cycle chain that has come off. A loose connection is the most common reason a circuit will not work. Other reasons are a flat cell, a broken filament, or a part made of an insulator.
- e.g. A torch that flickers usually has a loose spring or a dirty contact.
- e.g. When one bulb in an old string of festival lights breaks, the whole string can go dark.
- e.g. A rusty or greasy spring in a torch can stop the loop, because rust is a poor conductor.
### Conductors and insulators · *defined*
**Definition:** A conductor is a material that lets electricity flow through it easily. An insulator is a material that does not.

Metals are good conductors. Copper, aluminium, iron, steel and brass all conduct. That is why wires are made of copper. Plastic, rubber, wood, glass, paper, cloth and dry air are insulators. That is why wires are wrapped in plastic. The metal inside carries the electricity, and the plastic outside keeps it in, away from our hands. Some things are in between. The graphite in a pencil is not a metal, but it conducts a little, so a bulb may glow dimly through it. Tap water conducts a little, because of the salts dissolved in it. Salty water conducts better. Watch out for coatings. A paper clip covered in coloured plastic will not conduct until you scrape the plastic off one spot.
- e.g. A plug has metal pins, which carry electricity, inside a plastic cover, which you hold.
- e.g. A screwdriver for electricians has a steel tip and a thick plastic handle.
- e.g. Electricians' gloves are made of rubber.
### How a switch works · *defined*
**Definition:** A switch is a part of a circuit that can open a gap, to stop the electricity, and close it, to let the electricity flow.

When a switch is on, two pieces of metal inside it touch, and the circuit is complete. When it is off, the metal pieces move apart, and there is a gap of air. Air is an insulator, so the electricity stops. You can make your own switch with a steel paper clip and two drawing pins on a piece of cardboard. Push the pins in, about 2 centimetres apart, and join a wire to each. Hook the paper clip round one pin. Swing it onto the other pin, and the circuit is complete. Swing it away, and the circuit is broken. The cardboard must be an insulator, or the electricity would go through it. Switches come as press switches, like a doorbell, rocker switches, like a wall switch, and slide switches, like many torches.
- e.g. A wall switch is a rocker switch.
- e.g. A doorbell button is a press switch. It is on only while you press it.
- e.g. Many torches have a slide switch.
### What is moving in the wire · *defined*
**Definition:** An electric current is a flow of electric charge. In a metal wire, the charge is carried by electrons, tiny particles that are in every atom.

Every atom has electrons. In a metal, some of the electrons are not held tightly by any one atom. They drift about inside the metal. So a copper wire is already full of electrons, all the way round the loop, before you join it to a cell. The cell pushes on all of them at once, and they all start moving round together. That is why the bulb lights the moment you close the loop, even if the wire is two metres long. Each electron moves very slowly, less than a millimetre each second. The cell does not fill the wire with electrons, and the bulb does not use them up. The same electrons go round and come back. What the cell uses up is the energy stored in its chemicals. When that runs out, the cell is flat. In plastic, the electrons are held tightly by their atoms, so they cannot flow. That is why plastic is an insulator.
- e.g. A cycle chain moves all at once when you push the pedal. Every link moves, though no link goes fast.
- e.g. A tube full of marbles: push one in at one end, and one pops out at the other end at once.
- e.g. The arrows on circuit drawings go from plus to minus. People drew them that way before electrons were found in 1897. The electrons actually move from minus to plus.
### Electricity in living things · *defined*
**Definition:** Nerves, muscles and many plants send signals using tiny charged particles that move in and out of their cells.

In 1791, an Italian doctor called Luigi Galvani said that living bodies use electricity. He was right. Your brain sends signals along your nerves to your muscles. Each signal is carried by charged particles moving in and out of the nerve cells. This is how you move your fingers. Your heart beats because of electrical signals, which a doctor can record with an ECG machine. Plants use electrical signals too. A touch-me-not plant feels a touch, and an electrical signal travels along their leaf stalk, so the leaves fold. Jagadish Chandra Bose measured electrical signals in plants in Kolkata over a hundred years ago. Some fish, like the electric eel of South America, store up so much charge that they can give a shock of more than 600 volts.
- e.g. A doctor puts sticky pads on your chest to record your heart's electrical signals.
- e.g. The leaves of a touch-me-not fold within a second or two of a touch.
- e.g. An electric eel uses shocks to stun the fish they hunt.

## Mostly agreed
- A bulb or other part works only in a complete circuit, a loop from one terminal of the cell back to the other. *How we know:* Anyone can test it with a cell, a wire and a bulb. Lift any connection and the bulb goes out.
- Metals are good conductors. Plastic, rubber, wood, glass and paper are insulators. *How we know:* Testing materials with a circuit tester, as in this unit, gives the same result every time. Scientists have measured how well thousands of materials conduct.
- In a metal wire, the electric current is carried by electrons. *How we know:* J. J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897. Later experiments showed that electrons carry the current in metals.
- Mains electricity can kill, and wet skin lets electricity through much more easily than dry skin. *How we know:* Doctors and electrical engineers have studied electric shocks for over a hundred years. Safety rules all over the world are based on this.
- A cell does not make electric charge. It uses the energy of a chemical reaction to push charge round a circuit. *How we know:* Volta's pile, built in 1799, and every cell since then work this way. When the chemicals are used up, the cell stops pushing.

## How we found out
**Luigi Galvani, a doctor in Bologna, and Alessandro Volta, a physicist in Pavia, 1791 (Galvani's book on animal electricity) and 1799 to 1800 (Volta's pile).** In the 1780s, Luigi Galvani, a doctor in Bologna in Italy, was studying how frog legs move. His wife, Lucia Galeazzi, also worked in his laboratory. Galvani found that a dead frog's leg twitched when he touched the leg with two different metals, such as a brass hook and an iron rail. In 1791 he wrote that the frog's body held 'animal electricity', and the metals only let that electricity flow. Alessandro Volta, a physicist in Pavia, repeated the experiments. He thought the electricity came from the two metals, and the frog only showed that electricity was there. To prove it, he left the frog out. He piled up discs of zinc and copper, with card soaked in salty water between them. The pile gave a steady flow of electricity, and he could feel it as a tingle. In 1800 he described it in a letter to the Royal Society in London. It was the first battery. So Galvani's idea that the metals' electricity came from the frog was wrong. But he was right that living bodies use electricity. Our nerves really do send electrical signals.  
*What people believed before:* Galvani thought the electricity that made the frog legs twitch was stored in the frog's body. Volta showed that two different metals with something salty and wet between them make electricity on their own, with no frog. Galvani was still right that nerves use electricity.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A cell is full of electricity, and the bulb uses it up.** (A very common idea among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: A cell does run down, and a flat cell will not light a bulb, so it seems something has been used up.  
  Against: The electrons that flow come back to the cell, and the same number go round all the time. What runs out is the energy stored in the cell's chemicals.
- **Electricity only needs to get from the cell to the bulb. One wire should be enough.** (Many children, when they first try to light a bulb; *rejected*)  
  For: A water pipe needs only to go from the tank to the tap. It seems electricity should be the same.  
  Against: Stand a bulb on a cell with one wire to its tip only, and it stays dark. It lights only when there is a path back to the other terminal of the cell.
- **All metal things conduct, so a coloured paper clip will light the bulb.** (A natural guess in the conductor test; *rejected*)  
  For: Paper clips are made of steel, and steel is a conductor.  
  Against: A paper clip with a plastic coat does not light the bulb, because the plastic is an insulator. Scrape a little plastic off each end, and it conducts.
- **A small cell cannot hurt anyone, so any cell is safe for children.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: You do not notice any shock from an AA cell, even when you touch both ends.  
  Against: An AA cell is safe to touch, but a swallowed button cell can badly burn a child's food pipe in as little as two hours. A shorted cell can make a wire hot. Cells that are opened or burnt can leak harmful chemicals or burst.
- **The electricity in a frog's legs came from the frog's own body.** (Luigi Galvani, in Bologna, in 1791; *accepted*)  
  For: The legs twitched when touched with metal, and Galvani later made them twitch with no metal at all, using the frog's own nerve.  
  Against: Alessandro Volta showed that two different metals with something wet between them make electricity, with no frog at all. Both were partly right. Volta's metals made the first battery, and Galvani's animal electricity is real too: nerves really do use electrical signals.

## Roots
We have built circuits, broken them, and found what lets electricity through. Now we ask what a circuit tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** Your bulb lit only when a second path led back to the cell. So the electricity does not stop in the bulb. It goes round and comes back to the cell. The bulb is not using up electrons, only the energy the cell gives them as they pass.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a cell as a tank full of electricity that the bulb drinks up. But the electrons that flow are part of the wire itself, and they come back. What a cell runs out of is the energy in its chemicals, not electrons.  
*Evidence:* In the bulb challenge, the bulb stayed dark with one wire from the cell to its tip. It lit only when a path also led back to the other end of the cell. In Where Is the Gap?, a gap after the bulb put it out too.

- *who am i:* Every move you make starts with electrical signals running along your nerves to your muscles.
- *what is life:* A touch-me-not feels a touch and sends an electrical signal along their leaf stalk, and their leaves fold.
- *what is this world:* Lightning, a torch and the fan above you all work because charges move.

In a wire with no cell, the electrons drift about in every direction. Their movements add up to nothing. Join a cell, and one steady push sets them all moving round the loop together. That one flow can light a bulb, spin a fan, ring a bell or warm an iron. Many different jobs come from one simple movement of charges that were there all along.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Thirty spokes share one hub. It is the empty space at the centre that makes the wheel useful. We shape clay into a pot, and it is the empty inside that holds what we want. (A switch is useful because of its gap. The air gap is what lets us turn a light off. A circuit works through its metal, but we control it through its empty space.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.13:** Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the boy cannot see the salt. But he tastes the water from the top, the middle and the bottom, and it is salty everywhere. (The electrons were in every part of the wire before you joined the cell, though you could not see them. The bulb lighting at once is how you could tell, the way the taste told Shvetaketu the salt was there.)
- Science (settled): Electric charge is never made or destroyed. A cell moves charges round a circuit, using energy from its chemicals.
- Science (settled): Nerves, the heart and many plants send signals by moving charged particles in and out of their cells.
- Science (open): How the brain's electrical signals become thoughts and feelings is not known.
- Other view, Many physicists and engineers: A circuit is electrons pushed round by a chemical reaction, and nothing more. Comparing it with a wheel's empty hub or with hidden salt is a nice picture. But it tells us nothing new about the wire.
- Practice, The family circuit (3 min): Let's stand in a circle and hold hands. Close your eyes. I will squeeze the hand on my right. When you feel a squeeze, pass it on to the next person. Let's see it come back to me. Now one person lets go. I squeeze again. Does it come back? Hold hands again and breathe slowly three times.
- Ask: How did a gap in your circuit show you that electricity goes round the loop, and not just along it?
- Ask: A switch works because of a gap. Can an empty space do a job?
- Ask: Your thoughts travel as electrical signals. Is a thought something that moves, or something more?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* The electrons were already in the wire before you joined it to the cell. So what did the cell actually give the bulb? (hint: Think about what runs out when a cell goes flat, and what goes round and comes back.)
- *deep:* Why does a copper wire let electricity through, while the plastic around it does not? (hint: Think about whether the electrons in each material are free to move, or held tight by their atoms.)
- *big:* Nerve cells do not quite touch. A signal must cross a tiny gap between one nerve cell and the next. Is that gap a kind of switch? (hint: A switch decides when electricity can cross. Think about when a signal in your body crosses the gap, and when it stops.)
- *deep:* Electricity from a power station travels hundreds of kilometres along wires to your fan. Does it come back to the power station? (hint: Every plug has at least two pins. Why do you think it needs more than one?)
- *big:* Imagine a world with no electricity at all, not even in living things. Could anything in it be alive? (hint: Your heart beats because of electrical signals. A touch-me-not folds their leaves because of one too.)

## Story: Ammachi's Torch
*The story is made up. Everything about the torch is true: two cells in a row only push together if they face the same way, and a crust on a spring can stop the electricity.*

The rain came down on Kochi so hard that it drummed on the roof like a hundred fingers. Kavya was eight, and she was doing her sums at the dining table. Her little brother Arjun was building a tower of steel tumblers. Then, with a soft click, every light in the flat went out. The fan slowed down and stopped. The fridge went quiet.

"Power cut," said Ammachi, their grandmother, from the dark. "Kavya, there is a torch in the top drawer of my almirah. Bring it, mole."

Kavya felt her way along the wall to Ammachi's room. She found the drawer and the torch inside it. It was a heavy old torch made of silver metal, the kind that takes two fat cells. She pushed the switch forward. Nothing happened. She pushed it back and forward again. Still nothing.

"It doesn't work, Ammachi," she called.

"Then the cells must be finished," said Ammachi. "There are new ones in the kitchen, near the matchbox."

Kavya's mother lit a candle, and in its yellow light Kavya found two new cells. She unscrewed the bottom of the torch. The old cells slid out into her hand. She dropped in the two new ones and screwed the bottom back on. She pushed the switch.

Nothing.

Arjun giggled. "Your torch is broken."

Kavya did not giggle. She was thinking. That week, at her homeschool table, she had made a bulb light with one AA cell and a piece of wire. She knew the bulb only lit when the electricity had a whole path, from one end of the cell, through the bulb, and back to the other end. If there was a gap anywhere, the bulb stayed dark. So somewhere in this torch there was a gap.

"Amma, can I hold the candle close?" she asked. Her mother brought it over and sat beside her.

Kavya unscrewed the bottom again and tipped the cells out onto the table. She looked at them carefully. Each cell had a little bump at one end and a flat end at the other. The bump was the plus end. The flat end was the minus end.

"How did you put them in?" asked her mother.

"Bump first," said Kavya. Then she stopped. "No. The first one went in bump first. The second one I put in the other way, because it rolled."

"Does that matter?" asked Arjun.

Kavya thought about it. In a torch, the two cells sit in a line, one behind the other. Each cell pushes the electricity one way, from its minus end round to its plus end. "If they face the same way, they push together," she said slowly. "If one is turned round, it pushes back against the other one. Then they cancel out, and there is no push at all."

She put both cells in bump first, both facing the bulb. She screwed on the bottom and pushed the switch.

Still nothing.

Arjun opened his mouth, but his mother gave him a look, and he closed it again.

"There must be another gap," said Kavya. She tipped the cells out once more and peered into the bottom cap with the candle. At the bottom was the spring that presses the cells up towards the bulb. The spring was covered in a crust, white and greenish, like old chalk.

"What is that?" asked her mother.

"I think the old cells leaked," said Kavya. "That crust is not shiny metal. Maybe it doesn't let electricity through."

"Don't touch it with your fingers," said her mother. She brought an old toothbrush and a dry cloth. Carefully, Kavya scrubbed the spring until the silver metal showed again. Then her mother wiped the spring and the inside of the cap with the cloth, and they both washed their hands.

Kavya put the cells back in, both facing the bulb. She screwed the cap on tight. She held her breath and pushed the switch.

A circle of white light jumped onto the ceiling.

Arjun shouted, and his tower of tumblers fell over with a great clatter. Ammachi laughed from her room. "Who fixed it?"

"Kavya did," said her mother. "She found two gaps."

Kavya shone the torch round the room, onto the fallen tumblers, onto Arjun's surprised face, onto Ammachi's smile in the doorway. She was thinking about the electricity going round. Out of the cells, along the metal case of the torch, through the switch, into the bulb, and back down through the two cells and the clean spring. Round and round, in one loop, for as long as the switch was on.

Then the fan began to turn and the lights came back on. Kavya switched the torch off. She put it back in Ammachi's drawer with the cells facing the right way, ready for the next power cut.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a torch is one loop. The electricity goes from the cells, along the metal case, through the switch and the bulb, and back through the cells and the spring. Two cells in a row push together only when they face the same way. Turn one round, and the two pushes cancel. A leaked cell leaves a crust that is a poor conductor, so it made a gap in the loop at the spring. Kavya found both gaps because she knew the bulb needs a complete path.

- Ask: Why did the torch still not work after Kavya put in new cells?
- Ask: What happened when one cell was turned the wrong way round? Why?
- Ask: Why did the crust on the spring stop the torch from working?
- Ask: Kavya's mother told her not to touch the crust. Why do you think she said that?
- Ask: Can you trace the loop the electricity takes in a torch, using your finger on a drawing?

## Games
### g1 · Close the Loop: a circuit card game (20 min)
- **rules:** Take out the 4 CELL cards. Give each player 1 CELL and put it in front of them. Put any spare CELL cards away. Shuffle the other 36 circuit cards, deal 5 to each player, and put the rest face down as the draw pile. Put the question cards in a second pile.; Your aim is a loop that starts at your CELL, goes through a BULB card and at least 3 WIRE, CONDUCTOR or SWITCH cards, and comes back to your CELL. Lay your cards in a ring round your CELL.; On your turn, draw one card. Then do one thing: play a BULB, WIRE, CONDUCTOR or SWITCH card on your own loop, or play a GAP! or INSULATOR card on another player's loop, or play a FIX IT card. If you cannot or do not want to play, put one card from your hand on the discard pile.; A GAP! or INSULATOR card on your loop breaks it. Your loop cannot light until the gap is mended. To mend it, play a FIX IT card (then you may also play one more card), or spend your whole turn taking the card out.; A SWITCH card in your loop protects it. When a GAP! or INSULATOR lands on you, turn the SWITCH face down instead. At the start of your next turn, turn it face up again for free.; When your loop is complete, trace it with a finger from + round to −. ## Experiments
### e1 · The Conductor Test: which things complete the circuit? (30 min)
- **question:** Which everyday things let electricity through, so that the bulb lights, and which things stop it?
- **predict:** Look at the things on the table. Guess which ones will make the bulb light. Put them in two piles. Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same cell, the same bulb and the same wires for every object. Touch both wire ends firmly to the object, about 2 cm apart. Only the object changes. Test each object twice, because a loose touch can fool you.
- **safety:** Use only AA cells, never a wall socket or a charger. Never join the two ends of a cell with just a wire or foil, because the wire gets hot. A grown-up strips the wire ends and stays close.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw two big circles, one for conductors and one for insulators, and write each object in the right circle.
- **results table:** Object | What it is made of | My guess: will it light? | Try 1: lit? | Try 2: lit? | Conductor or insulator?
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Join one wire from the cell holder to the bulb holder. 2. Join a second wire to the other side of the bulb holder. 3. Leave the free ends of the cell wire and the bulb wire apart. 4. Touch the two free ends together.  
  *Parent says:* First we build a tester. It is a circuit with a gap in it. Watch what happens when I touch the two loose ends together.  
  *Child mode:* Build a circuit with one gap. Touch the two loose ends together. Does the bulb light?
  - Note: The bulb should light when the two bare ends touch, and go out when they part. If it does not light, check that the bulb is screwed in firmly and the bare copper is touching. A 2.5 volt bulb glows dimly on one cell, so close the curtains. If your tester never lights, try a fresh cell.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay out the 12 things. 2. Ask your child to put them in two piles: 'will light' and 'won't light'. 3. Ask for a reason for each pile. Write the guesses in the table.  
  *Parent says:* The gap is like a bridge that is missing. Which of these things will carry the electricity across it? Make two piles. Why did you put each one there?  
  *Child mode:* Make two piles. Will light. Won't light. Why did you choose that pile?
  - Ask: Why do you think the spoon will light the bulb? (good: Because it is metal.; Because electricity goes through metal, like in a wire.)
  - Note: Do not say what will happen. Any reason is good if your child says it. The reasons tell you what they think now.
- **s3 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Press both bare wire ends firmly onto the first object, about 2 cm apart. 2. Look at the bulb. 3. Write the answer. 4. Try again, then go on to the next object.  
  *Parent says:* Let's test them one at a time. Press both ends on firmly. Watch the bulb closely. A dim glow counts too.  
  *Child mode:* Press both wire ends onto the object. Look at the bulb. Write yes or no. Try it twice.
  - Note: The steel spoon, coin, plain paper clip, foil and key should light the bulb brightly. The wood, plastic, rubber, paper and glass should not. The coated paper clip will not light it until you scrape the plastic off each end. For the pencil, touch one wire to the lead at each sharpened end. The bulb may glow only very dimly, or not at all, because graphite conducts much less well than metal. If it does not glow, try a short piece of lead, about 2 cm, between the two wire ends.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the coated paper clip result together.  
  *Parent says:* The plain clip lit the bulb, but the coloured clip did not. Both are steel inside. Why do you think that happened?  
  *Child mode:* The plain clip lit the bulb. The coloured clip did not. Why?
  - Check: A paper clip with a plastic coat does not light the bulb. Why? → **The plastic coat is an insulator, so the electricity cannot reach the steel**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Now tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: metal things let electricity through, and other things stop it. What I saw: the spoon, the coin, the key and the foil lit the bulb. The wood, plastic, paper and glass did not. Why: metals let electricity through, so they are conductors. The others do not, so they are insulators. That is why wires are copper inside plastic.
  - Check: Which group were nearly all the conductors in? → **Metals**
### e2 · Where Is the Gap? Breaking the loop in different places (20 min)
- **question:** Does it matter where the gap in a circuit is? And does a very long wire make the bulb light later?
- **predict:** Guess first. If you break the loop far away from the bulb, will the bulb still glow a little? With a very long wire, will the bulb light at once, or a moment later? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same cell, bulb and wires each time. Only the place of the gap changes. Then only the length of one wire changes. Try each one three times.
- **safety:** Use only AA cells, never a wall socket or a charger. Never join the two ends of a cell with just a wire or foil, because the wire gets hot. A grown-up strips the wire ends and stays close.
- **record:** Draw the loop three times. Mark where you made the gap each time, and draw the bulb lit or dark.
- **results table:** What I changed | My guess | Did the bulb light? | At once, or later?
- **s1 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Make a complete circuit with the cell, the bulb and three wires. 2. Twist the wire ends together firmly where they join. 3. Check that the bulb lights.  
  *Parent says:* Here is a complete loop. Trace the path with your finger, from the cell, through the bulb and back to the cell.  
  *Child mode:* Build a full loop. Trace it with your finger. Cell, bulb, back to the cell.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Untwist the join next to the plus end of the cell. 2. Join it again. 3. Untwist the join on the far side of the bulb. 4. Join it again. 5. Untwist the join in the middle of the loop.  
  *Parent says:* We will make a gap in three places. Before each one, tell me: will the bulb go out, or glow a little?  
  *Child mode:* Make a gap next to the cell. Then after the bulb. Then in the middle. Guess first each time.
  - Note: The bulb goes out completely and at once, wherever the gap is. Many children expect the electricity on the cell's side of the gap to keep the bulb glowing a little, or expect a gap after the bulb not to matter.
  - Check: You make a gap after the bulb, on the way back to the cell. What happens? → **The bulb goes out, because the loop is broken**
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* Now one wire is very long. When I touch it on, will the bulb light at once? Or will it wait for the electricity to get there?  
  *Child mode:* Now one wire is very long. Watch the bulb. Does it light at once? Or later?
  - Ask: Why do you think the bulb lit straight away? (good: The electricity was already in the wire.; The whole loop starts moving at once.)
  - Note: The bulb lights at once, with no delay you can see. With a paper clip chain it may be a little dimmer, because each join between clips lets the electricity through less well. That is fine. What matters is that there is no wait.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about the cycle chain. Then ask for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Think of a cycle chain. Push the pedal, and every link moves at once, even the far ones. Electrons in a wire are like that. Now tell me what you think, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Think of a cycle chain. Every link moves at once. Now say: what I think, what I saw, why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a gap anywhere stops the bulb, and a long wire does not make it wait. What I saw: the bulb went out every time, wherever I made the gap. With the 2 metre wire it still lit at once. Why: the tiny electrons are already in the wire all the way round. The cell pushes them all together, like a cycle chain. So a break anywhere stops them all.
  - Think: If the electrons go round and come back to the cell, what is the cell running out of when it goes flat?
### e3 · Two Cells: brighter, or nothing at all? (20 min)
- **question:** What happens to the bulb when you add a second cell? And what happens if the second cell faces the wrong way?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will two cells make the bulb brighter, the same, or dimmer? And what if one cell is turned round? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same bulb and wires each time. Only the cells change. Look at the bulb in the same light each time, and compare it with the one-cell bulb.
- **safety:** Use only AA cells, never a wall socket or a charger. Never join the two ends of a cell with just a wire or foil, because the wire gets hot. A grown-up strips the wire ends and stays close. Use no more than two AA cells with a torch bulb, or the bulb may burn out.
- **record:** Draw the bulb three times. Draw lines around it to show how bright it was.
- **results table:** Cells | My guess | What the bulb did
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Light the bulb with one cell. 2. Look at how bright it is.  
  *Parent says:* Here is our bulb with one cell. Remember how bright it looks.  
  *Child mode:* Light the bulb with one cell. Remember how bright it is.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Join the red wire of one holder to the black wire of the other holder. 2. Join the two free wires to the bulb. 3. Compare the brightness.  
  *Parent says:* Now two cells are in a row, both facing the same way. Will the bulb be brighter, the same or dimmer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Join two cells in a row, plus to minus. Guess first. Brighter or dimmer?
  - Note: The bulb is clearly brighter, because two cells in a row push twice as hard, 3 volts instead of 1.5 volts. Red wires usually come from the plus end and black from the minus end. Check yours.

  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: two cells facing the same way make the bulb brighter, and two facing opposite ways cancel out. What I saw: the bulb was brighter with two cells in a row. When I turned one round, it went dark. Why: each cell pushes the electricity round the loop, and two pushes the same way add up.

## Worksheets
### Circuit Detective (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (sort) Sort these things you might find at home. Does each one run on mains electricity from the wall, or on cells?  
   **Answer:** fridge → Mains; TV remote → Cells; geyser → Mains; wall clock → Cells; mixer-grinder → Mains; torch → Cells; ceiling fan → Mains; toy car → Cells. *Why:* Things with a wire and a plug use mains electricity. Things with a little door for cells run on cells. A strong sorter looks for the plug or the cell door, not for how big the thing is.
2. (write) Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3, says, "A ceiling fan and a torch both use electricity, so they get it the same way." How can you tell, just by looking, that they do not?  
   **Answer:** The fan has a wire going into the wall, so it uses mains electricity. The torch has no wire. It has cells inside, behind a little cap or door. So they get their electricity in different ways.. *Why:* A strong answer names what you can see: a wire and a plug for mains, a cell door for cells.
3. (write) Grown-ups tell children never to plug in or pull out plugs. Give the reason behind this rule, not just the rule.  
   **Answer:** A wall socket in India is 230 volts. That is a strong enough push to send electricity through a person's body and badly hurt or kill them. Fingers can slip onto the metal pins while a plug is going in or coming out, so grown-ups do it.. *Why:* A strong answer gives the number, 230 volts, and says what can go wrong: fingers touching the metal pins.
4. (mcq) A girl joins a wire from the top of a cell to the tip of a bulb. She joins a second wire from the bottom of the cell to the tip of the bulb too. The bulb stays dark. What went wrong?  
   Options: Both wires touch the same place on the bulb, so the electricity does not go through the bulb / Her wires are too short / The cell is upside down  
   **Answer:** Both wires touch the same place on the bulb, so the electricity does not go through the bulb. *Why:* A bulb has two metal contacts: the tip at the bottom and the metal side. The electricity must go in at one and out at the other, through the thin wire inside the bulb. With both wires on the tip, it never passes through the bulb.
5. (draw) Draw a cell, a bulb and two wires joined so that the bulb lights. Then draw arrows to show the path of the electricity, all the way round.  
   **Answer:** A loop: from one end of the cell, along a wire to the bulb's tip, through the bulb, out of its metal side, along the second wire, and back to the other end of the cell. The arrows go round in one direction.. *Why:* A strong drawing has no gaps, uses both contacts of the bulb, and brings the path back to the other end of the cell.
6. (sort) You test each of these in a circuit tester. Sort them. Will the bulb light, or stay dark?  
   **Answer:** steel spoon → Bulb lights; one-rupee coin → Bulb lights; aluminium foil → Bulb lights; safety pin → Bulb lights; plastic ruler → Bulb stays dark; rubber band → Bulb stays dark; wooden ice-cream stick → Bulb stays dark; paper → Bulb stays dark. *Why:* The metal things are conductors, so they complete the loop. Plastic, rubber, wood and paper are insulators, so they leave a gap.
7. (mcq) In the conductor test, what did all the things that lit the bulb have in common?  
   Options: They were all metal / They were all shiny / They were all hard / They were all heavy  
   **Answer:** They were all metal. *Why:* Every thing that lit the bulb was metal. Shiny is not the reason: a glass bangle is shiny but does not conduct. Hard is not the reason either: wood is hard, and soft foil conducts.
8. (truefalse) Aluminium foil is thin and soft, not hard like a key. So foil will not light the bulb.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Foil is a metal, so it is a conductor, however thin and soft it is. What matters is the material, not how hard or thick it is.
9. (fill) A material that lets electricity through, like copper or steel, is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['conductor']. *Why:* Conductors let electricity flow through them. Most conductors are metals.
10. (fill) A switch works by opening and closing a ____ in the circuit.  
   **Answer:** ['gap', 'break']. *Why:* When a switch is off, there is a gap and the loop is broken. When it is on, the gap closes and the loop is complete.
11. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Cell → pushes electricity round a circuit; Circuit → a complete loop for electricity; Conductor → a material that lets electricity through; Insulator → a material that stops electricity; Switch → opens and closes a gap in a circuit. *Why:* These five words come up all through the unit. Knowing them makes the rest easier to explain.
12. (write) A lamp wire has a crack in the plastic, and shiny copper shows through. Why is this dangerous? What should you do?  
   **Answer:** The copper is a conductor and carries electricity from the wall, at 230 volts. The plastic was the insulator that kept it away from hands. Now a finger could touch the copper and get a shock. You should not touch it, and should tell a grown-up, who switches off and unplugs the lamp.. *Why:* A strong answer says what the copper does, what the plastic was doing, and what to do: don't touch, tell a grown-up.
13. (truefalse) A torch bulb lights whichever way round you join it to the cell.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A torch bulb lights either way round, because the electricity just needs a complete loop through it. A small LED light is different: it lights only one way round.
14. (write) A doorbell rings only while you press the button. What happens inside the button when you press it, and when you let go?  
   **Answer:** When I press, two metal parts inside touch, so the loop is complete and the bell rings. When I let go, a spring pushes them apart. Now there is a gap of air, the loop is broken, and the bell stops.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea of a gap: touching metal closes the loop, a gap of air breaks it.
15. (fill) A torch bulb has two metal contacts. One is the tip at the bottom. The other is the metal ____.  
   **Answer:** ['side']. *Why:* The electricity goes in at one contact, through the thin wire inside, and out at the other. That is why one wire to the tip alone does not light it.
### Inside the Wire (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) You make a loop with a wire 2 metres long, as long as a door is tall. You close the gap. When does the bulb light?  
   Options: At once / After about a second / After about a minute  
   **Answer:** At once. *Why:* The bulb lights at once. The tiny moving bits called electrons are already in the wire all the way round. The cell pushes them all at the same moment, so none has to travel from the cell to the bulb first.
2. (write) When you push a cycle pedal, the back wheel turns at once, even though each link of the chain moves slowly. How is a circuit like a cycle chain?  
   **Answer:** In a circuit, the wire is full of electrons all the way round, like the chain is full of links. The cell pushes them all at once, like the pedal pushes the whole chain. So the bulb lights at once, even though each electron moves very slowly.. *Why:* A strong answer matches the parts: links and electrons, pedal and cell, wheel and bulb. It says why the bulb lights at once.
3. (mcq) In the two-cell test, you join two cells in a row, both facing the same way, with one bulb. What does the bulb do?  
   Options: It is brighter than with one cell / It is dimmer than with one cell / It is the same as with one cell  
   **Answer:** It is brighter than with one cell. *Why:* Two cells in a row, facing the same way, push together. That is 3 volts instead of 1.5 volts, so more electricity flows and the bulb is brighter.
4. (write) Why does the wire in a short circuit get hot, when the wires in a circuit with a bulb stay cool?  
   **Answer:** The thin wire inside a bulb slows the flow of electricity. Without the bulb, nothing slows it, so a lot of electricity rushes round. All that flow heats the wire. With a bulb in the loop, less flows, so the wires stay cool and the bulb's thin wire glows instead.. *Why:* A strong answer says the bulb slows the flow, and more flow means more heat.
5. (mcq) Pencil lead is made of a material called graphite. Touched to both ends of the tester, it makes the bulb glow very dimly. What does that show?  
   Options: Graphite is an insulator / Graphite conducts, but much less well than metal / The cell has gone flat  
   **Answer:** Graphite conducts, but much less well than metal. *Why:* A dim glow means some electricity got through, but less than through a metal. So graphite is a conductor that is not a metal. Not every conductor is a metal, though most are.
6. (sort) Each of these is touched to both ends of a circuit tester. Sort them. Will the bulb light, or stay dark?  
   **Answer:** paper clip with a plastic coat → Bulb stays dark; the same clip with the coat scraped off the ends → Bulb lights; a pencil, touching only the wood → Bulb stays dark; pencil lead touching both ends → Bulb lights; a steel tumbler → Bulb lights; a key wrapped in tape → Bulb stays dark. *Why:* Only the material the wire ends touch matters. A plastic coat, wood or tape is an insulator and leaves a gap, even with metal inside. Pencil lead lights the bulb only dimly.
7. (mcq) An old string of festival lights goes dark when just one bulb breaks. Why?  
   Options: The broken bulb makes a gap in the one loop that all the bulbs share / All the other bulbs broke at the same moment / The plug got too hot and switched off  
   **Answer:** The broken bulb makes a gap in the one loop that all the bulbs share. *Why:* In old light strings, every bulb is in one loop. A broken bulb leaves a gap, so electricity stops flowing through all of them. Many new strings are wired so that each bulb has its own path.
8. (order) Put these in order, from the weakest push to the strongest.  
   **Answer:** ['one AA cell', 'two AA cells in a row', 'a wall socket in India']. *Why:* One AA cell is 1.5 volts. Two in a row facing the same way are 3 volts. A wall socket is 230 volts, about 150 times one cell, which is why it is never for experiments.
9. (draw) Draw a circuit with a cell, a bulb and a paper clip switch. Draw it twice: once with the switch open, once with it closed.  
   **Answer:** Two loops. In the open one, the clip is off one pin, there is a gap, and the bulb is dark. In the closed one, the clip touches both pins and the bulb is lit.. *Why:* The only difference between the two drawings is the gap at the switch. A strong drawing shows that clearly.
10. (write) Your torch does not light. Write three things you would check, and say why you would check each one.  
   **Answer:** I would check that both cells face the same way, because cells facing opposite ways cancel. I would check the spring and the metal contacts for crust or rust, because those make a gap. I would try new cells, because the old ones may be flat.. *Why:* A strong answer hunts for gaps in the loop and thinks about the cells, with a reason for each check.
11. (perspective) A family is deciding who should change the cells in the torch and the TV remote.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and backs it with a fact: an AA cell is only 1.5 volts, so it is safe to touch, but a swallowed button cell can badly burn a child's food pipe within two hours. It then admits a fair point from another side.. *Why:* There is a real trade-off here. Children learn by doing, and AA cells are safe to touch. But button cells are dangerous for babies, so where they are kept matters.
12. (truefalse) Tap water lets a little electricity through, because of the salts dissolved in it.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Very pure water hardly conducts, but tap water has salts dissolved in it. That is one reason water near sockets and switches is dangerous.
13. (match) Match each part of a torch to its job.  
   **Answer:** Cells → store energy and push the electricity round; Spring → presses the cells against the contacts and carries the electricity; Switch → opens and closes a gap in the loop; Plastic case → an insulator that you can hold safely; Bulb → its thin wire gets hot and glows. *Why:* A torch is one loop. Every part has a job in keeping the loop complete, or in keeping it safe to hold.
14. (write) A table lamp goes dark, but its bulb is fine. List three places where a gap could be, and say how a grown-up could find it. The lamp must be unplugged first.  
   **Answer:** A gap could be at the switch, if its metal parts no longer touch. It could be in the cable, if the copper has snapped inside the plastic. It could be where the wire joins the plug or the bulb holder, if a connection is loose. The grown-up checks each place, one at a time.. *Why:* A strong answer thinks of the lamp as one loop and names real places a gap could hide.
15. (truefalse) Three AA cells in a row, all facing the same way, push with 4.5 volts.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Each cell adds 1.5 volts when they face the same way. 3 times 1.5 is 4.5. That is far less than the 230 volts of a wall socket.
### Frogs, Metals and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)

   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with evidence. For Volta: his pile of zinc, copper and salty cloth made electricity with no frog at all. For Galvani: our nerves and muscles really do use electrical signals. A strong answer then gives the other side its due, because both were partly right.. *Why:* Volta was right that two metals and something wet make electricity, and he built the first battery in 1799. Galvani was right that living bodies use electricity. Each was partly right.
2. (write) Galvani's test had a brass hook, an iron rail and a wet frog's leg. Volta's pile had zinc discs, copper discs and cloth soaked in salty water. What did both set-ups have in common?  
   **Answer:** Both had two different metals: brass and iron, or zinc and copper. Both had something wet and salty between them: the frog's leg or the salty cloth. Two different metals with something wet between them make electricity.. *Why:* A strong answer finds both things they share: two different metals, and something wet between them.
3. (mcq) A touch-me-not plant folds their leaves when you touch them. What travels along the leaf stalk to make this happen?  
   Options: A sound / An electrical signal / A drop of water

   **Answer:** The crow touches only one wire, so there is no loop through their body. *Why:* Electricity flows only round a loop. 5. (mcq) Why does a plug have at least two pins?  
   Options: One pin is a spare / The electricity needs a way in and a way back, to make a loop / So that the plug stands up straight  
   **Answer:** The electricity needs a way in and a way back, to make a loop. *Why:* An iron or a fan is part of a loop, just like a bulb. The electricity needs one path in and one path back. Many plugs have a third pin, called earth, for safety.

   **Answer:** ['the socket', 'one pin of the plug', 'one wire in the cable', 'the heating coil inside the iron', 'the other wire in the cable', 'the other pin, back into the socket']. *Why:* An iron is one big loop, like a torch. The electricity goes out through one pin and wire, through the coil that gets hot, and back through the other wire and pin.
7. (write) Your friend says, "A cell is like a water tank full of electricity, and the bulb drinks it up." What would you tell your friend? Use evidence from your experiments.  
   **Answer:** I would tell her the electricity is not drunk up. It goes round the loop and back to the cell. My evidence is that a gap after the bulb puts it out, just like a gap before it. What a cell runs out of is the energy in its chemicals.. *Why:* A strong answer corrects the idea and backs it with a result from the gap test.
8. (mcq) Before testing anything, you touch the two loose wire ends of your circuit tester together, and the bulb lights. Why is this a good first step?  
   Options: It shows the tester works, so a dark bulb later means the thing is an insulator, not that the tester is broken / It charges the cell / It warms up the bulb so it lights faster  
   **Answer:** It shows the tester works, so a dark bulb later means the thing is an insulator, not that the tester is broken. *Why:* A dark bulb could mean two things: the thing is an insulator, or the tester itself has a gap or a flat cell.    **Answer:** Tape is an insulator, so touching it leaves a gap. *Why:* The wand and the path are the two sides of a gap. Touching metal to metal closes it. Tape is an insulator, so the gap stays open and the bulb stays dark.
10. (draw) Design a steady hand game. Draw the foil path, the wand, the cell and the bulb, and mark where the gap is.  
   **Answer:** A wavy foil path joined to one end of the cell. A wand joined to the bulb, and the bulb joined to the other end of the cell. The gap is between the wand and the path.. *Why:* The game works because the wand and the path are the two ends of a gap. A shaky hand closes it.
11. (draw) Draw a loop with one cell, one bulb and two paper clip switches, so the bulb lights only when both switches are closed.  
   **Answer:** One loop with both switches in it, one after the other. If either switch is open, there is a gap, so the bulb is dark. Only when both are closed is the loop complete.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts both switches in the same loop, so each one can make a gap. A machine that must not start by accident can use two switches like this.
12. (mcq) An electrician's gloves are made of thick rubber. Why rubber, and not cotton?  
   Options: Rubber is an insulator, and it stays one even when it is wet / Rubber is a conductor / Cotton is too hot to wear  
   **Answer:** Rubber is an insulator, and it stays one even when it is wet. *Why:* Rubber stops electricity, and water cannot soak into it. Cotton soaks up sweat and water, and wet cloth lets electricity through. So rubber keeps the hands safe.
13. (fill) Three AA cells sit in a row. Two face the same way, and the third is turned round. Together they push with ____ volts.  
   **Answer:** ['1.5', '1.5 volts']. *Why:* The two cells facing the same way push with 3 volts. The turned-round cell pushes back with 1.5 volts. 3 minus 1.5 leaves 1.5 volts, the same as one cell on its own.
14. (write) To test his idea, Volta left the frog out and still made electricity. Why was leaving the frog out a good test? Think of another idea you could test by leaving something out.  
   **Answer:** Leaving the frog out showed whether the frog was needed. The electricity still appeared, so the frog was not the source. I could test whether a bulb needs a cell by taking the cell away, or whether a plant needs light by keeping a plant in the dark.. *Why:* A strong answer says a test removes one thing to see if the result still happens, and gives another example.
15. (fill) Drawings show electricity going from plus to minus, but the electrons really move from ____ to plus.  
   **Answer:** ['minus']. *Why:* People drew the arrows before electrons were found in 1897. The electrons are pushed out of the minus end of the cell and come back in at the plus end.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these runs on mains electricity from the wall?  
   Options: A TV remote / A wall clock / A mixer-grinder / A torch  
   **Answer:** A mixer-grinder. *Why:* A mixer-grinder has a wire and a plug, so it uses mains electricity. The remote, the clock and the torch run on cells. Picking the clock is easy to do, because some big clocks are plugged in, but most wall clocks use one AA cell.
2. (mcq) Why must you never touch a switch with wet hands?  
   Options: Water makes the switch rusty / Water lets electricity through your skin much more easily / Water turns the light off  
   **Answer:** Water lets electricity through your skin much more easily. *Why:* Wet skin lets electricity through far more easily than dry skin, so a shock is more likely. Picking 'rusty' is a real problem for switches over time, but it is not the danger to you.
3. (mcq) How many volts is a wall socket in India?  
   Options: 1.5 volts / 12 volts / 230 volts / 2 volts  
   **Answer:** 230 volts. *Why:* Mains electricity in India is 230 volts, about 150 times as much as an AA cell's 1.5 volts. That is why sockets are never for experiments.
4. (mcq) Kiran, a girl in Class 3, stands a bulb on top of an AA cell. It does not light. What does she need to add?  
   Options: A second bulb / A wire from the bulb's metal side to the bottom of the cell / Nothing. She must press harder  
   **Answer:** A wire from the bulb's metal side to the bottom of the cell. *Why:* The bulb's tip touches only the top of the cell. The electricity needs a path back to the bottom of the cell, through the bulb's side. One wire makes the loop complete. Pressing harder does not make a path.
5. (mcq) In a working circuit, you lift a wire off the bulb, on the side away from the cell. What happens?  
   Options: The bulb stays lit / The bulb goes out at once / The bulb gets slowly dimmer  
   **Answer:** The bulb goes out at once. *Why:* A gap anywhere in the loop stops the flow everywhere at once. Picking 'stays lit' means thinking electricity flows into the bulb and stops. It actually goes round and back to the cell.
6. (mcq) Which of these is a conductor?  
   Options: A wooden ice-cream stick / A plastic ruler / A steel spoon / A glass bangle  
   **Answer:** A steel spoon. *Why:* Steel is a metal, and metals are conductors, so the spoon lights the bulb in a circuit tester. Wood, plastic and glass are insulators.
7. (mcq) A girl tests a paper clip with a pink plastic coat. The bulb does not light. Why?  
   Options: Steel is an insulator / The plastic coat is an insulator, so the electricity cannot reach the steel / Pink things never conduct  
   **Answer:** The plastic coat is an insulator, so the electricity cannot reach the steel. *Why:* The steel inside conducts, but the wire ends touch only plastic. Scrape the plastic off each end, and the bulb lights. Colour has nothing to do with it.
8. (mcq) A switch is turned off. What is inside it?  
   Options: Two metal parts touching / A gap of air between two metal parts / A tiny cell  
   **Answer:** A gap of air between two metal parts. *Why:* When a switch is off, its metal parts are apart, with air between them. Air is an insulator, so the loop is broken. When it is on, they touch and the loop is complete.
9. (mcq) Why was the paper clip switch built on cardboard and not on a steel plate?  
   Options: Cardboard is cheaper / Steel would join the pins, so the bulb would never turn off / Steel would make the bulb dimmer  
   **Answer:** Steel would join the pins, so the bulb would never turn off. *Why:* Steel is a conductor. It would join the two pins all the time, so opening the clip would not break the loop. Cardboard is an insulator, so only the clip can join the pins.
10. (mcq) When a bulb lights, what happens to the electrons in the wire?  
   Options: They are used up in the bulb / They go round the loop and come back to the cell / They stay still and the bulb pulls in light  
   **Answer:** They go round the loop and come back to the cell. *Why:* The electrons go round the loop and come back. The same ones go round again and again. What the cell uses up is the energy in its chemicals. Picking 'used up' is the common idea that a cell is a tank of electricity.
11. (mcq) Galvani thought the twitching frog legs were caused by electricity inside the frog. What did Volta show?  
   Options: Frogs make no electricity at all / Two different metals with something wet between them make electricity, even with no frog / The twitching was caused by the weather  
   **Answer:** Two different metals with something wet between them make electricity, even with no frog. *Why:* Volta made electricity with discs of two metals and salty cloth, with no frog, and built the first battery in 1799. Galvani was partly right too, because nerves really do use electrical signals. Picking 'frogs make no electricity' goes too far.

## Applications
- Every light, fan and appliance at home is part of a circuit with a switch.
- Torches, remotes, clocks and toys run on cells in a small circuit.
- Electricians use insulated tools and rubber gloves, because plastic and rubber are insulators.
- Thick aluminium wires on tall towers carry electricity from power stations to towns. Copper wires carry it inside our homes.
- A fridge light uses a press switch that closes when the door opens.
- Doorbells use a press switch.
- Circuit breakers, called MCBs, in your home's switch box open the circuit by themselves if too much electricity flows.
- Phones, computers and cars are full of tiny circuits.
- Doctors record the heart's electrical signals with an ECG machine.
- Solar panels on roofs make electricity from sunlight and send it round circuits.

## Spotted around us
- The switchboard in your home, with a row of rocker switches.
- The MCB box near your front door, where a grown-up switches off the power in an emergency.
- Power lines on tall towers beside the highway, carrying electricity between cities.
- The white or brown cup-shaped insulators on electric poles, which keep the wires from touching the pole.
- Festival lights at Diwali and Karthigai Deepam, wired in long strings.
- The torch in the kitchen drawer, ready for a power cut.
- The red and black wires on a toy or a cell holder, showing plus and minus.

## Open questions
- **How exactly does the brain turn electrical signals into thoughts and feelings?** Scientists can record the signals, but nobody knows how they become the experience of seeing, feeling or thinking.
- **Can we make wires that carry electricity with no loss at all, at room temperature?** Some materials, called superconductors, do this when very cold. Nobody has yet found one that works at room temperature and normal air pressure.
- **How can we store enough electricity from sunlight and wind for the night, cheaply?** Batteries are getting better every year, but storing electricity for a whole city is still costly. Scientists are testing many kinds.
- **What did the 'Baghdad battery', an ancient clay jar with copper and iron inside, really do?** Some people think it was a battery. Most archaeologists think it was a jar for storing scrolls. Nobody knows for sure.

## Connected maths
- A wall socket in India pushes at 230 volts. An AA cell pushes at 1.5 volts. Two AA cells in a row push at 3 volts. How many AA cells in a row would push as hard as 30 volts? **20 cells.**. Each cell adds 1.5 volts. Two cells make 3 volts. 30 is 10 lots of 3. So 10 lots of 2 cells, which is 20 cells. Check: 20 times 1.5 is 30. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Conductor Test, you tested 12 things. 5 lit the bulb brightly, 1 lit it very dimly, and the rest did not light it. How many did not light it? What fraction of the things lit the bulb at all? **6 did not light it. Half of the things lit the bulb at all.**. 5 + 1 = 6 lit the bulb. 12 - 6 = 6 did not. 6 out of 12 is the same as 1 out of 2, which is a half. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)
- A wall clock uses one AA cell for about 12 months. A TV remote uses two AA cells, and they last about 6 months. How many cells does each use in 2 years? **The clock uses 2 cells. The remote uses 8 cells.**. The clock needs 1 cell every 12 months. 2 years is 24 months, so 2 cells. The remote needs 2 cells every 6 months. 24 months is 4 lots of 6, so 4 times 2 is 8 cells. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- You build a loop with 4 wires, a bulb and a cell. You can make a gap at any one join. The loop has 6 joins. If you make a gap at a different join each time, how many times will the bulb go out? **6 times, once for every join.**. A gap anywhere in the loop stops the bulb. There are 6 joins, so all 6 gaps stop it. This is a reasoning problem: the answer comes from knowing the rule, not from counting. (queues `math.logic.puzzles.L3`)
- Each electron in a wire moves about 1 millimetre in 10 seconds. How long would one electron take to travel round a loop of wire 1 metre long? A metre is 1000 millimetres. **About 10,000 seconds, which is nearly 3 hours.**. 1000 millimetres, at 1 millimetre every 10 seconds, takes 1000 times 10 = 10,000 seconds. An hour is 3600 seconds, so that is nearly 3 hours. Yet the bulb lights at once, because all the electrons start moving together. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
