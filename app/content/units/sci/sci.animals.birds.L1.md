# Birds around us: feathers, beaks, nests and calls
`sci.animals.birds.L1`

**Summary.** A hen cannot fly far, and a bat can fly well. So which of them is a bird? In this lesson you will find out what makes a bird a bird, count bird calls with beans at three times of the day, and see how a beak fits the food a bird eats.

**Intro.** *Close your eyes at the window for one minute. How many birds can you hear?* Even in a big city, birds call outside almost every window. You may hear a crow, a myna, a pigeon or a koel. Count the calls on your fingers. In this lesson we find out what birds are, and what their calls mean.

## Learning outcomes
0. By the end I can say what makes a bird a bird: feathers.
1. By the end I can say two jobs that feathers do.
2. By the end I can say how a beak fits the food a bird eats.
3. By the end I can say why birds build nests, and name two kinds of nest.
4. By the end I can listen, count bird calls with beans, and say what I found.
5. By the end I can say how to feed and give water to birds safely.

## Terms
- **Bird**: A bird is an animal with feathers. Every bird has a beak, two wings and two legs, and lays eggs. *e.g.* A crow, a hen, a peacock and an ostrich are all birds.
- **Feather**: A feather is a light, strong part that grows out of a bird's skin, as hair grows out of yours. *e.g.* A pigeon has stiff feathers on the wings and soft, fluffy feathers close to the body.
- **Beak**: A beak is a bird's hard mouth. Birds have no teeth. The beak picks up, holds and breaks the food. *e.g.* A parakeet cracks a hard seed with a strong, hooked beak.
- **Nest**: A nest is a safe place a bird makes to lay eggs and keep the chicks. *e.g.* A crow builds a nest of sticks high in a tree.
- **Call and song**: A call is a short sound a bird makes to send a message. A song is a longer string of sounds. *e.g.* A crow calls 'kaa' to other crows. A koel sings 'ku-oo, ku-oo', louder and louder.
- **Preening**: Preening is when a bird cleans and oils their feathers with their beak. The oil keeps the feathers tidy and helps them shed water. *e.g.* A crow on your balcony rail runs each wing feather through her beak, one by one, after the rain.

## Explanations
### What makes a bird a bird · *defined*
**Definition:** A bird is an animal with feathers. Today, only birds have feathers.

Many people think a bird is any animal that flies. But a bat flies and has fur, not feathers. A bat is not a bird. A hen can only flap a short way, and an ostrich cannot fly at all, but both have feathers. So they are birds.
- e.g. A hen is a bird. She has feathers.
- e.g. A bat is not a bird. A bat has fur.
- e.g. A butterfly is not a bird. A butterfly is an insect.
### Feathers do many jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Feathers help a bird fly, keep warm, keep dry and show colours.

Stiff feathers on the wings push on the air, so the bird can fly. Soft, fluffy feathers near the skin hold warm air close to the body. Each feather is made of many thin strands. Tiny hooks hold the strands together, like the teeth of a zip. A bird runs her beak along her feathers every day, to hook the strands back together.
- e.g. A pigeon on a winter morning looks round and fat, because the feathers are fluffed up.
- e.g. A peacock shows his long, shining tail feathers.
- e.g. Birds drop old feathers and grow new ones. That is why we find feathers on the ground.
### A beak is a tool for the food · *defined*
**Definition:** The shape of a bird's beak fits the food that bird eats.

A sparrow has a short, thick beak to crack small seeds. A sunbird has a long, thin, curved beak to reach the sweet juice deep in a flower. A kingfisher has a long, sharp beak to catch fish. A crow has a strong beak for almost any food. You can guess what a bird eats by looking at the beak.
- e.g. A sparrow cracks grains with a short, thick beak.
- e.g. A purple sunbird sips nectar with a long, curved beak.
- e.g. A kingfisher catches a fish with a long, sharp beak.
### Nests are for eggs and chicks · *defined*
**Definition:** A bird builds a nest to keep the eggs and chicks safe and warm.

Most birds do not live in a nest all year. They build one when it is time to lay eggs. Each kind of bird builds in their own way. A crow uses sticks, and in cities sometimes wire. A tailorbird stitches two leaves together with thread from plants or spider silk. A pigeon makes a small pile of twigs on a ledge.
- e.g. A crow's nest of sticks sits high in a tree.
- e.g. A tailorbird's nest hides inside two leaves stitched together.
- e.g. A red-wattled lapwing lays her eggs on bare ground, and sometimes on a flat roof.
### Bird calls are messages · *debated*
Birds call to tell other birds things. One call can mean 'danger, a cat is near'. Another can mean 'here is food' or 'where are you?'. Birds sing to say 'this place is mine' and to find a partner. Other birds act on these calls, so the calls are messages. Some scientists say only humans have a real language, and bird calls are simpler signals.
- e.g. Crows call loudly together when they see a cat or an owl.
- e.g. A koel calls again and again in the hot months before the rains.
- e.g. A chick calls when hungry, and the parents bring food.
- **Scientists who study bird calls:** Birds talk to each other. Japanese tits even put two calls together in a set order, and other tits understand the order.
- **Many scientists who study language:** Bird calls carry a few messages, like danger or food. Only humans put words together to say endless new things.
### Feeding birds safely · *defined*
**Definition:** Birds need clean water every day, and the right food if we feed them.

In an Indian summer, city birds can find it hard to get water. A shallow bowl of clean water helps them drink and bathe. Change the water every day, so mosquitoes cannot breed in it. If you put out food, give grains, plain cooked rice or cut fruit. Never give salty, spicy, sugary or fried food.
- e.g. A clay bowl of water on a balcony in May.
- e.g. A pinch of broken rice or millet on a ledge.
- e.g. No namkeen, chips or biscuits for birds.

## Mostly agreed
- Only birds have feathers today. *How we know:* Scientists have looked at every kind of animal alive. Birds are the only ones with feathers. Bats, butterflies and flying insects fly without them.
- Many birds learn their songs by listening, the way children learn to talk. *How we know:* In the 1950s, William Thorpe raised young chaffinches who never heard a grown-up chaffinch. They grew up singing only a simple, odd song.
- Birds call and sing most in the early morning. *How we know:* Bird watchers all over the world hear the loudest singing around sunrise. It is called the dawn chorus.

## How we found out
**Margaret Morse Nice, an American scientist who studied song sparrows from her home, From 1928, for about eight years; her books on the song sparrow came out in 1937 and 1943.** About a hundred years ago, many bird experts studied dead birds kept in museum drawers. A small brown bird in a backyard seemed to have little to teach. Margaret Morse Nice thought differently. She lived in Columbus, a city in America, and had no job at a university. From 1928, she studied the song sparrows by the river below her home. She caught the birds gently and put tiny coloured rings on their legs. Each bird got a different set of colours, so she could tell them apart. Over about eight years she ringed hundreds of birds and watched them again and again. She found that each male sang his own set of songs, and guarded his own patch of ground. Some birds stayed all winter, and some flew away and came back. No two sparrows lived in the same way. Other scientists later said her work started a new way of studying birds: watching living birds, one by one.  
*What people believed before:* People thought you could learn what you needed about a kind of bird from dead birds in a museum, as if every bird of that kind were the same. Nice put coloured rings on living birds and watched them for years. She showed that each male had his own songs and his own patch of ground, and lived a life different from the next.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A bird is any animal that flies.** (A common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Most birds fly, and flying is the first thing we notice about them.  
  Against: A bat flies but has fur. An ostrich and a penguin cannot fly but have feathers. Feathers make a bird, not flying.
- **If you touch a baby bird, the mother will leave the chick.** (A very common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: People want to stop children from picking up chicks.  
  Against: Most birds do not leave a chick because a person touched them. But it is still best to leave a chick alone. A chick on the ground is often learning to fly, and the parents are close by.
- **Feeding birds is always kind.** (Many families, and some bird scientists who disagree; *debated*)  
  For: Water and grain help birds in hot summers and in cities with few trees.  
  Against: Salty or sugary food can make birds ill. Feeding big flocks of pigeons in one place can push other birds away, and the droppings can make some people ill.

## Roots
In the experiment A Bean for Every Call, we listened to the birds outside our window and counted their calls. Now we ask what all that calling tells us about birds, and about us.

**Deep truth.** When one crow calls loudly, other crows look, fly over or go quiet. The call changed what they did. So a bird call is a message, and the birds who hear it understand it.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call bird sounds 'chirping' or 'noise', as if they meant nothing. But birds act on each other's calls. So the calls are messages from one living being to another.  
*Evidence:* When one crow calls loudly at a cat, the other crows look, fly over or go quiet. In the experiment A Bean for Every Call, you also listened for one bird answering another.

- *who am i:* You learned to talk by listening to the people around you. A young bird learns to sing by listening too.
- *what is life:* A bird's day is full: finding food, calling to friends, warning of danger and caring for chicks.
- *what is this world:* The air outside your window is full of messages between birds. We hear only a few of them.

Every bird has the same few parts: feathers, a beak, two wings and two legs. There are about 11,000 kinds of birds, and more than 1,300 of them live in India. Each kind has a different beak, nest and call, so each lives in a different way.

- **Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.1 (also in Rig Veda 1.164.20):** “द्वा सुपर्णा सयुजा सखाया समानं वृक्षं परिषस्वजाते / तयोरन्यः पिप्पलं स्वाद्वत्त्यनश्नन्नन्यो अभिचाकशीति” Two birds sit on the same tree. One bird eats the fruit. The other bird does not eat, and only watches. (In the experiment A Bean for Every Call, you sat still and only listened, like the watching bird. Just listening let you notice the birds talking.)
- **Farid ud-Din Attar, The Conference of the Birds (Mantiq al-Tayr), about 1177:** In the Persian poem The Conference of the Birds, written by Attar, the birds of the world meet, talk together and set off on a long journey as one flock. (Real birds call to each other and gather together too, as crows, mynas and parakeets do in big trees before dark.)
- Science (settled): Many birds learn their songs by listening to grown-up birds, as human babies learn to talk.
- Science (debated): Japanese tits put two calls together in a set order, and other tits understand the order. Whether any bird has a language like ours is still argued about.
- Other view, Many scientists who study language: They say only humans have a true language. Bird calls carry a few messages, like danger or food, but birds cannot put calls together to say endless new things, as people do with words.

- Ask: If birds are talking all day, what do you think they talk about?
- Ask: Would a bird think our talking is just noise?
- Ask: Who taught you your first words? Who teaches a young bird to sing?

## Story: The Bulbuls in the Money Plant
*Thenmozhi, Arul and Paati are made up. Red-vented bulbuls really do nest in balcony plants in Indian cities, lay two or three spotted eggs, give sharp alarm calls when a cat comes near, and feed their chicks insects and fruit.*

Thenmozhi lived on the third floor of a flat in Madurai. She was six. Her little brother Arul was four. Their Paati, their grandmother, kept a row of plants on the balcony.

One morning, Paati called softly. "Come and look. But walk slowly."

In the money plant, two brown birds were hopping in and out. Each one had a black cap on the head and a patch of red under the tail. They were red-vented bulbuls.

One bulbul had a dry grass stem in her beak. She pushed the grass in between the leaves. Then she flew away and came back with a bit of soft root.

"They are building a nest," said Paati. "A nest is a cradle for their eggs."

Arul wanted to touch the nest. Thenmozhi held his hand. "If we go too close, they will be frightened," she said. "Let's watch from the door."

For three days the bulbuls worked. The nest became a small, neat cup, like a tiny katori made of grass. Then, one morning, there were two pink eggs inside, with dark spots.

Now one bulbul sat on the eggs almost all day. The other bulbul brought food. The family watered the plants very quietly, and only from the edge.

One afternoon, the neighbour's cat walked along the balcony wall. At once the bulbul on the railing called, sharp and fast. The bulbul on the nest pressed down flat and kept very still. A myna on the next balcony began to scold the cat too.

The cat looked up, looked around, and jumped down.

"Did you hear that?" said Thenmozhi. "That was not singing. That bulbul was shouting, 'Danger! A cat!'"

"How do you know?" asked Arul.

"Because the other bulbul hid as soon as she heard it," said Thenmozhi. "And the myna understood too."

Paati smiled. "Birds talk all day," she said. "We just don't listen."

About two weeks later, the eggs hatched. Out came two tiny chicks with no feathers, only pink skin and big yellow mouths. Whenever a parent landed, the chicks opened their beaks wide and called, "Cheep, cheep, cheep!" That call meant, "Feed me!" The parents pushed small insects and bits of fruit into the open mouths.

Every day the chicks grew. Grey prickles came out on their skin, and the prickles opened into feathers. Soon the chicks filled the whole nest.

Arul made a rule. "No loud music on the balcony," he said. "The babies are sleeping." Thenmozhi said it was the best rule Arul had ever made.

Then one morning the chicks stood on the edge of the nest. They flapped their short wings. One chick fluttered to the railing. The other followed. The parents called from the neem tree below, again and again. The chicks called back. Then all four bulbuls were gone into the green.

The nest sat empty in the money plant. Arul looked sad.

"Listen," said Thenmozhi. From the neem tree came a bulbul call, and then a small answer.

"They are still talking," said Arul.

That night Thenmozhi drew the nest, the two eggs, the cat and the four bulbuls. Under it she wrote, with Paati's help: "Our balcony is their home too."

**For the parent:** The bulbul's sharp call was a message. The other bulbul and even the myna understood it.

- Ask: How did Thenmozhi know the bulbul was saying 'danger'?
- Ask: Why did the family stay away from the nest?
- Ask: What did the chicks' 'cheep, cheep' mean?

### g2 · Danger Call: a running and freezing game (10 min)
- **rules:** Choose a safe place, like the sofa or a mat. This is the tree.; Tear newspaper into small scraps and scatter them on the floor. These are the seeds.; One player is the lookout bird. The others are birds, pecking up seeds with two fingers. With only two players, there is one lookout and one bird.; The lookout picks a call card and makes that call. The other birds do what the call means.; A bird who does the wrong thing says 'oops' and tries again at the next call.; Each lookout makes three calls, then passes the cards to the next player. Play until every player has been the lookout once.
- **Printable cards: 6 call cards for the lookout:** Danger! A cat! (back: Call 'pit-pit-pit-pit' fast. Birds fly to the safe place.); Danger! A hawk above! (back: Call one long 'seeee'. Birds freeze and stay still.); Food here! (back: Call 'chuk-chuk'. Birds come to the lookout.); Where are you? (back: Call 'kaa'. Every bird calls 'kaa' back.); All safe now (back: Sing a long, happy tune. Birds go back to pecking.); Feed me! (back: Call 'cheep cheep'. The nearest bird brings you a paper scrap as a pretend worm.)
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tear a sheet of newspaper into small scraps and scatter them. 2. Choose the tree. 3. Read the call cards aloud together.  
  *Parent says:* Each card has a call and a job for the birds. Read each one, then practise the sound.  
  *Child mode:* Practise each call sound. Remember what the birds must do.
  - Note: These calls are made-up sounds for the game. Real alarm calls differ between kinds of birds, but many small birds do use one call for a danger on the ground and another for a hawk in the sky.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play. The grown-up is the first lookout, then the others take turns. Each lookout makes three calls.  
  *Parent says:* Peck, peck, peck. Listen for the lookout's call.  
  *Child mode:* Peck the seeds. Listen for the lookout's call. Then act fast.
  - Ask: Why did you freeze for the hawk call but run for the cat call? (good: Staying still hides me from a hawk above.; Going up the tree keeps me away from a cat on the ground.)
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Sit down together. Ask why a bird needs more than one danger call.  
  *Parent says:* Think about where a cat is, and where a hawk is. Would the same hiding place work for both?  
  *Child mode:* Where is a cat? Where is a hawk?
  - Ask: What should a bird do when a cat is near? What about a hawk? (good: A cat is on the ground, so birds fly up to the tree.; A hawk is in the sky, so birds keep still and hide.; Each danger needs a different thing to do.)
  - Check: Why do birds need more than one danger call? → **Each danger needs a different thing to do**

## Experiments
### e1 · A Bean for Every Call: when do birds call most? (30 min)
- **question:** When do the birds outside our window call most: in the early morning, in the afternoon or in the evening?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which cup will have the most beans: morning, afternoon or evening? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We listen at the same window each time, for the same 5 minutes, with the TV and music off. The same person drops the beans. One bean means one call, or one burst of calls when they come too fast.
- **safety:** A grown-up stays with the child at an open window or on the balcony. Keep the beans away from babies and toddlers, who could choke on them.
- **record:** Line up the beans from each cup and take a photo, or draw the three lines. Then fill in the table with a grown-up: the beans in each cup, the birds you heard, and whether one bird answered another.
- **results table:** Time | Beans in the cup | Which birds did we hear? | Did one bird answer another?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the three cups Morning, Afternoon and Evening. 2. Show your child the beans. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason. 4. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* We will listen three times today: morning, afternoon and evening. Each bird call is one bean.  
  *Child mode:* Pick the cup you think will get the most beans.
  - Ask: Why do you think that cup will get the most beans? (good: Birds wake up in the morning.; In the afternoon it is too hot.; Birds go to sleep in the evening.)
  - Note: Write the guess and the reason on a slip of paper and put it under the cup. Don't say what usually happens.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Early morning, within an hour after sunrise, sit at the window. 2. Start a 5-minute timer. 3. Your child drops one bean in the Morning cup for every call. 4. Whisper the bird's name if you know it.  
  *Parent says:* Shh. Every time you hear a bird, drop a bean in the cup.  
  *Child mode:* Listen. Drop one bean in the Morning cup each time a bird calls.
  - Ask: Did one bird call, and then another bird answer? (good: Yes, two crows were calling to each other.; The koel called and another koel called back.)
  - Note: If calls come too fast, drop one bean for each burst of calls. Crows, mynas, pigeons, bulbuls, sparrows, parakeets and, in the hot months, koels are the usual voices. Count only sounds you are sure are birds.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* In the afternoon, between 1 and 3 o'clock, do the same at the same window with the Afternoon cup.  
  *Parent says:* Same window, same 5 minutes. Drop a bean for each call, just like this morning.  
  *Child mode:* Sit at the same window for 5 minutes. Drop one bean in the Afternoon cup for each bird call.
  - Ask: Is it louder or quieter than the morning? (good: Quieter.; Only a crow and a pigeon.)
  - Note: Afternoons are often quiet, especially in hot weather, when many birds rest in the shade.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* About an hour before dark, listen again with the Evening cup.  
  *Parent says:* This is the last time we listen. Listen for birds going home to sleep. Are they calling together?  
  *Child mode:* Listen one last time. Drop one bean in the Evening cup for each bird call.
  - Note: In many Indian cities, crows, mynas and parakeets gather noisily in big trees before dark. Your evening cup may be fuller than you expect.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tip each cup out. 2. Help your child lay the beans from each cup in a straight line, side by side. 3. Count each line together.  
  *Parent says:* Tip out each cup. Put the beans in a straight line, one line for each cup.  
  *Child mode:* Make one line of beans for each cup. Count each line.
  - Ask: Which time had the most calls? (good: The morning. That line is the longest.; The evening had more than the afternoon.)
  - Ask: Was your guess right? (good: Yes, I guessed morning.; No, I thought evening.)
  - Note: Lining up the beans makes a picture graph your child can see at a glance. In most homes the morning line is longest. If your result is different, that is a real result. Talk about why: rain, traffic or a tree nearby can change the result.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they found, what they saw and why they think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me what the three lines of beans showed.  
  *Child mode:* Tell your grown-up what the three lines showed.
  - Ask: Why do you think the birds called most at that time? (good: To say "I am here".; To find their friends after the night.)
  - Note: Nobody can be sure what each call means. Good answers are 'Where are you?', 'I am here' or 'Danger'. Any answer that treats the call as a message is a good one.
  - Check: When do most birds call the most? → **Early morning**
  - Think: You may have heard one bird call and another answer. What might they have been saying to each other?

## Activities
### a1 · A Feather Up Close: unzip it and zip it back (10 min)
- **safety:** A feather found outside can carry germs. Use a craft feather, or ask a grown-up to wash a found feather in soapy water and dry it. Wash your hands afterwards.
- **record:** Tell your grown-up the three parts you found: the stiff middle, the soft strands and the fluffy bit. Or draw the feather and name its parts. A photo of your feather also works.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Give your child the feather. Ask them to touch the stiff middle and the soft strands, and the fluffy part at the bottom.  
  *Parent says:* This feather has three parts. Feel each one and tell me what it is like.  
  *Child mode:* Feel the feather. Find the stiff part, the soft part, the fluffy part.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Gently pull two strands apart to make a gap. 2. Ask your child to guess if the gap can be closed. 3. Stroke from the stem to the tip.  
  *Parent says:* I have made a gap. Do you think we can close it?  
  *Child mode:* Pull two strands apart to make a gap. Then stroke the feather gently from the stem to the tip.
  - Ask: What happened to the gap? (good: It closed up.; The strands stuck together again.)
  - Note: Each strand has tiny hooks along the side, too small to see without a strong magnifier. Stroking hooks them together again. Birds do this with their beaks every day. Some craft feathers are cut or glued and zip poorly. A pigeon or crow feather picked up from the ground works best, once a grown-up has washed it in soapy water and dried it.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the feather by the stem and blow gently on the fluffy part at the bottom.  
  *Parent says:* Blow on the fluffy part. Watch how it moves.  
  *Child mode:* Blow on the fluffy part. Feel how soft it is.
  - Ask: Why do birds have fluffy feathers? (good: To keep warm.; Like a blanket.)
  - Note: She is holding warm air close to her skin, to keep warm. The fluffed-up feathers trap the warm air, like the soft fluff you just blew on.
  - Think: A pigeon fluffs up her feathers on a cold morning. What is she doing, and why?
### a2 · Be a Sunbird: reach the juice at the bottom (10 min)

### a3 · Bird Café: what is good for birds, and what is not? (15 min, 2 days)

### c1 · Stitch a Cradle Like a Tailorbird (20 min)
- **problem:** Make a cradle from leaves or paper leaves that holds two chana 'eggs'. The eggs must not fall out when you tip the cradle a little. Use thread, not glue or tape.

- **constraints:** No glue and no tape.; The two chana must stay in the cradle when you tip it gently.; Use leaves, or leaf shapes cut from paper.
- **general:** A tailorbird pokes holes along the edges of a leaf with her sharp beak.; She pulls plant thread or spider silk through the holes, and ties knots so the thread holds.; Inside the cone of leaves, she builds a soft cup of grass and fluff.
- **creative:** Fold one big leaf into a cone and stitch only one side.; Stitch three small leaves into a bowl.; Line the cradle with cotton wool, like the soft cup inside a real nest.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child how a tailorbird sews leaves into a cradle with her beak and thread.

## Worksheets
### The World of Birds (core) (core, 10 questions)
1. (sort) Circle the birds. Cross out the animals who are not birds.  
   **Answer:** hen → Bird; bat → Not a bird; peacock → Bird; butterfly → Not a bird; ostrich → Bird; cat → Not a bird. *Why:* Hens, peacocks and ostriches have feathers, so they are birds. A bat and a cat have fur, and a butterfly is an insect.
2. (match) Draw a line from each beak to the food.  
   **Answer:** Short, thick beak (sparrow) → Seeds; Long, curved beak (sunbird) → Nectar in a flower; Long, sharp beak (kingfisher) → Fish. *Why:* A thick beak cracks seeds, a long curved beak reaches into flowers, and a long sharp beak grabs slippery fish.
3. (draw) Draw a bird. Colour the feathers. Show the beak, two wings and two legs.  
   **Answer:** A bird with feathers, a beak, two wings and two legs.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows all four parts every bird has: feathers, a beak, two wings and two legs.
4. (mcq) A bird sitting on her nest hears another bird's danger call. Why does she press down flat and stay still?  
   Options: To hide from the danger / She is sleepy / She is looking for food  
   **Answer:** To hide from the danger. *Why:* The danger call warns that a cat or other hunter is near. Pressing flat and staying still makes her hard to see.
5. (draw) In the experiment A Bean for Every Call, you dropped a bean in a cup for every bird call. Draw the three cups. Draw the beans from each cup in a line underneath.  
   **Answer:** Three lines of beans of different lengths, labelled morning, afternoon and evening.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the longest line under the time with the most calls, as the child found.
6. (truefalse) A bird uses a nest to keep the eggs and chicks safe.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Most birds build a nest when it is time to lay eggs, and leave it when the chicks have flown.
7. (label) Write what each feather does: fly or keep warm.  
   **Answer:** ['wing feather: fly', 'fluffy feather: keep warm']. *Why:* Stiff wing feathers push on the air. Fluffy feathers hold warm air next to the bird's skin.
8. (match) Draw a line from each bird to the nest.  
   **Answer:** Crow → A nest of sticks in a tree; Tailorbird → Two leaves stitched together; Pigeon → A few twigs on a ledge. *Why:* Crows pile up sticks, tailorbirds stitch leaves, and pigeons make a small, loose pile of twigs.
9. (write) A crow calls loudly and other crows fly to her. What might she be saying? Tell a grown-up, who writes your answer down.  
   **Answer:** She might be saying 'danger', or 'come here', or 'there is food'.. *Why:* A strong answer says the call is a message, and gives a reason from what the other crows did.
10. (perspective) Two friends see a baby bird hopping on the ground under a tree.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason, such as 'The second friend, because the parents are still feeding the chick.' Then it is fair to the first friend, who wants to keep the chick safe from cats.. *Why:* A chick hopping on the ground is often learning to fly, and the parents still feed the chick. Watching from far away is usually best. If the chick is hurt, a grown-up can call a bird rescue group.
### The World of Birds (stretch) (stretch, 10 questions)
1. (odd) Which of these is NOT a job that feathers do?  
   Options: Keep a bird warm / Help a bird fly / Help a bird see in the dark / Keep a bird dry  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Feathers help a bird fly, keep warm, keep dry and show colours. They do not help a bird see.
2. (draw) Draw a pigeon on a cold morning and a pigeon on a hot afternoon.  
   **Answer:** A round, fluffed-up pigeon for the cold morning, and a thin, sleek pigeon for the hot afternoon.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the fluffed-up feathers in the cold, because fluffing holds more warm air.
3. (mcq) Juice is at the bottom of a tall, thin bottle. Why can a straw reach it when a spoon cannot?  
   Options: The straw is long and thin / The straw is red / The straw is soft  
   **Answer:** The straw is long and thin. *Why:* A long, thin straw reaches deep into the bottle, as a sunbird's long, thin beak reaches deep into a flower.
4. (write) When you count bird calls, why is it better to drop a bean for each call than just to remember? Tell a grown-up, who writes your answer down.  
   **Answer:** Because you can count the beans afterwards and compare the cups. Remembering is hard.. *Why:* A strong answer says the beans keep a true count we can see and compare later.
5. (sort) Colour the good bird foods green and the bad ones red.  
   **Answer:** clean water → Good for birds; broken rice → Good for birds; salty chips → Not good for birds; a slice of banana → Good for birds; a sweet biscuit → Not good for birds; spicy namkeen → Not good for birds. *Why:* Water, grains and fruit are good. Salty, sugary and spicy foods can make birds ill.
6. (order) Put the story The Bulbuls in the Money Plant in order.  
   **Answer:** ['The bulbuls build a nest', 'Two eggs appear in the nest', 'The eggs hatch', 'The chicks fly away']. *Why:* Birds build the nest first, then lay eggs, then the eggs hatch, then the chicks grow feathers and fly.
7. (count) You drop beans for bird calls: 5 in the first minute and 3 in the next minute. How many calls did you hear in the two minutes?  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* 5 and 3 more make 8. Each bean is one call.
8. (write) A crow has a strong beak and eats almost anything. Why is that useful in a city? Tell a grown-up, who writes your answer down.  
   **Answer:** In a city a crow can find grain, scraps, insects and fruit, and her strong beak can eat all of them. She will not go hungry if one food is gone.. *Why:* A crow can eat many foods, so she can live where food changes. A strong beak is the tool that makes this work.
9. (truefalse) Today, only birds have feathers.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Today no other animal has feathers. Bats and insects fly without them.
10. (perspective) Two sisters talk about the pigeons on their balcony.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. For the older sister: the grain helps the birds. For the younger sister: too many pigeons push other birds away, and the droppings can make people ill. Then the child is fair to the other side.. *Why:* This is a real argument. Feeding helps some birds, but feeding a lot in one place can bring huge flocks of one kind. Water in summer helps every kind of bird.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) What does every bird have?  
   Options: Feathers / Fur / Teeth  
   **Answer:** Feathers. *Why:* Every bird has feathers. Birds have no teeth, and fur belongs to animals like cats and bats.
2. (mcq) A bat can fly. Is a bat a bird?  
   Options: Yes, because a bat flies / No, because a bat has no feathers / Only at night  
   **Answer:** No, because a bat has no feathers. *Why:* A bat has fur, not feathers. Flying does not make an animal a bird.
3. (mcq) What do soft, fluffy feathers do for a bird?  
   Options: Keep the bird warm / Help the bird see / Help the bird eat  
   **Answer:** Keep the bird warm. *Why:* Fluffy feathers hold warm air close to the skin, like a blanket.
4. (mcq) A sparrow has a short, thick beak. What does a sparrow mostly eat?  
   Options: Fish / Seeds and grains / Nectar from flowers  
   **Answer:** Seeds and grains. *Why:* A short, thick beak is strong enough to crack seeds and grains.
5. (mcq) Why does a bird build a nest?  
   Options: To keep the eggs and chicks safe / To store food for winter / To hide from the rain every night of the year  
   **Answer:** To keep the eggs and chicks safe. *Why:* A nest is a cradle for eggs and chicks. Most birds sleep on branches when they are not nesting.
6. (mcq) How does a tailorbird make a nest?  
   Options: She digs a hole in the ground / She stitches leaves together / She uses mud only  
   **Answer:** She stitches leaves together. *Why:* A tailorbird pokes holes in leaves with her beak and stitches them with plant thread or spider silk.
7. (mcq) You drop one bean into a cup each time you hear a bird call. What does one bean stand for?  
   Options: One bird / One bird call / One minute  
   **Answer:** One bird call. *Why:* One bean drops for every call. So more beans means more calls.
8. (mcq) A bulbul gives a fast, sharp call when a cat comes near. What does the call mean?  
   Options: Danger! / Good morning / I am hungry  
   **Answer:** Danger!. *Why:* A fast, sharp call when a cat comes near is an alarm call. Other birds hide when they hear it.
9. (mcq) Which is the best thing to give birds on a hot summer day?  
   Options: Salty chips / A shallow bowl of clean water / Sweet biscuits  
   **Answer:** A shallow bowl of clean water. *Why:* Birds need water to drink and bathe. In summer, city birds can find it hard to get water. Salty and sweet snacks can make them ill.
10. (mcq) Why should you change the water in a bird bowl every day?  
   Options: So it stays clean and mosquitoes cannot breed / So it stays cold / So the bowl looks shiny  
   **Answer:** So it stays clean and mosquitoes cannot breed. *Why:* Old water gets dirty, and mosquitoes can lay eggs in still water left for days.

## Open questions
- **Why do birds sing most at dawn?** Scientists have several ideas. Sound may carry far in the still morning air, or it may be too dark to find food, so birds sing instead. Nobody is sure.
- **What does a bird feel when singing?** Birds sense, choose and remember. But nobody knows what singing feels like to a bird from the inside.
- **How much do birds understand of each other's calls?** We know birds react to some calls, even calls of other kinds of birds. We do not yet know how much they understand.
