# Biomimicry: copying how living things solve their problems
`sci.making-things.biomimicry.L3`

**Summary.** Every plant and animal has problems to solve in its own life. A burdock plant must send its seeds far away, so its burrs carry hooks that catch on animal fur. A lotus leaf must stay clean in a muddy pond, so tiny waxy bumps make rain roll off and take the dirt with it. A kingfisher must dive without a splash that warns the fish, so its beak widens very slowly. These designs got better over many generations. People who studied them closely copied them, and made Velcro, self-cleaning paint and a quieter bullet train. Copying how a living thing solves a problem is called biomimicry.

**Intro.** *Find a shoe or a bag with a Velcro strap. Look closely at its two sides. One side has tiny stiff hooks. The other side has soft loops. A plant was using hooks like these long before anyone made Velcro. Why would a plant need hooks?* A burdock plant cannot walk. If its seeds just dropped to the ground, they would grow right under the parent plant. There they would have to share its light, water and soil. So the burdock's seed heads, called burrs, are covered in stiff spines with tiny hooks at the tips. When a fox, a cow or a dog brushes past, the hooks catch in its fur. The animal carries the burr away, and the seeds fall off in new ground. Over many generations, burdock plants whose hooks gripped better spread further, so strong hooks became common. In 1941, a Swiss engineer called George de Mestral came home from a walk in the mountains with burrs all over his clothes and his dog. He looked at one under a microscope. He saw hundreds of tiny hooks caught in loops of fur and thread. He copied exactly that: one strip of hooks and one strip of loops. That became Velcro.

## Terms
- **Biomimicry**: Learning how a plant or an animal solves a problem in its own life, and using the same idea to solve a human problem. 'Bio' means life. 'Mimic' means copy. *e.g.* Velcro copies the hooks that a burdock burr uses to catch on animal fur.
- **Invention**: A new thing, or a new way of doing something, that somebody thought up. *e.g.* The bicycle, the pressure cooker and Velcro are inventions.
- **Engineer**: A person who designs and builds things that solve problems, and tests them to see if they work. *e.g.* Engineers design bridges, trains, phones and water pumps.
- **Design**: A plan for making something, drawn or written before you build it. *e.g.* A drawing of your invention with labels showing what each part does.
- **Prototype**: A first, rough model of an invention, made to test if the idea works. *e.g.* A cardboard and paperclip fastener you make before a real one.
- **Fair test**: A test where you change only one thing and keep everything else the same. Then you know what caused the difference. *e.g.* Drop two dough shapes from the same height into the same bucket. Only the shape changes.
- **Burr**: The seed head of some plants, covered in stiff spines with tiny hooks. The hooks catch in animal fur, so the animal carries the seeds to new ground. *e.g.* After a walk through dry weeds, your socks can be full of prickly seeds that hold on in the same way.
- **Seed dispersal**: The ways plants send their seeds away from the parent plant. Seeds that land far away have more light, water and space to grow. *e.g.* Burrs ride on animal fur, dandelion seeds fly on the wind, and coconuts float across the sea.
- **Hook and loop**: A way of joining two things. One side has tiny stiff hooks. The other side has tiny soft loops. The hooks catch in the loops. *e.g.* Velcro on school shoes and bags is hook and loop.
- **Lotus effect**: The way some leaves keep themselves clean. Tiny waxy bumps make water roll off in balls, and the balls carry the dirt away. *e.g.* After rain, lotus and arbi (colocasia) leaves have silver balls of water rolling on them, and look spotless.
- **Wax**: A smooth, slippery material that water does not soak into. Many leaves make a thin layer of it on their surface. *e.g.* Candle wax, and the shiny coat on a cabbage leaf.
- **Water-repelling**: When a surface makes water stay in round drops instead of spreading or soaking in. *e.g.* A raincoat is water-repelling. A paper towel is not. It soaks water up.
- **Streamlined**: Shaped so that air or water can flow smoothly around it. A streamlined thing is usually pointed at the front and smooth all over. *e.g.* Fish, dolphins, kingfishers and racing cyclists' helmets are streamlined.
- **Drag**: The push of air or water against something moving through it. Drag slows things down. *e.g.* Put your hand out of a moving bus window. The air pushing it back is drag.
- **Tunnel boom**: A loud bang when a fast train comes out of a tunnel. The train squeezes the air ahead of it inside the tunnel, and the squeezed air bursts out of the far end. *e.g.* Houses near tunnels in Japan were shaken by tunnel booms from early bullet trains.
- **Gecko**: A small lizard that can run up walls and walk upside down on ceilings. The house gecko, the chhipkali, lives in many Indian homes. *e.g.* A gecko waiting for insects near the tube light.
- **Setae**: The tiny hairs on a gecko's toes. Each one splits into hundreds of even tinier tips. Say it 'SEE-tee'. *e.g.* One gecko has millions of setae on its toes.
- **Termite mound**: A tall tower of mud that termites build. Millions of termites live in tunnels under and inside it. *e.g.* In parts of south India, termite mounds taller than a child stand in fields and forests.
- **Insulation**: A thick layer that slows down heat getting in or out. *e.g.* A thick blanket keeps you warm. A thick mud wall keeps a hut cool in summer.
- **Evolution**: The slow change of living things over many, many generations. Young ones differ a little from their parents. Those whose differences help them live and have young pass those features on, so the features spread. *e.g.* Over many generations, burdock burrs with better hooks were carried further, so burdock plants today have strong hooks.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that everything is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* A leaf, a drop of water and your hand are all made of atoms.
- **Patent**: An official paper from the government. It says an invention belongs to its inventor for some years, so nobody else can copy it and sell it. *e.g.* George de Mestral applied for a patent for Velcro in 1951.

## Explanations
### What biomimicry is · *defined*
**Definition:** Biomimicry means studying how a plant or an animal solves a problem in its own life, and using the same idea to solve a human problem. People copy the idea, not the whole living thing.

Every living thing has problems in its own life. A plant must spread its seeds and keep its leaves clean. An animal must catch food, grip a branch or keep its home cool. Over many generations, living things have worked out designs that solve these problems. A person who wants to copy one must first understand it. What problem does the living thing have? How exactly does its design solve it? Only then can the same idea be built from materials people can make. An American scientist, Otto Schmitt, used the word 'biomimetics' for this in the 1950s. In 1997, an American nature writer, Janine Benyus, wrote a book called 'Biomimicry'. It made the word famous around the world.
- e.g. A burdock burr's hooks carry its seeds away on animal fur. Velcro copies them to fasten shoes.
- e.g. A kingfisher's beak lets it dive without warning the fish. A bullet train's nose copies it to leave tunnels quietly.
- e.g. A lotus leaf's waxy bumps keep it clean in a muddy pond. Self-cleaning paint copies them.
### Why a burdock burr has hooks, and how Velcro copied them · *defined*
**Definition:** A burdock burr is covered in stiff spines with tiny hooks. The hooks catch in animal fur, so animals carry the seeds to new ground. Velcro copies this: one strip has hooks and the other has loops.

A burdock plant stays rooted in one place all its life. Its seeds need to grow somewhere else. Under the parent plant, they would have to share light, water and soil with it, and with each other. The burdock's answer is the burr, a ball of seeds covered in hooked spines. When an animal brushes past, the hooks catch in its fur. The burr may ride a long way before it is scratched off or falls. Then its seeds can grow in new ground, with less crowding. Burdock plants whose hooks gripped well spread further, so over many generations strong hooks became the rule. In 1941, George de Mestral, a Swiss engineer, looked at burrs from his dog's fur under a microscope. He saw the hooks caught in loops of fur. He wanted two strips of cloth that would join in the same way. Making it work took about ten years. Cotton loops wore out fast, but nylon, a strong plastic thread, worked well. He applied for a patent in 1951. He named his fastener Velcro, from two French words: 'velours', meaning velvet, and 'crochet', meaning hook.
- e.g. Velcro straps on school shoes, bags and blood pressure cuffs.
- e.g. Astronauts used Velcro to stop things floating away inside their spacecraft.
- e.g. A burr catches on a woollen sock but slides off a smooth raincoat, because the raincoat has no loops for its hooks.
### Why a lotus leaf stays clean · *defined*
**Definition:** A lotus leaf is covered in tiny bumps, and the bumps are coated in even tinier bits of wax. Water cannot spread on it, so it makes balls that roll off and carry dirt away. Scientists call this the lotus effect.

A lotus grows in muddy ponds, and its big round leaves lie open to the sky. Dust, splashes of mud and the spores of fungi land on them all the time. Dirt on a leaf blocks the sunlight it needs to make its food. Spores and germs left on a leaf can make it sick. Yet lotus leaves look clean. In the 1970s, a German plant scientist called Wilhelm Barthlott looked at leaves with a very powerful microscope, called a scanning electron microscope. The lotus leaf was not smooth at all. It was covered in tiny bumps, and the bumps were coated with even tinier bits of wax. A water drop rests only on the tips of the bumps, with air underneath. So it stays round and rolls off at the smallest tilt. Dirt grips the water better than it grips the waxy tips, so each rolling drop picks up dirt and carries it away. Every shower of rain washes the leaf. Over many generations, lotus plants whose leaves stayed cleaner grew better and left more seeds. Barthlott and Christoph Neinhuis described the lotus effect in 1997. In the late 1990s, a paint company made an outside wall paint with the same kind of tiny bumps, so that rain washes the wall clean.
- e.g. Arbi (colocasia) leaves, nasturtium leaves and cabbage leaves show the same effect.
- e.g. Self-cleaning paint, glass and cloth copy the lotus effect.
- e.g. Touching a lotus leaf rubs off its wax. Then that spot gets wet like an ordinary leaf.
### Deep down: why water makes balls · *defined*
**Definition:** Water is made of tiny particles called molecules. Water molecules pull on each other strongly. On a waxy, bumpy surface, they pull on each other far more than on the surface. So the water pulls itself into a ball.

Zoom into a drop of water, far past anything a microscope can show. It is made of countless water molecules, each one built from three atoms. Every molecule tugs on the ones around it. At the edge of a drop, all that tugging pulls the water into a round shape, like a stretched skin. Scientists call this surface tension. On paper, the paper pulls the water too, so it spreads and soaks in. Wax hardly pulls on water at all. On the lotus, the drop also rests mostly on air between the bumps. Air does not pull the water either. So the water's own pull wins, and the drop becomes almost a perfect ball. The pull between molecules, and between wax and water, is an electric pull between their atoms. It is the same kind of force that gives us friction and holds a gecko on a wall.
- e.g. A drop on a candle stays round. A drop on a tissue spreads and soaks in.
- e.g. Water striders, small insects, stand on a pond because surface tension holds them up.
### The kingfisher and the bullet train · *defined*
**Definition:** A kingfisher must dive from air into water fast, without a big splash that warns the fish. Its long beak widens very slowly, so it parts the water gently. Engineers copied that shape for the nose of a Japanese bullet train, so it left tunnels without a loud boom.

A kingfisher lives by catching small fish. It sits still on a branch above the water, watching. When it sees a fish, it dives. Water is about 800 times heavier than the same amount of air, so it is hard to push aside. A blunt head would slap the water, slow the bird down and send out ripples that warn the fish. The kingfisher's beak is long and narrow, and it widens slowly towards the head. So it pushes the water aside a little at a time, and the bird slips in with only a small splash. Over many generations, kingfishers that dived more smoothly caught more fish and raised more chicks. Japan's bullet trains, called Shinkansen, had a problem like this. When a fast train rushed into a tunnel, it squeezed the air ahead of it. The squeezed air burst out of the far end with a boom that shook nearby houses. Eiji Nakatsu, an engineer and a birdwatcher, saw that the train and the kingfisher had the same problem. Each moves fast into a place where the water or air cannot easily get out of the way. His team tested many nose shapes. The shape most like a kingfisher's beak worked best. The 500 Series train, which began running in 1997, had a long beak-shaped nose. It was quieter in tunnels, and it used less electricity. Nakatsu has said that he also learned from owl feathers, to make the arm on the train's roof quieter.
- e.g. A kingfisher dives into a pond and leaves only a small ring on the water.
- e.g. A pointed dough shape dropped into a bucket splashes far less than a flat one.
- e.g. India's first bullet train line, between Mumbai and Ahmedabad, is being built using Japanese Shinkansen technology.
### The same shape, again and again · *defined*
**Definition:** Animals that are not close relatives often end up with the same shape, because they face the same problem. Scientists call this convergent evolution. Engineers who test shapes often reach the same answer too.

Sharks are fish. Dolphins are mammals, like us. Ichthyosaurs were reptiles that lived in the sea in the time of the dinosaurs. They are not close relatives at all. Yet all three have the same shape: a pointed nose, a smooth body and a strong tail. Why? Because water pushes back in the same way on every swimmer. A fast swimmer with a blunt nose wastes its energy pushing water. Over millions of years, the fast, pointed ones caught more food and left more young. Engineers testing train noses in the 1990s did not wait millions of years. They tested shapes in days. But they arrived at the same answer as the kingfisher. Nature and engineers are solving the same problems, under the same rules.
- e.g. Penguins, seals and fish all have smooth, pointed bodies for swimming.
- e.g. Bats, birds and insects all grew wings, in three different ways.
- e.g. Racing cyclists and diving birds both tuck into a pointed shape.
### How a gecko sticks to a wall · *debated*
A house gecko hunts insects on walls, windows and ceilings, often near a light at night. It must run up smooth glass, hang upside down and not fall. Its feet have no glue and no suckers. Each toe has rows of tiny hairs called setae. Each hair splits into hundreds of even tinier tips. The tips are so small and bendy that they get extremely close to the wall, even into its tiny dips. When atoms get that close, they pull on each other very slightly. One tip's pull is tiny. But a gecko has millions of hairs and hundreds of millions of tips. All those tiny pulls add up. Geckos whose feet gripped better caught more insects and escaped more snakes, so over many generations their feet became the grippers we see today. Scientists agree on this much. They argue about one part: does water help?
- e.g. A house gecko walks upside down on the ceiling near the tube light.
- e.g. Geckos find it hard to grip Teflon, the very slippery coating on non-stick pans.
- **Kellar Autumn and his team, in the USA, in 2000 and 2002:** They measured the pull of a single gecko hair for the first time. Then they tested geckos on surfaces that water sticks to and surfaces water rolls off. Geckos gripped both about the same. So they said the grip comes from the pull between atoms, called van der Waals forces, not from water.
- **Gerrit Huber and others, in Germany, in 2005:** They found single gecko hair tips grip harder when the air is damp. A very thin film of water from the air may help the tips hold on. So water helps too.
- **Many scientists today:** The pull between atoms does most of the work. A thin film of water can add to it on damp days. How much each matters is still being studied.
### How a gecko lets go, and the inventions it inspired · *defined*
**Definition:** A gecko lets go by peeling its toes up from the tip, like peeling sticky tape. Engineers have copied gecko hairs to make dry tape and robot grippers that need no glue.

Holding on is only half of the gecko's problem. A gecko running up a wall must stick and unstick each foot many times every second. If you pull tape straight off a wall, it holds hard. If you peel it from one corner, it comes off easily. Gecko toes peel from the tip, a few hairs at a time. So letting go takes almost no effort. Gecko feet also stay clean. Dirt falls off the tiny tips as the gecko walks. In 2003, a team in Manchester, England, led by Andre Geim made a small patch of 'gecko tape' from plastic hairs. At Stanford University in the USA, engineers built a climbing robot called Stickybot in 2006. NASA engineers have built gecko-style grippers to grab things in space, where glue and suckers do not work well.
- e.g. Peel sticky tape from a corner and it comes away easily. Pull it straight and it holds.
- e.g. Gecko-style grippers can pick up a smooth glass plate and let it go with no mark left behind.
### How termite mounds stay cool · *debated*
Termites are small insects that live in huge families, sometimes of millions. Some kinds build mounds taller than a grown-up. Deep inside, many kinds grow a fungus garden. The fungus breaks down the dry plants the termites bring in, and turns them into food the termites can eat. The garden and the termites need air that stays fresh, damp and not too hot. Millions of termites and their fungus breathe out a lot of carbon dioxide, which must get out. Outside, the sun may be baking. Termites whose mounds kept the nest fresh and steady raised more young, so over very many generations their way of building spread. Two things are agreed. First, the thick earth walls slow down the heat, so the inside changes temperature much less than the outside. Second, air moves through tiny holes in the walls. How the air is moved is the part scientists have changed their minds about.
- e.g. In parts of south India, the termite Odontotermes obesus builds mounds taller than a child.
- e.g. Inside a mound on a hot afternoon, it is far cooler than the ground in the sun.
- **The old idea, from Martin Lüscher, a Swiss scientist, in 1961:** The termites' own warmth heats the air, which rises up a central chimney and draws fresh air in, like a fire drawing air up a chimney.
- **Hunter King, Samuel Ocko and L. Mahadevan, scientists at Harvard University, in 2015:** They put tiny sensors into real mounds near Bengaluru. They found the mound breathes with the day and night. In the day, the thin outer walls warm faster than the middle, and air moves one way. At night, the walls cool faster, and air moves the other way.
- **J. Scott Turner, an American scientist who studied African mounds:** Wind gusting over the mound also pushes and pulls the air inside. Different kinds of mound may work in different ways. The full story is still being worked out.
### Buildings that copy termites · *defined*
**Definition:** Some architects design buildings with thick walls, and air channels that let cool night air flush through. These ideas were inspired by termite mounds. They keep buildings cooler with less air conditioning.

In 1996, the Eastgate Centre opened in Harare, the capital city of Zimbabwe in Africa. Its architect, Mick Pearce, studied termite mounds. The building has thick concrete walls and floors that soak up heat slowly in the day. At night, fans pull cool outside air through hollow spaces in the floors, and warm air leaves through chimneys on the roof. Its designers reported that it uses much less energy for cooling than similar buildings. The building was based on the old chimney idea of how mounds work. Scientists later found mounds work differently. The building still works well. So a copy can be useful even when the original is not fully understood. In India, old houses with thick mud walls, small windows and courtyards also stay cool in the heat.
- e.g. Thick mud walls of village houses keep rooms cool in summer.
- e.g. A matka, an earthen pot, keeps water cool because water seeps through its clay and evaporates from the outside, and that carries heat away.
### Copy how it works, not how it looks · *defined*
**Definition:** Good biomimicry copies how something works, not just how it looks. A machine that only looks like an animal usually does not work like one.

Around the year 1500, Leonardo da Vinci, the Italian artist and inventor, drew flying machines with flapping wings like a bird's. None of them flew. Copying the look of flapping was not enough. Four hundred years later, people studied how birds really fly. Otto Lilienthal, a German engineer, studied storks and wrote a book about bird flight in 1889. He made gliders that really flew. Wilbur and Orville Wright, two brothers in the USA, watched birds twist the tips of their wings to balance. They copied that twist, not the flapping. In 1903 they made the first powered aeroplane flight. Here in India, the Lotus Temple in Delhi copies the look of a lotus flower. It is beautiful, but its walls do not clean themselves. Self-cleaning paint looks nothing like a lotus, but it copies the lotus leaf's design.
- e.g. The bullet train's nose does not have feathers or eyes. It copies only the beak's slow widening.
- e.g. Velcro does not look like a burr. It copies only the hooks.
### Where nature's designs come from · *defined*
**Definition:** Nature's designs come about through evolution. Over many generations, living things whose features solve their problems better leave more young. So those features spread.

The burdock's hooks were not made in one go. They got better along a very long line of burdock plants. Young plants differ a little from their parents, the way you differ from yours. Long ago, some burdock plants had burrs that gripped fur slightly better. Their burrs were carried further, so more of their seeds grew. Their young had good hooks too, because living things pass their features on. Over many generations, strong hooks became common. Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace explained this in 1858. Scientists call it evolution by natural selection. Life on Earth began about 3.8 billion years ago. Every plant and animal alive today comes from a long line of living things that solved the problems of their lives well enough to have young. Janine Benyus says nature has been testing its designs for 3.8 billion years. Engineers are learning to read the results.
- e.g. Kingfishers whose beaks made less splash caught more fish, and raised more chicks.
- e.g. Geckos that gripped better escaped from snakes, and lived to lay eggs.
### Is nature the best engineer? · *debated*
People who love biomimicry often say nature's designs are the best. Others say that is not quite right. Both sides have good points.
- e.g. Your eye has a blind spot, where the nerve leaves it. An octopus eye is built the other way round and has no blind spot.
- e.g. Bacteria have a tiny spinning motor to swim, but no animal grew a wheel to roll on.
- **Janine Benyus and many people who work in biomimicry:** Nature has tested its designs for billions of years. Nature's designs are also made from water, sunlight and simple materials, at ordinary temperatures, and they rot away safely when they are finished. We should learn from that.
- **Many biologists who study evolution:** Evolution makes things good enough to survive, not perfect. It can only change what is already there, a little at a time. So living things carry old mistakes, like the blind spot in your eye.
- **Many engineers:** People have made things nature never made: wheels that roll, metal tools, fire for cooking, rockets. The best ideas often mix nature's designs with human ones.
### Who owns nature's ideas? · *debated*
An inventor who copies a burr can get a patent. A patent is an official paper that says nobody else may copy and sell the invention for some years. But nobody pays the burr. And sometimes the 'new' idea was known by people for hundreds of years. In 1995, two scientists in the USA got a patent for using turmeric to heal wounds. Families in India had used it for centuries. India's science council, the CSIR, showed the judges very old Indian books that described it. In 1997, the patent was cancelled.
- e.g. Haldi on a cut is an old Indian home remedy.
- e.g. Jagadish Chandra Bose, the Indian scientist, did not want to make money from his radio research, and shared it freely. Friends persuaded him to take just one patent, in the USA in 1904.
- **Inventors and many governments:** Patents reward people for years of hard work. George de Mestral spent about ten years making Velcro work. Without patents, fewer people would try.
- **Many people in India and other countries:** Knowledge that communities have had for centuries should not be taken and owned by one company. In 2001, India started a huge library of traditional knowledge, so it cannot be patented by others.
- **Some scientists, like J.C. Bose:** Ideas copied from nature, and ideas that help people, should be free for everyone.
### What engineers are copying now · *idea*
Biomimicry is still young. Scientists are trying many new ideas, and most are not yet in our homes. The pitcher plant grows where the soil has little food in it for plants. It gets extra food by catching insects. Pitcher plants whose rims trapped more insects grew better and made more seeds, so over many generations the slippery rim spread. The rim of its pitcher is so slippery when wet that insects slide in and cannot climb out. India's own pitcher plant grows in the hills of Meghalaya. In 2011, a team led by Joanna Aizenberg at Harvard University made a coating that copies the slippery rim. Almost nothing sticks to it, not even oil. In the Namib Desert in Africa, almost no rain falls, but fog blows in from the sea. A beetle there stands with its back to the foggy wind. Water from the fog gathers on bumps on its back and runs down to its mouth. Over many generations, beetles that caught more fog water survived the dry times better. Scientists described it in 2001, and others are testing fog-catching surfaces like it. Some scientists are trying to build an 'artificial leaf' that makes fuel from sunlight and water, as a plant makes food. Nobody yet knows which of these will work well enough to use every day.
- e.g. A ketchup or honey bottle that empties completely, with nothing stuck to the sides.
- e.g. Nets that catch drinking water from fog on dry hills.

## Mostly agreed
- Many useful inventions copy designs that plants and animals use to solve their own problems. *How we know:* George de Mestral told how burrs from his walk gave him the idea for Velcro. Eiji Nakatsu has described how the kingfisher shaped the 500 Series train nose.
- Lotus leaves stay clean because they are covered in tiny waxy bumps. *How we know:* Powerful microscopes show the bumps and the wax. You can see drops roll off and carry dust away. If you rub the wax off, that spot gets wet.
- Geckos stick to walls without glue or suckers, using millions of tiny hairs. *How we know:* Gecko feet leave no glue behind. Scientists have measured the pull of a single hair. Geckos grip badly on Teflon, a coating that atoms hardly pull on.
- A pointed, streamlined shape moves through air and water with less drag than a blunt one. *How we know:* Engineers measure this with models in water tanks and wind tunnels. You can see it at home: a pointed dough shape splashes far less than a flat one.
- Nature's designs come from evolution over very long times. *How we know:* Fossils, the stony remains of ancient life, show living things slowly changing. DNA, the instructions inside every cell, shows how living things are related.

## How we found out
**Martin Lüscher (the old idea), and Hunter King, Samuel Ocko and L. Mahadevan (the measurements near Bengaluru), 1961, and 2015.** For a long time, people thought they knew how termite mounds stay fresh inside. In 1961, a Swiss scientist, Martin Lüscher, wrote about mounds in Africa. He said the warmth of millions of termites and their fungus garden heats the air in the middle. The warm air rises up a central chimney, like smoke, and pulls fresh air in from below. Books repeated this for decades, and an architect copied the idea for a building in Zimbabwe. But nobody had measured the air inside a living mound through a whole day and night. In 2015, Hunter King, Samuel Ocko and L. Mahadevan of Harvard University did. They put tiny sensors into mounds of the termite Odontotermes obesus near Bengaluru. By day, the sun warmed the thin outer ridges of the mound faster than its thick middle, and the air moved one way. At night, the outer ridges cooled first, and the air moved the other way. The termites' own warmth was not what drove the air. The mound breathes with the day and the night.  
*What people believed before:* The termites' own warmth heats the air, which rises up a chimney and pulls fresh air in. Sensors in real mounds near Bengaluru showed that the sun's warming by day and the cooling at night move the air instead.

## Challenging perspectives
- **NASA, the American space agency, invented Velcro.** (A popular story, often heard in schools; *rejected*)  
  For: Astronauts used Velcro in the 1960s, in the Apollo spacecraft that went to the Moon. It held their pens and food packets in place, so they did not float away.  
  Against: George de Mestral invented Velcro. He applied for his patent in 1951, years before NASA started using it. NASA made Velcro famous, but did not invent it.
- **The smoother and shinier a surface, the cleaner it stays.** (A common belief. Most of us think this without noticing.; *rejected*)  
  For: Smooth glass and polished steel are easy to wipe clean.  
  Against: The lotus leaf stays clean because it is bumpy, not smooth. On a smooth surface, water drops stick and leave dirty marks when they dry. On the bumpy, waxy lotus, drops roll off and take the dirt with them.
- **To copy nature, make your machine look like the animal.** (Many early inventors, including some who tried to fly; *rejected*)  
  For: Birds fly by flapping, so a flying machine should flap too.  
  Against: Flapping machines did not fly. The Wright brothers copied a design instead: birds twist their wingtips to balance. Good biomimicry copies how a thing works, not what it looks like.
- **The Eastgate Centre in Zimbabwe cools itself exactly the way a termite mound does.** (Many books and websites about biomimicry; *debated*)  
  For: Its architect, Mick Pearce, studied termite mounds. The building has thick walls, air channels and chimneys, and uses little energy for cooling.  
  Against: It was based on the old chimney idea of how mounds work. Scientists later found real mounds breathe with day and night, and with the wind. The building also uses fans. It was inspired by termites, but it does not work quite like a mound.
- **A gecko sticks with sticky glue or tiny suction cups.** (A common guess, and an idea some scientists once had; *rejected*)  
  For: Some animals, like octopuses, really do use suckers. And sticky things usually have glue.  
  Against: Gecko feet leave no glue. Experiments showed geckos still cling when the air is pumped away, so it is not suction. The grip comes from hundreds of millions of tiny hair tips getting extremely close to the wall.
- **A car shaped like a boxfish proved that fish shapes are the best for cars.** (The company Mercedes-Benz, with its boxfish concept car in 2005; *debated*)  
  For: The boxfish is a small reef fish that looks like a box. The car copied its shape, and in tests it slipped through the air very easily.  
  Against: Later, scientists studied real boxfish. They found the boxfish's shape helps it turn quickly, more than it helps it swim straight with little drag. The car never went on sale. Copying a living thing needs a careful look at what its shape is really for.

## Roots
We have been learning how burdock plants, lotus leaves, kingfishers, geckos and termites solve problems in their own lives, and how people copied them. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** On the arbi leaf, your drops rolled into balls and carried the dust away. The leaf needs this, because dirt would block its sunlight and germs could make it sick. Plants like it found this answer over many generations, long before people made self-cleaning paint. So the first inventors on Earth were living things solving their own problems.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of inventing as something only people do, in workshops and labs. But the arbi leaf, the burdock and the kingfisher each solved a problem in their own lives long before any person copied them.  
*Evidence:* Drops roll off an arbi leaf and take the dust with them, while paper soaks them up. Self-cleaning wall paint, first sold in the late 1990s, copies the same tiny waxy bumps.

- *who am i:* Your hands and your brain were shaped over many generations too. When you invent, you do what living things have always done: solve the problems of your life.
- *what is life:* Every living thing is solving problems all the time: spreading its seeds, keeping clean, catching food, keeping cool.
- *what is this world:* Water, air and heat follow the same rules for every living thing and every machine. That is why a bird's answer can work for a train.

The burdock, the lotus and the kingfisher each solved a different problem. Yet they all worked with the same few rules: water pulling on itself more than on wax, water pushing back on a beak, a hook holding in a loop. From one set of rules for the whole world came countless different answers, one in each kind of living thing. A person who copies a burr is one living thing learning from another, and the idea passes from plant to person.

- **Bhagavad Gita 5.10:** A person who works without clinging, offering the work to something greater, is not stained by wrong, just as a lotus leaf is not wetted by water. (The Gita used the lotus leaf as its picture long before microscopes showed why water rolls off it. The leaf lives on the pond, yet the water does not wet it, and the mud does not stay on it.)
- **Tao Te Ching 25:** People follow the ways of the Earth. The Earth follows the ways of Heaven. Heaven follows the Tao, the Way. The Way follows what is natural. (Biomimicry is people learning from the ways of the Earth, quite literally: an engineer watching a kingfisher to learn how to build a train.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988), on 'interbeing':** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you can see the cloud, the rain and the tree inside it. Nothing exists all by itself. (Look deeply at a Velcro strap and you can see the burdock plant, the dog that carried its burrs, and the engineer who looked closely. The invention is made of all of them.)
- Science (settled): Life on Earth has been changing for about 3.8 billion years. Every living design today comes from a long line of living things that survived. This is evolution.
- Science (settled): Animals that are not related often evolve the same shape when they face the same problem. Scientists call this convergent evolution.
- Other view, Many biologists and engineers: Many biologists and engineers say evolution has no plan and no goal. In their view, a burr did not invent anything: seeds with better hooks simply travelled further. People plan, imagine and choose, so human invention is something new and different. That is an honest view.

## Story: The Quietest Splash
*Eiji Nakatsu, the tunnel boom, his birdwatching, the kingfisher idea, the owl feathers and the 500 Series train of 1997 are real. The kingfisher who tells the story, Mei, her baby brother and her letters are made up.*

I am a kingfisher. I am the size of a sparrow. My back is the blue of a gas flame, and my front is the orange of a ripe mango. My kind lives by slow rivers and ponds in many countries. We live in England, all across India, and in Japan, where I live. This is the story of my river, of the hole in the mountain beside it, and of a man who sat very still.

My river runs past rice fields and a bamboo grove, and then under a mountain. The people dug a long hole through the mountain. They call it a tunnel. Through it run the white trains, some of the fastest trains in the world. People call them bullet trains.

I did not mind the trains going in. They went in with a rush and a hiss. What I minded was the other end. A moment after a train went in, the far side of the mountain coughed. BOOM. It was a sound like a giant slamming a door. The herons in the rice fields jumped into the air. The windows of the houses rattled. And in the house by the bamboo, a baby began to cry. He cried every time a train went through, from morning until night.

The baby's sister was called Mei. She was nine. She often sat on the riverbank with a notebook, drawing me. She was not very good at sitting still, so I usually flew off before she finished. But I liked her. She wrote letters too. I saw her post them in the red box by the road. Later I heard her mother say that Mei had written to the train company. 'Please make your train quieter,' she had written. 'My brother cannot sleep.'

I knew why the mountain boomed. Birds understand air. When a train rushed into the tunnel, it pushed the air in front of it, like a hand pushing water along a narrow drain. The air had nowhere to go. It squeezed tighter and tighter, and it raced ahead of the train. At the far end, it burst out into the open. BOOM. The faster the trains went, the louder the boom became. And every year the trains went faster.

One spring a man began to come to my river. He came on his days off. He had binoculars, a notebook and a flask of tea. He sat on a rock under the willow for hours and hours, and he did not move. Most people walk along a river talking and pointing, and every bird flies away. This man watched. People who watch birds properly learn to be part of the riverbank.

So I fished as usual. I sat on my branch above the water, as still as he was. I saw a silver flick below. I dropped.

This is how I fish. I fall from the thin, soft air into the thick, heavy water, as fast as I can. Water is hard to push. If I hit it flat, it would slap me like a wall, and the fish would see the splash and dart away. But my beak is long and straight and sharp. From its tip, it gets wider very slowly, and then my head gets wider slowly, and then my body. So I do not push the water all at once. I push it aside a little at a time, from the tip backwards. I went in with a tiny sound, ploop. I left a ring on the water no bigger than a coin. I came out with a fish.

The man stood up on his rock. He nearly dropped his tea. He began to write in his notebook very fast. I heard him say something to himself. 'From thin air into thick water,' he said. 'From open air into a tight tunnel. It is the same problem.'

I did not understand his words then. I only noticed he was happy. That night, an owl hunted over the rice fields. Owls fly with almost no sound, because the edges of their feathers are soft and fringed. The man was there in the dark too, listening to nothing.

Then he stopped coming for a long time. The trains still boomed. Mei's brother still cried. Mei stopped writing letters and started drawing trains instead. Her trains had long, pointed faces.

Three summers went by. I raised two families of chicks in my burrow in the riverbank. I taught them to sit still, to watch, and to drop.

Then one morning a new train came. It was grey and blue, and it had the longest nose I had ever seen. The nose did not end in a round face like the old trains. It stretched out long and thin, and it widened very slowly, all the way back to the windows. It looked like my beak. It slid into the tunnel with a long, soft hiss.

I waited on my branch for the far side of the mountain to cough. The herons waited. The house by the bamboo waited.

Nothing came. Only a hush, like wind in the bamboo.

The door of the house opened, and Mei came out with her baby brother in her arms. He was fast asleep. She stood looking at the mountain for a long time. Then she looked at the river, at my branch, at me.

Later that day she sat on the bank and drew in her notebook again. She drew the new train. Next to it, she drew me, diving. She was careful to make the beak and the nose exactly the same shape. For once, I sat still until she had finished.

People tell this story now in many countries. They say that a train copied a kingfisher. I am not sure it is quite like that. The water taught my family its lesson over millions of years. The air taught the man the same lesson in a few. We were both listening to the same teacher. I just heard it first.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the train's boom and the kingfisher's splash are the same problem. A fast thing pushes into something that cannot easily get out of its way: water for the bird, the air inside a tunnel for the train. Hitting it with a blunt front shoves it all at once, which makes a slap or a boom. A long, slowly widening front pushes it aside a little at a time. The engineer did not copy the bird's look. He copied how the beak works. The story also quietly asks who teaches whom, and whether 'natural' and 'man-made' are really separate.

- Ask: Why did the tunnel boom when the old trains came out?
- Ask: How does the kingfisher dive without a big splash? What is special about the shape of its beak?
- Ask: The man said, 'It is the same problem.' What did he mean?
- Ask: Why do you think the man sat so still for so long?
- Ask: At the end, the kingfisher says they were both listening to the same teacher. Who or what was the teacher?

### Atoms, Evolution and Big Arguments (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)

   **Answer:** Water molecules pull on each other more strongly than the wax pulls on them. *Why:* Water molecules tug on each other. Wax hardly pulls on water at all. 2. (mcq) The pull between a gecko's hair tips and a wall is called a van der Waals force. What kind of force is it, deep down?  
   Options: An electric pull between atoms that are very close / Gravity / Suction / Magnetism  
   **Answer:** An electric pull between atoms that are very close. *Why:* When atoms get extremely close, their electric charges shift a little, and they pull on each other weakly. Picking 'gravity' is a common guess, but gravity between a gecko and a wall is far too weak. Experiments ruled out suction.

   Options: Test it and a plain version on the same slope, with the same weight / Test your invention on a rough floor, and the plain version on a smooth floor / Test only your invention  
   **Answer:** Test it and a plain version on the same slope, with the same weight. *Why:* In a fair test, only one thing changes. If the floors are different, you can't tell whether the invention or the floor made the difference. Testing only your invention gives nothing to compare it with.
14. (mcq) Look at the made-up splash results. Which shape splashed least?  
   Options: Pointed beak / Flat pancake / Ball  
   **Answer:** Pointed beak. *Why:* The shortest bar is the pointed beak, with 2 splash spots. Picking 'flat pancake' means reading the tallest bar as the best. Here a tall bar means a big splash.
15. (mcq) Where did the burr's hooks and the gecko's hairs come from?  
   Options: Evolution over millions of years / A scientist designed them / They were made in a factory  
   **Answer:** Evolution over millions of years. *Why:* Over many generations, plants with better hooks and geckos with better grip left more young. So those features spread. Picking 'a scientist designed them' mixes up nature's designs with people's copies of them.
16. (mcq) Deep down, the gecko's grip and the water drop's round shape both come from…  
   Options: Electric pulls between atoms and molecules / Gravity / Magnets / Glue  
   **Answer:** Electric pulls between atoms and molecules. *Why:* Gecko hair tips and the wall pull on each other with tiny electric forces. Water molecules pull on each other with electric forces too, which rounds a drop. Picking gravity is common, but gravity here is far too weak.
17. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Whether a thin film of water helps geckos grip / Whether lotus leaves have bumps / Whether Velcro has hooks / Whether kingfishers dive into water  
   **Answer:** Whether a thin film of water helps geckos grip. *Why:* Scientists agree the pull between atoms does most of the gecko's grip. They still argue about how much a thin film of water helps. The other three can be seen with a microscope or your own eyes.

- Swimming goggles, gliders and aeroplane wings shaped after birds and fish.
- Hospitals testing gecko-inspired dry tapes that hold without glue and peel off gently.
- Traditional thick-walled mud houses and earthen matkas that stay cool in Indian summers.

## Open questions
- **Exactly how much does a thin film of water help a gecko grip?** Scientists agree the pull between atoms does most of the work. Experiments disagree on how much damp air adds.
- **How exactly do termite mounds move their air?** Different kinds of termite build different mounds. Day and night warming, wind and the termites themselves may all play a part, in different mixes.
- **Can we make gecko tape that works as well as a gecko, for years?** Copies grip well at first, but get dirty or worn. Real gecko feet clean themselves and grow new hairs when the gecko sheds its skin.
- **How many of nature's designs have we not even noticed yet?** Scientists have described only a small part of the kinds of living things on Earth. Most have never been studied closely.
- **Can we build an 'artificial leaf' that makes fuel from sunlight, as well as plants make food?** Scientists have made small ones that work in the lab. Nobody has yet made one cheap and long-lasting enough to use everywhere.
- **Can we make self-cleaning surfaces that last?** The lotus grows new wax all the time. Man-made copies wear off with rubbing, and scientists are still looking for tougher ones.
- **Should a company be able to patent a design copied from a plant or an animal?** This is a question of fairness, not only science. Countries and people still disagree.
- **Are there designs that only living things can make, or that only people can make?** Living things build at ordinary temperatures with water and sunlight. People can use fire, metal and electricity. Where the limits are, nobody knows.

## Connected maths
- After the Sock Walk, 18 seeds were stuck to the fluffy sock and 3 to the smooth sock. Look at the chart. How many times more seeds did the fluffy sock collect? **6 times as many.**. The fluffy sock collected 18 seeds. The smooth sock collected 3. 18 divided by 3 is 6. So the fluffy sock collected 6 times as many. You can check: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 18. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- In the fastener challenge, 4 paperclip hooks held 6 coins. If every hook holds the same, how many coins would 8 hooks hold? And 12 hooks? **8 hooks hold about 12 coins. 12 hooks hold about 18 coins.**. 8 hooks is 2 lots of 4 hooks, so it holds 2 lots of 6 coins. 2 times 6 is 12. 12 hooks is 3 lots of 4 hooks. 3 times 6 is 18. Real tests wobble a little, so your numbers may be close to these, not exactly the same. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the ice experiment, the ice in the bare cup was gone after 25 minutes. The ice in the newspaper cup lasted 50 minutes. How many minutes longer did the newspaper cup keep its ice? **25 minutes longer.**. 50 minutes take away 25 minutes is 25 minutes. So the thick newspaper walls kept the ice for 25 minutes longer. That is twice as long as the bare cup, because 25 + 25 = 50. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In the splash experiment, the pancake made 22, 18 and 25 splash spots in three tries. The beak made 2, 0 and 3. Find the middle value for each. Then draw a bar chart. **The pancake's middle value is 22. The beak's middle value is 2. Here is the bar chart.**. Put each set of numbers in order, smallest first. Pancake: 18, 22, 25. The middle one is 22. Beak: 0, 2, 3. The middle one is 2. Draw one bar for each shape, as tall as its middle value. The pancake's bar is 11 times as tall as the beak's, because 2 times 11 is 22. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Riya, a girl in Class 3, did the leaf experiment with 8 drops on each surface. She tilted the steel plate, and 4 drops rolled off. On the arbi leaf, all 8 rolled off. What fraction of the drops rolled off the steel plate? **Half of them.**. 4 out of 8 drops rolled off. 4 is half of 8, because 4 + 4 = 8. So half the drops rolled off the steel. On the leaf, 8 out of 8 rolled off, which is all of them, the whole. (queues `math.fractions.halves-and-quarters.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What does biomimicry mean? → Learning how a plant or an animal solves a problem in its own life, and copying the idea to solve a human problem.
- Which plant part gave us the idea for Velcro? → A burdock burr, with its tiny hooks.
- Why does a burdock burr have hooks? → So they catch in animal fur, and the animal carries its seeds to new ground.
- Is a lotus leaf smooth or bumpy, under a microscope? → Bumpy, with tiny waxy bumps.

- Which bird's beak shaped a Japanese bullet train's nose? → The kingfisher's.
- How does a gecko stick to a wall: glue, suction or tiny hairs? → Tiny hairs. Millions of them get so close that their atoms pull on the wall.
- What keeps a termite mound cool inside? → Thick earth walls, and air moving with day and night.
- Does good biomimicry copy how a living thing works, or how it looks? → How it works.
- Where do nature's designs come from? → Evolution, over millions of years.
- What is a first, rough model of an invention called? → A prototype.
- Who invented Velcro: NASA or George de Mestral? → George de Mestral, a Swiss engineer. NASA only used it later.
