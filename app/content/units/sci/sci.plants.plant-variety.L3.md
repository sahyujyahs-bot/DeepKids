# Kinds of plants: what a stem tells you about how a plant lives
`sci.plants.plant-variety.L3`

**Summary.** Some plants stand up on a woody stem, and others climb or spread because their stems are too soft to stand. A tree has one thick, woody trunk, and a shrub has several woody stems from near the ground. A herb has a soft green stem and stays small. A climber holds on to something to grow up, and a creeper spreads along the ground. Your child tests stems from the kitchen to see which can stand alone. They also find out how people use plants, and meet plants that do not fit the five kinds, like the banana, which has no wood at all.

**Intro.** *Look out of every window in your home for one minute. Count every plant you can see. Now look again. Which one could you climb? Which one could you squash between two fingers? Is any plant holding on to something?* For hundreds of years, ships sailed to Kerala to buy black pepper. Many people in Europe ate pepper every day but had never seen the plant growing. A pepper plant has no trunk of their own. They grow on a thin stem that holds on to a tree trunk or a post and climbs up it. So how can a plant grow tall without a trunk? In this unit you test stems to find out which can stand up alone. Then you learn to tell a tree from a shrub, a herb, a climber and a creeper by looking at their stem.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can sort plants into trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers and creepers, and say which part of the stem told me.
1. I can test stems fairly to tell soft stems from woody ones, and record what I find in a table.
2. I can explain why some plants climb or creep instead of standing up on their own.
3. I can name many ways people use plants: food, medicine, shade, wood, cloth, rope, paper and more.
4. I can measure and compare, for example how much cooler the ground is in a tree's shade, and show it in a bar chart.
5. I can say what scientists agree about kinds of plants, and give plants that do not fit the boxes.
6. I can go deep: wood took millions of years to appear, trees grew from many different plant families, and nobody knows how many plants are still undiscovered.

## Terms
- **Plant**: A living thing that usually stays in one place, has green leaves and makes their own food using sunlight. *e.g.* Grass, a neem tree and the tulsi in a pot are all plants.
- **Stem**: The part of a plant that holds them up. It carries water and food between the roots and the leaves. *e.g.* A coriander stalk is a soft stem. A tree trunk is a big hard stem.
- **Woody**: Hard and stiff like wood. A woody stem does not bend or squash easily. *e.g.* A neem twig is woody. It snaps when you bend it too far.
- **Tree**: A big plant with one thick, hard, woody stem called a trunk. Their branches start high up. *e.g.* Neem, mango, peepal and banyan are trees.
- **Trunk**: The one thick woody stem of a tree. *e.g.* You can hug the trunk of a mango tree.
- **Bark**: The hard outer covering of a trunk or branch. It protects the tree, like our skin protects us. *e.g.* Rub your hand on a tree trunk. The rough part you feel is bark.
- **Shrub**: A medium-sized bushy plant. They have several hard, woody stems growing from close to the ground. *e.g.* Hibiscus, rose, mehendi and old tulsi plants are shrubs.
- **Herb**: A small plant with a soft, green stem that does not become woody. *e.g.* Mint, coriander, spinach and tomato plants are herbs.
- **Grass**: A plant with long, thin leaves and a stem with joints, like a wheat stalk. Most grasses have soft stems, so they are herbs. Bamboo is a grass with a hard stem. *e.g.* Doob grass, wheat, rice, sugarcane and bamboo are all grasses.
- **Climber**: A plant with a thin, bendy stem that grows upward by holding on to something, like a wall, a stick or another plant. *e.g.* Money plant, jasmine, pepper and bitter gourd are climbers.
- **Creeper**: A plant with a thin, bendy stem that spreads along the ground. *e.g.* Pumpkin, watermelon and sweet potato plants are creepers.
- **Tendril**: A thin, curly thread that some climbers wrap around things to hold on. *e.g.* A bitter gourd plant holds the fence with their tendrils.
- **Wood**: The hard material inside trunks and branches. It is made from the walls of millions of tiny dead cells, packed together. *e.g.* Your table, a pencil and a cricket bat are made of wood.
- **Medicine**: Something that helps the body get better or stay well. Many medicines first came from plants. *e.g.* Aspirin, a common pain medicine, was made by changing a chemical first found in willow bark.
- **Fibre**: A long, thin thread, often from a plant, that can be spun into cloth or twisted into rope. *e.g.* Cotton fibre makes your T-shirt. Coconut fibre, called coir, makes doormats and ropes.
- **Shade**: A cooler, darker place where something blocks the sunlight. *e.g.* On a hot day, cows and people rest in the shade of a big tree.
- **Species**: One kind of living thing. All neem trees belong to one species. All mango trees belong to another. *e.g.* Scientists have named more than 350,000 species of plants, and they name about 2,000 new ones every year.
- **Weed**: A plant growing where people do not want them. The word is about our wishes, not about the plant. *e.g.* Grass in a vegetable patch is called a weed. The same grass on a cricket field is wanted.

## Explanations
### Sorting plants by their stem · *defined*
**Definition:** Botanists, the scientists who study plants, often sort plants by how they grow. The biggest clue is the stem: is it hard or soft, thick or thin, standing up or held up by something else?

There are hundreds of thousands of kinds of plants. To make sense of them, people sort them into groups. In Class 3 we sort them by how they grow. First, feel the stem. Is it woody, like a stick, or soft and green, like a mint stem? Then look at its shape. Is there one trunk, or many stems from the ground? Last, ask: can the plant stand up alone, or do they climb or spread on the ground? These three questions give us five kinds: trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers and creepers.
- e.g. A neem has one thick, woody trunk. So a neem is a tree.
- e.g. A hibiscus has many woody stems near the ground. So a hibiscus is a shrub.
- e.g. A pumpkin has a long, soft stem lying on the ground. So a pumpkin is a creeper.
### Trees · *defined*
**Definition:** A tree is a big plant with one thick, hard, woody stem, called a trunk. Their branches usually start well above the ground.

Trees are the biggest plants. Their trunk is made of wood, which is very strong. A strong trunk lets a tree hold their leaves high above other plants, in the sunlight. Trees live a long time, and many live for a hundred years or more. Bark covers the trunk and protects it from insects, heat and knocks. Coconut and areca palms also have one tall trunk, so we call them trees too. But a palm trunk does not grow fatter every year, the way a mango trunk does.
- e.g. Neem, mango, peepal, banyan, jamun and tamarind are trees.
- e.g. The coconut palm has one tall trunk and no branches. Their leaves grow from the very top.
- e.g. There are about 3 trillion trees on Earth. That is nearly 400 trees for every person.
### Shrubs · *defined*
**Definition:** A shrub is a medium-sized, bushy plant. They have several woody stems that grow from close to the ground.

A shrub is smaller than a tree, usually not much taller than a grown-up. Their stems are hard and woody, like a tree's. But instead of one trunk, a shrub has many stems coming up from near the soil. This makes them look bushy. People often plant shrubs as hedges, because they grow thick and close together. Many flowering plants in Indian gardens are shrubs.
- e.g. Hibiscus, rose, bougainvillea, mehendi, kaner and lemon plants are shrubs.
- e.g. Tulsi starts as a soft herb. As they get older, their stem turns woody, and they become a small shrub.
- e.g. A hedge around a park is a row of shrubs, cut into a wall shape.
### Herbs and grasses · *defined*
**Definition:** A herb is a small plant with a soft, green stem that does not turn into wood. Most grasses are herbs too. Grasses have long, thin leaves.

Squeeze the stem of a mint plant. It is soft and juicy, and it bends easily. That is a herb. Most herbs are small, and many live for only one season. They grow quickly, make their seeds and die. Then the seeds grow into new plants. Grasses have long, narrow leaves, and their stems are often hollow, with joints. The grains we eat most, like rice, wheat, maize, bajra and ragi, all come from grasses. In cooking, 'herbs' means leaves that add taste, like mint. In science, a herb is any plant whose stem stays soft.
- e.g. Mint, coriander, spinach, methi, tomato and mustard plants are herbs.
- e.g. Rice, wheat, ragi, sugarcane and doob are grasses.
- e.g. Bamboo is a grass too. Their stem is hard, but it never grows fatter year after year like a tree trunk.
### Climbers · *defined*
**Definition:** A climber is a plant with a thin, weak stem that grows upward by holding on to something else, like a wall, a fence, a stick or another plant.

A climber's stem is too thin to stand up by itself. So they hold on to a support and grow up towards the light. Climbers hold on in different ways. Some twist their whole stem around a stick, like the bean plant. Some grow curly threads called tendrils, which wrap around things, like bitter gourd and grapes. Some grow tiny roots along the stem that grip bark or walls, like money plant and pepper. Some hook on with thorns, like bougainvillea. A thin stem needs much less food to build than a thick trunk. So a climber can grow long fast, using another plant's trunk to get up.
- e.g. Money plant and pepper climb with little roots on the stem.
- e.g. Bitter gourd, bottle gourd and grapes climb with tendrils.
- e.g. Bean plants and morning glory climb by twisting their stem around a support.
### Creepers · *defined*
**Definition:** A creeper is a plant with a long, thin, weak stem that spreads along the ground instead of standing up.

A creeper's stem is too thin to stand up, and they have nothing to climb. So they spread out flat over the ground. This way they can cover a big area with leaves, where they catch the sunlight. Many creepers grow new roots wherever their stem touches the soil, so one creeper can slowly cover a whole patch. A heavy pumpkin or watermelon can rest on the soil, so the stem does not have to hold it up. Some plants can do both. A pumpkin creeps on the ground, but if their tendrils find a fence, they will climb it.
- e.g. Pumpkin, watermelon, cucumber and sweet potato plants are creepers.
- e.g. Doob grass creeps along the ground and grows roots as they go.
- e.g. A strawberry plant sends out long stems along the ground, and new baby plants grow at their tips.
### Why climb? Why creep? Getting leaves into the light · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants need sunlight to make their food. Trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers and creepers are different ways of reaching that light.

Every plant needs sunlight to make their food. In a crowded place, the tallest plants get the light first, and the plants below are in shade. A tree reaches the light by building a tall, woody trunk. Wood takes a lot of the tree's food, year after year. A herb does the opposite. They grow fast, make seeds and are done in a few months. A climber grows a thin stem, which takes little food, and holds on to a tree or a wall to reach the light. A creeper spreads sideways and catches light in open spaces. No way is best. Each one works well in its own kind of place.
- e.g. In a forest, giant climbers called lianas can reach the very top of the trees.
- e.g. On a sunny open field, creepers like doob grass cover the ground.
- e.g. Indoors, a money plant grows towards the brightest window and climbs the grill there.
### Plants that break the boxes · *debated*
Our five kinds are useful, but not every plant fits them. A banana plant looks like a tree, but they have no wood at all. Their 'trunk' is made of leaf bottoms rolled tightly around each other, so botanists call them a giant herb. Bamboo grows as tall as a tree, but bamboo is a grass. Tulsi starts soft, like a herb, and becomes woody, like a shrub. Papaya has a single trunk, but it is soft and spongy inside. A pumpkin creeps or climbs, depending on what they find. So scientists use the five kinds as a handy guide, not as strict rules.
- e.g. A banana plant can be taller than a grown-up, but a grown-up can cut through their stem with a big kitchen knife, because there is no wood.
- e.g. Some bamboos can grow almost a metre in one day.
- e.g. The same lantana plant can grow as a shrub in an open field, or climb up into trees at the forest edge.
- **Many botanists who sort plants by how they grow:** Life forms, like tree and climber, are real and useful. They tell you how a plant lives and wins light. In 1904 a Danish botanist, Christen Raunkiaer, made a famous system of plant life forms that is still used.
- **Botanists who sort plants by family:** How a plant grows tells you little about who they are related to. A rose and an apple tree are close cousins, but one is a shrub and one is a tree. So for finding relatives, scientists compare flowers, seeds and genes, not stems.
### What is wood? · *defined*
**Definition:** Wood is the hard part of trunks and branches. It is made from the stiff walls of millions of tiny, long cells that have died and stayed in place.

A plant's body is made of tiny living units called cells. In a woody stem, many cells build thick, tough walls around themselves. Two materials make the walls strong. One is cellulose, the same material as in cotton and paper. The other is lignin, which works like a hard glue. When these cells die, their walls stay, packed together like a bundle of straws. That is wood. Each year, many trees add a new layer of wood just under the bark. In places with a cold winter or a long dry season, these layers show as rings. Many trees in the warm tropics have rings that are hard to read.
- e.g. A soft herb stem has very little lignin, so it bends and squashes.
- e.g. Teak, sheesham and sal wood are hard and strong, so people make furniture and doors from them.
- e.g. Paper is made from wood with most of the lignin taken out, leaving the soft cellulose.
### Wood took millions of years to appear · *defined*
**Definition:** The first plants on land had no wood. The first woody stems appeared about 400 million years ago, and the first trees about 390 million years ago.

For most of Earth's history there were no plants on land at all. The first land plants were tiny, like moss, and lay close to the ground. Over millions of years, some plants began to build thick stems packed with lignin, the hard glue in wood. Now they could stand tall. One of the oldest fossil forests ever found is at Gilboa, in New York State. Its trees lived about 385 million years ago, long before dinosaurs. They looked like giant ferns on top of a trunk. Wood changed the whole planet. Forests spread, made soil and changed the air.
- e.g. The Gilboa fossil forest trees, called Wattieza, were about 8 metres tall.
- e.g. Coal is the squashed remains of ancient forests and swamps from about 300 million years ago.
- e.g. Before trees, the tallest things on land were pillars called Prototaxites, up to 8 metres tall. Scientists still argue about what kind of living thing they were.
### There is no single 'tree family' · *defined*
**Definition:** Being a tree is a way of growing, not a family. Plants from many unrelated families have become trees on their own.

You might think all trees are close cousins. They are not. Many unrelated plant families grew tall woody trunks separately, at different times. A pine, a coconut palm and a mango are all trees, but they are distant relatives. The coconut is closer to rice and bamboo than to the mango. It also went the other way. Some herbs came from woody ancestors, and on some islands, herbs turned back into woody shrubs. So 'tree' describes how a plant grows, not which family they belong to.
- e.g. Coconut palms are related to grasses, not to mango trees.
- e.g. There are about 73,000 kinds of trees, from hundreds of different plant families.
- e.g. On the Canary Islands, some plants whose relatives are soft herbs grow as woody shrubs.
### One plant or a whole forest? · *debated*
A banyan tree drops roots from their branches. These prop roots grow thick and become new trunks. The Great Banyan in the Botanic Garden at Howrah, near Kolkata, is about 250 years old. They have thousands of prop roots and covers more ground than two football fields. Their first trunk died and was removed in 1925, but the tree lives on. So are they one tree or many? In Utah, in America, there is a grove of about 47,000 aspen trunks, called Pando. They all grow from one root system, with the same genes. Some scientists call them one plant, maybe the heaviest living thing on Earth.
- e.g. A banyan can look like a small forest, but they are one plant.
- e.g. A bamboo clump is many stems joined underground.
- e.g. Doob grass on a lawn may be one plant, spread out and joined by their creeping stems.
- **Scientists who count by genes and roots:** If every trunk has the same genes and they share one root system, they are one living thing. So Pando is one plant.
- **Scientists who count by bodies:** Each trunk can live on its own if the roots are cut. So each one is its own plant. The word 'individual' does not fit plants as neatly as it fits animals.
### How people use plants · *defined*
**Definition:** People depend on plants for food, medicine, shade, wood, cloth, rope, paper, oil, colours and much more.

Look at your breakfast. Rice, wheat, dal, vegetables, fruit, sugar, tea and spices all come from plants. Milk comes from cows that eat grass. So almost all our food comes from plants in the end. Just three grasses, rice, wheat and maize, give the world nearly half of all its food energy. Cotton clothes are made from the fluffy fibres of the cotton plant. Chairs, doors and pencils are wood. Natural rubber comes from the milky sap of the rubber tree. Turmeric and mehendi give us colours. Coconut and mustard give us oil. Trees give us shade and cool our streets.
- e.g. Food: rice, wheat, dal, mango, banana, tea and sugar.
- e.g. Things: cotton cloth, jute bags, coir doormats, wooden furniture, paper and rubber.
- e.g. Colours: turmeric gives yellow, mehendi gives orange-red and indigo gives blue.
### Medicines from plants · *defined*
**Definition:** Many medicines were first found in plants. Plants make strong chemicals to protect themselves, and some of these chemicals can help us heal.

A plant cannot run away from insects and animals that eat them. So many plants make bitter or smelly chemicals in their leaves, bark and seeds. These chemicals keep many leaf-eaters away. Some of them also help people. For thousands of years, people found out which plants helped with which illness. The Charaka Samhita, an old book of Ayurveda, describes hundreds of plant medicines. Scientists today test these plants and find out what is inside them. A chemical from willow bark led to aspirin. Sadabahar, the pink periwinkle in many Indian gardens, gave two cancer medicines. Tu Youyou, a Chinese scientist, found a malaria medicine in sweet wormwood and won a Nobel Prize in 2015. Asima Chatterjee, a chemist in Kolkata, studied medicines from Indian plants for most of her life.
- e.g. Sadabahar (periwinkle) gave the cancer medicines vincristine and vinblastine. Vincristine is used for a blood cancer in children.
- e.g. Sweet wormwood gave artemisinin, a medicine that has saved millions from malaria.
- e.g. Willow bark has a chemical called salicin. Chemists changed it slightly to make aspirin in 1897.
### Some plants are poisonous · *defined*
**Definition:** Some plants make chemicals strong enough to make people very ill. A plant being natural does not make them safe.

The same chemicals that protect a plant from insects can hurt us. Kaner, also called oleander, is a common shrub along Indian roads and gardens. Every part of kaner is very poisonous. Castor seeds and datura are poisonous too. Even plants that help as medicines can harm if a person takes the wrong part or too much. That is why we never eat or taste a wild plant, leaf, seed or berry without a grown-up who truly knows them. Scientists and doctors test plant medicines carefully, to find the right amount.
- e.g. Kaner (oleander) is poisonous in every part, even though they are planted everywhere for their flowers.
- e.g. Castor seeds are poisonous to eat, even though castor oil is made from them by a careful process.
- e.g. Wash your hands after handling plants you do not know.
### Shade: how trees cool a street · *defined*
**Definition:** A leaf in strong sun can get too hot. Water evaporating from the leaf cools it, the way sweat cools your skin. The leaves also block sunlight, so the ground and air under a tree stay cooler.

A leaf sitting in the afternoon sun soaks up heat. If it gets too hot, it can be damaged. Water evaporates from tiny holes in the leaf, which means it turns into an invisible gas. That takes heat away, the same way sweat cools your skin. A big tree lets a lot of water evaporate on a hot day. This cools the air around the tree too. The leaves also catch the sunlight before it reaches the ground, so the ground underneath stays cooler. That is why you feel the difference at once when you step from a sunny road under a tree. Streets with many trees can be several degrees cooler than streets with none.
- e.g. Cows, dogs and auto drivers all choose the shade of a tree on a hot afternoon.
- e.g. Old roads in India were planted with lines of trees, like banyan, neem and mango, to shade travellers.
- e.g. A sunny tiled roof can get too hot to touch, while a wall covered by a climber stays much cooler.
### People who stood up for trees · *defined*
**Definition:** Many people, often women from villages, have protected trees and forests because their lives and their families depended on them.

In 1730, at Khejarli in Rajasthan, the king's men came to cut khejri trees. Amrita Devi, a woman of the Bishnoi community, hugged a tree to stop them. She and more than 360 Bishnoi people were killed, the story says, and the king then banned tree cutting in their villages. In 1974, in the village of Reni in the Himalayas, Gaura Devi led the women to hug trees to stop loggers. This was part of the Chipko movement. In Karnataka, Saalumarada Thimmakka and her husband planted and cared for hundreds of banyan trees along a road. In Kenya, Wangari Maathai started a movement that planted tens of millions of trees, and won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2004.
- e.g. Chipko means 'to cling' or 'to hug' in Hindi.
- e.g. Saalumarada means 'row of trees' in Kannada. Thimmakka received the Padma Shri in 2019.
- e.g. Khejri trees give shade, fodder for animals and food in the desert.
### How many kinds of plants are there? · *idea*
Scientists have named more than 350,000 species of plants, and about 2,000 new ones are named every year. India alone has many thousands of kinds of flowering plants. Nobody knows the true total, because many plants grow in places few botanists have visited, like the tops of rainforest trees or remote hills. Scientists think that about 2 in every 5 plant species may be in danger of dying out, as forests are cut and the climate changes. Some may disappear before anyone has even named them.
- e.g. New plant species are still found in India, in places like the Western Ghats and the north-east.
- e.g. There may be 9,000 kinds of trees that nobody has named yet.
- e.g. Seed banks, like Kew's Millennium Seed Bank in England and the one in Svalbard in Norway, store seeds to keep plants safe.

## Mostly agreed
- Plants can be sorted by how they grow: trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers and creepers. Most grasses are herbs. *How we know:* Botanists and plant books use groups like these, and anyone can check them by looking at a plant's stem.
- Woody stems are hard because their cells have thick walls with lignin in them. *How we know:* Under a microscope, wood is seen as packed, thick-walled dead cells. Chemists can measure the lignin.
- Almost all the food people and animals eat comes from plants in the end. *How we know:* Follow any food chain back far enough. It always starts with a plant, or with tiny plant-like living things in the sea.
- Many medicines were first found in plants. *How we know:* Aspirin, artemisinin, vincristine and many more were found by studying plants, and then tested on patients.
- Trees make the ground and air cooler around them. *How we know:* Thermometers in the shade of trees read lower than those in the sun, and cities with more trees measure cooler.
- Trees appeared on Earth about 390 million years ago, and plants from many different families became trees separately. *How we know:* Fossil forests, like the one at Gilboa in New York State, and the family trees scientists build by comparing plant genes.

## How we found out
**Theophrastus, a Greek thinker; John Ray, an English botanist; Antoine Laurent de Jussieu, a French botanist, About 300 BCE (Theophrastus), the 1680s (Ray), 1789 (de Jussieu) and the 1990s (gene studies).** About 2,300 years ago, a Greek thinker called Theophrastus wrote one of the first books about plants. He sorted plants by how they grow: trees, shrubs, undershrubs and herbs. For almost 2,000 years after that, many scholars sorted plants this way. Even in the 1680s, the English botanist John Ray split plants into trees and herbs first. Many people thought that all trees must be one natural group of relatives. Then botanists began comparing flowers, fruits and seeds instead of stems. In 1789, a French botanist, Antoine Laurent de Jussieu, grouped plants into families this way. His families mixed trees, shrubs and herbs together. The rose family holds the rose, a shrub, the apple, a tree, and the strawberry, a herb. From the 1990s, scientists compared plant genes, and found the same thing. Trees grew in many separate families, again and again.  
*What people believed before:* That all trees form one family of relatives. Comparing flowers and seeds, and later genes, showed that trees, shrubs and herbs are mixed together in the same families.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A banana plant is a tree.** (Most people, and many children's books.; *rejected*)  
  For: They are tall, and they have one thick trunk with big leaves at the top.  
  Against: Their trunk has no wood at all. It is made of rolled-up leaf bottoms. When they fruit, the whole stem dies. So botanists call them a giant herb.
- **Plants exist for people to use.** (Aristotle, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,350 years ago, wrote that plants exist for the sake of animals, and animals for the sake of people. Many people still feel this way.; *debated*)  
  For: People really do use plants for almost everything, and farming has fed billions of people.  
  Against: Plants lived on Earth for hundreds of millions of years before any people. Most plants are never used by people at all. Plants live their own lives, and many living things depend on them, not only us.
- **A weed is a bad kind of plant.** (Many gardeners and farmers.; *debated*)  
  For: Some plants spread fast and take water and light from crops. A few, like congress grass, can harm people and animals.  
  Against: 'Weed' just means a plant growing where we do not want them. The same grass is a weed in a vegetable patch and a lawn on a cricket field. Many 'weeds' feed bees and butterflies or hold soil in place.
- **Natural plant medicines are always safe.** (Many people, and many advertisements.; *rejected*)  
  For: People have used some plant remedies for a very long time.  
  Against: Some plants, like kaner and datura, are deadly. Even helpful plants can harm in the wrong amount. That is why plant medicines must be tested carefully, like any medicine.
- **All trees are close relatives.** (A very natural idea, because trees look alike.; *rejected*)  
  For: Most trees share the same body plan: one trunk, branches and leaves.  
  Against: When scientists compare genes, they find that trees come from many separate plant families. A coconut palm is closer to rice than to a mango tree.
- **Trees in a forest share food and messages through fungi under the ground, like a 'wood wide web'.** (Suzanne Simard, a Canadian forest scientist, and many popular books.; *debated*)  
  For: In 1997, Simard and her team showed that some carbon passed between trees through fungi joined to their roots.  
  Against: In 2023, other scientists looked again at the studies. They said the evidence that trees really help each other this way is weaker than the popular story says.

## Roots
We have been testing stems and sorting plants into trees, shrubs, herbs, climbers and creepers. Now we ask what these kinds tell us about how a plant lives.

**Deep truth.** In the Stem Test, the woody twig stood up alone, but the soft stems flopped over. Every plant must get their leaves into sunlight to make food. A tree holds them up on wood, a climber leans on a support, and a creeper spreads them flat. So a plant's kind is their way of reaching the light.  
*Habit it breaks:* We learn tree, shrub, herb, climber and creeper as names to memorise, like labels on jars. But each kind is a way of living. It tells you how that plant reaches the light, and what they need from the place they grow.  
*Evidence:* In the Stem Test, the woody twig stayed stiff, the soft stems bent, and a long money plant stem flopped over. A money plant still reaches the top of a window grill, because they hold on to the grill or a pole with little roots along their stem. Farmers in Kerala plant a tree or a post before they plant pepper, for the same reason.

- *who am i:* Your bones hold you up, the way wood holds up a tree. Your muscles work all the time to keep you balanced.
- *what is life:* Every plant you sorted is reaching for the same sunlight. Living means finding a way to get what you need.
- *what is this world:* A climber grows on a tree, and a creeper grows in the open space between trees. Each kind of plant lives in a place made partly by other plants.

All plants come from the same ancient ancestors. Over millions of years, their families found many ways to reach the light: wood in some, soft fast stems in others, tendrils and gripping roots in climbers. The tree shape grew in many separate families. So the five kinds are not five separate families. They are five ways that one great family of living things has found to reach the sunlight.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12:** A father, Uddalaka, asks his son to break open a tiny banyan seed. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing at all.' 'From that tiny thing you cannot see, this great banyan tree grows. That is you.' (A banyan seed holds no wood at all. The whole trunk is built later, mostly from air and water, using the Sun's light.)
- **Tao Te Ching 76:** Green plants are soft and full of sap while they live. When they die, they become dry and hard. So the stiff and hard go with death, and the soft and bending go with life. (In the Stem Test, the dry twig snapped and the green stems bent. Wood itself is made of cells that have died and left their hard walls behind. The part that grows new wood is a thin living layer just under the bark.)
- Science (settled): Wood appeared about 400 million years ago. It let plants stand tall and hold their leaves above other plants.
- Science (settled): Plants from many unrelated families became trees on their own, and some trees' descendants became climbers or herbs.
- Other view, Many botanists: Sorting plants by how they grow tells you little about who their relatives are. A rose is a shrub and an apple is a tree, but they are close cousins. For finding families, scientists compare flowers, seeds and genes, not stems.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Is a banana plant a tree? Who decides what counts as a tree? (hint: Think about what a tree's trunk is made of. Then think about who made up the word 'tree'.)
- *deep:* Why doesn't every plant grow into a big tree, if trees get the most light? (hint: Wood costs a plant a lot of food. What could a plant do with that food instead?)
- *big:* A climber leans on a tree to reach the light. Is that fair to the tree? Is it sharing or stealing? (hint: Does the tree lose anything? What if the climber grows very heavy?)
- *deep:* When many trunks grow from one root, like a banyan, is that one plant or many? (hint: What makes you one person? Is it your body, your genes, or something else?)
- *big:* Could people live on Earth without plants? Could plants live without people? (hint: Think about food, air, clothes and shade. Then think about what plants needed before people existed.)
- *big:* Who decides whether a plant is a weed? (hint: Is it the plant, the bee that visits them, or the person who owns the garden?)
- *big:* Healers in villages knew about medicine plants long before scientists tested them. Who should get the credit, and who should get paid, when a medicine is made? (hint: Think of the healer, the scientist who tested it and the company that sells it.)
- *big:* If you could ask a plant, would they want to be useful to us? (hint: Some plants, like mango, grew tastier because people chose the best ones for thousands of years.)
- *big:* Why do so many cultures think certain trees are special, like the peepal, the banyan or the khejri? (hint: Think of how old these trees can be, how big, and what they give to people and animals.)

## Story: The Pepper Tree That Wasn't
*The book is real. Hortus Malabaricus, 'The Garden of Malabar', was made for the Dutch governor Hendrik van Rheede and printed in Amsterdam in 12 volumes between 1678 and 1693. Itty Achuden, a healer from the Ezhava community, really was its main plant expert, and his statement in Malayalam really is printed in the first volume. Ranga Bhat, Vinayaka Pandit and Appu Bhat really helped too. Ammini, the girl who tells the story, Pieter and the pepper drawing are made up.*

In the year 1675, when I was nine years old, a Dutch governor in Kochi decided he wanted to know every plant in Malabar. His name was Hendrik van Rheede. He wanted a book so big and so true that nobody in Europe would ever again have to guess what grew on our coast. But a governor cannot learn a thousand plants by himself. So he sent for my uncle.

My uncle was Itty Achuden. He was a vaidyan, a healer, from a village by the sea, south of Kochi. He knew plants the way other people know their cousins. He knew them by their leaves and their bark, by their smell when you crushed them, and by the way they grew. He carried palm-leaf books that his family had copied and recopied for longer than anyone could remember. Three learned men came too: Ranga Bhat, Vinayaka Pandit and Appu Bhat, who knew the old Sanskrit books of healing. But when the governor wanted to see a plant alive, it was my uncle who walked out and found one.

My job was small. I was a girl of nine, and I carried the baskets. I held leaves flat on the table while the painters drew them. The painters were young Dutchmen with ink on their cuffs and sweat on their noses. The youngest was called Pieter. Pieter was the one who could not find the pepper.

This seemed very funny to me, and also a little sad. The Dutch had sailed halfway round the world for pepper. Their ships left Kochi so full of black pepper that the sailors said they could smell it from the mast. Pieter had eaten pepper every day of his life. But he had never once seen the plant growing.

'The governor wants the pepper tree drawn first,' he told me, through the man who changed his words into ours. 'The whole tree. Roots, flowers, fruit, everything.'

For three days Pieter walked the gardens with his paper and his pens. He looked at every tree. He drew the coconut palms, tall and bare, with their leaves bursting out at the very top. He drew the mango, round and dark like a crowd of people standing close. He drew the tamarind, the drumstick and the neem. He drew the jackfruit, with their huge spiky fruits growing straight out of the trunk, as if the tree had forgotten to grow branches for them. Not one of them had a single peppercorn.

On the fourth day he came back cross. He spread a drawing on the floor of our verandah. It was a jackfruit tree, beautifully done. And hanging among their thick, shiny leaves were long, thin strings of little green berries, like beads on a thread.

'There,' he said. 'Pepper growing on a jackfruit tree. Your uncle says it is wrong. He says a jackfruit cannot make pepper. Then where do the berries come from?'

Uncle only smiled, and said, 'Ammini will show you.' He liked to let people find things for themselves. He said a thing you find stays with you, and a thing you are told falls out of your pocket on the way home.

So I took Pieter back to the garden behind the market, where he had sat drawing. I walked up to the jackfruit tree and put my hand flat on their trunk. The bark was rough and warm. 'This is one plant,' I said. Then I moved my hand a little to one side, onto a thin green rope pressed tight against the bark. 'And this is another.'

The rope was a stem. It was no thicker than my finger. It wandered up the trunk in a long, crooked line. Every little way along it, there were tiny brown roots. They were pushed into the cracks of the bark, like fingers holding on to a wall. The leaves along it were not jackfruit leaves at all. They were shaped like hearts, glossy and pointed, with lines running from the stalk to the tip. And up in the branches, the strings of berries hung from that thin stem, not from the tree.

Pieter stared. He touched the stem. He tugged it, gently, and it held on.

'They have no trunk,' he said slowly.

'They don't need one,' I said. 'The jackfruit made the trunk. The pepper climbs it.'

Then I told him what every child in our village knew. When people plant pepper, they never plant them alone. First they plant a tree, or set a strong post in the ground. Then they plant the pepper at their foot. The pepper stem is soft and bendy. Left on the ground, they creep about in the shade and give you hardly a handful of berries. Give them something to hold, and up they go, towards the sun, gripping with their little roots all the way.

'Then I have drawn two plants as if they were one,' said Pieter. He looked at his picture for a long time. I thought he would be ashamed. Instead, he laughed, so loudly that a crow flew off the wall. 'All the way from Amsterdam,' he said, 'to find out that pepper is a climber.'

That evening he made a new drawing. This time he drew only the pepper. He drew the thin stem, the clinging roots, the heart-shaped leaves and the hanging strings of berries. He left the jackfruit out completely. On the page, the stem went straight up the paper with nothing to hold it, like a rope climbing into the sky.

Uncle looked at the drawing for a long time. 'It is correct,' he said at last. 'But it is also strange. Nobody has ever seen pepper growing alone like that.'

Pieter thought about this. Then he dipped his pen and, very lightly, at the edge of the page, drew a single strip of rough bark. 'There,' he said. 'Now the pepper remembers what they lean on.'

Many years later, the governor's book was printed far away in Amsterdam. It came in twelve great volumes, with hundreds of drawings of the plants of our coast. In the very first volume, there is a page printed in our own Malayalam letters. It is my uncle's statement, telling every reader who he was, and how he knew these plants. Most people who open that book look only at the pictures. I always look for that page first.

And now, when I see a coconut palm with a green vine winding up their trunk, or a jackfruit wrapped in heart-shaped leaves, I do not see one plant. I see two lives making one shape. One stands. One climbs. Both of them are reaching for the same sun.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the pepper is not part of the jackfruit tree. They are a climber, a plant with a thin, soft stem that cannot stand up alone. So they hold on to a tree and climb to the light, gripping with little roots. Knowing the kind of plant tells you where to look and what you are seeing.

- Ask: Why couldn't Pieter find a 'pepper tree' anywhere?
- Ask: How does the pepper plant hold on to the jackfruit tree?
- Ask: What happens to a pepper plant if they have nothing to climb?
- Ask: Uncle said 'Ammini will show you' instead of explaining. Why do you think he did that?
- Ask: The book was the governor's idea, but the plant knowledge was Itty Achuden's. Whose name do you think should be on the cover?

### g1 · Plant Families (20 min)

## Experiments
### e1 · The Stem Test: which stems can stand up alone? (25 min)
- **question:** Which stems can hold themselves up, and how are they different from the ones that cannot?
- **predict:** Before you test, line up the stems from the one you think is hardest to the softest. Which ones do you think will stand up by themselves, and why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, test every stem in the same three ways, in the same order. Use pieces of about the same length, as long as you can find (about 30 cm is good). Use stems from the kitchen or ones already cut or fallen, so no plant is hurt.
- **safety:** Use only stems from your kitchen, or pieces that have already fallen or been pruned. Do not use stems with milky sap or thorns. Wash your hands afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table for every stem. Then draw each stem and write woody or soft under it.
- **results table:** Stem | Nail test: does it dent? | Bend test: springs back, snaps or flops? | Stand test: does it stand up alone? | Woody or soft? | Kind of plant
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Collect 4 or 5 stem pieces, from the kitchen or fallen from plants. 2. Lay them on newspaper. 3. Ask your child to line them up, hardest first, and say which will stand up alone.  
  *Parent says:* Which of these stems do you think is the hardest? Line them up, hardest first. Which ones will stand up by themselves? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Line up your stems, hardest first. Which will stand up alone? Why?
  - Ask: Why did you put that one first? (good: It looks brown and dry.; It feels hard like a stick.; It came from a big tree.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pick up each stem. 2. Press a fingernail into it gently, near the middle. 3. Look closely, with the hand lens if you have one. 4. Write yes or no in the 'dent' column.  
  *Parent says:* Press your nail in gently. Does it leave a mark? A soft stem dents easily. A woody stem hardly dents at all.  
  *Child mode:* Press your nail gently into each stem. Does it dent? Write yes or no.
  - Note: Soft herb stems like mint dent easily and may feel juicy. Woody twigs barely mark.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold each stem at both ends. 2. Bend it gently into a curve, then let go. 3. Write what happened: springs back, snaps or flops.  
  *Parent says:* Now bend each one slowly. Does it spring back, snap, or flop over?  
  *Child mode:* Bend each stem slowly. Does it spring back? Does it snap? Or does it flop?
  - Note: A dry woody twig often snaps with a crack. A fresh green twig may spring back. A soft herb stem bends and stays bent, or flops. A money plant stem bends easily.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Hold each stem upright by its bottom end only. 2. See whether it stands straight or droops. 3. Fill in the stand column.  
  *Parent says:* Hold it from the bottom. Can it stand up straight by itself, or does it droop over?  
  *Child mode:* Hold each stem at the bottom. Does it stand up by itself?
  - Note: Short pieces may stand even if they are soft, so use the longest pieces you have. A long stem has more weight to hold up. The woody twig stays stiff. A long money plant stem flops over.
  - Check: Suppose a long money plant stem flops over when you hold it up by its bottom end. At home, a money plant grows up a pole or a window grill. What kind of plant is a money plant? → **A climber**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the finished table together and fill in the last two columns.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the table. Which stems were woody? Which plants could they come from?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Which stems are woody? Which are soft?
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: woody stems can hold a plant up, and thin soft stems cannot. What I saw: the twig did not dent and stood up straight, but the money plant flopped over. Why: wood is hard, so a tree can stand alone. A money plant has to hold on to something to grow up.
### e2 · Shade Detective: how much cooler is it under a tree? (30 min)
- **question:** How much cooler is the ground in the shade of a tree than in the sun?
- **predict:** Before you measure, guess. Which place will be hottest: the sunny ground, the shade of a tree, or the shade of an umbrella? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, measure all the places at about the same time of day, on the same kind of ground, for example all on paving. Leave the thermometer for the same time in each place, 3 minutes.
- **safety:** Do this with a grown-up. Wear a cap. Do not stand in strong sun for long. Drink water. Keep away from traffic.
- **record:** Write the temperature for each place. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each place.
- **results table:** Place | My guess: hottest, middle or coolest | Temperature (degrees Celsius)
- **s1 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose a sunny time, near midday. 2. Find a spot in full sun, a big tree with thick shade, a shrub and a place for an umbrella. 3. Ask your child to guess which will be hottest and coolest.  
  *Parent says:* Which place do you think will be the hottest? Which will be the coolest? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Guess first. Which place will be hottest? Which will be coolest?
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the thermometer on the sunny ground. 2. Wait 3 minutes. 3. Read it and write it down. 4. Do the same under the tree.  
  *Parent says:* We'll leave it 3 minutes in each place, so it has time to warm up or cool down. That keeps the test fair.  
  *Child mode:* Put the thermometer on sunny ground. Wait 3 minutes. Read it. Then try under the tree.
  - Note: Read the number together. Most digital thermometers show degrees Celsius. In summer, sunny paving can be more than 10 degrees hotter than deep shade.
- **s3 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Measure under a shrub and under an open umbrella, 3 minutes each, the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now let's try a shrub and an umbrella. Will they be as cool as the tree?  
  *Child mode:* Now try under a shrub. Then under an umbrella. Write each number.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart, one bar for each place.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn the numbers into a picture. The taller the bar, the hotter the place.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each place. Taller bar means hotter.
  - Check: In the Shade Detective test, why might the ground under a big tree be cooler than under an umbrella? → **The tree's leaves block sunlight, and the tree also gives off water that cools the air**
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you measure? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I measured. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a big tree gives the coolest shade. What I saw: the sun was 46 degrees and the tree shade was 31 degrees. Why: the leaves stop the sunlight, and the tree gives off water that cools the air.
### e3 · The Smell Test: which leaves are the strongest? (25 min)
- **question:** Which kitchen leaves have the strongest smell, and why do plants make smells at all?
- **predict:** Before you sniff, guess. Line up the leaves, strongest smell first. Why do you think that one will smell strongest?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, use one leaf of each, about the same size. Crush each the same way, between your fingers, for 5 seconds. Each person scores every leaf from 0 (no smell) to 5 (very strong).
- **safety:** Smell only. Never taste the leaves. Use only plants a grown-up knows are safe, like kitchen herbs. Do not use kaner, datura or any unknown plant. Wash your hands afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then circle the leaf with the strongest smell.
- **results table:** Leaf | My guess (0 to 5) | Person 1 score | Person 2 score | Person 3 score (if a third person smells) | Middle score
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put one leaf on each numbered plate. 2. Ask your child to guess which smells strongest, and line them up.  
  *Parent says:* Which leaf do you think has the strongest smell? Let's line them up from strongest to weakest.  
  *Child mode:* Look at the leaves. Which will smell strongest? Line them up.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Crush leaf 1 between your fingers for 5 seconds. 2. Each person sniffs it and gives a score from 0 to 5. 3. Rest your nose for a moment, or sniff your own sleeve. 4. Wipe your fingers and do the next leaf.  
  *Parent says:* Crush it for five seconds. Now sniff. Give it a score. Zero means no smell. Five means very strong.  
  *Child mode:* Crush the leaf for five seconds. Sniff. Score it from 0 to 5.
  - Note: Noses get tired quickly. A short rest, or sniffing your own sleeve, between leaves helps. Do not let anyone taste the leaves.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the middle score for each leaf. With three scores, put them in order and take the middle one. With only two people, add the two scores instead and compare the totals.  
  *Parent says:* Each of us scored differently. To be fair, we take the middle score, or add our scores if there are two of us.  
  *Child mode:* Put the three scores in order. The middle one is the middle score. With two people, add your scores.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Talk about why plants make smells.  
  *Parent says:* Plants can't run away from insects. Many make strong smelly chemicals in their leaves. Insects often don't like them. Some of these same chemicals are what we use as spices and medicines.  
  *Child mode:* Plants cannot run away. So some make strong smells. Many insects dislike them. People use these smells in food and medicine.
  - Check: In the Smell Test, mint had a strong smell when crushed. What is the most likely reason a plant makes a strong smell? → **To protect themselves from insects and animals that eat leaves**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child for claim, evidence and reasoning.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you smell? Why do you think some leaves smell so strong?  
  *Child mode:* Say three things. What I think. What I smelled. Why.
  - Note: A good answer: What I think: mint and curry leaf have the strongest smell. What I saw: they scored 5 and 4, and spinach scored 1. Why: these plants make smelly chemicals in their leaves that keep many insects away. People use the same chemicals in food.

### Five Kinds of Plants (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (sort) Sort these plants into their kinds.  
   **Answer:** neem → Tree; peepal → Tree; rose → Shrub; mehendi → Shrub; coriander → Herb; spinach → Herb; money plant → Climber; bitter gourd → Climber; pumpkin → Creeper; watermelon → Creeper. *Why:* Trees have one woody trunk. Shrubs have many woody stems. Herbs have soft stems. Climbers hold on to grow up. Creepers spread on the ground.
2. (mcq) Which plant is a shrub?  
   Options: Mango / Hibiscus / Pumpkin / Coriander  
   **Answer:** Hibiscus. *Why:* Hibiscus has several woody stems from near the ground, so hibiscus is a shrub. Mango is a tree, pumpkin is a creeper and coriander is a herb. Picking 'mango' often means choosing any plant with flowers or fruit.
3. (mcq) In the Stem Test, a stem dented easily under your fingernail and bent over when you held it up. What kind of plant did it most likely come from?  
   Options: A tree / A herb / A shrub  
   **Answer:** A herb. *Why:* A soft stem that dents and flops has no wood in it, so the plant is most likely a herb. Trees and shrubs have woody stems that hardly dent.
4. (truefalse) All tall plants are trees.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Bamboo grows as tall as a house, but bamboo is a grass. A banana plant is tall too, but they have no wood, so they are a giant herb.
5. (fill) A plant with a thin stem that spreads along the ground is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['creeper']. *Why:* Pumpkin, watermelon and sweet potato are creepers. They spread over the ground.
6. (match) Match each climber to how they hold on.  
   **Answer:** Bitter gourd → curly tendrils; Money plant → little roots on the stem; Bean → twisting their stem round a stick; Bougainvillea → hooked thorns. *Why:* Climbers hold on in different ways: tendrils, little roots, twisting stems or thorns.
7. (mcq) Why does a pumpkin grow along the ground instead of standing up?  
   Options: Their stem is too thin and weak to hold them up / They are afraid of the sun / They are a tree that fell over  
   **Answer:** Their stem is too thin and weak to hold them up. *Why:* A pumpkin's stem is long and soft, and their fruits are heavy. Lying on the ground, the stem does not have to hold them up. Picking 'a tree that fell' means thinking every plant wants to be a tree.
8. (match) Match each thing to the plant that gave it.  
   **Answer:** Cotton T-shirt → cotton plant; Natural rubber → rubber tree; Coir doormat → coconut palm; Sugar → sugarcane; Wooden door → a tree like teak or sal. *Why:* Every one of these everyday things began as part of a plant.
9. (write) Look at a plant near you. Are they a tree, shrub, herb, climber or creeper? Write how you know.  
   **Answer:** Any honest answer with a reason about the stem. For example, they are a shrub, because they have many hard, woody stems coming up from the soil.. *Why:* The best clue is the stem: hard or soft, one or many, standing up, climbing or spreading.
10. (draw) Draw one tree, one shrub and one herb. Show how their stems are different.  
   **Answer:** A tree with one thick trunk, a shrub with many woody stems from the ground, and a small herb with a soft green stem.. *Why:* The stem is the main way to tell these three kinds apart.
11. (write) Nila, a girl in Class 3, says, 'A rose is a tree, because their stems are woody.' What has Nila got right, and what is her mistake?  
   **Answer:** Nila is right that a rose has woody stems. But a tree has one thick trunk. A rose has many woody stems coming up from near the ground, and stays small. So a rose is a shrub.. *Why:* A strong answer uses two clues, not one: how hard the stem is, and how many main stems there are. Woody alone does not make a tree.
12. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Coriander, spinach, mint, guava.  
   **Answer:** Guava, because a guava is a tree with one woody trunk. Coriander, spinach and mint are herbs with soft green stems.. *Why:* The stem is the clue. Press a coriander or mint stem and they dent and bend. A guava trunk is hard wood.
13. (perspective) A family in a Chennai flat has room for one pot on their sunny balcony. They are deciding what to grow.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer links the choice to the plant's kind. A curry leaf shrub has woody stems and grows big, so they need a deep pot, but they give leaves for years. A tulsi is a herb. They grow fast in a small pot, and people use their leaves as a home remedy. A money plant is a climber, so they need the grill as a support, and they can shade a window. A strong answer then admits another person's good point.. *Why:* Each choice is sensible. They trade off food, medicine, shade and space. What matters is using the plant's kind to say how they will grow in one small pot.
14. (mcq) Which of these is NOT a way people use plants?  
   Options: Food / Medicine / Shade / Making iron  
   **Answer:** Making iron. *Why:* Iron is a metal dug from rocks. Food, medicine and shade all come from plants. Picking 'shade' often means forgetting that shade is a use too. People plant trees on purpose to cool their streets.
15. (truefalse) A shrub is just a baby tree that has not grown up yet.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A hibiscus or a rose stays a shrub all their life. They keep many woody stems from near the ground and never grow one big trunk. A young mango tree is small, but they already have one main stem.
16. (fill) A plant that holds on to a wall or a stick to grow upward is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['climber']. *Why:* Money plant, pepper and bitter gourd are climbers.
### Plants and People (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) In the story 'The Pepper Tree That Wasn't', why did the painter think pepper grew on a jackfruit tree?  
   Options: The pepper climber was holding on to the jackfruit trunk, so they looked like one plant / Jackfruit trees really do make pepper / Someone tied pepper on the tree  
   **Answer:** The pepper climber was holding on to the jackfruit trunk, so they looked like one plant. *Why:* Pepper is a climber. They grow up a tree, holding on with little roots. From a distance, the two plants look like one. Picking 'jackfruit trees make pepper' is exactly the painter's mistake.
2. (write) Tara, a girl in Class 4, puts a thermometer on the ground in three places at noon: in the sun, under an umbrella and under a neem tree. Predict which place will be coolest, and explain why.  
   **Answer:** Under the neem tree will be coolest. The umbrella and the tree both block the sunlight. But the tree's leaves also let out water, and water turning into vapour takes heat away. So the air under the tree is cooled twice over.. *Why:* A strong answer says both block the Sun, and then gives the extra reason for the tree: water evaporating from the leaves cools them and the air around them.
3. (write) A watermelon plant creeps over the hot sand of a riverbank. Their big leaves lie spread out on the ground. How might creeping help them there? What danger does it bring?  
   **Answer:** Creeping helps because the plant does not spend food on a stem strong enough to stand up. They can spread fast over the open sand and catch lots of sunlight. Their leaves also shade the sand, which keeps it a little damp. The danger is that animals and people can step on them or eat them, and taller plants nearby can shade them.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a good point and a bad point. Every way of growing has a cost as well as a gain.
4. (mcq) Rice, wheat and ragi are all herbs. Why do farmers grow herbs, not trees, to get grain?  
   Options: A herb grows from seed to grain in a few months, so a field gives a crop every season / Trees cannot make seeds / Herbs need no water / Trees grow only in forests  
   **Answer:** A herb grows from seed to grain in a few months, so a field gives a crop every season. *Why:* A herb does not spend years building a woody trunk. They put their food into seeds quickly, often within four months. So farmers can sow and harvest again and again. Picking 'trees cannot make seeds' is wrong: mango and tamarind trees make plenty of seeds, but only after many years.
5. (mcq) What does the word 'weed' really mean?  
   Options: A special poisonous kind of plant / A plant growing where people do not want it / Any plant with no flowers  
   **Answer:** A plant growing where people do not want it. *Why:* 'Weed' is a word about our wishes. The same grass can be a weed in a vegetable patch and a lawn on a cricket field. Picking 'poisonous' often comes from hearing weeds called bad plants.
6. (mcq) Why do some plants, like mint and tulsi, have strong smells?  
   Options: To protect themselves from insects and animals that eat leaves / Because they are rotting / To call the rain  
   **Answer:** To protect themselves from insects and animals that eat leaves. *Why:* Many plants make strong-smelling chemicals that keep leaf-eaters away. People use these same chemicals as spices and medicines.
7. (write) Teak wood is used for doors. Coriander is used for chutney. Why do we take wood from trees, but never from herbs?  
   **Answer:** A tree builds a hard, woody trunk, year after year. A herb's stem stays soft and green, with very little wood in it. So there is no wood in a coriander plant to make a door from.. *Why:* A strong answer links the use to the kind of stem. The kind of plant tells us what we can use them for.
8. (order) Put these in order, from the plant that gives the most shade to the least: a big banyan tree, a rose shrub, a coriander herb.  
   **Answer:** ['a big banyan tree', 'a rose shrub', 'a coriander herb']. *Why:* The bigger the plant and the more leaves high above you, the more shade they give.
9. (write) Name three things in your home that came from plants. Say which plant, and which part of the plant, if you know.  
   **Answer:** Any three good answers. For example: my cotton shirt came from the fluffy seed hairs of the cotton plant. Paper came from wood. Rice came from the seeds of a grass.. *Why:* Plants give us food, cloth, wood, paper, rubber, oil, colours and medicine.
10. (match) Match each thing to the part of the plant that gave it.  
   **Answer:** Jute sack → the stem of the jute plant; Turmeric powder → the underground stem of turmeric; Cinnamon → the bark of the cinnamon tree; Coconut oil → the white flesh inside a coconut; Tamarind in rasam → the fruit of the tamarind tree. *Why:* People use almost every part of a plant: seeds, stems, fruits and wood. Knowing the part tells you how the plant must be grown and harvested.
11. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Money plant, pepper, bitter gourd, pumpkin.  
   **Answer:** Pumpkin, because a pumpkin is a creeper that spreads along the ground. The others are climbers.. *Why:* Money plant, pepper and bitter gourd grow upwards by holding on to a support. A pumpkin usually spreads on the soil. But if their tendrils find a fence, they will climb it, so the boxes are not perfectly neat.
12. (perspective) In 1974, men came to cut down a forest near the village of Reni in the Himalayas. The trees had been sold for their wood. The women of the village hugged the trees to stop them. This was part of the Chipko movement.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason. For Gaura Devi: the forest gave the village food, fuel and medicine every day, and tree roots hold soil on slopes. For the carpenter: wood really is useful, and people need it. A strong answer then gives the other side its due, and may suggest a middle way, like planting trees to cut later and protecting forests on steep hills.. *Why:* This was a real conflict between people who lived from the forest and people who wanted its wood. Both needs are real. Gaura Devi and the women of Reni saved their forest, and the movement led to new laws protecting Himalayan forests.
13. (draw) Draw the view from your window or balcony. Mark one tree, one shrub, one herb and one climber or creeper, if you can see them. Label how you knew each kind.  
   **Answer:** A drawing with plants labelled by kind, and a short clue for each, like 'one thick trunk' or 'soft green stem'.. *Why:* Sorting real plants you can see is harder than sorting names. The labels show which stem clue you used.
14. (write) A friend says, 'Every plant with a green stem is a herb.' In the Stem Test, how could you find out if your friend is right?  
   **Answer:** Press a fingernail into the stem and bend it. A young stem from a tree can be green too, but it is firmer than a herb stem and turns woody with age. A green stem that does not dent and snaps when bent has wood in it, so it is not a herb.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the tests from the experiment (dent, bend, stand) as evidence, not the colour alone.
15. (truefalse) A climber needs less food to build a stem than a tree needs to build a trunk, so a climber can grow long quickly.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A thin stem costs little food, because a climber uses a tree, a wall or a stick to hold it up. A tree spends food on wood, year after year.
### Puzzling Plants and Big Ideas (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A coconut palm and a mango are both trees. Which is the coconut more closely related to?  
   Options: Mango / Rice and bamboo / Neem  
   **Answer:** Rice and bamboo. *Why:* Coconut palms belong to a big group of plants that also includes grasses like rice and bamboo. 'Tree' is a way of growing, not a family. Picking 'mango' means thinking trees must be cousins.
2. (truefalse) A tree trunk that is 2 metres around must be older than a trunk that is 1 metre around.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Different kinds of trees grow at different speeds. A fast tree in a wet, sunny place can grow fat quickly. A slow tree on a dry hill may take far longer. The rule of 2 and a half cm a year in Hug a Tree is only a rough guess.
3. (fill) The hard material that makes wood strong, like a glue in the walls of its cells, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lignin']. *Why:* Lignin and cellulose together make wood strong. Herbs have very little lignin, so their stems are soft.
4. (perspective) The Great Banyan in the Botanic Garden at Howrah, near Kolkata, has thousands of prop roots that have become trunks. Their first trunk died and was removed in 1925. Scientists disagree about how to count them.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side with a reason. For one plant: every trunk grew from the same seed, and they still share one body of branches. For many: each trunk can stand and feed themselves on their own. A strong answer then sees that the word 'one plant' was made for animals like us, and does not fit plants neatly.. *Why:* This is still argued about. There is no single right answer. The puzzle shows that our ideas of 'one living thing' come from animals, and plants do not always fit them.
5. (write) Tulsi starts with a soft stem and later grows a woody one. Is tulsi a herb or a shrub? Explain your answer.  
   **Answer:** Both, at different times. A young tulsi has a soft stem, so they are like a herb. An old tulsi has a woody stem, so they are a small shrub. The kinds are boxes we made, and some plants move between them.. *Why:* Plants change as they grow. Our kinds are useful guides, not walls.
6. (write) Many people think climbers are lazy, because they use other plants to hold them up. A climber still has hard work to do. Name two things a climber must do that a tree does not.  
   **Answer:** For example: a climber must find a support, so their tip circles slowly, searching. They must grab and hold on, with tendrils that coil, little roots that grip, or a stem that twists. They must also grow long very fast, to reach the light before other plants shade them.. *Why:* A strong answer names real work, like searching, gripping or growing fast. Climbers do not build a trunk, but they spend their food and effort in other ways.
7. (write) In a thick forest, very little light reaches the ground. Why might being a climber help a plant there? Why might being a climber be harder in an open field with no trees?  
   **Answer:** In a forest, a climber can use the tree trunks as supports and reach the light at the top, without building a trunk of their own. In an open field there is plenty of light, but nothing to climb, so the climber has to creep along the ground and may be trampled or shaded by grass.. *Why:* A strong answer says a climber's way of growing works only when there is a support, and that the best kind of plant depends on the place.
8. (mcq) In the Circling Shoot, a climber's tip moved in slow loops. Revathi, a girl in Class 4, says the wind did it. How could you test her idea?  
   Options: Keep the pot indoors with no fan or breeze, and see if it still circles / Shout at the plant / Water them more  
   **Answer:** Keep the pot indoors with no fan or breeze, and see if it still circles. *Why:* To test one idea, take away just that one thing. If the tip still circles with no wind, the plant is moving on their own. This is a fair test.
9. (truefalse) Some herbs come from woody ancestors, and on some islands herbs have turned back into woody shrubs.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Plants have switched between woody and soft many times in their history. It is one reason 'tree' and 'herb' are not families.
10. (write) A company makes a medicine from a plant that village healers have used for hundreds of years. Who do you think should get credit, and why?  
   **Answer:** Any thoughtful answer with a reason. For example, the healers should get credit, because they found the plant first. The scientists should too, because they tested it and made it safe. The village could share the money.. *Why:* This is a real question. India and other countries have laws about sharing benefits from plants with the people who knew them first.
11. (order) Put these in order of when they first appeared on Earth, oldest first: flowering plants, the first trees, the first tiny land plants.  
   **Answer:** ['the first tiny land plants', 'the first trees', 'flowering plants']. *Why:* Tiny land plants came first, about 470 million years ago. Trees came about 390 million years ago. Flowering plants came much later, more than 130 million years ago.
12. (write) Aristotle said plants exist for the sake of animals and people. Do you agree? Give one reason for and one reason against.  
   **Answer:** Any answer with a reason each way. For: people use plants for almost everything. Against: plants lived for hundreds of millions of years before any people, and most plants are never used by people.. *Why:* This is a debate, so there is no single right answer. A good answer gives reasons on both sides.
13. (draw) Draw a forest with a tree, a climber, a shrub and a creeper. Draw arrows to show how each one reaches the sunlight.  
   **Answer:** A tall tree reaching up, a climber climbing the tree, a shrub in a sunny gap and a creeper spreading in an open patch, each with an arrow to the sun.. *Why:* All the kinds are different ways of reaching the same light.
14. (mcq) About 390 million years ago, plants that could make wood began to grow much taller than all the others. Why did wood let them grow tall?  
   Options: Wood is stiff and strong, so a woody stem can hold leaves high up in the light / Wood is heavy, so the wind cannot move the plant / Wood makes seeds / Wood soaks up sunlight  
   **Answer:** Wood is stiff and strong, so a woody stem can hold leaves high up in the light. *Why:* The lignin in wood makes the stem stiff. A stiff stem can stand tall and lift leaves above the plants around them, where they catch more light. Picking 'heavy' misses the point. Wood is strong for its weight, not just heavy.
15. (write) Scientists think about 9,000 kinds of trees have not yet been found and named. Why might nobody have found them yet?  
   **Answer:** They may grow in places few botanists have visited, like remote hills and thick rainforest, or only at the very tops of tall trees. There are also far fewer botanists than there are places to search.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason about where trees grow and who looks for them.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) You want to tell a tree, a shrub and a herb apart. Which part of the plant is the best clue?  
   Options: The colour of their flowers / Their stem / The smell of their leaves  
   **Answer:** Their stem. *Why:* The stem tells us most: one woody trunk for a tree, many woody stems for a shrub, a soft green stem for a herb. Picking flowers often means thinking all plants with flowers are alike.
2. (mcq) Which plant is a climber?  
   Options: Money plant / Mango / Rose / Spinach  
   **Answer:** Money plant. *Why:* Money plant holds on to a wall or pole with little roots and grows up it. Mango is a tree, rose is a shrub and spinach is a herb.
3. (mcq) Which plant is a creeper?  
   Options: Neem / Watermelon / Tulsi / Bamboo  
   **Answer:** Watermelon. *Why:* A watermelon plant spreads along the ground. Picking 'tulsi' often means thinking every small plant creeps. Tulsi stands up by themselves. Neem is a tree, and bamboo is a tall grass.
4. (mcq) A climber has only a thin stem. How do they get their leaves up high?  
   Options: They hold on to a support and grow up it / They do not need light / They grow underground  
   **Answer:** They hold on to a support and grow up it. *Why:* A climber holds on to a tree, a wall or a stick, so they can reach the light with a thin stem. Picking 'they do not need light' is a common mistake. Climbers climb because they need light.
5. (mcq) Which of these is made from a plant?  
   Options: A paper notebook / A steel spoon / A glass bottle  
   **Answer:** A paper notebook. *Why:* Paper is made from wood, which comes from tree trunks. Steel is made from iron in rocks, and glass is made from sand. Picking glass often means thinking anything see-through comes from leaves.
6. (mcq) Why do towns plant big trees like neem along hot streets?  
   Options: Their shade and their leaves cool the street / Trees make the road stronger / Trees keep the street dark at night  
   **Answer:** Their shade and their leaves cool the street. *Why:* The leaves block sunlight, and water evaporating from them cools the air. Shade is one of the most important ways people use trees. Picking 'make the road stronger' mixes up shade with roots.
7. (mcq) In Shade Detective, why do we leave the thermometer for 3 minutes in each place before reading it?  
   Options: So it has time to warm up or cool down to that place / So the thermometer can rest / Because 3 is a lucky number  
   **Answer:** So it has time to warm up or cool down to that place. *Why:* A thermometer takes a little time to reach the temperature around it. If you read it at once, it still shows the last place. Waiting the same time everywhere also keeps the test fair.
8. (mcq) A papaya plant has one tall trunk, but the trunk is soft and spongy inside, with no hard wood. What is the best way to describe papaya?  
   Options: A plant that does not fit neatly into the five kinds / A climber / A creeper  
   **Answer:** A plant that does not fit neatly into the five kinds. *Why:* Papaya stands up like a tree, but their trunk is not woody like a tree's. So they sit between the boxes. The five kinds are a useful guide, but some plants do not fit them well.
9. (mcq) Which of these is true about trees?  
   Options: All trees belong to one family / Plants from many different families became trees separately / Trees were the first plants on Earth  
   **Answer:** Plants from many different families became trees separately. *Why:* 'Tree' is a way of growing. Many unrelated families found it. Tiny land plants came long before trees.
10. (mcq) Which of these do scientists still not know?  
   Options: Whether bamboo is a grass / How many kinds of plants exist on Earth / Whether trees have trunks  
   **Answer:** How many kinds of plants exist on Earth. *Why:* More than 350,000 plant species are named, and about 2,000 new ones are named each year. Nobody knows the true total.
11. (mcq) Many medicines first came from plants. Why must a new plant medicine be tested by scientists before people use it?  
   Options: Some plant chemicals are poisonous, and the safe amount must be found / Plants are always safe, so testing is only for fun / To make the medicine taste nice  
   **Answer:** Some plant chemicals are poisonous, and the safe amount must be found. *Why:* The same plant can help in a small amount and harm in a large one. Kaner and datura are natural but very poisonous. Testing finds out what works and how much is safe. Picking 'always safe' is the mistake of thinking natural means harmless.

## Applications
- Farmers choose kinds of plants by how they grow. Pepper and gourds need supports, pumpkins need space to creep.
- Gardeners plant shrubs as hedges and climbers to cover walls and fences.
- City planners plant trees along roads to shade people and cool the streets.
- Carpenters choose woods like teak, sal and sheesham for strong furniture and doors.
- Paper mills make paper from wood, bamboo and sugarcane waste.
- Cloth is made from cotton, jute, linen from flax, and even banana fibre.
- Rope and doormats are made from coir, the fibre from coconut husks.
- Rubber for tyres and chappals comes partly from rubber trees grown in Kerala and the north-east.
- Medicine makers test plants used by traditional healers to find new medicines.
- Seed banks store seeds of many kinds of plants to protect them for the future.
- Foresters measure trunks at chest height to work out how much wood a forest holds.
- Botanists use the kinds of plants to describe forests, from tall rainforest to scrubby desert.

## Open questions
- **How many kinds of plants are there on Earth?** More than 350,000 are named, and about 2,000 new ones are named every year. Many places have never been properly explored.
- **Is a clone like Pando or the Great Banyan one plant or many?** Scientists disagree about what 'one individual' means for plants that share roots and genes.
- **Do trees in a forest really share food through fungi, like a 'wood wide web'?** Some studies found carbon moving between trees through fungi. Other scientists say the evidence is still weak. Research continues.
- **Why did lignin, the hard glue in wood, appear when it did?** Scientists know roughly when wood appeared, but not exactly how plants first made lignin, or why so few living things can break it down.
- **How old are the oldest trees in India?** Many old tropical trees have no clear growth rings, so their ages are hard to measure. Scientists use other methods, which can give different answers.
- **Which plants in India's forests hold medicines nobody has found yet?** Only a small part of India's plants has been tested carefully. Many forests are being lost before they can be studied.

## Connected maths
- Lakshmi, a girl in Class 3, did a Plant Count from her windows. She counted 12 trees, 8 shrubs, 20 herbs, 4 climbers and 2 creepers. How many plants did she count in all? Which kind did she see most of? **46 plants. Herbs were the most.**. Add them up: 12 + 8 is 20. 20 + 20 is 40. 40 + 4 is 44. 44 + 2 is 46. The biggest number is 20, for herbs, so she saw herbs most. (queues `math.data.sorting-and-tallies.L3`)
- In Shade Detective, the sunny paving was 46 degrees and the ground under a big tree was 31 degrees. How many degrees cooler was the tree's shade? **15 degrees cooler.**. Take the smaller number from the bigger one. 46 minus 31 is 15. Check: 31 + 15 is 46. (queues `math.temperature.reading-thermometers.L3`)
- In Hug a Tree, a neem trunk measured 150 cm around. Using the rough rule that many trees grow 2 and a half cm around each year, about how old are they? **About 60 years old.**. Two lots of 2 and a half make 5. So every 5 cm around is about 2 years. 150 divided by 5 is 30. 30 lots of 2 years is 60 years. The tree is roughly 60 years old. Remember this is only a rough guess. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- Some bamboo can grow about 90 cm in one day. If they kept growing at that speed, how tall would a new shoot be after 4 days? **360 cm, which is 3 metres and 60 centimetres.**. 90 cm each day for 4 days is 4 groups of 90. 90 + 90 is 180. 180 + 180 is 360. So 360 cm. That is more than twice as tall as a grown-up. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Smell Test, three people scored tulsi 2, 5 and 4. What is the middle score? **4.**. Put the scores in order: 2, 4, 5. The middle one is 4. The middle score is fair, because one very high or very low score cannot pull it too far. (queues `math.number-sense.comparing-ordering.L3`)

- Tu Youyou, Nobel Lecture 2015 (artemisinin)

- Raunkiaer, plant life forms (1904, collected in The Life Forms of Plants and Statistical Plant Geography, 1934)
- Darwin, On the Movements and Habits of Climbing Plants (1865, 1875)
- K. S. Manilal, English edition of Hortus Malabaricus, University of Kerala, 2003 (on Itty Achuden and the Malayalam certificate)
- Theophrastus, Enquiry into Plants (about 300 BCE), John Ray, Historia Plantarum (1686 to 1704), and A. L. de Jussieu, Genera Plantarum (1789), for how plants were first sorted
