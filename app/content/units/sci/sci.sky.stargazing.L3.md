# Constellations and nakshatras: the star patterns people see from Earth
`sci.sky.stargazing.L3`

**Summary.** The seven stars of the Saptarishi look like a ladle, but they are not close together in space. Five of them are about 80 light-years away. The other two are much further. A constellation is a pattern of stars that people have named, and it has its shape only when seen from near Earth. People in every culture joined the stars into pictures and told stories about them. In India, astronomers split the Moon's path across the sky into 27 parts, the nakshatras. The Moon moves into about one new nakshatra each day. Stars also move very slowly, so in tens of thousands of years the constellations will change shape.

**Intro.** *Look at a picture of the Saptarishi, the seven bright stars that make a ladle in the northern sky. They look like seven lamps hanging side by side. Are they really side by side, or could some be much further away than others? How could we tell from down here?* Every culture has looked at the night sky and joined the stars into pictures. In this unit you build a model of the Saptarishi with thread, using the real distances of its stars, and look at it from the side. You learn the nakshatras, the 27 star groups that the Moon passes through each month. You also find out how an astronomer showed, about 300 years ago, that the stars are moving.

## Terms
- **Constellation**: A pattern of stars that people have named, such as the Saptarishi or Orion. Today astronomers divide the whole sky into 88 constellations, like a map divided into states. *e.g.* Orion is a constellation. The Greeks saw it as a hunter with a belt of three stars.
- **Asterism**: A well-known star pattern that is only part of a constellation, or made from stars of more than one. *e.g.* The Saptarishi is an asterism. Its seven stars belong to a bigger constellation called Ursa Major, the Great Bear.
- **Nakshatra**: One of 27 star groups along the Moon's path across the sky. Indian astronomers named them to keep track of where the Moon is each night. *e.g.* Krittika is a nakshatra. It is a small, tight group of stars, called the Pleiades in English.
- **Light-year**: The distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 lakh crore kilometres. It measures distance, not time. *e.g.* Vasishtha, a star in the handle of the Saptarishi, is about 83 light-years away. The light you see from him tonight set off about 83 years ago.
- **Star cluster**: A group of stars that really are close together in space. They were born together from the same cloud of gas and travel together. *e.g.* Krittika, the Pleiades, is a star cluster about 440 light-years away.
- **Dark adaptation**: The way your eyes slowly get better at seeing in the dark. It takes about 20 to 30 minutes to see as many stars as you can. *e.g.* When you go from a bright room to a dark terrace, you see only a few stars at first. After 20 minutes you see many more.
- **Light pollution**: Light from streetlights, buildings and signs that shines up into the sky and hides the fainter stars. *e.g.* From a big city you may see fewer than a hundred stars. Far from any town, on a clear dark night, you can see a few thousand.

## Explanations
### Constellations are patterns seen from Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** A constellation is a pattern of stars that people have named. Today it also means one of the 88 areas the sky is divided into, agreed by the International Astronomical Union in 1922.

The sky looks like a dark dome with stars stuck on it. But the stars are not on a dome. They are spread out through space at very different distances. A star pattern joins stars that happen to lie in the same direction from Earth. Some of them are near, and some are many times further away. The stars of the Saptarishi range from about 80 to about 123 light-years away. We see them as a ladle because we look from one place, near the Sun. From a planet round another star, the same stars would make a different pattern, or none at all.
- e.g. Kratu, the star at the top corner of the Saptarishi's bowl, is about 123 light-years away. Pulaha, just below him, is about 80 light-years away.
- e.g. In Orion, Betelgeuse (the red star called Ardra or Thiruvathirai) is about 550 light-years away, while Bellatrix, on the other shoulder, is about 250. Astronomers are still refining the Betelgeuse distance.
- e.g. Two fingertips, one near your eye and one at arm's length, can seem to touch. Move your head and they come apart. Stars in a constellation line up in the same way.
### Nakshatras: the Moon's 27 stations · *defined*
**Definition:** The nakshatras are 27 equal parts of the Moon's path round the sky, each named after a star or a group of stars in it.

The Moon goes round the Earth. Against the stars, the Moon moves a little to the east each night. It comes back to the same stars after about 27 and one-third days. So Indian astronomers split the Moon's path into 27 parts, one for each day. The Moon passes into about one new nakshatra every day. The oldest lists, in the Vedas, begin with Krittika. Later lists begin with Ashwini. Chinese astronomers made a similar system of 28 'lunar mansions', and Arab astronomers had 28 'stations of the Moon'. Many South Indian families remember the nakshatra the Moon was in when a baby was born. Some celebrate birthdays on that star day.
- e.g. Onam is celebrated in Kerala on Thiruvonam, the day the Moon is in the nakshatra Shravana, in the month of Chingam.
- e.g. Karthigai Deepam is lit in Tamil Nadu on the day the Moon is in Krittika, in the month of Karthigai.
- e.g. Chitra is the bright star Spica. Swati is the bright orange star Arcturus. Rohini is the red star Aldebaran.
### The 'fixed stars' are moving · *defined*
**Definition:** Every star moves through space. From Earth, the nearer and faster stars shift slowly against the others, so constellations change shape over tens of thousands of years.

Stars look fixed because they are so far away. A car far down a long road seems to crawl, even when it is fast. In the same way, a star racing through space shifts by only a tiny amount in a human life. In 1718, Edmond Halley found that a few bright stars had moved since the time of Ptolemy, over 1,500 years before. Now we can measure every bright star's motion. Five stars of the Saptarishi travel together in the same direction. Kratu and Marichi, at the two ends, move differently. So in tens of thousands of years, the ladle will be bent out of shape.
- e.g. Arcturus, the nakshatra Swati, has moved about twice the width of the full Moon since Ptolemy's time.
- e.g. Krittika's stars move together, so the cluster will keep its shape for a very long time.
### Seeing stars from a city · *defined*
**Definition:** Your eyes see more stars after 20 to 30 minutes in the dark, and you see more stars where there is less light pollution.

Your eye has two kinds of light-sensing cells. One kind sees colour in bright light. The other kind works in dim light and slowly becomes much more sensitive in the dark. That is why more stars seem to appear the longer you wait. A white light, even a phone screen, undoes much of this in a moment. Red light disturbs it much less, which is why stargazers use red torches. City lights shining upwards light up the air, and the faint stars are lost in that glow. Bright stars and planets still shine through.
- e.g. During a power cut, many more stars appear above a city street after a few minutes.
- e.g. In 2016, a team led by Fabio Falchi found that over 80 percent of the world's people live under light-polluted skies.
### Why do so many cultures see seven sisters in Krittika? · *debated*
Krittika, the Pleiades, is a small, tight group of stars. Most people can see six of them, sometimes seven. Stories from Greece, from many Aboriginal peoples of Australia and from North America speak of seven sisters, and often of one sister who is lost or hidden. In India, the Krittikas are six mothers who cared for the baby Kartikeya, called Murugan in Tamil Nadu. Nobody knows why such far-apart peoples tell such similar stories.
- e.g. In Greek stories, the Pleiades are seven sisters, and one of them is hard to see.
- e.g. For the Maori of New Zealand, the rising of Matariki, the Pleiades, in midwinter marks the new year.
- **Ray Norris and Barnaby Norris, astronomers (2021):** The story may be over 100,000 years old, from before people spread out of Africa. Back then, two of the stars were further apart, so seven were easier to see. As they drew together, one sister 'disappeared'.
- **Many other scholars:** There is no need for one ancient story. In any culture, some people see six stars and some see seven. A story about a missing sister is a natural way to explain that, so it could have been invented many times.

## Mostly agreed
- The stars of a constellation are usually at very different distances from us. *How we know:* Astronomers measure star distances by parallax. As the Earth goes round the Sun, a near star seems to shift a little against the far ones. The Gaia spacecraft has measured the distances of over a billion stars this way.
- Stars move through space, so constellations slowly change shape. *How we know:* In 1718, Edmond Halley found that Sirius and Arcturus had moved since Ptolemy's star list of about 150 CE. Today their movements are measured every year.
- The Moon comes back to the same stars about every 27 and one-third days. *How we know:* Anyone can see it. Note which star the Moon is near tonight. About 27 nights later it is back near the same star.
- The sky is divided into 88 official constellations. *How we know:* The International Astronomical Union agreed the list in 1922. The Belgian astronomer Eugene Delporte drew the borders between them, published in 1930.
- Krittika, the Pleiades, is a real star cluster: its stars were born together. *How we know:* Its stars are all about 440 light-years away and move together. They are about 100 million years old, young for stars.
- City lights hide most stars from most people. *How we know:* In 2016, Fabio Falchi and his team made a world map of sky brightness from satellite pictures and measurements on the ground. Over 80 percent of people live under light-polluted skies.

## How we found out
**Edmond Halley, an English astronomer, using the star list of Ptolemy of Alexandria, 1718 (Ptolemy's star list is from about 150 CE).** For about 2,000 years, astronomers called the stars the 'fixed stars'. The Moon, the Sun and the planets wandered across the sky, but the stars seemed to keep the same places against each other for ever. So the constellations would never change shape. In 1718, the English astronomer Edmond Halley tested this. He compared star positions measured in his own time with the star list in Ptolemy's book, the Almagest, made in Alexandria about 1,550 years earlier. Most stars matched. But a few bright stars, including Sirius and Arcturus, did not. They were out of place by about the width of the full Moon or more. Halley reasoned that Ptolemy could hardly have made such large mistakes on the brightest stars, while getting their neighbours right. So these stars must have moved. Later astronomers measured the movements of thousands of stars. Every star moves. They only look fixed because they are so far away. Arcturus is the nakshatra Swati, and it is one of the fastest-moving bright stars in our sky.  
*What people believed before:* People thought the stars were fixed, so the constellations would keep the same shapes for ever. Halley showed that Sirius and Arcturus had moved since Ptolemy's time, so the stars do move and the patterns slowly change.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The stars of a constellation are close neighbours, like a family living together.** (Most people at first, and many children today; *rejected*)  
  For: They look close together, and they move across the sky together every night.  
  Against: Measured distances show they are usually far apart. Kratu in the Saptarishi is about 43 light-years further away than Pulaha, the star just below him. They move across the sky together only because the Earth spins.
- **The stars are fixed, so the constellations keep their shapes for ever.** (Most astronomers, from ancient Greece and India until about 300 years ago; *rejected*)  
  For: In one lifetime, or even in a thousand years, nobody can see a constellation change by eye.  
  Against: In 1718 Edmond Halley showed that bright stars had moved since Ptolemy's time. Today we measure every bright star's movement.
- **The nakshatra or star sign a person is born under shapes their character and their future.** (Astrology, followed by many people in India and around the world; *rejected*)  
  For: It is a very old tradition, and many people feel their star describes them well.  
  Against: In 1985 the physicist Shawn Carlson ran a careful test. Astrologers tried to match people's birth charts to their personality tests. They did no better than guessing. Using a birth star to choose a name or to time a festival is part of culture. Whether the stars shape a person is a question that tests have answered no.
- **The story of the seven sisters in Krittika is over 100,000 years old.** (Ray Norris and Barnaby Norris, astronomers, in 2021; *debated*)  
  For: Peoples who had been apart for tens of thousands of years tell similar stories about the cluster and a lost sister. Long ago, one star was further from its neighbour, so it was easier to see.  
  Against: A missing-sister story could be invented again and again, because some people see six stars and some see seven. There is no way to test a story that old directly.

## Roots
We have been learning that a constellation is a pattern of stars seen from Earth, and that the Moon walks through the 27 nakshatras. Now we ask what star patterns tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the thread model, the seven paper stars made the Saptarishi only from the Earth card. From the side, two of them were far behind the other five. The real seven stars are spread out in space in the same way. So a constellation is a shape that the stars make only when seen from near Earth.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk about a constellation as a group of stars, like a family living in one house. Most constellations are stars at very different distances, which only line up when seen from where we are.  
*Evidence:* In the thread model, the ladle showed only from the Earth card and broke apart from the side. Measured distances say the same: Kratu is about 123 light-years away and Pulaha, just below him, about 80.

- *who am i:* You are standing in the one place where the Saptarishi is a ladle. Part of every star pattern you see comes from where you are.
- *what is life:* Indigo buntings fly thousands of kilometres at night and need to keep their direction. As chicks they learn the stars that turn round the Pole Star, and use them to steer.
- *what is this world:* The stars are not stuck on a dome. They are scattered through deep space, near and far, and the flat sky is how that space looks from one small place.

There is one set of stars in space, and every people on Earth looks at it from the same place. Tamil families see Vasishtha and Arundhati. The Greeks saw a bear, and the Mi'kmaq of Canada see a bear followed by hunters. The stars are the same, and each people joined them into its own pictures and stories. In the same way, every place in space has its own sky made from the same stars. Each view is true from where it is seen, and none of them is the whole.

- **Rig Veda 1.164.46:** Truth is one, and the wise call it by many names. (The same seven stars are called the Saptarishi, the Plough, the Great Bear and a bear with hunters. There is one set of stars and many names and stories for it.)
- **The parable of the blind men and the elephant, told in Jain teaching on anekantavada (many-sidedness) and in the Buddhist Udana 6.4:** Each blind man touches one part of an elephant, a leg, the trunk or an ear, and is sure the elephant is like a pillar, a snake or a fan. Each is partly right, and none sees the whole animal. (From the Earth card the paper stars made a ladle. From the side they made a scattered group. Each view was true from its place, and you needed both to understand the stars.)
- Science (settled): The shape of a constellation depends on where you see it from. The Gaia spacecraft has measured the distances of over a billion stars, so astronomers can work out how our constellations would look from other stars.
- Science (debated): Some astronomers think the seven sisters story told about Krittika in many cultures may be over 100,000 years old.
- Other view, Many astronomers: A constellation is a handy map region and a set of human stories. The stars themselves have nothing to do with our patterns. The meaning is in the people who tell the stories, not in the sky, and it is fine to enjoy them as stories.
- Practice, Two eyes, two views (4 min): Let's sit quietly in a dim room. Hold up one finger at arm's length, in front of a far light or a picture on the wall. Close your left eye and look. Now open it and close your right eye. Did your finger jump? Each eye sees from a slightly different place, so each sees its own picture. Now close both eyes. Breathe slowly six times. When you open them, look round the room. Everything you see, you see from exactly here.
- Ask: If the Saptarishi is a ladle only from near Earth, is the ladle in the sky or in us?
- Ask: From a planet round Kratu, someone may be joining other stars into her own pictures. Would her pictures be wrong?
- Ask: The Krittikas are six mothers in India and seven sisters in Greece. Can both stories be true in their own way?

## Story: Arundhati Behind the Streetlights
*Lakshmi, Paati, Revathi, Vikram and their family are made up. The Arundhati custom is real and is followed at many weddings in South India and elsewhere. Arundhati is the star Alcor and Vasishtha is Mizar. Their distances, about 82 and 83 light-years, are from star surveys. Alcor can be seen by eye on a dark night.*

On the night before her cousin Revathi's wedding, Lakshmi sat on the terrace of her grandmother's house in Kumbakonam. It was May, and the power had gone off, as it often did. The whole street was dark. Above the water tank, the sky was full of stars.

'Look north,' said Paati, her grandmother. She pointed past the coconut trees. 'Do you see seven bright stars, like a big ladle? That is the Saptarishi, the seven sages.' Lakshmi found four stars in a box and three stars in a curved handle. Paati named them one by one: Kratu, Pulaha, Pulastya, Atri, Angiras, Vasishtha and Marichi.

'Now look hard at Vasishtha, the middle star of the handle,' said Paati. 'Can you see a tiny star just beside him?' Lakshmi stared. At first she saw nothing. Then, as her eyes got used to the dark, a faint speck appeared, close to Vasishtha. 'That is Arundhati, his wife,' said Paati. 'At many weddings, the couple is shown Arundhati. The two stars are always together, and the couple should be too.'

The next day the wedding hall was loud with nadaswaram music and bright with tube lights. After the thali was tied, the priest spoke about Arundhati. Vikram, the groom, leaned over to Lakshmi and whispered, 'I have never actually seen this star. In Chennai I can hardly see any stars at all.' Lakshmi grinned. 'I can show you tonight,' she said.

That evening, when most of the guests had gone, Lakshmi took Vikram up to the terrace. This time the power was on. The streetlights glowed orange, and a shop sign flashed below. Lakshmi found the ladle easily, because its seven stars are bright. But when she looked for Arundhati, there was nothing beside Vasishtha. 'She is hidden,' said Lakshmi. 'The lights are too bright.'

Vikram took out his phone to look up a sky map. 'Don't,' said Lakshmi. 'Paati says your eyes need time in the dark. The phone screen undoes it.' Just then, the power went off again. Somebody downstairs groaned. Lakshmi laughed. 'Now wait,' she told Vikram. 'Don't look at anything bright for a while.'

They sat on the parapet and waited, talking softly. Slowly, more stars came out, as if someone were switching them on one by one. After about fifteen minutes Vikram said, 'There. Next to Vasishtha. A tiny one.' He sounded as pleased as if he had found a coin on the road. 'They really are side by side,' he said. 'Like two houses on one street. All seven stars are, I suppose.'

Lakshmi frowned. In her science book there was a page about the Saptarishi. It said that the stars of a star pattern are usually not close to each other at all. 'Not all of them,' she said. 'Kratu, at the top of the bowl, is much further away than the others. Marichi, at the end of the handle, is further too. The seven stars only make a ladle from here.'

'So the ladle is not real?' asked Vikram.

'It is real from here,' said Lakshmi. She held up one finger close to her eye and another at arm's length, and moved them until the fingertips seemed to touch. 'Look. From my eye they touch.' Vikram leaned to the side. From where he sat, the two fingertips were far apart, one near Lakshmi's nose and one out by the parapet. He laughed. 'All right, teacher,' he said. 'So is Arundhati a trick too? Is she really far behind Vasishtha?'

Lakshmi did not know. She thought about it all through dinner. The next morning, she asked her mother to help her look it up. They found that Vasishtha is about 83 light-years away. Arundhati is about 82 light-years away. The two stars are really close together in space, and they move through space together. Astronomers are still checking whether they slowly go round each other.

Lakshmi ran to find Vikram, who was packing the car with Revathi. 'Arundhati is not a trick,' she said, out of breath. 'Most of the ladle is a view from Earth. But Arundhati and Vasishtha really are neighbours. They travel together.'

Vikram put down a suitcase. 'So the old story picked the one pair in the ladle that really is a pair,' he said. 'Did the people who made it know that?'

'Nobody could have measured it then,' said Lakshmi. 'Maybe it was luck. Maybe they just loved them for always being together.' Revathi laughed and hugged her. 'When we are in Chennai,' Vikram promised, 'I will go up on our terrace during a power cut and look for them.' 'Then switch off your phone first,' said Lakshmi. 'And wait.'

**For the parent:** For the parent: from Earth, the seven stars of the Saptarishi look like a single group, but they are at different distances, so the ladle is a pattern seen from one place. Vikram assumes that stars which look together are together. Lakshmi knows they usually are not. Then comes a surprise: Arundhati (Alcor) and Vasishtha (Mizar) really are close in space and move together. So a pattern seen from Earth can hide real neighbours as well as false ones. Only measuring distances can tell which is which. The story also shows why the dark matters: with the streetlights on, Arundhati vanished.

- Ask: Why could Lakshmi see Arundhati on the first night but not when the streetlights were on?
- Ask: Why did Lakshmi tell Vikram not to look at his phone?
- Ask: Vikram thought all seven stars were side by side. How did Lakshmi show him that two things can look together but be far apart?
- Ask: What did Lakshmi find out about Arundhati and Vasishtha that surprised her?
- Ask: Lakshmi says the ladle is 'real from here'. What do you think she means?

### e1 · The Thread Saptarishi: are its seven stars really close together? (35 min)
- **question:** Are the seven stars of the Saptarishi at the same distance from us? Does their ladle shape still show when you look from somewhere other than Earth?
- **predict:** Before you build it, guess. When you look at the model from the side, will the seven stars still make a ladle? Will they all be the same distance from Earth? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Build the model once, using the real distances, and do not move the paper stars. Change only one thing: where you look from. Look with one eye closed each time, with your eye at the same height as the Earth card.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the hole in the card with the pencil, pushing into an eraser or folded cloth, never towards a hand. Keep the threads away from the doorway, so nobody trips on them.
- **record:** Fill in the results table for each place you looked from. Draw the model twice: as seen from the Earth card, and as seen from the side.
- **results table:** Where I looked from | Did I see the ladle? (yes, a little, no) | Which paper stars looked closest together | My quick sketch
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the picture of the Saptarishi in the app. 2. Ask the guess question below and wait for the answer and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* These seven stars make a ladle. If we could fly out into space and look from the side, would they still look like a ladle? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the seven stars of the Saptarishi. They make a ladle. Would they still look like a ladle from the side? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Do you think all seven stars are the same distance from us? Why? (good: Yes, because they look like they are in a row.; Maybe not. Far things look small, so the faint ones could be further.)
  - Note: Accept any guess with a reason. Do not say the answer. The model will show it.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tape the chart paper flat on a wall at your child's eye height when kneeling. 2. Measure from the bottom left corner of the paper and mark seven dots, using the list in the note. 3. Write each star's name next to its dot.  
  *Parent says:* This paper is the far sky. Let's mark where the seven stars are. Measure across from the left edge first, then up from the bottom.  
  *Child mode:* This paper is the sky. Measure across, then up. Mark each star with a dot. Write its name.

Pulastya 37 across and 10 up. Pulaha 49 across and 17 up. Kratu 46 across and 26 up. The dots make the ladle at the right size for the model.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**

  - Note: The threads should start as close to the hole as you can get them, so they all seem to come out of your child's eye. If your room is small, 80 cm from the wall also works.

  - Note: Distances along each thread from the card, at 1 centimetre for 2 light-years: Kratu (Dubhe) 62 cm, Pulaha (Merak) 40 cm, Pulastya (Phecda) 42 cm, Atri (Megrez) 40 cm, Angiras (Alioth) 41 cm, Vasishtha (Mizar) 41 cm, Marichi (Alkaid) 52 cm.   - Ask: Do the paper stars make the ladle? (good: Yes, each paper star sits right on its dot.; It looks just like the picture.)

  *Child mode:* Walk to the side. Look at the paper stars. Is it still a ladle? Which stars are close together? Sketch it.
  - Ask: Which paper stars are far from the others? (good: Kratu and Marichi are further back, near the wall.; Five are in a bunch, and two are far behind.)
  - Check: From the side, what did the seven paper stars look like? → **Five close together, and two much further from Earth**

  *Parent says:* Can you find anywhere else in the room where the seven stars still make the ladle?  
  *Child mode:* Hunt for another spot where you see the ladle. Try left, right, and on tiptoe.
  - Note: Your child should find that the ladle shows only when the eye is at or very close to the Earth card. A step away and the shape bends.

### e2 · Eyes in the Dark: how long do your eyes take to see faint stars? (25 min)
- **question:** How many faint 'stars' on a paper can you see in a dark room after 1 minute, 5 minutes, 10 minutes and 15 minutes?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will you see more stars, fewer, or the same number as the minutes go by? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same paper, in the same place, with the same tiny bit of light in the room every time. The same person counts each time. Change only one thing: how long you have been in the dark.
- **safety:** Clear the floor before switching off the light, so nobody trips. A grown-up stays close by.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each time.
- **results table:** Minutes in the dark | Number of stars I can see (out of 12)
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up draws 12 small stars on the white paper, without your child watching. 2. Make 2 with the black sketch pen, 3 with the pencil pressed hard, 3 pressed medium, and 4 pressed very lightly. 3. Scatter them over the page.  
  *Parent says:* I'm drawing some stars on this paper. Some are dark and some are very faint, like real stars. Don't peek yet.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up draws 12 stars. Some are dark. Some are very faint. Don't peek!
  - Note: Mixing the stars around the page stops your child from counting them by memory. The faintest ones should be just visible in normal room light.
- **s2 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Prop the paper against a wall at the far side of the room, about 2 metres from where your child will sit. 2. Ask the guess question.  
  *Parent says:* When I switch off the light, you will count the stars you can see. Will you see more as time goes on, or fewer, or the same? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Soon the light goes off. You will count the stars. Will you see more, fewer, or the same as time goes on? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: My eyes get used to the dark.; When I wake at night I can see more after a while.)
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Switch off the light. Leave only a thin line of light under the door. 2. After 1, 5, 10 and 15 minutes, your child counts the stars and calls out the number. 3. You write the numbers down without a bright light. A grown-up can call out the time from outside.  
  *Parent says:* Now count. How many stars can you see? We'll count again in a few minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Count the stars you can see. Say the number. Wait. Count again when the grown-up says.
  - Note: If your child cannot see any stars even after 15 minutes, open the door a little more. If they see all 12 at once, the room is too bright: close the curtains or the door more. While you wait, chat quietly or tell the Krittika story from this unit.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Switch the light back on. Ask your child to say or write what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why I think it happened.
  - Note: A good answer: what I think, my eyes get better in the dark. What I saw, after 1 minute I saw 3 stars, and after 15 minutes I saw 10. Why, my eyes got used to the dark. Then explain: this is why stargazers wait 20 minutes on the terrace before they look for faint stars, and do not look at a phone.
  - Check: In the dark, you saw more stars the longer you waited. Why? → **My eyes slowly got better at seeing in dim light**
### e3 · The Moon's Daily Step: how far does the Moon move among the stars? (30 min, 4 days)
- **question:** Against the stars, how far does the Moon move from one night to the next, and in which direction?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. If you look at the Moon at the same time tomorrow night, will it be in the same place among the stars, or somewhere else? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Look at the same time each night, from the same spot on the balcony or terrace. Measure with your fist held out at arm's length, the same way every time.
- **safety:** A grown-up stays with the child on the balcony or terrace at night. Keep away from the edge of a terrace without a wall.
- **record:** Fill in the table each night. Draw the star and the Moon each night on one page, so you can see the Moon stepping along.
- **results table:** Night and time | Bright star I measured from | Fists from that star to the Moon | Moon east or west of the star | Nakshatra (from a sky app, if you have one)
- **schedule:** day 1: Find the Moon at 7:30 in the evening. Measure in fists from a bright star nearby. Note the time. (10 min); day 2: Same time, same spot. Measure again from the same star. (7 min); day 3: Same time, same spot. Measure again. (7 min); day 4: Look at your table. Work out how far the Moon moves each night, and how long it would take to go all the way round. (6 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Choose a week when the Moon is a crescent or half Moon in the evening sky. 2. That is about 3 to 8 days after Amavasya, the new Moon. 3. Ask the guess question.  
  *Parent says:* Tomorrow at the same time, will the Moon be in the same place among the stars, or somewhere else? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Will the Moon be in the same place among the stars tomorrow? Guess. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: The Moon moves round the Earth.; The Moon looks different each night, so maybe it moves.)
  - Note: A calendar that shows Amavasya and Purnima helps you pick the week. In the week after Amavasya, the Moon is in the western sky in the early evening.
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At 7:30 in the evening, find the Moon. 2. Find the brightest star near it. 3. Hold your fist out at arm's length and count how many fists fit between the star and the Moon. 4. Write it down, and whether the Moon is east or west of the star.  
  *Parent says:* Hold your fist out straight, like this. How many fists fit between that bright star and the Moon?  
  *Child mode:* Hold your arm out straight. Make a fist. Count fists from the bright star to the Moon. Write it down.
  - Note: A fist at arm's length covers about 10 degrees of sky, for children and grown-ups alike. If a building hides the star, choose another bright star and use it every night. If it is cloudy, skip that night and note the date.
- **s3 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* On the next two nights, at the same time and from the same spot, measure again from the same star.  
  *Parent says:* Same time, same star. Where is the Moon tonight? How many fists away?  
  *Child mode:* Same time. Same star. Count the fists again. Did the Moon move?
  - Note: The Moon should be about one and a third fists further east each night, away from where the Sun set. Because the Moon also rises about 50 minutes later each day, it will look higher in the sky each evening.
  - Wait: Do this on the next two nights, at the same time.
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 4, look at the table together. Ask your child to say what I think, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* How far did the Moon move each night? If it keeps going, how many nights to go all the way round the sky?  
  *Child mode:* How far did the Moon move each night? Which way? Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A good answer: what I think, the Moon moves east among the stars every night. What I saw, on night 1 it was 1 fist from the star, on night 2 a little over 2 fists, on night 3 about 3 and a half. Why, the Moon is going round the Earth. Then explain the nakshatras: the Moon's path is split into 27 parts, so it enters about one new nakshatra every night.
  - Check: The Moon moves about one nakshatra each night. There are 27 nakshatras. About how long does the Moon take to come back to the same stars? → **About 27 days**

   **Answer:** City lights light up the air, and faint stars are lost in the glow. *Why:* The stars are the same above every place. Light shining upwards in a city makes the sky glow, so only the brightest stars show through.

## Applications
- Sailors and travellers have found their way at night by the stars for thousands of years, including sailors on the Indian Ocean.
- Calendars: Indian calendars name the Moon's nakshatra for every day, and many festivals, from Onam to Karthigai Deepam, are timed by it.
- Astronomers use the 88 constellations as addresses, so that anyone in the world can find the same star or comet.
- Spacecraft find their direction with star trackers: small cameras that recognise star patterns, as a stargazer does.
- The Gaia spacecraft measured the distances and movements of over a billion stars, showing how the constellations will change.
- Birds such as indigo buntings, which fly at night, learn the stars round the Pole Star when young, and use them to keep their direction on their long journeys.
- Planetariums, such as those in Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Thiruvananthapuram, use star maps to show the sky as it was long ago or will be in the future.
- Towns and parks that cut light pollution, called dark-sky places, let people see the stars again and protect night animals.

## Spotted around us
- The Saptarishi, a ladle of seven stars, low in the northern sky on evenings from about February to June.
- Krittika, a tiny misty group of six or seven stars, high in the evening sky from about November to February.
- Orion, with three stars in a row and the red star Ardra, high in the evening sky from about December to April.
- The nakshatra of the day, printed in the corner of each date on a Tamil or Malayalam calendar.
- Star birthdays: many South Indian families celebrate a birthday on the day of the person's birth nakshatra.
- Many more stars appearing a few minutes into a power cut, above a city street.
- Arundhati being spoken of at weddings in South India, as a star always beside her husband Vasishtha.

## Open questions
- **Do Arundhati and Vasishtha go round each other?** They are about the same distance away and move together. Astronomers are still working out whether gravity holds them together as a pair.
- **How old are the oldest star stories?** Some astronomers think the seven sisters story may be over 100,000 years old. Others think it was made up many times. There is no written record that old.
- **How old is the list of nakshatras?** The nakshatras appear in the Vedas, which are very old, but scholars disagree about exactly when those texts were composed.
- **Do other animals see patterns in the stars?** Some birds learn the turning of the stars round the Pole Star, and dung beetles use the Milky Way to roll in a straight line. Nobody knows what the night sky is like for them.
- **How many of the stars we see have planets with life?** Thousands of planets have been found round other stars, but no sign of life on any of them yet.

## Connected maths
- In the Thread Saptarishi model, 1 centimetre stands for 2 light-years. Kratu is about 123 light-years away and Pulaha about 80. How far along their threads do their paper stars go? How much further from Earth is Kratu's paper star? **Kratu at about 62 centimetres, Pulaha at 40 centimetres. Kratu's paper star is about 22 centimetres further along.**. 123 divided by 2 is 61 and a half, so about 62. 80 divided by 2 is 40. 62 minus 40 is 22. (queues `math.division.sharing-and-grouping.L3`)
- The Moon moves into about one new nakshatra each night. Tonight it is in Krittika, the 3rd nakshatra. Which number nakshatra will it be in after 7 nights? Use the list: 1 Ashwini, 2 Bharani, 3 Krittika, 4 Rohini, 5 Mrigashira, 6 Ardra, 7 Punarvasu, 8 Pushya, 9 Ashlesha, 10 Magha. **The 10th nakshatra, Magha.**. 3 plus 7 is 10. The 10th nakshatra in the list is Magha. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L3`)
- The Moon is in Revati, the 27th and last nakshatra. Where will it be in 3 nights? Remember that after the 27th comes the 1st again. **Krittika, the 3rd nakshatra.**. After Revati (27) comes Ashwini (1), then Bharani (2), then Krittika (3). So 3 nights after Revati, the Moon is in Krittika. Going round a circle, 27 plus 3 is 30, and 30 minus 27 is 3. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- In the Eyes in the Dark test, Lakshmi counted 3 stars after 1 minute, 6 after 5 minutes, 9 after 10 minutes and 10 after 15 minutes. Draw a bar chart. Between which two times did she gain the fewest stars? What does that tell you? **Between 10 and 15 minutes, when she gained only 1 star. Her eyes were getting close to their best.**. From 1 to 5 minutes she gained 3. From 5 to 10 she gained 3. From 10 to 15 she gained only 1. The gains get smaller, because the eyes have nearly finished adapting. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Light from Kratu takes about 123 years to reach us. If the light reaching your eye tonight left Kratu 123 years before 2026, in which year did it set off? **1903.**. 2026 minus 123: 2026 minus 100 is 1926, and minus 23 more is 1903. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L3`)
- In the Moon's Daily Step experiment, the Moon moved about 13 degrees each night. A full circle round the sky is 360 degrees. About how many nights does the Moon take to go all the way round? Use 13 times 27 to check. **About 27 nights.**. 13 times 27 is 351, which is very close to 360. 13 times 28 would be 364, a little too much. So about 27 nights, and a bit. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L3`)
