# Forces we meet every day: reading the pushes and pulls on a still book, a falling cone and your own arm
`sci.forces.everyday-forces.L4`

**Summary.** A book lies on a table and does not move. Is any force acting on it? In this lesson you learn to read the forces on everyday things, from the pull of gravity to the pull of your own muscles. You drop paper cones to find out what happens when the push of the air grows to match the pull of the Earth. Then you look underneath, and find that these many forces come down to very few, and that nobody can yet say what a force finally is.

**Intro.** *Hold a school bag out in front of you with a straight arm and keep it perfectly still for one minute. Nothing is moving. So why is your arm getting tired?* Your arm tires because your muscles are pulling upward the whole time. The Earth is pulling the bag down with a force. Your arm must pull up with an equal force, second after second, just to keep the bag where it is. So a thing that is not moving can still have strong forces on it. Today you will learn to find those forces, on a bag, a book, a kite and a fish, by asking two questions about each thing.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can find the forces on an everyday thing by asking what touches it and what pulls it from a distance.
1. I can name gravity, support force, friction, tension, air resistance, upthrust, magnetic force and muscle force, and give an everyday example of each.
2. I can explain why a thing at rest, or moving at a steady speed, has balanced forces on it.
3. I can draw a labelled arrow for every push and pull on a book, a bucket or a kite.
4. I can explain why a falling paper cone stops speeding up.
5. I can say how a muscle makes a pull, and why a muscle often pulls harder than the load weighs.
6. I can say which everyday forces are, underneath, the same electric and magnetic force, and what is still not known about gravity.

## Terms
- **Force**: A push or a pull on something. A force can start a thing moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down, turn it or squash it. *e.g.* You pull a drawer open. Your hand's pull is a force on the drawer.
- **Push**: A force that moves a thing away from whatever is pushing it. *e.g.* You push a swing away from you, and it goes forward.
- **Pull**: A force that moves a thing towards whatever is pulling it. *e.g.* You pull a bucket up from a well, and it comes towards you.
- **Gravity**: The pull that the Earth has on everything, and everything has on the Earth. It acts towards the middle of the Earth. *e.g.* A coconut that comes loose from a palm falls straight down because of gravity.
- **Weight**: The size of the pull of the Earth on a thing, measured in newtons. A heavier thing has a bigger weight. *e.g.* A one-kilogram bag of sugar has a weight of about 10 newtons.
- **Newton**: The unit we use to measure a force. A small apple is pulled down by the Earth with about one newton. *e.g.* A child of 30 kilograms is pulled down with 300 newtons, near enough.
- **Mass**: How much stuff a thing is made of, measured in kilograms. A thing has the same mass on the Earth, on the Moon and in space. *e.g.* A packet of 2 kilograms of rice has a mass of 2 kilograms in your kitchen and the same mass on the Moon.
- **Centre of the Earth**: The middle point deep inside the planet. Gravity pulls everything towards it, so 'down' always means towards it. *e.g.* A child in Chennai and a child in Mexico are both pulled towards the centre, so their 'down' directions point nearly opposite ways.
- **Weightless**: Feeling no push from the floor or the seat, so that you float. A weightless person is still being pulled by gravity. *e.g.* At the very top of a jump on a trampoline, for a moment, you feel weightless.
- **Orbit**: The curved path of something that keeps falling round a planet, a moon or a star, and keeps missing it. *e.g.* The International Space Station goes round the Earth in an orbit, about once every 90 minutes.
- **Support force**: The push that a surface gives upward on anything resting on it. *e.g.* The table pushes up on a book with the support force.
- **Surface**: The outside layer of a thing, the place where it touches other things. *e.g.* The top of a table is a surface, and so is the skin of your hand.
- **Balanced forces**: Forces on one thing that are equal and point in opposite ways, so they cancel each other out. *e.g.* A book on a table has 5 newtons down and 5 newtons up, so the forces on it are balanced.
- **At rest**: Not moving at all compared with the ground or the room you are in. *e.g.* A school bag lying on a chair is at rest.
- **Steady speed**: Moving at the same speed in the same direction, with no speeding up, slowing down or turning. *e.g.* A ceiling fan blade turning at the same rate for an hour has a steady speed.
- **Friction**: The force between two surfaces that touch. It tries to stop them sliding past each other. *e.g.* A chappal grips the road because of friction, and slips on a wet floor because there is less of it.
- **Air resistance**: The push of the air against anything moving through it. It points against the motion and grows as the speed grows. *e.g.* Hold your hand flat out of a bus window. The air pushes it backward.
- **Force diagram**: A drawing of one thing alone, with one arrow for every force that acts on it. *e.g.* A force diagram of a book on a table shows a dot or box with two arrows, one up and one down.
- **Arrow**: In a force diagram, a line with a point at the end. The point shows the way the force acts, and a longer arrow shows a bigger force. *e.g.* A long arrow down and a short arrow up on a falling leaf shows that gravity is winning.
- **Tension**: The pull that runs along a tight rope, string, cable or chain. *e.g.* A bag hanging from a string is held up by the tension in the string.
- **Pulley**: A wheel with a groove for a rope. It turns the direction of a pull. *e.g.* A well pulley lets you pull the rope down to bring the bucket up.
- **Cable**: A very thick rope made of twisted steel wires. It carries tension. *e.g.* The slanting cables of the Bandra-Worli Sea Link in Mumbai hold up its road.
- **Terminal speed**: The steady speed at which a falling thing stops speeding up, because the push of the air has grown as big as its weight. *e.g.* A large raindrop reaches a terminal speed of about 9 metres in each second.
- **Parachute**: A wide piece of cloth that catches a lot of air. The push of the air is then large, and the faller reaches a slow terminal speed. *e.g.* A skydiver falls at about 200 kilometres per hour, and with the parachute open at about 20.
- **Upthrust**: The upward push that water or air gives to anything put in it. *e.g.* When you push an empty bucket down in water, you feel the water pushing it back up. That push is upthrust.
- **Float**: To stay up on the top of a liquid, because the upthrust equals the weight. *e.g.* A steel katori set gently on water floats.
- **Sink**: To go down in a liquid, because the weight is bigger than the biggest upthrust the water can give. *e.g.* A steel spoon sinks in water.
- **Magnet**: A piece of iron, steel or other metal that pulls on iron and on other magnets without touching them. *e.g.* A fridge magnet holds a paper on the fridge door.
- **Magnetic force**: The pull or push of a magnet, which acts across a gap. *e.g.* A magnet pulls a steel paper clip across a table before it touches it.
- **Pole**: One of the two ends of a magnet, where the force is strongest. They are called north and south. *e.g.* A bar magnet has a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other.
- **Attract**: To pull together. Unlike poles of two magnets attract. *e.g.* A north pole and a south pole snap together.
- **Repel**: To push apart. Like poles of two magnets repel. *e.g.* Two north poles pushed together slide away from each other on a table.
- **Lodestone**: A rock that is a natural magnet. It was found in the ground long before people made magnets. *e.g.* The Greek thinker Thales, about 600 BCE, is reported to have thought that a lodestone has a soul, because it moves iron.
- **Compass**: A small instrument with a magnetic needle that is free to turn. The needle points to the north. *e.g.* A walker in a forest turns the compass until the needle matches N, and then knows which way is north.
- **Electric charge**: A property of tiny particles that makes them push or pull each other. There are two kinds, called positive and negative. *e.g.* A rubbed comb gets electric charge and pulls tiny paper bits.
- **Electron**: A very small particle, found in every atom, that carries negative electric charge. *e.g.* Rubbing a balloon on wool moves electrons from the wool to the balloon.
- **Static electric force**: The pull or push between things that have electric charge, when the charge stays where it is. *e.g.* A rubbed balloon sticks to a wall because of the static electric force.
- **Muscle**: A part of the body made of fibres that can shorten and so pull on a bone. *e.g.* The muscle in your upper arm pulls your forearm up.
- **Fibre**: A long thin thread. A muscle fibre is a long cell that shortens when it works. *e.g.* A strip of cooked chicken pulls apart into long fibres.
- **Protein**: A big molecule made by living cells. Proteins do much of the work in a body, such as making muscles pull. *e.g.* Dal, eggs, curd and paneer contain protein.
- **Energy**: What lets anything move, heat up or change. Food gives a body energy, and fuel gives a bus energy. We can measure it, but science cannot yet say what energy finally is. *e.g.* A child who has eaten a banana has the energy to run across the playground.
- **Nerve**: A long bundle of fibres that carries signals between the body and the brain. *e.g.* A nerve carries the signal 'pull' to the muscle in your arm.
- **Lever**: A stiff bar that turns about one fixed point. A small push far from that point can balance a big push near it. *e.g.* A see-saw is a lever with its middle as the turning point.
- **Pivot**: The fixed point that a lever turns about. *e.g.* The elbow is the pivot of the forearm. The hinge is the pivot of a door.
- **Biceps**: The muscle at the front of your upper arm. It pulls the forearm up when it shortens. *e.g.* Bend your arm with a bag in your hand and feel the biceps tighten.
- **Atom**: A tiny piece of matter, much too small to see. Everything we touch is made of atoms. *e.g.* A single grain of salt holds roughly a million million million atoms.
- **Electromagnetic force**: The one force that includes both the electric force and the magnetic force. It holds atoms together and is behind pushes by tables, ropes and muscles. *e.g.* A table pushes up on a book through the electromagnetic force between their atoms.
- **Field**: A region of space where a force can be felt by something placed there. *e.g.* A magnet has a magnetic field around it. A paper clip that enters the field feels a pull.
- **Time**: What a clock measures: the seconds, minutes and hours between one thing happening and another. Clocks measure it very exactly, but science cannot yet say what time finally is. *e.g.* A cone that falls for 1.5 seconds has taken 1.5 seconds of time, as the stopwatch shows.
- **Structure**: Something built from parts that hold each other up, such as a bridge, a dam or a building. *e.g.* A bridge is a structure. The deck, cables, towers and ground all pass forces to each other.
- **Rivet**: A metal pin that is hammered through two steel plates and flattened at both ends to join them. *e.g.* The Howrah Bridge in Kolkata is joined with rivets and has no nuts and bolts.
- **Dam**: A wall built across a river to hold water back. It must resist the sideways push of deep water. *e.g.* The Kallanai dam on the Kaveri in Tamil Nadu has held back water for about two thousand years.

## Explanations
### To read the forces on a thing, ask two questions · *defined*
**Definition:** The forces on an object come from whatever touches it and from a few things that can pull or push it without touching.

A force is a push or a pull. Several forces usually act on one thing at the same moment. To find them all, pick one thing and ask two questions. First: what is touching it? Each thing that touches can push or pull. Second: what is pulling or pushing it from a distance? In daily life the answer to the second question is nearly always the Earth, through gravity, and sometimes a magnet. Take a school bag hanging on a hook. The hook touches the bag and pulls up. The air touches the bag and hardly pushes at all. The Earth pulls the bag down from a distance. That gives two forces that matter.
- e.g. A dosa on a tawa: the tawa touches the dosa and pushes up. The Earth pulls the dosa down.
- e.g. A ceiling fan: the rod touches the fan and pulls up. The Earth pulls down.
- e.g. A steel pin under a magnet: the magnet pulls up from a distance, and the Earth pulls down from a distance. Nothing touches the pin for a moment as it jumps.
### Gravity pulls on everything, all the time, towards the centre of the Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** Gravity is the pull that the Earth has on every object. The size of this pull on an object is called its weight, and it is measured in newtons.

The Earth pulls on you when you fall. The Earth also pulls on you just as hard when you sit, when you sleep and when you jump upward. Gravity never switches off. The pull on a one-kilogram bag of sugar is about 10 newtons. The newton is the unit of force. A child of 30 kilograms is pulled down with roughly 300 newtons. 'Down' means towards the centre of the Earth. So 'down' in Chennai and 'down' in Mexico point in nearly opposite directions. Gravity also works both ways. While the Earth pulls you with 300 newtons, you pull the Earth with 300 newtons. The Earth is so massive that your pull does not move it by any amount we could notice.
- e.g. A ball thrown up is pulled down the whole time, even on the way up. That is why it slows.
- e.g. A mango on a branch is pulled down for months before the stalk gives way.
- e.g. On the Moon the pull is about one-sixth as strong, so a 30 kilogram child would weigh about 50 newtons there.
### Astronauts float because they are falling, and gravity is still pulling them · *defined*
**Definition:** In a spacecraft going round the Earth, gravity is almost as strong as on the ground. The crew feel weightless because they and the craft are falling together.

People often say there is no gravity in space. That is not so. The International Space Station flies about 400 kilometres above the ground. Up there the Earth's pull is still about nine-tenths as strong as in your room. So why do astronauts float? Because the station is falling. It also moves sideways at about 28,000 kilometres per hour. That is so fast that as it falls, the round Earth curves away beneath it. It keeps falling and keeps missing the ground. The astronauts fall along with the station, so the floor never pushes up on their feet. What you feel as your weight is that push from the floor.
- e.g. In a lift that starts downward quickly, you feel lighter for a moment, because the floor pushes up on you a little less.
- e.g. At the top of a jump on a trampoline, nothing pushes on you, and for a moment you feel weightless.
- e.g. Rakesh Sharma in 1984, Sunita Williams on several flights, and Shubhanshu Shukla in 2025 all floated inside spacecraft. Gravity was holding those craft in their paths round the Earth.
### A table pushes up: the support force · *defined*
**Definition:** The support force is the push that a surface gives to anything pressing on it. It acts straight out from the surface.

Put a book on a table. The Earth pulls the book down with about 5 newtons. The book stays still. So something must push up on the book with 5 newtons. Only the table is touching the book. The table is pushing. How can a table push? When the book lands, the table top is squashed by a tiny amount, far too small to see. A squashed thing pushes back, as a mattress does when you sit on it. The table is like a very stiff mattress. Put a heavier book down, and the table is squashed a little more and pushes back harder. The table always gives exactly the push that is needed, until it breaks.
- e.g. Lay a metre ruler across two piles of books and put a tumbler in the middle. You can see the ruler bend. A table does the same, only far less.
- e.g. A bathroom scale does not measure the pull of the Earth on you. The scale measures how hard its own top is pushing up on your feet.
- e.g. Lean on a wall. The wall pushes sideways on your shoulder, or you would fall through.
### A thing at rest has balanced forces, not zero forces · *defined*
**Definition:** Forces on an object are balanced when they cancel each other exactly. The object then stays at rest if it was at rest.

The book has two forces on it: 5 newtons down from the Earth and 5 newtons up from the table. They are equal and opposite, so they cancel. Scientists say the forces are balanced. A balanced pair is not the same as no force. If you could switch off the table's push, the book would fall. If you could switch off gravity, nothing would hold the book to the table. Almost every still thing around you is held still by forces in balance. A building, a hanging lamp and a child sitting on a chair are all in the middle of a tie.
- e.g. In a tug of war where neither team moves, each team may be pulling with 2,000 newtons.
- e.g. A picture on a wall: the nail pulls up, the Earth pulls down.
- e.g. A pot on a tripod stand: three legs push up, and together their pushes equal the weight of the pot.
### A thing moving at a steady speed also has balanced forces · *defined*
**Definition:** If the forces on an object are balanced, its motion does not change. It stays at rest, or keeps moving in a straight line at the same speed.

Most people believe that a moving thing needs a force to keep it moving. It feels true, because a cycle slows down when you stop pedalling. But the cycle slows because friction and air push backward on it. Suppose you pedal so that your forward push is exactly equal to those backward pushes. The forces are balanced. The cycle does not stop. It keeps going at a steady speed. So balanced forces do not mean 'not moving'. They mean 'not changing'. A force is needed to speed up, slow down or turn. No force is needed just to keep going.
- e.g. A carrom striker slides far on a powdered board, because the powder leaves very little friction to slow it.
- e.g. The spacecraft Voyager 1 was launched in 1977. Its engines are off, and it is still moving away from the Sun at about 17 kilometres each second.
- e.g. In a train moving steadily, a cup of tea on the table sits as still as it would at home.
- **Aristotle, in Greece, about 350 BCE:** He taught that everything on Earth comes to rest unless something keeps pushing it. This fitted carts, ploughs and boats, and it was taught for nearly two thousand years.
- **John Philoponus of Alexandria about 530 CE, and Ibn Sina in Persia about 1020 CE:** They argued that a thrower gives the thrown thing an inner push that carries it along. Ibn Sina added that in empty space this push would never fade. The Vaisheshika school in India described something similar and called it vega.
- **Galileo Galilei in 1638 and Isaac Newton in 1687:** Galileo rolled balls on smoother and smoother slopes and saw them go further each time. He reasoned that with no friction a ball would never stop. Newton made this his first law of motion.
### The force diagram: draw one thing alone, with an arrow for each force on it · *defined*
**Definition:** A force diagram shows a single object with one arrow for each force acting on it. Each arrow points the way the force acts, and a longer arrow means a larger force.

When many things push on many things, it is easy to get lost. So scientists draw one thing only. Draw the thing as a dot or a box. Leave out the table, the hand and the rope. Then add an arrow for each force that acts on your thing, and label it with what is doing the pushing or pulling. Take a kite held steady in the sky. Three forces act on the kite. The Earth pulls it down. The string pulls it down and back towards the flier. The wind pushes it up and away from the flier. When the kite hangs still, the three arrows cancel.
- e.g. A book on a table: two arrows, weight down and support force up, of equal length.
- e.g. A bucket being raised from a well at a steady speed: rope pulling up and weight down, of equal length.
- e.g. A bucket just starting to rise: the rope's arrow is longer than the weight's arrow.
### Friction points against sliding, and that is how it pushes you forward · *defined*
**Definition:** Friction is a force between two surfaces in contact. It acts along the surfaces, in the direction that stops them sliding past each other.

People say friction always slows things. Look at your foot as you walk. Your foot pushes backward on the ground. If the ground were ice, your foot would slide backward. Friction stops that slide. So the friction on your foot points forward. That forward friction is the force that moves you along. The same is true for a cycle and a bus. The tyre pushes backward on the road, and friction from the road pushes the tyre forward. Without friction no walker or wheel could start, turn or stop.
- e.g. On a wet bathroom floor there is little friction, so your foot slips backward and you do not go forward.
- e.g. A bus stuck in mud spins its wheels. The wheels turn but the mud cannot give enough forward friction.
- e.g. When you brake, friction from the road points backward on the tyre and slows you.
### Tension is the pull that runs along a tight rope, thread or chain · *defined*
**Definition:** Tension is the pulling force carried along a stretched rope, string, cable or chain. It pulls inward on whatever is tied at each end.

A rope cannot push. It can only pull, and only when it is tight. The pull that a tight rope carries is called tension. Tension acts along the rope. It pulls on the things at both ends, towards the middle of the rope. Hang a 2 kilogram bag of rice from a string. The Earth pulls the bag down with about 20 newtons. The string pulls up on the bag with 20 newtons. The same string pulls down on the hook with 20 newtons. A pulley, which is a wheel with a groove for a rope, can turn the direction of the tension. That is why you pull down on a well rope to bring the bucket up.
- e.g. A clothesline loaded with wet saris is under tension along its whole length.
- e.g. The steel cables of a lift carry the weight of the cabin and the people in it.
- e.g. A kite string is tight when the wind pulls the kite, and goes slack when the wind drops.
- e.g. A spider hanging from her thread is held up by tension in the silk.
### Air and water push back on anything moving through them · *defined*
**Definition:** Air resistance and water resistance are forces that act on an object moving through air or water. They point against the motion and grow larger as the object moves faster.

Air is made of tiny particles. A thing moving through air must shove them aside, and they push back. This push is called air resistance. It has two features that friction between solids does not have. It grows as the speed grows: go twice as fast, and the push becomes about four times as large. It also depends on shape and size. A wide, flat thing meets more air than a narrow, pointed one. Now think of something falling. At first it speeds up. As it speeds up, the air pushes back harder. At some speed the push of the air equals the pull of gravity. The forces are balanced, and the thing falls at a steady speed from then on. This steady speed is called its terminal speed.
- e.g. A large raindrop falls at about 9 metres each second and no faster, however high the cloud.
- e.g. A skydiver lying flat falls at about 200 kilometres per hour. When the parachute opens, its wide cloth meets far more air, and the steady speed drops to about 20.
- e.g. In 2023 the Indian badminton player Satwiksairaj Rankireddy hit a smash measured at 565 kilometres per hour. The feathers of a shuttlecock catch so much air that the shuttle slows to a small part of that speed before it crosses the court.
- e.g. Wading through a swimming pool is far harder than walking beside it, because water pushes back much more than air.
### Upthrust: water and air push upward on things in them · *defined*
**Definition:** Upthrust is the upward push that a liquid or a gas gives to any object placed in it. It equals the weight of the liquid or gas that the object pushes aside.

Lower a steel tumbler into a bucket of water, open end up. You feel the water pushing up on it. That push is called upthrust. Water presses on a thing from all sides, and it presses harder deeper down. So the push on the bottom of the tumbler is greater than the push on the top, and the result is a push upward. About 250 BCE, Archimedes of Syracuse found the rule. The upthrust equals the weight of the water that the object pushes out of the way. If the upthrust can equal the object's weight, the object floats. If it cannot, the object sinks, but still feels lighter under water.
- e.g. A steel katori floats when set down gently, because its hollow shape pushes aside a lot of water. The same katori sinks when filled.
- e.g. A big ship of steel floats for the same reason as the katori.
- e.g. A stone that is hard to lift on the river bank is easier to lift under water.
- e.g. Air gives upthrust too. A balloon filled with the light gas helium rises because the air pushes it up more strongly than the Earth pulls it down.
### Magnetic force in a home: it acts across a gap and fades quickly with distance · *defined*
**Definition:** Magnetic force is the pull or push between magnets, and the pull of a magnet on iron, nickel and cobalt. It acts without touching.

A magnet pulls on a steel pin before they touch. The force crosses air, paper, cloth and your finger. It is strong close up and fades very quickly with distance. Pull a fridge magnet one centimetre away from the door and you can hardly feel the pull. A magnet pulls strongly on only a few materials: iron and steel, nickel and cobalt. It does not pull copper, aluminium, gold or wood. Every magnet has two ends called poles, north and south. Two like poles push each other apart, which scientists call repelling. Two unlike poles pull together, which is called attracting. The Earth is itself a huge, weak magnet, and that is why a compass needle turns to point north.
- e.g. A soft magnetic strip runs inside the rubber edge of a fridge door and holds the door shut.
- e.g. The speaker in a phone and the motor in a toy car both have magnets inside.
- e.g. Some pencil boxes, bag flaps and cupboard doors close with small magnets.
- e.g. At a scrapyard, a crane with a magnet that can be switched on and off lifts steel and leaves plastic behind.
- **Shen Kuo, in China, in his 'Dream Pool Essays' of about 1088 CE:** He described how to make a needle magnetic by rubbing it on a lodestone, a natural magnetic rock. He then hung the needle from a thread to find direction. He also noted that the needle points a little away from true south.
- **William Gilbert, in England, in his book 'De Magnete' of 1600:** He shaped a lodestone into a ball and showed that small compass needles behave near it as real compasses behave on the Earth. He concluded that the Earth is a great magnet.
- **Thales of Miletus, about 600 BCE, as reported by Aristotle in 'On the Soul':** Thales is said to have held that the lodestone has a soul, because it moves iron. For him, whatever could cause movement by itself was alive in some way.
### The static electric force: a rubbed comb pulls paper without touching · *defined*
**Definition:** The static electric force is the pull or push between objects that carry electric charge. Like the magnetic force, it acts across a gap.

Run a plastic comb through dry hair a few times and hold it near tiny pieces of paper. The pieces jump up to the comb. No magnet is involved, and paper is not iron. This is a different force, called the electric force. Rubbing moves some very small particles, called electrons, from the hair onto the comb. The comb now has extra electric charge. A charged thing pulls on light things nearby. Two things with the same kind of charge push each other apart. That is why freshly combed hair stands up and spreads: each hair is pushing the others away.
- e.g. A polyester shirt pulled off on a dry winter day crackles and clings.
- e.g. A balloon rubbed on a woollen sweater sticks to a wall.
- e.g. Dust gathers on a television screen because the screen becomes charged.
- e.g. The Greek word for amber, a yellow tree gum, hardened over millions of years, that pulls dust when rubbed, is 'elektron'. The word electricity comes from it.
### How a muscle makes a pull · *defined*
**Definition:** Muscle force is the pull made when a muscle shortens. Inside the muscle, two kinds of protein thread slide past each other.

Every push you give begins as a pull by a muscle. A muscle is a bundle of long cells called fibres. Inside each fibre lie two kinds of very thin thread, made of proteins. One kind carries tiny heads along its length. When the muscle gets a signal from a nerve, each head grabs the other thread and tugs. Then it lets go and grabs again further along, like hands hauling a rope. The threads slide past each other and the fibre shortens. One head pulls with a force far too small to feel. A single muscle has many billions of heads, and their tiny pulls add up. This work uses energy from your food. Energy is what lets a body move things or change them.
- e.g. Your jaw muscles let your back teeth press together with a force of several hundred newtons, enough to crack a groundnut shell.
- e.g. Your heart is a muscle that squeezes roughly 100,000 times a day without a rest.
- e.g. An ant can carry 10 to 50 times their own weight.
- **Jean Hanson with Hugh Huxley, and Andrew Huxley with Rolf Niedergerke, in two papers printed together in 1954:** Before 1954 most scientists thought the threads themselves shrank or folded. These four showed, with new microscopes, that the threads keep their length and slide past each other. Jean Hanson's share in this is often left out.
- **Ayurvedic texts such as the Charaka Samhita:** They explain all movement in the body, from a blink to a step, by vata, the moving principle, linked with air and breath. This is an older account, framed differently from the measured one, and the two are not the same kind of claim.
### Your bones are levers, so a muscle often pulls much harder than the load weighs · *defined*
**Definition:** A lever is a stiff bar that turns about a fixed point. In the arm, the forearm bones are the bar, the elbow is the turning point and the muscle pulls close to the elbow.

Hold a one-litre bottle of water in your hand, with your elbow bent and your forearm level. The bottle weighs about 10 newtons. How hard is the muscle in your upper arm pulling? You might guess 10 newtons. The true answer is about 80. The muscle, called the biceps, is joined to the forearm only about 4 centimetres from the elbow. The bottle is about 32 centimetres from the elbow, which is 8 times as far. A pull close to the turning point must be much larger to balance a load far from it. So the muscle pulls with about 8 times the load. This is a simple picture, because other muscles share the work. What the body gains in return is speed and reach. A small shortening of the muscle swings the hand a long way.
- e.g. A door is hard to push near the hinges and easy to push at the handle. It is the same rule.
- e.g. When you stand on tiptoe, your foot is a lever. The ball of the foot is the pivot, and the calf muscle pulls up on the heel bone.
- e.g. A grasshopper's leg muscle pulls on a short lever inside the leg, and the long leg swings fast. The leg also stores the muscle's work in a springy part first, then lets go all at once, which throws the grasshopper many body lengths.
### Every living being meets the same forces, each in their own way · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants, animals and microbes sense and use gravity, grip, upthrust and magnetic force, each with their own body.

Forces are not only a matter for people and machines. A seed planted upside down still sends their root downward. Cells in the root tip hold tiny heavy grains. The grains settle to the lower side, so the root senses which way gravity points and grows that way. A gecko walks across a ceiling. Each toe carries a huge number of very fine hairs. The hairs come so close to the surface that a weak electric pull between hair and ceiling holds the gecko up. Most fish hold a small bag of gas inside the body and change its size to rise or sink. Some birds sense the Earth's magnetic field and steer by it on long journeys.
- e.g. Charles Darwin and his son Francis showed in 1880 that the tip of a root is the part that senses gravity.
- e.g. Kellar Autumn and his colleagues measured the grip of a single gecko hair in 2000.
- e.g. In the 1960s Wolfgang Wiltschko put European robins in a closed room. When he turned the magnetic field round them with coils, the robins changed the way they faced.
- **Plant scientists such as Monica Gagliano and Stefano Mancuso:** A root tip takes in signals about gravity, water, touch and chemicals and settles on a direction. They describe this as deciding.
- **Plant scientists such as Lincoln Taiz:** He and others reply that the settling grains and the growth chemicals they set moving fully explain the root's turn. They say a word like 'decide' fits animals with brains, and that a root needs no deciding.
### Underneath, only four kinds of force are known, and most everyday forces are one of them · *defined*
**Definition:** Physicists know of four basic forces: gravity, the electromagnetic force and two forces that act only inside the centres of atoms. Every everyday force except gravity is electromagnetic.

This lesson has named many forces. Underneath, physicists have found only four kinds. Two of them act only inside the tiny centre of an atom, and you never meet them directly. That leaves two for daily life. One is gravity. The other is the electromagnetic force, which is the electric force and the magnetic force together. The push of a table is this one force. So are the grip of friction, the pull in a rope, the push of air and the tug of a muscle. They come from electrons in the atoms of one thing pushing or pulling on the atoms of another. Even touch is this force. When you press a table, the electrons in your finger repel the electrons in the wood before the atoms ever meet.
- e.g. A small magnet lifts a steel pin off the floor. In that contest, one small magnet beats the gravity of the whole Earth, which shows how weak gravity is by comparison.
- e.g. A rope holds because the atoms along each fibre attract their neighbours electrically.
- e.g. Hans Christian Ørsted found in 1820 that an electric current turns a compass needle. In 1865 James Clerk Maxwell showed that electricity and magnetism are two sides of one force.
- **Physicists since the 1970s, including Abdus Salam of Pakistan, Sheldon Glashow and Steven Weinberg:** They showed that the electromagnetic force and one of the forces inside the atom are two forms of one force. Many physicists hope that all four will turn out to be one.
- **Physicists who think gravity is not a basic force like the others, such as Erik Verlinde:** They point out that gravity has refused to join the others for a hundred years. They think gravity may be a different kind of thing, which is the subject of the next card.
### What is gravity? We know how it acts, and nobody knows what it finally is · *debated*
**Definition:** Gravity is described very exactly by rules that predict how things fall and move. There is no agreed account of why masses draw towards each other.

You can predict to a fraction of a second when a dropped ball will land. That does not mean anyone knows what gravity is. Isaac Newton gave the rule for how strongly two masses pull each other. He was asked what causes the pull, and wrote that he would make no guess. In 1915 Albert Einstein gave a different picture. A mass such as the Earth bends the space and time around it, and things move along the bends. Here 'time' means what a clock measures. Clocks measure it very exactly, yet science cannot yet say what time finally is. In his picture a falling ball has no force on it at all. Both pictures predict everyday falling equally well. A third account, which would join gravity with the physics of tiny particles, does not yet exist.
- e.g. Satellite navigation on a phone works only because its sums include Einstein's bending of time near the Earth.
- e.g. In 2015 instruments in the United States picked up ripples in space. They came from two black holes that crashed together far away, as Einstein's picture predicts. Another such instrument is being built at Hingoli in Maharashtra.
- **Kanada, in the Vaisheshika Sutra 5.1.7, composed in India more than 2,000 years ago:** He states that when nothing holds a thing up, it falls because of gurutva, heaviness. He treats heaviness as a quality in the object that causes falling.
- **Brahmagupta, in the Brahmasphutasiddhanta of 628 CE, and Bhaskara II, in the Siddhanta Shiromani of 1150 CE:** Brahmagupta wrote that things fall towards the Earth because it is the Earth's nature to draw them, as it is water's nature to flow. Bhaskara wrote that the Earth draws heavy things towards itself by its own power.
- **Isaac Newton, in the second edition of his 'Principia', 1713:** Gravity is a pull between any two masses, across empty space. He gave its exact rule and wrote that he framed no hypotheses about its cause.
- **Albert Einstein, in 1915:** Gravity is not a pull. Mass bends space and time, and a falling thing is simply moving freely through bent space and time.
- **Physicists who try to join gravity with the physics of tiny particles, such as Abhay Ashtekar:** Ashtekar works on the idea that space itself is made of tiny separate pieces. Others expect that gravity is carried by a particle called the graviton. No experiment has yet shown either.
### What is a force, really? A useful idea that may not be basic · *idea*
**Definition:** 'A force is a push or a pull' is a working definition. Physicists do not agree on whether force is a basic part of nature or a handy way of describing something deeper.

We have used the word force on every card. We can measure a force in newtons and predict what it will do. Can we say what a force is? Nobody has ever seen a force. We see a spring stretch or a ball speed up, and we say a force did it. Newton's own rule says that a force is what changes motion, which defines force by what it does. In modern physics the deepest descriptions do not use force at all. They use energy and things called fields, which fill space. Some physicists say force is only a useful everyday word, like 'sunrise'. So this is a word that sounds settled and is not. What do you think a force is?
- e.g. We say 'the Sun rises', and the phrase is useful, though it is the Earth that turns.
- e.g. A force diagram of a kite predicts correctly whether the kite will climb, without telling us what an arrow is made of.
- **Engineers and most physics teachers:** Force is as real as anything in science. Bridges stand and rockets fly because the forces were worked out correctly.
- **Heinrich Hertz in 1894 and Ernst Mach in 1883:** Both tried to write the science of motion without force as a basic idea. They thought force was a name we give to a pattern in how things move, not a thing in itself.
- **Frank Wilczek, a Nobel Prize physicist, writing in 2004:** He asked why the idea of force survives, when the deepest theories do without it. His answer was that force is a wonderfully practical way of thinking, and that this is not the same as being basic.
### Engineers arrange forces so that they balance: dams, bridges and nets · *defined*
**Definition:** A structure stands when every push and pull on each of its parts is balanced by another.

A bridge does not stand because it is strong in some general way. It stands because an engineer has traced where every force goes. The weight of a bus presses down on the deck. The deck pulls on cables or pushes on steel beams. The beams push on the towers, and the towers push on the ground. The ground pushes back. If one link cannot give the push or pull that is asked of it, the bridge falls. A dam must do the same against the sideways push of deep water. Good engineering is reading forces carefully before anything is built.
- e.g. The Kallanai dam on the Kaveri in Tamil Nadu was built of rough stone under the Chola king Karikala, about the 2nd century CE. It still turns the river's water into the fields today.
- e.g. The Howrah Bridge in Kolkata, opened in 1943, hangs its road from two great steel arms that balance on two piers. Its steel is joined with rivets, which are metal pins hammered tight, and it has no nuts and bolts.
- e.g. The Bandra-Worli Sea Link in Mumbai, opened in 2009, holds up its road with slanting steel cables in tension.
- e.g. The big fishing nets at Fort Kochi in Kerala are balanced by stones hung on ropes at the back. A few people can raise a net wider than a room.

## Mostly agreed
- With no air, a hammer and a feather fall side by side. *How we know:* In 1971 the astronaut David Scott stood on the Moon, which has no air, and dropped a hammer and a falcon's feather from the same height. Both reached the dust at the same moment. The film can be watched today.
- The pull of gravity is very slightly different from place to place on the Earth. *How we know:* Instruments on satellites and on the ground measure it. The pull is a little weaker at the equator than at the poles, and a little weaker on a mountain than at the sea. A band of slightly weaker pull also lies under the Indian Ocean, south of Sri Lanka, for reasons scientists are still working out.
- The Earth's magnetic north pole is not fixed. It wanders, and the whole magnetic field has flipped over many times in the past. *How we know:* Since the pole was first located in 1831, it has moved more than 2,000 kilometres across the Arctic. Lava records the direction of the field as it cools. Layers of old lava, including the Deccan Traps of Maharashtra, show the field pointing one way and then the other.
- Muscles and bones weaken when the usual forces are taken away. *How we know:* Astronauts who live for months in a space station lose bone and muscle, even with two hours of exercise a day. The main reason is that their bodies are not pressed against a floor. Doctors measure the loss when they return.

## How we found out
**Aristotle, John Philoponus, Simon Stevin, Galileo Galilei, Isaac Newton, David Scott, About 350 BCE to 1971.** For nearly two thousand years, people who studied nature followed Aristotle. He taught that heavy things fall faster than light ones, in proportion to their weight, and that anything moving stops unless pushed. Both ideas fitted daily life: a stone beats a leaf to the ground, and a cart stops when the bullock does. About 530 CE, John Philoponus in Alexandria wrote that he had dropped two weights, one many times heavier than the other. He said they landed at almost the same moment. Few listened. In 1586 Simon Stevin in Holland dropped two lead balls, one ten times the weight of the other, from a church tower onto a wooden board. They struck with one sound. In 1638 Galileo Galilei, in Italy, published his own tests with balls rolling down slopes. He showed that falling things gain speed steadily, and reasoned that a leaf lags only because the air holds it back. In 1687 Isaac Newton gathered all this into three laws of motion and one rule for gravity. In 1971 an astronaut on the airless Moon dropped a hammer and a feather together, and the world watched them land as one.  
*What people believed before:* People held that heavier things fall faster, and that motion needs a constant push. Both were believable because air resistance and friction are always present on Earth and hide what gravity alone does. Tests that made the hidden forces small showed the truth.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Gravity is not a basic force at all. It arises from something deeper, the way the push of air on a wall comes from many moving particles hitting it.** (Erik Verlinde, a Dutch physicist, in 2010, building on work by Ted Jacobson in 1995; *minority*)  
  For: The mathematics of gravity looks surprisingly like the mathematics of heat. Verlinde showed that Newton's rule for gravity can be worked out from ideas about information and heat alone.  
  Against: Most physicists are not persuaded. Tests of his idea against the motions of galaxies have given mixed results, and nobody has shown what the 'something deeper' is made of.
- **Newton's rule for gravity needs changing where the pull is extremely weak.** (Mordehai Milgrom, an Israeli physicist, in 1983; *debated*)  
  For: Stars at the edges of galaxies go round faster than Newton's rule allows for the matter we can see. Milgrom's small change to the rule predicts those speeds well for many galaxies.  
  Against: Most astronomers explain the fast stars by unseen 'dark matter' instead. Milgrom's change does not work well for great clusters of galaxies. Nobody has yet caught a particle of dark matter either.
- **A moving thing needs a force to keep it moving.** (Aristotle, about 350 BCE, and nearly everyone's common sense today; *rejected*)  
  For: Every cart, cycle and ball we push does stop when we stop pushing. Studies show that most adults, even after school physics, still think this way.  
  Against: The stopping is caused by friction and air resistance, which are forces. Where these are very small, as for a spacecraft, things keep moving for decades with no push.
- **Plants do not merely respond to gravity. They sense, weigh up and decide.** (Monica Gagliano, Stefano Mancuso and other researchers in plant behaviour, since about 2005; *debated*)  
  For: A root tip responds to gravity, water, touch, light and chemicals together, and what the root does cannot be predicted from any one of them. Plants also behave differently after experience.  
  Against: In 2019 Lincoln Taiz and seven other plant scientists wrote a reply. They said plants lack the body parts that go with experience and choice in animals. They said chemistry alone can explain the results.

## Roots
You have learnt to read the forces on still and moving things. Now we ask what that tells us about ourselves, about life and about this world.

**Deep truth.** On the scale under the books, the number went up when you pressed down and went down when you lifted, though nothing moved. The scale does not read the pull of the Earth by itself. It reads how hard its own top pushes up. So the weight you feel on a scale, or on your feet, is a push from below, and that push changes by exactly the amount needed.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think weight is something a thing simply has, like its colour. But a scale and your feet feel the push of a surface. Take the surface away, as in a falling lift, and the feeling of weight goes, though the Earth pulls just as hard.  
*Evidence:* In the scale test, pressing on the books raised the reading and lifting them lowered it, while the books stayed put. In the cone test, the push of the air grew until it matched the weight. In both, a push grew until it matched what it met.

- *who am i:* As you sit reading, what you feel as your weight is the chair pushing up on you. Your muscles pull on your bones, and your heart squeezes roughly 100,000 times a day. Feeling heavy is really feeling held up by the chair, the Earth and your own body together.
- *what is life:* A root senses the pull of the Earth and grows down. A fish lets the water carry their weight. A muscle is billions of tiny pulls added together. Every living being lives by reading forces and answering them, and is never outside them.
- *what is this world:* Follow the push under a book and you go from the table to the floor, then to the ground and the whole Earth. The Sun holds the Earth in its path. No object has been found that nothing pushes or pulls. The world is joined all the way through by these pushes and pulls.

This lesson asked you to pick one thing and list the forces on it. Every list led to other things: the table, the rope, the water, the Earth. You could not finish describing one thing without bringing in the rest. That is a plain finding about forces. It suggests that a 'separate thing' is not as separate as it looks. Some teachers of oneness go one step further. They say consciousness is the ground from which every thing and every force arises. In their words, the balance holding a book on a table is that one ground holding itself. Many scientists accept the finding and not this last step, and their view is given below.

- **Bhagavad Gita 4.18:** Krishna says that the one who sees inaction in action, and action in inaction, is wise among people. (The verse is about how a person should act, not about tables. The lesson's science gives a small, exact picture of its second half. A book lying still is the meeting point of two forces that never stop acting.)
- **Heraclitus of Ephesus, about 500 BCE, fragment 51 in the Diels and Kranz numbering:** He wrote that people do not understand how a thing can agree with itself while pulling apart. There is a harmony of opposite pulls, as in the bow and the lyre. (A bow keeps its shape because wood and string pull against each other. The fishing net, the bridge and the tug of war rope in this lesson stand in the same way.)
- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 78:** Laozi writes that nothing in the world is softer or more yielding than water, yet nothing is better at wearing down the hard and strong. (Water gives way to a finger and still holds up a ship. The lesson measured that upward push. Laozi draws from it a teaching about how to live, which science neither supports nor denies.)
- Science (settled): Every force is one side of a pair. When the Earth pulls you, you pull the Earth equally. No force has ever been found acting alone.
- Science (open): Ernst Mach proposed that a thing's resistance to being pushed comes from all the other matter in the universe. Einstein took the idea seriously. It has never been confirmed or ruled out.
- Other view, Physicalist scientists, and the Charvaka school of ancient India: Balanced forces are a matter of adding numbers. In this view nothing is 'holding' or 'keeping' anything, and a table does not act. Words like holding and keeping are only a way of speaking. The joined-up picture is true as physics, and it shows no ground beneath physics.

The floor is pushing up on them, exactly as hard as the Earth is pulling you down. Now lean very slowly forward, then back, and feel the push move under your toes and your heels. Come back to the middle. Take three slow breaths, and notice that you are being held up without doing anything.

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A book on a table has two large forces on it and does nothing. Is anything happening to the book? (hint: Imagine one of the two forces vanishing. Then ask if 'doing nothing' is the right description of what was there before.)
- *deep:* Your finger never truly touches the table, because electrons push apart first. So what do you mean when you say you touched something? (hint: Think about what you feel in your fingertip and where that feeling is made.)
- *deep:* Nobody has ever seen a force, only what forces do. Is a force a real thing, or an idea we use to explain what we see? (hint: Think of other things you know only by what they do, such as the wind. Is the wind real?)
- *deep:* Einstein said a falling ball has no force on it. Newton said gravity pulls it. Both predict the same landing. Can both be right? (hint: Ask what job each picture is doing. Then ask what would have to be found to choose between them.)
- *big:* A fish held up by water does not feel their own weight. Is there something holding you up all the time that you have stopped noticing? (hint: Start with the floor. Then think more widely about what you lean on every day.)
- *deep:* You pull the Earth exactly as hard as the Earth pulls you. Why does it feel as if only the Earth is doing the pulling? (hint: Think about who moves more when a small child and a large adult push off from each other on roller skates.)

## Story: The Net That Was Not Resting
*A made-up story set at Fort Kochi in Kerala. The people are imagined. The big shore nets are real. Each is a wooden frame that tips like a see-saw on a post, with a net at the front and stones hung on ropes at the back, and is worked by a small crew. People in Kochi say that traders from China brought the design about 600 years ago, and historians are not sure. Most fish hold a small bag of gas in the body that lets the water carry their weight.*

Joel, who was seven, thought the big fishing net at Fort Kochi was asleep. It stood over the water like a giant spider made of teak poles and rope, and not one part of it moved. His cousin Nilofer, who was ten, had brought him at dawn to watch Rosy chechi's crew. Rosy had worked this net for twenty years, and now she led its crew of five.

'It is not doing anything,' said Joel.

'Put your hand on this rope,' said Rosy chechi.

He did. The rope was as hard as an iron rod, and it hummed under his palm.

'That rope is pulling up on the net as hard as it can,' she said. 'The net is pulling down on the rope just as hard. Neither one wins. That is why you see nothing.' She pointed to the back of the frame, where a row of big stones hung in the air, each on a rope of its own. 'The Earth pulls those stones down. The stones pull the back of the frame down. The frame tips on that post like a see-saw. So the stones behind us are holding up the net in front of us.'

Nilofer liked to work things out one piece at a time. 'Take one stone,' she said. 'The Earth pulls it down. The rope pulls it up. The two are equal, so the stone hangs still.' She looked at her own feet. 'And me. The Earth pulls me down, and the planks push me up.' She jumped. The planks creaked. 'They push harder when I land.'

Old Kunjumon was mending a float nearby. He snorted. 'Forty years I have hauled this net. I never drew an arrow. The net is heavy when the tide runs and light when the water is slack. My shoulders know.'

'Your shoulders are measuring the same pulls she is counting,' said Rosy chechi.

'Arrows do not get tired,' said Kunjumon. 'So arrows miss half of it.'

Nobody had an answer to that.

Rosy chechi called out, and the crew walked forward along the main beam. Their weight tipped the see-saw. The net sank under the water, and the stones at the back rose. Everyone waited. Then the crew walked back and hauled on the ropes. The stones came down, one after another, and the net came up streaming. Joel hauled too. The rope pulled on his arms exactly as hard as he pulled on the rope.

In the net a dozen small silver fish were flapping. Nilofer watched one of them. 'In the water they were not heavy,' she said slowly. 'The water pushed up on them as hard as the Earth pulled down. They never had to hold themselves up. Now the water is gone, and only the net is holding them.'

'First time in their lives they meet their whole weight,' said Rosy chechi. She tipped the smallest ones back into the sea.

'So in the water they had no weight?' asked Joel.

'No,' said Nilofer. 'The Earth was pulling them the whole time. They could not feel it, because something was pushing back from every side.' She stopped. 'Like us. We do not feel the planks pushing.'

Kunjumon looked up from his float. 'Then what is it that I feel, when the net is heavy?'

Nilofer thought for a long time. 'You feel your own muscle pulling one way and the rope pulling the other. You feel what it is like to be in the middle.'

The old man grunted, and went back to his knot. But he was smiling a little.

The Sun was up now. The net hung over the water again, quite still. Joel looked at it, and this time he could see. The rope was tight. The stones hung. The post pressed into the mud, and the mud pressed back. The wind leaned on the poles. A crow sat on the highest pole, and the crow's toes gripped the wood.

'It is not asleep,' he said. 'It is a tug of war, and everybody is winning the same amount.'

'Nobody is winning,' said Rosy chechi. 'That is what standing still is.'

Joel looked along the shore, at the boats, the wall and the sea. 'Is there anything, anywhere, that nothing is pulling or pushing?'

Rosy chechi looked at the water for a while. 'I have never met one,' she said. 'If you find one, bring it to me.'

**For the parent:** The still net was held by pulls and pushes in exact balance. The fish were carried by the water without feeling it, as Joel was carried by the planks. Standing still turned out to be something that many things do together.

- Ask: Kunjumon said arrows miss half of it. What do his shoulders know that a force diagram leaves out?
- Ask: The fish did not feel their weight until the water was gone. What holds you up that you have never noticed?
- Ask: Joel asked if there is anything that nothing pulls or pushes. What would you look at first to find out?
- Ask: Rosy chechi said nobody is winning, and that is what standing still is. Do you agree?

## Experiments
### e1 · The falling cones test: when does a falling thing stop speeding up? (35 min)
- **question:** A paper cone falls from your raised arm. Does it keep speeding up all the way down, or does it stop speeding up? And what happens to its speed if the cone is four times as heavy?
- **predict:** Before you drop anything, guess. Four cones are pressed into each other and dropped beside one cone. Will the stack land in a quarter of the time, half the time or the same time? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** Only the number of cones changes: one, two or four. The paper, the slope of the cones, the floor and the person dropping all stay the same. So does the starting height, with the tips level with a mark at 2 metres. The cone falls point down, with the fan off and the windows shut. Each load is dropped three times, because one drop can be spoiled by a draught or a wobble. The middle time of the three is used.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Circle the middle time of each load. Work out the speed over 2 metres for each load by dividing 2 by the middle time. Then write one sentence each for: what I think, what I saw and why.
- **results table:** Load | Try 1 (seconds) | Try 2 (seconds) | Try 3 (seconds) | Middle time (seconds) | Speed over 2 metres (metres per second)
- **e1s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw round the lid on a sheet of paper five times and cut out the five circles. Cut one straight slit from the edge of each circle to its middle. Overlap the cut edges until the cone is about 12 centimetres across the open end. Check the open end with a ruler. Tape the seam in two places. Make all five cones in exactly the same way, so that they fit inside each other.  
  *Parent says:* We need five cones that are all the same. Why do you think we must make them the same?  
  *Child mode:* Cut out five paper circles. Cut a slit to the middle of each one. Roll each into a cone and tape it. Make all five the same.
  - Note: Cutting circles is fine for a child of 6 or more with blunt-ended scissors. Check that two cones pressed together fit snugly, with no gap at the sides. The cones must overlap by the same amount, or their slopes differ and the test is not fair.
- **e1s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Measure 2 metres up from the floor on a wall or door frame and stick a piece of tape there. Clear the floor below. Switch off any fan and close the windows next to where you will drop. Then ask your child to make the prediction, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Four cones pressed into each other have four times the weight of one cone. Will the stack land in a quarter of the time, in half the time or in the same time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how long four cones will take to land compared with one cone. Write your guess and a reason.
  - Ask: Why do you think heavy things might fall faster? (good: Because they are pulled harder by the Earth, so they should go quicker.; Because they are not blown about by the air as much.; Any honest reason is a good start.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say a quarter, because four times the weight should mean four times as fast. Others say the same time, because they have heard that heavy and light things fall together. Both guesses are worth testing, and the drops decide.
- **e1s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The grown-up holds one cone, tip down, with the tip level with the 2 metre mark and the arm raised. The child holds the phone stopwatch. The grown-up says 'Go' as they open their fingers. The child starts the stopwatch at the word. The child stops it the moment the cone touches the floor. Write the time. Do this three times. A drop where the cone tumbles over or hits the wall does not count, so do that drop again.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the cone as it falls. Does it speed up all the way down, or does it settle at one speed? How can you tell?  
  *Child mode:* Start the stopwatch when you hear 'Go'. Stop it when the cone touches the floor. Do it three times.
  - Ask: Does the cone look as if it keeps speeding up, or does it seem to fall at one speed? (good: It seems to fall at one speed after the first moment.; It speeds up at the start and then looks steady.; Any honest answer. Looking is a hard way to tell, which is why we time it.)
  - Note: A single cone usually takes about 1.2 to 1.8 seconds to fall 2 metres. Never stand on a chair or stool to drop. If nobody can reach 2 metres, use a mark at 1.5 metres for every drop. All the times will then be shorter. Your fingers are slow at starting and stopping, but they are slow by about the same amount both times, so most of the error cancels. If the single cone lands in under 1 second, the paper is thick or the cones are small. Make new cones from thinner paper, such as a notebook sheet or a newspaper page, or use paper muffin cases. A short time is still usable, because we compare the loads with each other.
- **e1s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now drop two cones pressed into each other, and time three drops as before. Then do the same with four cones pressed into each other. Press them in firmly each time, so that they fall as one piece, point down. Write each time in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Before you time the stack of four, what do you think the time will be, compared with the single cone? Write it down. Then we will see.  
  *Child mode:* Time two stacked cones three times, and then four stacked cones three times. Write each time.
  - Note: Expect two stacked cones to take about three quarters of the single cone's time, and four stacked cones to take about 0.55 to 0.7 of it. These numbers come from a calculation, not from a family test, so your own may differ. If the four-stack lands in about a quarter of the single time, or in the same time as one, check two things. Were the cones pressed firmly into each other? Were the tips level with the mark? If your result is a little different, keep it and say so. A real result is better than a tidy one.
- **e1s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Move the tape mark to 1 metre above the floor. Time one cone dropped from there, three times. Then work out how long the second metre took. Take the 1 metre middle time away from the 2 metre middle time.  
  *Parent says:* A stone dropped from 1 metre takes about half a second. Its second metre takes only about 0.2 seconds, because it keeps speeding up. How long did the second metre take for your cone?  
  *Child mode:* Drop one cone from the 1 metre mark three times. Take the 1 metre time away from the 2 metre time.
  - Ask: The second metre took the cone far longer than it would take a stone. What does that tell you about the push of the air? (good: The air is pushing up on the cone, and the push grows until it equals the pull of the Earth.; Something is holding the cone back, so it stops speeding up.)
  - Note: A single cone usually takes about 0.7 to 1.0 seconds from 1 metre, so the second metre takes about 0.5 to 0.8 seconds. That is only a little shorter than the first metre. A stone's second metre takes less than half as long as its first. So the cone is hardly speeding up in the second metre. It has reached a steady speed.
- **e1s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Work out each speed by dividing 2 metres by the middle time. Look at the three speeds. Then write what you think, what you saw and why. End by saying what the test cannot tell us.  
  *Parent says:* Four cones had four times the weight. Did they fall four times as fast? Why do you think the air did this?  
  *Child mode:* Divide 2 by each middle time. Which load was fastest? Write: what I think, what I saw, why.
  - Ask: What would happen to the four cones if there were no air at all? (good: They would all land together, at the same moment, as the hammer and the feather did on the Moon.; Nothing would push back, so the cones would keep speeding up.)
  - Note: A calculation suggests results near 1.4 metres per second for one cone and near 2.3 for four stacked, and yours may differ. So four times the weight gave far less than four times the speed. The steady speed itself is about double. The average over 2 metres is lower, because the cone is still speeding up at the start. The reason is that the push of the air grows faster than the speed. Double the speed and the push grows about four times. So each stack stops speeding up when the push of the air equals its weight. A stack four times heavier needs twice the speed to do that. The test does not show why the push of air grows in this way, and it does not show what happens at much greater speeds. Ask what else the child would test: a bigger cone, a flatter cone, a different height.
  - Think: If you wanted a cone to fall more slowly still, what would you change, and why?
### e2 · The scale under the book: how hard does a table push up? (25 min)
- **question:** A book rests on a scale and stays still. The Earth pulls it down. How hard does the scale push up, and does the push change if you press on the book?
- **predict:** Before you press on the book, guess. If you press down on the book with your hand, will the scale read more, the same or less? And if you lift the book a little with your hand, will it read more, the same or less? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same scale, on the same flat table, and the same books are used each time. Only what your hand does changes: nothing, pressing down or lifting. The scale is set to zero with nothing on it at the start. Each reading is taken three times, because the numbers wobble a little.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Work out the push of the scale in newtons for each book by changing the reading to kilograms and multiplying by 10. Then write what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** What I did | Reading before (grams or kilograms) | Reading while doing it | Push in newtons (reading in kilograms times 10)
- **e2s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the scale on a flat, firm table. Switch it on with nothing on it and wait until it reads zero. On a kitchen scale, press Tare if there is a Tare button. Do not lean on the table. Ask your child to say what the number means.  
  *Parent says:* The scale reads zero now. What do you think the scale is measuring when something is put on it?  
  *Child mode:* Put the scale on the table and switch it on. It should say zero.
  - Note: A kitchen scale has a platform that is squashed by a tiny amount when something is put on it. A sensor inside turns that squash into a number. Soft surfaces such as a bed or a carpet give wrong readings, so use a table or the floor. With a bathroom scale and no kitchen scale, use a stack of books of at least 3 kilograms, such as a school bag of books. Many bathroom scales are not accurate below 2 kilograms.
- **e2s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the lightest book in the middle of the scale and write the reading. Add the next book and write the new reading. Then add the third. Change each reading to kilograms and multiply by 10 to get the push in newtons. For example, 600 grams is 0.6 kilograms, and 0.6 times 10 is 6 newtons.  
  *Parent says:* How hard do you think the Earth pulls on this book? And how hard is the scale pushing back? Are they the same, or different?  
  *Child mode:* Put one book on the scale and write the number. Add the other books one at a time and write each number.
  - Ask: The book is not moving. Is anything pushing on it? (good: Yes. The scale is pushing up, and the Earth is pulling down.; The two are equal, so the book stays still.)
  - Note: The reading in grams or kilograms shows how hard the scale is being squashed. The push of the scale on the book equals the book's weight. The weight in newtons is the reading in kilograms times about 10. A bathroom scale in kilograms works the same way. If the scale seems to read a little too much or too little, do not worry. What matters is that adding a book adds the same number to the reading as that book's weight.
- **e2s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave all three books on the scale and wait until the reading is steady. Write it. Now press down on the top book with your flat hand and write the reading while you press. Then take your hand off and slide your fingers under the edge of the top book. Lift it very slightly, without taking it off the stack, and write the reading while you lift. Do each three times.  
  *Parent says:* You are pressing down on the books. Does the scale read more, the same or less? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Press down on the books and read the number. Then lift the top book a little and read the number again.
  - Ask: The books did not move when you pressed. How can the scale read a bigger number? (good: The scale pushes up harder, as hard as the books' weight and your hand's push together.; The support force changes to match whatever is needed.)
  - Note: Pressing down adds your push to the weight, so the scale reads more, and the extra reading is your push. Lifting takes some of the weight off the scale, so it reads less. In both cases the push from the scale is exactly what is needed to hold the books still. If the reading barely changes, press harder, but never hard enough to bend the scale.
- **e2s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* If you have a bathroom scale, stand on it with your feet still and read the number. Bend your knees quickly, as if about to sit, and watch the number during the bend and just after. If the scale is digital and holds its reading, skip this and go on to the next step.  
  *Parent says:* When you bend your knees quickly, does the number go up or down for a moment? What do you think the floor is doing?  
  *Child mode:* Stand on the scale. Bend your knees quickly. Watch the number.
  - Note: On a scale that shows the number as it changes, the reading dips below your usual number for a moment as you start to drop. Then it rises above it as you stop. For that moment the floor pushes up with less than your weight, and you speed up downward. This is a small taste of what an astronaut feels all the time. Many digital scales are slow and freeze their number, so do not worry if nothing changes.
- **e2s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table. Write one sentence each for what I think, what I saw and why. Finish with what the test cannot tell us.  
  *Parent says:* Nothing moved, yet the scale pushed. Why does a thing that is not moving need a push from underneath?  
  *Child mode:* Write: what I think, what I saw, why. Then write one thing the scale cannot tell you.
  - Note: The scale does not measure mass directly. It measures how hard its own top is pushed, and shows that as grams. On the Moon, the same books would give a lower reading, because the pull of the Moon is weaker. The mass of the books is the same. Ask your child what else they would test: a book on a soft mattress, or the same book in a lift.
  - Think: If the table pushed up with less than the book's weight, what would happen to the book?
### e3 · How far can a magnet pull through cardboard? (25 min)
- **question:** A fridge magnet pulls a steel paper clip across a table without touching it. How thick a stack of card can sit in between before the pull is too weak to drag the clip along?
- **predict:** Before you test, guess how many millimetres of card the magnet can pull through. Will the pull fade away slowly as the card gets thicker, or will it hold well and then let go all at once? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same magnet, the same steel paper clip, the same smooth table and the same slow slide of the magnet are used every time. Only the number of card squares between the magnet and the clip changes. The squares are all cut from the same card. Each thickness is tried three times, because the magnet may slip. The answer is 'follows' only if the clip follows the magnet for at least 10 centimetres.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Write 'yes', 'sometimes' or 'no' for each try. Find the thickest stack where the clip still followed all three times, and the thinnest where it never followed. Then write what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Number of card squares | Thickness of the stack (millimetres) | Try 1: does the clip follow? | Try 2 | Try 3
- **e3s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut about 20 squares, 5 centimetres on each side, from thin card, such as a cereal box. Check the paper clip: it must stick to the magnet. If it does not, try another clip, because some clips are not steel. Stack the squares and measure the thickness of 10 of them with the ruler, then work out one square's thickness.  
  *Parent says:* The magnet pulls the clip even when it is not touching. How do you think the pull will change when there is more card in between?  
  *Child mode:* Cut 20 squares from card. Check that the clip sticks to the magnet. Measure the stack of 10.
  - Note: Thin card from a cereal box is about 0.5 millimetres, so 10 squares are about 5 millimetres. If your card is thicker, the thickness per square will be bigger, and that is fine as long as you measure the stack with the ruler. If you have a kitchen magnet that is very strong, it may hold the clip through all 20 squares. In that case use a notebook (about 5 millimetres) in place of squares.
- **e3s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to make the prediction, and write it down. Lay the clip flat on the smooth table.  
  *Parent says:* How many millimetres of card do you think the magnet can pull through? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the thickness at which the clip will stop following. Write your guess and a reason.
  - Ask: Does the pull fade slowly or stop suddenly? What makes you say so? (good: It fades slowly, so the clip should follow up to a big thickness.; It lets go quickly, so only a thin stack will work.; Any honest reason is good.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Children often guess by feeling. The test then tells them how good the feeling was.
- **e3s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start with no card. Slide the magnet slowly, about 10 centimetres, across the table beside the clip, and watch the clip follow. Now put 2 squares over the clip and hold them flat. Slide the magnet over the top of the stack, directly above the clip. Note if the clip follows for at least 10 centimetres. Add 2 squares at a time, and try three times at each thickness. Write 'yes', 'sometimes' or 'no'.  
  *Parent says:* At which stack does the clip start to be left behind? How can you tell that the pull is still there, even when the clip does not follow?  
  *Child mode:* Slide the magnet over the stack. Does the clip follow? Add 2 squares and try again. Write what happens each time.
  - Ask: What happens just before the clip stops following? (good: It follows a little and then falls behind.; It follows sometimes and sometimes not, which means the pull is just enough.)
  - Note: Slide the magnet slowly, about 2 centimetres each second, or the clip will be left behind even if the pull is strong. The clip may jump up and stick to the underside of the card, which is fine. Pull the magnet sideways at the same height each time. If a try is spoiled because the stack slipped, do that try again. Around the thickness where the clip begins to fail, 'sometimes' is a real and useful answer.
- **e3s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table. Find the thickest stack where the clip followed all three times, and the thinnest where it never followed. Write what you think, what you saw and why. Finish with what the test cannot tell us.  
  *Parent says:* Was your guess close? How quickly did the pull fade as the card got thicker?  
  *Child mode:* Find where the clip stopped following. Write: what I think, what I saw, why.
  - Note: Between 'follows every time' and 'never follows' there are often only a few squares, which is only a millimetre or two. This shows that the pull fades quickly with distance. The test tells us only whether the pull beat the grip of the table on the clip. It does not give the pull in newtons. To measure that we would need a spring that stretches, such as the one in a force meter. Ask your child what else they would test: a stronger magnet, two magnets stuck together, a heavier clip.
  - Think: If you had a magnet twice as strong, do you think it would pull through twice as many squares? How could you find out?

### a1 · Two questions on six things at home: draw the forces (25 min)
- **record:** Draw a box or dot for each thing, with one arrow for each force and a label on every arrow. Make the arrows for forces that balance the same length.
- **a1s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at a school bag on a hook, or any hanging thing. Ask your child the two questions: what is touching the bag, and what is pulling it from a distance? Then draw the bag as a box on paper and add the two arrows. The hook pulls the bag up and the Earth pulls it down. Make the arrows the same length.  
  *Parent says:* What is touching the bag? What is pulling it without touching? What would happen if the hook let go?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the bag. What is touching it? What is pulling it from far? Draw a box and two arrows.
  - Ask: What is touching the bag? (good: The hook, and a little bit of the air.; The hook is the one that matters.)
  - Ask: What pulls it from a distance? (good: The Earth, through gravity.; Gravity pulls it down.)
  - Note: The air touches the bag too, but its push is so small that it does not count here. A force diagram shows the forces that matter. Both arrows are the same length because the bag is not speeding up or slowing down.
- **a1s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now do the same for four more things. First choose a fan or a hanging light. Then choose a cup on a shelf, your child on a chair and a fridge magnet on a door. For each one, ask the two questions and draw a box with arrows. The magnet is the hardest, because it has four forces. The Earth pulls it down and friction from the door holds it up. The magnetic pull draws it into the door, and the door pushes back out.  
  *Parent says:* Which forces do you think act on the fridge magnet? Why does it not slide down the door?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a fan, a cup, a person and a magnet. Give each one arrows.
  - Ask: Why does the magnet not slide down the fridge? (good: Friction between the magnet and the door holds it up.; The magnet pulls hard into the door, which makes the grip bigger.)
  - Note: The cup has gravity down and the support force from the shelf up. The fan has the pull of the rod up and gravity down. The person has gravity down and the chair's support force up. For the magnet, the magnetic pull in and the push of the door out are equal. Weight and friction are also equal. Praise any labelled arrow that is honest, even if a child finds only two of the four.
- **a1s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose two of your drawings. Check that the arrows for forces that cancel are the same length. Then ask: which of the things you drew would fall if one force were switched off? Which force would you switch off?  
  *Parent says:* If you could switch off one force on the cup, which one would make the cup fall? What does that tell you about the table?  
  *Child mode:* Pick two drawings. Check that the arrows that cancel are the same length. Which force would you switch off to make the thing fall?
  - Note: The still things at home are all held still by forces in balance. Take your time and let your child say it in their own words.
  - Think: Can you find a thing in your home with no force on it at all?
### a2 · A pulley from a thread reel: two bottles that balance (25 min)
- **record:** Write the number of teaspoons of water that you needed to add before the bottle went down. Draw the pulley and put an arrow for the pull in the thread on each side.
- **a2s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Push the pencil through the reel. Lay the pencil across two stacks of books, or the backs of two chairs. The reel should hang in the gap and turn freely. Tie a loop of thread round the neck of each bottle. Join the two loops with a long thread laid over the reel. Fill both bottles with water to the same level. Put a tray on the floor below.  
  *Parent says:* The thread goes over a wheel. What do you think the wheel does to the pull in the thread?  
  *Child mode:* Push a pencil through a thread reel. Lay it across two stacks of books. Hang a bottle on each side of the thread over the reel.
  - Note: A grown-up should check that the stacks are steady and the pencil cannot slip off. The bottles are plastic, so nothing can break. If the thread keeps slipping off the reel, tie a small piece of tape across the groove.
- **a2s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lift one bottle a hand's width higher than the other and let go. See if the bottles stay where they are. They should stay still because the pull on each side is the same. Then ask your child how big the pull in the thread is on each side. They can use what they know about gravity and weight.  
  *Parent says:* Both bottles are the same. The Earth pulls each one down. What is pulling each bottle up? How strong is that pull, compared with the weight of one bottle?  
  *Child mode:* Let go of the bottles. Do they move? Why do they stay still?
  - Ask: The thread goes over a reel. Is the pull in the thread the same on both sides? (good: Yes, because the bottles are the same and neither moves.; The reel changes only the direction, not the size, of the pull.)
  - Note: Each bottle is held by the pull of the thread, which equals the bottle's weight. The same thread carries the same pull on both sides, so the bottles balance. The reel only changes the direction of the pull. A small amount of friction in the reel helps the bottles stay in place.
- **a2s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add one teaspoon of water to one bottle, then another, then count the teaspoons. Stop as soon as that bottle starts to go down. Write the number. Do it again on the other bottle.  
  *Parent says:* Why does it take a few teaspoons before the bottle goes down? What is the reel doing against you?  
  *Child mode:* Add water, a teaspoon at a time, to one bottle. Count the spoons. Stop when it goes down.
  - Ask: Which direction is friction pointing when the bottle starts to sink? (good: Against the sinking, so upward on the thread.; Friction opposes the motion.)
  - Note: The extra weight needed is the friction in the reel and the stiffness of the thread. A reel that turns very freely needs only one or two teaspoons. A thread that rubs needs many. Each teaspoon is about 5 grams, which is about one twentieth of a newton, so even a pulling difference that small can show.
- **a2s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now pull one side of the thread down with your hand. The other bottle rises, as a bucket does at a well. Then pour the water onto a plant and dry the tray.  
  *Parent says:* At a well you pull the rope down to bring the bucket up. How does the pulley let you do that?  
  *Child mode:* Pull one side of the thread down. The other bottle goes up. Pour the water onto a plant.
  - Note: The reel turns the pull around. Pulling down on one side gives a pull up on the other. It is easier for a person to pull down, because the body's weight helps.
  - Think: Why do you think people pull a rope down at a well, and not up?
### a3 · Feel the water push up: floating, sinking and a steel katori (25 min)
- **record:** Write how many coins the katori held before it sank, and how that number changes if the katori is set down tilted or pushed under water. Draw the two arrows on the floating katori.
- **a3s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the basin on a tray on the floor and fill it half full of water. Push the empty, capped bottle slowly down into the water, and notice how hard the water pushes back up. Ask your child to push it all the way to the bottom. Let go and watch what happens.  
  *Parent says:* How hard do you have to push the bottle down? What is pushing back? Why does the bottle shoot up when you let go?  
  *Child mode:* Push the empty bottle down in the water. Feel the push back. Let go.
  - Note: The push you feel is upthrust. The deeper the bottle goes, the more water it pushes aside, until it is fully under, and from then on the push stays about the same. If the bottle shoots up and splashes, it is working. A towel under the basin helps.
- **a3s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Set the steel katori gently on the water, open end up. Let go. It floats. Then push it under, open end down, so that it traps air. Feel the push. Then tip it so that it fills with water and watch it sink.  
  *Parent says:* A steel katori is heavier than water, and yet it floats. Why do you think it floats when set down gently?  
  *Child mode:* Set the katori on the water. Let go. Now push it down and feel it push back.
  - Ask: Why does the katori float when set down, and sink when full of water? (good: When it is full of water it no longer pushes aside enough water to equal its weight.; A hollow shape pushes away more water than a lump of the same steel.)
  - Note: The katori floats because its hollow shape pushes aside a lot of water. The weight of that water equals the katori's weight.   *Child mode:* Float the katori. Add coins one at a time. Count them. Write the number when it sinks.
  - Ask: What changed each time you added a coin? (good: The katori went lower, so it pushed aside more water.; The weight went up and the upthrust went up to match, until it could not.)
  - Note: More coins push the katori deeper, so it pushes aside more water, and the upthrust grows. It grows until the katori is nearly full or the water spills over the edge. ### a4 · The coin see-saw: where a small weight balances a big one (20 min)
- **record:** Fill in a table with columns: coins on the left, distance on the left, coins on the right, distance on the right, balanced or not. Write the rule you find.
- **a4s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the pencil on a smooth table. Rest the ruler across the pencil with the 15 centimetre mark over it. Move the ruler a little until it balances flat. If the pencil rolls, put a small piece of tape under it or put it in a groove made by two books.  
  *Parent says:* A see-saw balances when the weights on both sides are equal, and are the same distance from the middle. What do you think will happen if we put 2 coins on one side and 1 coin on the other?  
  *Child mode:* Put the ruler across the pencil so that it balances. Mark the middle.
  - Note: The pencil is the pivot. The ruler is the lever.
- **a4s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put 2 coins on the ruler at the 10 centimetre mark, which is 5 centimetres from the pivot. Place 1 coin on the other side and slide it until the ruler is level. Then try 1 coin at 10 centimetres from the pivot, and 2 coins on the other side. Write every result in the table.

  *Child mode:* Put 2 coins near the pivot. Slide 1 coin on the other side until the ruler is level. Write where it is.
  - Ask: The single coin had to go farther from the pivot. Why? (good: It is lighter, so it must be farther out to balance the heavier side.; A small weight far away balances a big weight near.; Number of coins times distance is the same on both sides.)
  - Note: Two coins 5 centimetres from the pivot are balanced by one coin 10 centimetres from the pivot on the other side. Three coins at 5 centimetres are balanced by one coin at 15 centimetres, which is the very end of the ruler. Count the coins times the distance on each side, and compare. If a result is a little off, the ruler may not be balanced on its own, or the coins may be different. Results within about 1 centimetre are good.
- **a4s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table. Find the rule. Then link it to a door: push at the handle and push near the hinge. Which is easier, and why?  
  *Parent says:* A door is a lever whose pivot is the hinge. Why is it hard to push a door near the hinge?  
  *Child mode:* Find the rule for the coins. Then push a door near the hinge and at the handle.
  - Note: The rule is: the number of coins times their distance from the pivot is equal on both sides. A big push close to the pivot balances a small push far from it. The big fishing nets in Kochi use this rule, and so does your arm.
  - Think: Where is the pivot in your arm, and where is the push?

### a6 · Which surface grips? The tilted board test (25 min)

  *Child mode:* Put the surfaces in order. Draw the eraser with three arrows.
  - Note: Friction does not slow things only. It also lets a walker push off the ground, and a tyre grip the road. On a slope, friction is what holds a standing person still.

### a7 · A rubbed balloon and torn paper: the static pull on a dry day (15 min)

  - Think: If a balloon can pull a bit of paper up against the whole Earth, what does that say about how strong the electric force is?

  - Ask: Why is the parachute's time longer than the coin's? (good: The plastic gives the air a large surface to push against.; The push of the air upward grows until it nearly equals the weight, so the fall is slow.)
  - Note: A parachute catches a lot of air, so the push of the air upward is large for a small weight. The parachute reaches a slow steady speed. Expect about 1.5 to 2.5 seconds for 2 metres, about two to four times the plain coin's time. A very big canopy takes longer to open, so in a short fall it may not reach its steady speed.
- **ch2s3 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Change one thing at a time and test again, three drops each time. Try a bigger square or a smaller one. Try a hole in the middle the size of a coin. Try a different number of threads, or a lighter load. Write what you changed and the new time.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think will happen if you cut a small hole in the middle? Why might real parachutes have one?  
  *Child mode:* Change one thing. Test three drops. Write the time.
  - Ask: What changed the fall time most? (good: The size of the plastic, because a larger parachute pushes more air.; Any change that gave the air more to push on made the fall slower.)
  - Note: A small hole at the top lets some air through and makes the fall steadier, because without it the parachute may swing from side to side. Real parachutes have a vent for this reason. A bigger square gives more air resistance and a slower fall. Changing two things at once makes it hard to know which one did the work.
- **ch2s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare your designs. Which gave the longest time? Draw the best one with arrows for the forces at its steady speed.  
  *Parent says:* At the steady speed, what two forces are balanced? Are the arrows the same length?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your best parachute with two arrows.
  - Note: At the slow steady speed, the weight down and the air's push up are equal. The parachute does not stop the Earth's pull. It makes the air's push big enough to match it at a small speed.
  - Think: Could a parachute work on the Moon, where there is no air? Why or why not?
### ch3 · A foil boat that carries the most coins (30 min)
- **problem:** Make a boat from one square of kitchen foil, 20 centimetres on each side. It should carry the most coins in a bowl or bucket of water before it sinks. There is more than one good way to do it.
- **your turn:** Make a boat of your own design from the foil. What did you change from your first boat, and how did it help? Do you think a boat could be too heavy to float?
- **constraints:** Use one square of foil, 20 by 20 centimetres, and nothing else for the boat.; Float the boat in a basin of water. Add coins one at a time, gently, to the middle.; Count the coins that the boat holds when it takes in water or tips over.; Test each boat twice.
- **general:** Make the sides higher.; Make the boat wider so it pushes more water aside.; Place the coins low in the middle.
- **creative:** Add a ridge along the bottom, like the keel of a boat.; Make a boat with two hulls joined across the top.; Fold a boat with a pointed front and a wide back.
- **ch3s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fold up each edge of a foil square about 2 centimetres to make a shallow tray. Float it in a basin of water on a table. Add coins one at a time, in the middle, until the boat takes in water or tips. Write the number. This is your first design.  
  *Parent says:* A square of foil sinks by itself. Why do you think it floats when it is folded into a tray?  
  *Child mode:* Fold up the edges of the foil. Float it. Add coins one at a time.
  - Note: The tray pushes aside more water than a flat foil, so the upthrust is greater. The boat floats until the water reaches the edge. After that, water pours in over the side. Expect between 20 and 80 coins, depending on the shape. If your first boat leaks, check for small holes in the foil.
- **ch3s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Try the usual solutions. Make the sides higher, about 4 centimetres. Make a long narrow boat or a wide flat one. Fold a double layer on the bottom. Test each design twice and write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Which do you think will carry the most coins: a deep boat, or a wide, shallow one? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Make a boat with higher sides. Test it. Make a long narrow one. Test it.
  - Ask: Why does a bigger boat carry more coins? (good: It pushes aside more water, so the upthrust is greater.; It can sink lower before water comes in.)
  - Note: A deeper, wider boat pushes aside more water before water spills in, so it carries more. A boat that is too tall and narrow may tip over. Coins in the middle keep the weight low and central.
- **ch3s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now try creative ideas. Put the coins at the bottom in a thin layer along the middle. Make a boat with a pointed front like a canoe. Fold a boat with a keel, which is a ridge along the bottom. Or make a boat that stays dry on the inside with a very tall edge. Test each, twice.  
  *Parent says:* Where do you think is the best place for the coins: in the middle or at the end? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Make a boat with a pointed front. Put the coins in the middle. Test it.
  - Ask: Why do the coins tip the boat when they are put on one side? (good: The weight on one side pulls that side down, and the boat can turn over.; The upthrust and the weight must act along the same line for the boat to stay level.)
  - Note: A low, central load makes the boat steadier. A pointed bow moves through water more easily but carries less than a wide boat of the same foil. There are many good answers.
- **ch3s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare your best boats. Which one carried the most coins? Draw the best boat with arrows for weight and upthrust. Dry the foil and the basin, and pour the water on a plant.  
  *Parent says:* At the moment before it sinks, what do you think is true of the weight and the upthrust?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the best boat with two arrows. Dry up.
  - Note: The weight of the boat and the coins equals the upthrust, which equals the weight of the water the boat pushes aside. At the moment of sinking, the boat can push aside no more water.
  - Think: Could you make a boat from the same foil that carries more than 100 coins? What would you need?

### Finding forces on everyday things (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (forces) A cup rests on a shelf. Show the forces on the cup.  
   Options: weight / support force / friction / tension  
   **Answer:** weight down, support force up. *Why:* The Earth pulls the cup down. The shelf touches the cup and pushes up. The cup is still, so the two are equal. There is no rope, so no tension, and the cup is not sliding, so no friction is needed.
2. (mcq) A bag of sugar has a mass of 1 kilogram. About how hard does the Earth pull it?  
   Options: 10 newtons / 1 newton / 100 newtons  
   **Answer:** 10 newtons. *Why:* Each kilogram is pulled down by about 10 newtons. One newton is about the weight of a small apple, so a whole kilogram is about ten of them.
3. (fill) A child with a mass of 30 kilograms is pulled down by the Earth with roughly ____ newtons.  
   **Answer:** ['300']. *Why:* 30 × 10 = 300. Each kilogram is pulled by about 10 newtons.
4. (truefalse) The Earth pulls on a book on a table only while the book is falling.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The Earth pulls on the book all the time. On the table, the table pushes up as hard as the Earth pulls down, so the book stays still.
5. (sort) Sort each one by how the force reaches the thing.  
   **Answer:** the Earth's pull on a mango on a branch → Acts across a gap; a hook holding up a bag → Needs touching; a magnet pulling a steel pin through the air → Acts across a gap; the road gripping a chappal → Needs touching; a rubbed balloon pulling tiny paper bits → Acts across a gap; a table pushing up on a book → Needs touching. *Why:* Gravity, magnetic force and the electric force reach across a gap. Tension, friction and the support force come from things that touch.
6. (match) Match each force to a place where you meet it.  
   **Answer:** tension → the strap holding a hanging bag; upthrust → water holding up a floating katori; friction → the grip of a chappal on a road; support force → the table under a book; air resistance → the push on a hand held out of a car window. *Why:* Each force comes from a different kind of thing. A rope or strap gives tension, water gives upthrust, a rough surface gives friction, a solid surface gives support and moving air gives air resistance.
7. (order) Put these steps for drawing a force diagram in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Pick one thing and draw it as a box', 'Ask what is touching it', 'Ask what is pulling it from a distance', 'Draw one arrow for each force and label it', 'Check that arrows for balanced forces are the same length']. *Why:* You must choose the thing first, then find its forces, then draw them, then check. Skipping the question about the pull from far is how the Earth's pull gets forgotten.
8. (write) A friend says, 'A book on a table has no force on it, because it is still.' What would you say?  
   **Answer:** The book has two forces: the Earth pulls it down and the table pushes it up. They are equal, so they balance and the book does not move. Not moving does not mean no force. A good answer names both forces and says they are equal.. *Why:* A strong answer names the weight and the support force, says they are equal and gives the reason why the book stays still.
9. (draw) Draw a force diagram for a bucket hanging still from a rope in a well. Label each arrow.  
   **Answer:** A box or bucket with one arrow up labelled 'rope pulls (tension)' and one arrow down labelled 'the Earth pulls (weight)', the same length.. *Why:* The bucket is still, so the two arrows cancel. The rope is touching it and pulls up. The Earth pulls down from a distance.
10. (perspective) Is a book on a table 'doing nothing'?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says both descriptions give the same prediction. The first stresses what is going on, and the second stresses what can be calculated.. *Why:* Both views agree that the forces add to zero. They differ on whether the words 'holding' and 'keeping' describe something that is going on. No view is marked right.
11. (mcq) A coin and a flat sheet of paper are dropped. The coin lands first. Which is the best reason?  
   Options: Gravity does not pull on paper / Heavy things always fall faster, even without air / Air pushes back much harder on the sheet for its weight  
   **Answer:** Air pushes back much harder on the sheet for its weight. *Why:* The sheet has a big area for its weight, so the push of the air balances its weight at a low speed. Gravity pulls on both. Without air they would land together.
12. (count) In a tug of war, nobody moves. The red team pulls with 1,500 newtons. How hard does the blue team pull?  
   **Answer:** 1500. *Why:* The rope is not moving, so the forces on it are balanced. The blue team must pull with 1,500 newtons as well.
13. (truefalse) A magnet pulls a steel pin, a wooden pencil and a copper coin with equal force.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A magnet pulls strongly on iron and steel, and on a few other materials such as nickel and cobalt. It hardly pulls wood or copper.
14. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Gravity, magnetic force, tension, electric force.  
   **Answer:** Tension, because it needs a tight rope in contact. The other three act across a gap.. *Why:* Gravity, magnetic force and the electric force can pull on a thing that is not touching them. A rope's tension acts only through the rope itself.
15. (mcq) The biceps is a muscle in the upper arm. Which is true of every muscle?  
   Options: It can push and pull / It can only pull / It can only push  
   **Answer:** It can only pull. *Why:* A muscle shortens, and so pulls on what it is joined to. The arm needs one muscle to bend it and another to straighten it.
16. (label) Label the forces on a kite held steady in the sky.  
   **Answer:** ['pull of the string: down and towards the flier, along the string', 'weight of the kite: straight down', 'push of the wind: up and away from the flier, against the kite']. *Why:* The kite is held steady, so the three forces balance. The string and the weight both pull partly downward, and the wind pushes up and away from the flier to cancel them.
### Muscles, levers and the push of the air (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A 20 newton load hangs 30 centimetres from a pivot. A muscle joins 5 centimetres from it. How hard must it pull?  
   Options: 4 newtons / 20 newtons / 120 newtons  
   **Answer:** 120 newtons. *Why:* The muscle must make as much turning effect as the load. Load times distance is 20 × 30 = 600. The muscle's distance is 5, so its pull is 600 ÷ 5 = 120 newtons.
2. (count) A 20 kilogram child sits 2 metres from a see-saw's middle. How far out must a 10 kilogram child sit?  
   **Answer:** 4. *Why:* Weight times distance must match on both sides. 20 × 2 = 40, and 10 × 4 = 40.
3. (fill) A 2 kilogram bag of rice hangs from a string. The tension in the string is about ____ newtons.  
   **Answer:** ['20']. *Why:* 2 × 10 = 20 newtons. The string must pull up exactly as hard as the Earth pulls down.
4. (truefalse) In a tug of war where neither team moves, neither team is pulling.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Each team may be pulling with thousands of newtons. The pulls are equal and opposite, so the rope does not move.
5. (sort) Sort each force by how it behaves when the thing goes faster.  
   **Answer:** the air's push on a cyclist → Grows as the speed grows; the weight of a school bag → Stays the same at any speed; the water's push on a person wading through a pool → Grows as the speed grows; the upthrust on a floating katori → Stays the same at any speed; the Earth's pull on a falling cone → Stays the same at any speed. *Why:* Air and water push back harder the faster you push through them. Weight and the upthrust on a floating thing depend on the thing and the water, not on speed.
6. (order) Put these in order of steady falling speed, from slowest to fastest.  
   **Answer:** ['a paper cone, about 5 kilometres per hour', 'a skydiver under an open parachute, about 20 kilometres per hour', 'a large raindrop, about 32 kilometres per hour', 'a skydiver lying flat, about 200 kilometres per hour']. *Why:* The more air a falling thing has to push against for its weight, the slower its steady speed. A cone is wide and light. A skydiver lying flat is heavy for the area and falls fastest.
7. (match) Match each still or steady thing to its pair of balanced forces.  
   **Answer:** a fish hanging in water → weight down, upthrust up; a bucket rising steadily from a well → weight down, rope tension up; a cyclist at a steady speed → forward friction, backward air resistance; a book on a table → weight down, support force up. *Why:* A thing that is still or at a steady speed has its forces in balanced pairs. Each pair comes from what is touching the thing or pulling it from far.
8. (write) Four cones fell far less than four times as fast as one. A friend expected four times as fast. Explain the mistake.  
   **Answer:** The friend forgets the push of the air. A cone stops speeding up when the push of the air equals its weight. The push grows faster than the speed: twice the speed gives about four times the push. So four times the weight is matched at about twice the speed.. *Why:* A strong answer says the push of the air grows faster than the speed, and uses the idea of a steady speed.
9. (draw) Draw a force diagram for a cone just let go. Draw another at its steady speed. How do the arrows differ?  
   **Answer:** Just after letting go: a long arrow down for weight and a very short arrow up for air resistance. At the steady speed: two arrows of equal length, one down and one up.. *Why:* At first the air pushes very little, so weight wins and the cone speeds up. As it goes faster, the air's arrow grows until it matches the weight.
10. (perspective) Gravity: a pull, or a bend in space and time?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says both predict an everyday fall equally well, and that Einstein's picture is needed for the very exact timing in satellite navigation. No view is marked right, because physicists use both and have no theory that joins gravity with tiny particles.. *Why:* Newton's rule is a very good description for everyday life. Einstein's gives better answers in extreme cases. Neither says what gravity finally is.
11. (mcq) A magnet stays on a fridge door and does not slide down. Which force holds it up?  
   Options: Friction between the magnet and the door / The magnetic pull, which points up / Gravity, which points up near a magnet  
   **Answer:** Friction between the magnet and the door. *Why:* The magnet's pull points into the door, not up. That pull presses the magnet to the door, and the grip of friction along the door holds the magnet up against its weight.
12. (mcq) Which force is behind the push of a table on a book?  
   Options: The electromagnetic force between atoms / Gravity between the table and the book / A force inside the centres of atoms  
   **Answer:** The electromagnetic force between atoms. *Why:* The atoms of the table and the book push on each other electrically. Gravity between a table and a book is far too weak to matter.
13. (count) A grown-up with a mass of 45 kilograms stands still. How many newtons does the floor push up with?  
   **Answer:** 450. *Why:* The grown-up is still, so the floor pushes up exactly as hard as the Earth pulls down: 45 × 10 = 450 newtons.
14. (odd) Which is not in balance? A resting book, a ball at the top of a throw, a hanging lamp, a steady cyclist.  
   **Answer:** The ball at the top of its throw, because only its weight is pulling it and nothing cancels that.. *Why:* The other three have forces that cancel. The ball is not moving for an instant but is about to fall, so its forces are not in balance.
15. (truefalse) A strong muscle can push a bone.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A muscle can only pull. A bone is pushed when a muscle on the other side of the joint pulls.
16. (label) Label this drawing of an arm holding a bottle: the pivot, where the biceps joins, and the bottle.  
   **Answer:** ['pivot: the elbow', 'where the biceps joins: about 4 centimetres from the elbow, on the forearm', 'bottle: about 30 centimetres from the elbow, in the hand']. *Why:* The bottle is far from the pivot and the muscle joins near it. That is why the muscle has to pull many times harder than the bottle weighs.
### Faulty reasoning and open questions about forces (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) An astronaut says, 'There is no gravity in my station, because I float.' What is wrong?  
   Options: Nothing. Floating shows gravity is zero / Gravity is strong there. She floats because she falls with the station / The station is too heavy to be pulled  
   **Answer:** Gravity is strong there. She floats because she falls with the station. *Why:* At 400 kilometres, gravity is still about nine-tenths of the ground value. Floating means no floor is pushing on her, not that nothing is pulling her.
2. (truefalse) A scale under your feet in a lift that is falling freely would read zero.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The lift and you fall together, so the floor does not push on you. The scale measures that push, not the Earth's pull.
3. (fill) A 10 newton bottle is 32 centimetres from the elbow. The biceps joins 4 centimetres away. It pulls about ____ newtons.  
   **Answer:** ['80']. *Why:* 10 × 32 = 320, and 320 ÷ 4 = 80. The muscle pulls about 8 times the load.
4. (count) A 15 newton load is 36 centimetres from the elbow. The muscle joins 4 centimetres away. How hard does it pull?  
   **Answer:** 135. *Why:* 15 × 36 = 540, and 540 ÷ 4 = 135 newtons.
5. (mcq) Two people pull opposite ends of a rope with 100 newtons each. The rope stays still. What is the tension?  
   Options: 200 newtons / 0 newtons / 100 newtons  
   **Answer:** 100 newtons. *Why:* The rope is pulled with 100 newtons at each end, and carries 100 newtons along its length. The two pulls do not add up to the tension. If you chose 200, imagine tying one end to a wall: the pull is still 100 newtons.
6. (write) Nobody has ever seen a force. What do we see, and what do we say a force did?  
   **Answer:** We see a spring stretch, a ball speed up, a book stay still or a rope tighten. We say that a force did it. A force is a name for what changes motion or shape, and we measure it from what happens.. *Why:* A strong answer separates what is seen from the word 'force'. It says that force is a working idea, and that scientists do not all agree it is basic.
7. (write) A heavier skydiver falls faster than a lighter one under the same kind of parachute. Use forces to explain why.  
   **Answer:** The heavier skydiver has a greater weight, so the push of the air must grow bigger to match it. The push grows with speed, so the steady speed is higher.. *Why:* A strong answer says the steady speed is the one at which the air's push equals the weight. A bigger weight needs a bigger push, and so a faster fall.
8. (mcq) A skydiver spreads her arms and legs wide. What happens to the steady falling speed?  
   Options: It gets lower, because the air has more to push on / It gets higher, because she is bigger / It stays the same, because her weight is the same  
   **Answer:** It gets lower, because the air has more to push on. *Why:* The same weight is now met by the air's push at a lower speed, because there is more area to push on. Many skydivers use this to slow down.
9. (perspective) When a root turns downward in the cup, is the root deciding or being moved?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says the facts seen in the cup are the same under both descriptions. It also suggests a test, such as putting water on one side while gravity points the other way. No view is marked right.. *Why:* Both agree that the root senses gravity and that its turn is not random. They differ on whether the word 'deciding' fits, which is a question about thinking and not about the measurements.
10. (draw) Draw the forces on a person leaning on a wall with straight arms, not slipping. Use four arrows.  
   **Answer:** A person leaning at a slant, with weight down and a floor push up. Friction from the floor points towards the wall, and the wall pushes back away from the wall. Up equals down, and the two sideways forces are equal.. *Why:* The person is still, so each pair of forces cancels. Friction from the floor must hold the feet from sliding away from the wall.
11. (sort) Sort each force by what lies underneath it.  
   **Answer:** the grip of a chappal on a road → The electromagnetic force between atoms; the pull of the Earth on a mango → Gravity; a magnet pulling a pin → The electromagnetic force between atoms; a muscle pulling on a bone → The electromagnetic force between atoms; a satellite held in orbit → Gravity. *Why:* Pushes by surfaces and ropes, the magnet's pull and the muscle's pull all come from electric and magnetic forces between atoms. The pull of the Earth on a mango and on a satellite is gravity.
12. (match) Match each odd observation to the force that explains it.  
   **Answer:** a steel katori floats when set down gently → upthrust equals its weight; a root turns down in a cup on its side → the pull of the Earth, sensed by the root; a rubbed balloon sticks to a wall → the static electric force; a fridge magnet holds a paper in place → magnetic force and friction. *Why:* Each observation points to a force that a diagram would show. A force explains a change or a balance.
13. (mcq) Newton's rule and Einstein's picture of gravity predict the same fall of a ball. What does this tell us?  
   Options: Newton was wrong, so his rule is useless / A description can work very well without being the last word / Einstein must be wrong, because Newton's rule is simpler  
   **Answer:** A description can work very well without being the last word. *Why:* Two different pictures can give the same prediction. Scientists keep both, and keep asking what gravity finally is.
14. (count) A 50 newton box slides at a steady speed under a 15 newton push. How many newtons of friction act on it?  
   **Answer:** 15. *Why:* At a steady speed the forces along the floor are balanced, so friction equals the push, which is 15 newtons.
15. (write) How could you show that water pushes up on a stone, when the stone sinks?  
   **Answer:** Lift the stone on a string with a spring or a rubber band. Note how far the band stretches in air. Lower the stone into water and see the band shorten. The stone seems lighter because the water pushes up on it.. *Why:* A strong answer lowers the stone on something that measures weight, such as a rubber band, and compares the reading in air and in water.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A lamp hangs still from a chain. Which forces act on the lamp?  
   Options: Only the Earth pulls, because the lamp is not moving / The chain pulls up and the Earth pulls down / The chain pulls up and nothing pulls down / A force pushes it down from the ceiling  
   **Answer:** The chain pulls up and the Earth pulls down. *Why:* Pick one thing and ask what touches it and what pulls it from far. The chain touches it and pulls up. The Earth pulls down from a distance. If you chose only gravity, you forgot that a still thing needs an upward force from something touching it.
2. (mcq) Which is true of a rope or a string?  
   Options: It can push and pull equally well / It pulls only when it hangs loose / It can pull, but it cannot push / It pushes when it is wet  
   **Answer:** It can pull, but it cannot push. *Why:* A rope pulls along its length when it is tight, and a slack rope does nothing. If you chose pushing, try pushing a thread across a table: it just folds.
3. (mcq) Astronauts in a station 400 kilometres up float. What is the reason?  
   Options: They fall with the station, so no floor pushes on them / There is no gravity so high up / The station's walls block the Earth's pull / They are too far from the Earth to be pulled at all  
   **Answer:** They fall with the station, so no floor pushes on them. *Why:* At that height gravity is still about nine-tenths as strong as in your room. They float because they and the station fall together and the floor does not push on their feet. Choosing 'no gravity' mixes up a missing push with a missing pull.
4. (mcq) A steel katori floats, but a steel spoon sinks. Which is the best reason?  
   Options: The steel in a katori weighs less than steel in a spoon / Water pushes up only on bowls / A spoon is too small for water to push on / Its hollow shape pushes aside enough water to hold it up  
   **Answer:** Its hollow shape pushes aside enough water to hold it up. *Why:* Upthrust equals the weight of the water pushed aside. A hollow shape pushes aside a lot of water and a lump of steel does not. If you chose 'weighs less', try weighing both: the steel itself is the same.
5. (mcq) A bag of rice hangs still. The string pulls up with 20 newtons. How hard does the Earth pull down?  
   Options: 20 newtons / 10 newtons / 0 newtons, because the bag is not moving  
   **Answer:** 20 newtons. *Why:* The bag is still, so the forces are balanced and the down pull must equal the up pull, which is 20 newtons. Choosing 0 treats 'not moving' as 'no force'. Choosing 10 has no reason behind it.
6. (mcq) A cyclist keeps a steady speed on a flat road. What is true of the forces along the road?  
   Options: The forward force must be bigger, or she would stop / No force acts on her, because the speed is steady / Forward force equals backward force, so her speed stays the same  
   **Answer:** Forward force equals backward force, so her speed stays the same. *Why:* Balanced forces mean no change in speed, and a steady speed is a kind of 'no change'. A bigger forward force would make her speed up. A cycle slows after you stop pedalling because friction and air still push backward.
7. (mcq) A ball is thrown straight up. At the very top, it is not moving for an instant. Is a force on it?  
   Options: Yes. Gravity still pulls it down, and it starts to fall / No, a still thing has no force on it / Yes, the push of the throw is still on it  
   **Answer:** Yes. Gravity still pulls it down, and it starts to fall. *Why:* Gravity never switches off. At the top, the ball's speed is zero for an instant, and the pull down is not balanced, so it speeds up downward. If you chose the push of the throw, that push ended when the ball left the hand.
8. (mcq) Which force diagram shows a book resting on a table?  
   Options: One long arrow down only / Two arrows of the same length, one down and one up / One arrow up only / A long arrow down and a short arrow up  
   **Answer:** Two arrows of the same length, one down and one up. *Why:* The book is still, so the arrows must cancel. The weight down and the support force up are equal. A long arrow down with a short one up would show a book that is sinking through the table.
9. (mcq) What does a force diagram of a kite show?  
   Options: The kite and the person, with arrows for both / One arrow for the biggest force only / The kite alone, with one arrow for each force on it  
   **Answer:** The kite alone, with one arrow for each force on it. *Why:* A force diagram draws one thing alone, so you can see everything that pushes or pulls it. Drawing the person too mixes up forces on different things. Drawing only the biggest force hides the ones that balance it.
10. (mcq) Four paper cones pressed together fall far less than four times as fast as one cone. Why?  
   Options: Cones cannot fall faster than one cone / The air pushes four times harder on the stack at any speed / Gravity pulls the stack only twice as hard / The air's push grows faster than the speed, so twice the speed is enough  
   **Answer:** The air's push grows faster than the speed, so twice the speed is enough. *Why:* A thing stops speeding up when the push of the air equals its weight. Double the speed gives about four times the push, so four times the weight is matched at about twice the speed. If you chose gravity, remember that four cones are pulled four times as hard.
11. (mcq) In a room with air, a hammer beats a feather to the floor. What happens with no air?  
   Options: They would land together / The hammer would still land first because it is heavier / The feather would land first because it is lighter  
   **Answer:** They would land together. *Why:* The feather is held back by the push of the air, which is big for its weight. With no air there is only gravity, and everything falls at the same rate. On the Moon in 1971 the astronaut David Scott showed this.
12. (truefalse) A large raindrop keeps speeding up all the way down from the cloud.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The push of the air grows as the drop speeds up, until it equals the drop's weight. Then the drop falls at a steady speed of about 9 metres each second.
13. (mcq) Why does your biceps pull about 8 times as hard as the bottle weighs?  
   Options: It is about 8 times stronger than other muscles / A muscle pushes as well as pulls, so it does double work / It joins close to the elbow, but the bottle is far away / Pulling up always takes eight times the force  
   **Answer:** It joins close to the elbow, but the bottle is far away. *Why:* The forearm is a lever with the elbow as its pivot. A pull close to the pivot must be much bigger to balance a load far from it. The muscle is not special, but its position gives it a big job.
14. (mcq) Which of these is, underneath, the same force as a magnet's pull?  
   Options: The pull of the Earth on a mango / The grip of a chappal on the road / The pull of the Sun on the Earth / Nothing is the same as a magnet's pull  
   **Answer:** The grip of a chappal on the road. *Why:* Friction, tension, the push of a table and a muscle's pull all come from the electromagnetic force between atoms. That force also gives a magnet's pull. Gravity is a separate force.
15. (mcq) Which of these does nobody yet know?  
   Options: Whether gravity can be joined with the physics of tiny particles / How hard the Earth pulls a 1 kilogram bag / Whether a magnet pulls steel / Whether a table pushes up on a book  
   **Answer:** Whether gravity can be joined with the physics of tiny particles. *Why:* Scientists can predict gravity's effects very exactly but have no agreed account that joins gravity with the physics of tiny particles. The other three are measured and agreed.

## Applications
- The large shore nets at Fort Kochi in Kerala are tipped like a see-saw. Stones hung on ropes at the back balance the weight of the wet net at the front. A small crew can lift a net wider than a room, because the lever works for them. Look at the picture in the lesson, then look at a see-saw in a park.
- A lift cabin hangs from steel cables. Each cable carries tension equal to part of the weight of the cabin and the people inside. Lifts have many cables, so that if one failed the others would still hold. If you travel in a lift, look at the sign that gives the greatest load in kilograms.
- A shop's old balance scale, the tarazu, is a lever with equal arms. A kilogram weight on one pan is balanced by 1 kilogram of rice on the other, because they are at equal distances from the middle. A parent can look at one in a local shop and ask how it would change if the arms were unequal.
- Shoes, tyres and cricket bats are made with grooves, patterns or rough rubber. These raise friction so that the shoe grips the ground and the tyre grips the road. The tyres of a bus have deep grooves that push water away so that the rubber, not a film of water, meets the road.
- A life jacket and a ship's hull work through upthrust. A hollow hull pushes aside a huge weight of water. Ships have a painted line on the side, the load line, which shows how low they may sit in the water. Look for it on a picture of a ship.
- A compass needle turns because the Earth is a huge, weak magnet. Sailors used it for navigation for many centuries. About 1088 CE the Chinese scholar Shen Kuo described a magnetised needle that points a little off true north, which is why compasses need correcting.
- A tower crane at a building site has a long arm in front and heavy concrete blocks at the back. The blocks balance the load, as the stones balance the net at Fort Kochi. Look for the blocks when you pass a building site.

## Spotted around us
- Stand in a bus holding the handrail, and look at a hanging strap. When the bus pulls away, the strap swings back. When the bus brakes, it swings forward. Notice that when the bus moves steadily, the strap hangs straight down, which shows that its forces are balanced.
- Next time you stop pedalling a cycle on a flat lane, count how many seconds it takes to slow right down. Notice that you slow down even though nobody is pushing against you. Friction and the air are pushing backward all the time.
- Pull a fridge door slowly open. At the edge, feel a small tug. A magnetic strip in the rubber seal is pulling the door to the fridge across the last bit of gap. Notice that it pulls harder the closer the door comes.
- At a bathroom door, watch how your feet grip when the floor is dry and when it is wet. On the wet floor, your foot slides back. Notice that friction is what pushes you forward when you walk.
- When it rains, drop a leaf, a coin and a small steel spoon into a puddle or a bucket. Notice which float and which sink. Then push the floating leaf under the water with a finger and feel it push back.
- Look at an electricity wire strung between two poles on your street. Notice that it sags in a smooth curve, never in a straight line. The wire pulls on both poles along its length, and its weight pulls it down. That pull is tension.
- At a playground, watch a swing at its lowest point. Notice that the chains are pulling hardest there, and that the swing is moving fastest. Then look at the highest point where it stops for an instant.
- If a potted plant has been knocked over, check the plant after two or three days. Notice that the new shoot at the top has turned upward, away from the pull of the Earth. The plant is sensing which way is down.

## Open questions
- **What is gravity, and can it be joined with the physics of tiny particles?** Einstein's picture of bent space and time passes every test at large sizes. The physics of particles passes every test at tiny sizes. The two cannot both be complete, and no experiment has yet reached the place where they meet.
- **Why is gravity so much weaker than the other forces?** A small magnet beats the whole Earth's pull on a pin. Between two of the particles inside an atom, the electric force is stronger than gravity by a number with 36 zeros. No accepted theory explains so great a difference.
- **Are the four basic forces really one?** Two of the four were shown to be one in the 1970s. Theories that join a third have predicted effects that experiments have not found. Gravity has not been joined at all.
- **How exactly do birds sense the Earth's magnetic field?** One idea is tiny magnetic grains in the beak. Another is a light-sensitive molecule in the eye that responds to magnetism. Experiments support each in part, and the birds may use both.
- **Is force a basic part of nature?** The deepest theories of physics are written in terms of energy and fields and do not need force. Yet force describes everyday life very well. Physicists and philosophers still discuss which description is closer to what is there.

## Questions to ask kids
- You hold a school bag out in front of you and your arm gets tired. The bag is not moving. What is your arm doing?
- If the Earth pulls you down, and you pull the Earth up just as hard, why does only one of you move?
- A floating leaf, a floating katori and a big ship are all held up by water. What do they have in common?
- Why does a heavy raindrop not hurt when it falls from a high cloud?
- A fridge magnet does not touch the paper clip, and it still moves it. What is reaching across the gap?
- Which is harder: pushing a door near the hinge or near the handle? Why do you think so?
- Think of a bridge you know. Where do the pushes and pulls of a bus on it finally go?
- Nobody has ever seen a force. Do you think a force is real, or a way of talking? What would you test?

## Connected maths
- A bag of 3 kilograms of rice is weighed on the Earth. On the Moon, the pull is about one sixth as strong. Find the weight of the bag in newtons on the Earth and on the Moon, and the mass of the bag on each. **30 newtons on the Earth, 5 newtons on the Moon. The mass is 3 kilograms on both.**. On the Earth: 3 × 10 = 30 newtons. On the Moon: 30 ÷ 6 = 5 newtons. The rice is the same stuff, so its mass stays 3 kilograms. (queues `math.multiplication.scaling.L4`)
- At the cone's steady speed of 1.4 metres per second, the air's push equals the cone's weight, 0.016 newtons. The push grows as the square of the speed. How big would the push be at 2.8 metres per second, which is twice the speed? How big at 4.2 metres per second? **0.064 newtons at 2.8 metres per second, and 0.144 newtons at 4.2 metres per second.**. Twice the speed: 2 × 2 = 4 times the push, 0.016 × 4 = 0.064. Three times the speed: 3 × 3 = 9 times the push, 0.016 × 9 = 0.144. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- A ruler rests on a pencil at its 15 centimetre mark. Three coins sit 5 centimetres from the pivot on the left. One coin sits on the right. Where must it sit to balance? Use the rule: number of coins times distance is equal on both sides. **15 centimetres from the pivot, at the very end of the ruler.**. Left side: 3 × 5 = 15. The right side must also be 15, so 1 coin × distance = 15, and the distance is 15 centimetres. (queues `math.algebra.balance-and-missing-numbers.L4`)
- A steel katori pushes aside 150 millilitres of water when it is full to the brim without sinking. One millilitre of water weighs about 0.01 newtons, and the katori weighs 0.7 newtons. How many 1 rupee coins, each weighing about 0.04 newtons (about 4 grams, which differs a little between coin series), can it carry before it sinks? **About 20 coins.**. The most upthrust is 150 × 0.01 = 1.5 newtons. The katori itself uses 0.7, so 1.5 − 0.7 = 0.8 newtons is left for coins. 0.8 ÷ 0.04 = 20 coins. (queues `math.weight-capacity.weight.L4`)
- A bottle of 10 newtons is 32 centimetres from the elbow. The biceps joins the forearm 4 centimetres from the elbow. How many times bigger is the biceps pull than the load, and how big is it? Then say what happens to the pull if the muscle joined 8 centimetres from the elbow. **8 times, so 80 newtons. At 8 centimetres it would be 4 times, so 40 newtons.**. The load's distance is 32 and the muscle's is 4. 32 ÷ 4 = 8, so the muscle pulls 8 × 10 = 80 newtons. At 8 centimetres: 32 ÷ 8 = 4, and 4 × 10 = 40. Halving the distance doubles the pull. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- On the International Space Station, the pull of the Earth is about 9 tenths of its value on the ground. A child of 30 kilograms is pulled down with 300 newtons on the ground. How big is the pull on the child on the station? Why does the child float anyway? **270 newtons, near enough. The child floats because the station and the child fall together, so no floor pushes up on them.**. 9 tenths of 300 is 300 ÷ 10 × 9 = 270 newtons. The pull is almost as strong as on the ground. What is missing is the push of a floor. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In three drops of one paper cone from 2 metres, the times are 1.5, 1.3 and 1.6 seconds. Find the middle time and the average, and the speed over 2 metres using the middle time. Then say why we use the middle and not the biggest. **The middle time is 1.5 seconds and the average is 1.47 seconds. The speed is about 1.3 metres per second. A drop that goes wrong is often the biggest or smallest, and the middle is not pulled by it.**. The middle of 1.3, 1.5 and 1.6 is 1.5. The average is (1.5 + 1.3 + 1.6) ÷ 3 = 4.4 ÷ 3 = 1.47. The speed is 2 ÷ 1.5 = 1.33 metres per second. (queues `math.data.averages.L4`)
