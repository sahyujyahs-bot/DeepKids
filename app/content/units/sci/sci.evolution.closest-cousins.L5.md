# Our closest cousins: what we share with the other apes, and whether humans are special
`sci.evolution.closest-cousins.L5`

**Summary.** Until 1960, books said that making tools was the thing that made us human. Then a chimpanzee in Tanzania pulled the leaves off a stem and used the stem to fish for termites. So what is left that only humans do? In this lesson you make three termite fishing tools from curry-leaf stalks and test which one catches the most. You also write your own list of things only humans can do, and check each one against what scientists have seen other apes do.

**Intro.** *A chimpanzee picks a stem, pulls off the leaves and pushes the stem into a termite mound. Is that a tool? Did the chimpanzee make it? And if a chimpanzee can make a tool, what can only a human do?* Chimpanzees and bonobos are our closest living relatives. For a long time, people kept a list of things that only humans do: make tools, have culture, know themselves, care about others. In this lesson you write your own list first. Then you test a tool the way a chimpanzee makes one, and you look at the evidence for each thing on the list. Some things come off the list. Some are still argued about. You decide what stays.

## Terms
- **Primate**: A primate is a member of the group of mammals that includes lorises, lemurs, monkeys and apes. Most primates have hands that grip, nails in place of claws, and eyes that face forward. *e.g.* A bonnet macaque on a temple wall in Madurai is a primate. So is the person feeding the macaque.
- **Ape**: An ape is a primate with no tail and with shoulders that let the arms swing in a full circle. Gibbons, orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos and humans are apes. *e.g.* Swing your arm round like a bowler in cricket. A monkey's shoulder does not turn that far, but an ape's shoulder does.
- **Great ape**: The great apes are the large apes: orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos and humans. Biologists put all of them in one family. *e.g.* A gibbon is an ape but not a great ape. A gibbon is much smaller, so gibbons are called small apes.
- **Common ancestor**: A common ancestor is a living being from long ago from whom two kinds of living beings today both descend. *e.g.* You and your cousin share a grandmother. Humans and chimpanzees share ancestors who lived about 6 to 8 million years ago. Those ancestors were neither humans nor chimpanzees.
- **DNA**: DNA is the long molecule inside almost every cell that stores the instructions for building and running a living being. The instructions are written in four chemical letters: A, T, C and G. *e.g.* One set of human DNA has about 3,000 million letters. A chimpanzee's DNA is about the same length.
- **Chromosome**: A chromosome is one long piece of DNA, wound up tightly. The DNA in a cell is divided into many chromosomes. *e.g.* A human cell has 46 chromosomes. A chimpanzee, gorilla or orangutan cell has 48.
- **Tool**: A tool is an object that a living being picks up and uses to get a job done. Making a tool means changing the object so that it does the job better. *e.g.* A stem lying on the ground is not a tool. A stem with the leaves pulled off, pushed into a termite mound to catch termites, is a tool that someone made.
- **Tool set**: A tool set is two or more different tools used one after the other for one job. *e.g.* Chimpanzees in the Goualougo forest in the Republic of Congo carry a thick stick to make a hole in a termite nest. Then they use a thin stem with a brush tip to fish.
- **Brush tip**: A brush tip is the end of a stem that has been split into many thin fibres, like a paintbrush. *e.g.* Goualougo chimpanzees pull the end of a stem through their teeth to fray it. More termites hold on to the frayed end.
- **Social learning**: Social learning is learning something from others, by watching them or being with them, and not only by working it out alone. *e.g.* You learned to eat with your hand, or with a spoon, by watching your family. A young chimpanzee learns to crack nuts by watching the mother for years.
- **Tradition**: A tradition is a way of doing something that members of a group learn from each other and keep doing over time. *e.g.* Drawing a kolam at the door each morning is a tradition. Cracking nuts with a stone is a tradition of the chimpanzees of the Taï forest.
- **Culture**: Culture is the whole set of traditions that a group has. Scientists say a group of animals has culture when the group's learned traditions differ from those of other groups of the same species. *e.g.* Chimpanzees at Taï crack nuts and chimpanzees at Gombe do not, though Gombe has nuts and stones. The difference is learned, so it is part of each group's culture.
- **Cumulative culture**: Cumulative culture is culture that builds up. Each generation learns what the last one knew and then adds to it, so skills become too complicated for one person to invent alone. *e.g.* No one person invented the bicycle from nothing. Wheels, metal working, chains and rubber tyres were each worked out by different people over thousands of years.
- **Imitate**: To imitate is to copy the actions that someone else does. *e.g.* A child who watches an aunt tie a sari and then makes the same folds in the same order is imitating her.
- **Gesture**: A gesture is a movement of the hand, arm or body that is made to tell another being something. *e.g.* You hold out your hand, palm up, to ask for a share of a mango. A chimpanzee makes the same gesture to ask for food.
- **Mirror test**: The mirror test checks whether an animal knows that the reflection in a mirror is their own body. A mark is put where the animal can see it only in the mirror. *e.g.* A chimpanzee with a spot of dye on the eyebrow looks in the mirror and then touches their own eyebrow. That shows the chimpanzee knows whose face it is.
- **Field study**: A field study is research done where the animals live, by watching them for a long time without controlling what they do. *e.g.* The study of the chimpanzees at Gombe in Tanzania began in 1960 and is still going on.
- **Endangered**: A species is endangered when so few are left that the species could die out. *e.g.* Every kind of great ape except humans is endangered. India's only ape, the hoolock gibbon, is endangered too.

## Explanations
### Humans are apes: where we sit in the family · *defined*
**Definition:** Apes are primates without tails. The great apes are orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos and humans. Chimpanzees and bonobos are our closest living relatives, and we are theirs.

People often say 'apes and humans', as if these were two groups. Biologists do not sort them that way. They sort living beings by who shares the most recent common ancestor, in the way you are closer to your sister than to your cousin. By that rule, humans are inside the great ape family. Here is the surprising part. A chimpanzee is more closely related to you than to a gorilla. The line that led to gorillas split off first. The line that led to humans and the line that led to chimpanzees and bonobos stayed together for longer. They split about 6 to 8 million years ago. The dates come from counting DNA differences and from fossils, and scientists still adjust them. Humans did not come from chimpanzees. Both came from ancestors who were neither. Since the split, both lines have gone on changing for the same length of time. So a chimpanzee is not an 'earlier' or 'unfinished' human. A chimpanzee is as up to date as you are.
- e.g. Orangutans split off first among the great apes, then gorillas. The last split was between the human line and the line of chimpanzees and bonobos.
- e.g. Chimpanzees and bonobos split from each other much later, perhaps 1 to 2 million years ago. So we are exactly as closely related to bonobos as to chimpanzees.
- e.g. Gibbons, the small apes, split off before all the great apes. The hoolock gibbon of Assam and other north-eastern states is India's only ape.
- e.g. In 2015, a 13-million-year-old tooth of a small ape was found at Ramnagar in Jammu and Kashmir. Scientists named the ape Kapi ramnagarensis in 2020. They think this ape was an early relative of gibbons. It is only one tooth, so they are careful about it.
### What 'almost 99 per cent the same DNA' really means · *defined*
**Definition:** When scientists line up human DNA and chimpanzee DNA letter by letter, about 99 letters in every 100 are the same. The exact number depends on what is counted.

In 2005 scientists published the first reading of nearly all of a chimpanzee's DNA, and compared it with human DNA. Where the two could be lined up letter by letter, they differed at about 1.2 letters in every 100. That is where 'almost 99 per cent the same' comes from. But there is a second kind of difference. In some places, a stretch of letters is present in one species and missing in the other. If you count those stretches too, the two are about 96 per cent the same. In 2025 scientists finished reading ape DNA from end to end, including the hardest parts. They found some regions so rearranged that they cannot be lined up letter by letter at all. So there is no single true number. The honest answer is 'very alike, and how alike depends on how you count'. Why does a small difference in letters give such different bodies? One reason is that 1.2 in 100 is still about 35 million letters. Another is that some DNA works like a switch. A switch decides when and where a gene is used, and for how long. Many scientists think that changes in switches, more than changes in the genes themselves, explain why our bodies and brains grow differently.
- e.g. Two humans picked from anywhere on Earth differ at about 1 letter in every 1,000. A human and a chimpanzee differ about ten times more than that.
- e.g. About 35 million single letters differ between a human and a chimpanzee. About 5 million more places have a stretch that is added or missing.
- e.g. In the Hundred Square activity, you colour 1 square in 100 for letters that differ, and 3 more for stretches that are added or missing.
- e.g. The same DNA comparison works for any two living beings. The more recent their common ancestor, the more alike their DNA.
### The joined chromosome: 48 and 46 · *defined*
**Definition:** Chimpanzees, gorillas and orangutans have 48 chromosomes in each cell. Humans have 46. Human chromosome 2 is two ancestral chromosomes joined end to end.

If humans and other great apes share ancestors, the different chromosome numbers need explaining. Scientists made a guess that could be tested. Perhaps two chromosomes joined into one on the human line, some time after the split. If that is true, the join should have left marks. Every chromosome has special repeated letters at each end, like the plastic tips on a shoelace. Each also has one pinched-in place, used when a cell divides. A joined chromosome should have end letters in its middle, where no ends belong. It should also have the remains of a second pinched-in place. In 1991, Jacob Ijdo and his team found end letters in the middle of human chromosome 2. In 1992, another team found the faded remains of a second pinched-in place. The DNA on either side matches two separate chimpanzee chromosomes. A guess that could have failed did not fail. That is strong evidence that we share ancestors with the other great apes.
- e.g. In the Joined Chromosome activity you tape two paper strips end to end, and predict what marks the join should leave.
- e.g. The two chimpanzee chromosomes that match our chromosome 2 are now called 2A and 2B.
- e.g. The join did not remove any genes. It only changed how the same DNA is packed.
### Same plan, different measurements: ape bodies · *defined*
**Definition:** All apes are built from the same parts in the same order. Humans differ from other apes mainly in the sizes and shapes of those parts.

A chimpanzee's hand has the same bones as yours, with nails and fingerprints. A chimpanzee has the same teeth as you, in the same number. The differences are measurements. Human legs are long and human arms are short, because we walk upright on two legs. A chimpanzee's arms are longer than their legs, which suits climbing and walking on the knuckles. Human thumbs are longer, so a human thumb tip can press firmly against each fingertip. Human bodies have thin, short hair and many sweat glands, so we cool down well on a long walk in the sun. The human brain is about three times as big as a chimpanzee's. Each body suits a way of living. Many people think a chimpanzee is five or more times as strong as a person. Careful tests give a smaller number. In 2017 a team led by Matthew O'Neill measured chimpanzee muscle. They found it gives about 1.35 times the force and power of human muscle of the same size. Chimpanzee muscle has more fast fibres, which are strong but tire quickly. Human muscle has more slow fibres, which keep going. Our bodies gave up some power for staying power.
- e.g. Press your thumb tip against the tip of your little finger. A chimpanzee's shorter thumb and longer fingers make this grip harder.
- e.g. A chimpanzee's big toe sticks out sideways and grips like a thumb. Your big toe lines up with the others, which helps you push off when you walk.
- e.g. A gorilla's large belly holds a long gut for digesting leaves and stems all day.
- e.g. An orangutan's arms can span more than 2 metres from fingertip to fingertip, for reaching between branches.
### Tools: what Jane Goodall saw, and what came after · *defined*
**Definition:** Chimpanzees, orangutans and some other animals use tools. Chimpanzees also make tools, by changing an object so that it does a job better.

In the autumn of 1960, at Gombe in Tanzania, Jane Goodall watched a chimpanzee she called David Greybeard. He pushed a grass stem into a termite mound, pulled it out and ate the termites holding on to it. Later she saw chimpanzees pick leafy twigs and strip the leaves off first. Stripping the leaves is the important part. It means the chimpanzee changed an object to fit a job. That is making a tool, and scientists at that time said only humans did it. Since then, watchers at other sites have found much more. At Goualougo in the Republic of Congo, chimpanzees fray the end of a stem into a brush. In 2009 Crickette Sanz and her team asked five local guides to fish at real termite nests with both kinds of stem. The brush tips brought out about 5 termites each try. The plain stems brought out about one termite in every four tries. In the Termite Fishing Test, you check whether a frayed tip also works better for you. At Fongoli in Senegal, chimpanzees sharpen sticks with their teeth and jab them into tree holes to hunt small animals called bushbabies. Jill Pruetz, who studies them, found that females hunted with these tools more than males did.
- e.g. Termite fishing with a stripped stem, first seen at Gombe in Tanzania in 1960.
- e.g. Brush-tipped fishing stems and tool sets at Goualougo in the Republic of Congo.
- e.g. Stone and wooden hammers for cracking nuts at Taï in Ivory Coast. Stones found there show that chimpanzees were cracking nuts 4,300 years ago.
- e.g. Sponges of chewed leaves for soaking up water from tree holes, at Budongo in Uganda and elsewhere.
- e.g. Tools are not only for apes. On Great Nicobar Island, Indian scientists led by Arijit Pal saw long-tailed macaques wrap and wipe food, and clean between their teeth with threads and fibres.
### Culture: learned traditions that differ from group to group · *defined*
**Definition:** Culture is a group's set of traditions, learned from each other. Chimpanzee and orangutan groups have cultures.

How can anyone tell that a habit is learned from others? In 1999 Andrew Whiten and scientists from seven long-running chimpanzee studies put their records together. They listed every behaviour that was common in at least one group and missing in another. Then they removed two kinds of difference. If a group had no termite mounds, then 'no termite fishing' was caused by the place, so they removed it. All the groups were chimpanzees, so differences in genes could not easily explain a habit found in one group and missing next door. After that, 39 behaviours were left. These included ways of using tools, of grooming and of courting. Each group had their own mix, as each human community does. In 2003 Carel van Schaik's team did the same for orangutans and found 24. Culture can also be watched as it spreads. In 2011 at Budongo in Uganda, Catherine Hobaiter filmed a new habit, a sponge made of moss, as it passed from one chimpanzee to another. Almost every chimpanzee who made a moss sponge had first watched another make one. The story in this lesson tells what she saw.
- e.g. Taï chimpanzees crack nuts with hammers. Gombe chimpanzees have nuts and stones but do not crack nuts.
- e.g. At Mahale in Tanzania, two chimpanzees who groom each other often clasp hands above their heads. Most other groups do not.
- e.g. In 1953 on Koshima island in Japan, a young macaque named Imo began washing sweet potatoes in water. Others learned it from her. Japanese scientists led by Kinji Imanishi had already suggested in 1952 that monkeys could have culture.
- e.g. In the game Learned, Place or Genes? you sort differences between groups in the way Whiten's team did.
### How young apes learn: years of watching · *defined*
**Definition:** Young apes learn their group's skills over many years, mostly by watching their mothers closely and practising.

A young chimpanzee stays close to the mother for years. The young one watches her fish, crack nuts and choose food, from a hand's width away. Cracking a nut looks easy. In fact it needs three things placed just right: the nut, a flat stone or root under it, and a hammer of the right weight. Hit too softly and nothing happens. Hit too hard and the nut is crushed. Young chimpanzees at Taï take years to get good at it, and do not reach an adult's skill until they are grown. You can feel why in the Hammer and Anvil activity. Do chimpanzee mothers teach? Mostly they let the young watch, and they put up with the young taking their tools and their nuts. Christophe Boesch, who has studied the Taï chimpanzees for decades, reported in 1991 that he twice saw a mother clearly show her child how to do it. Two clear cases in many years is very few. So scientists argue about how much teaching other apes do. Humans teach all the time. Humans also copy in a special way, which you can test in the Copycat Test.
- e.g. An orangutan child stays with the mother for about seven or eight years, learning which of hundreds of plants to eat and how to build a nest.
- e.g. At Taï, a young chimpanzee often uses the mother's own hammer, and does better with it.
- e.g. In a test in 2005, young chimpanzees who could see that a step did nothing left it out. Children of three and four copied every step.
### Feelings and bonds · *defined*
**Definition:** Other apes form close bonds that last for life. They comfort each other, grieve, make up after fights and care for the hurt.

Other apes feel. The evidence is in what they do, and it has been collected for more than sixty years. At Gombe in 1972, an old mother named Flo died. Her young son Flint stayed near the place, stopped eating, grew weak and died about three weeks later. After a fight, two chimpanzees often come back together, hold out a hand, embrace and groom. Frans de Waal, who first studied this making up, also saw a third chimpanzee put an arm round the loser of a fight. Apes look after wounds. In June 2022 in Sumatra, a male orangutan named Rakus had a wound on his face. Scientists watched him chew the leaves of a climbing plant that local people use as medicine. He put the juice on the wound and then covered it with the chewed leaves. The wound closed in five days. Nobody knows whether Rakus worked this out alone or learned it from others. These are the same kinds of acts that show feeling in a person who cannot speak, such as a baby. Some scientists say we should describe only the acts and not name the feeling. That view is given in the challenging views.
- e.g. A chimpanzee mother may carry her dead baby for days or weeks.
- e.g. At Taï, adult chimpanzees, including males, have adopted young orphans and shared food with them for years.
- e.g. Young chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas and orangutans all laugh when tickled.
- e.g. Grooming a friend takes up hours of a chimpanzee's day. It cleans the hair, and it keeps friendships strong.
### What goes on in an ape's mind: memory, self and other minds · *defined*
**Definition:** Other apes remember, plan, know themselves in a mirror and keep track of what others can see and know. In some tasks they do better than humans.

In 1970 Gordon Gallup gave some chimpanzees a mirror for several days. Then, while they were asleep under a vet's medicine, he put a spot of dye on one eyebrow. When they woke and looked in the mirror, they touched the spot on their own faces. They knew whose face they were seeing. In 2007 in Japan, Sana Inoue and Tetsuro Matsuzawa showed a young chimpanzee named Ayumu five numbers on a screen for about a fifth of a second. Then the numbers were covered. Ayumu touched the places in the right order about 8 times in 10. University students given the same task did much worse at that speed. Later, other scientists showed that people who practise a great deal can match Ayumu. So the test shows two things. A chimpanzee's memory for a quick glance is very good, and practice matters for everyone. Do apes know what another is thinking? In 2016 Christopher Krupenye and his team showed films to chimpanzees, bonobos and orangutans. In the films, a person saw an object hidden, and it was moved while the person was away. A camera followed the apes' eyes. Most of them looked at the place where the person wrongly thought the object was. That suggests they kept track of what the person believed. Scientists still argue about how far this goes.
- e.g. In the One Glance activity, you take a paper form of Ayumu's test.
- e.g. All five orangutans in a 2007 test got a groundnut out of a tall tube by carrying water in their mouths and spitting it in, so the nut floated up.
- e.g. When children were given the same floating nut test in 2011, about 1 in 12 four-year-olds and about 6 in 10 eight-year-olds found the answer.
- e.g. Chimpanzees remember where fruit trees are, and seem to plan routes to reach them early.
### Gestures, calls and the language question · *debated*
**Definition:** Other apes talk to each other with gestures, calls, faces and touch. Whether any of this, or anything apes have been taught, counts as language is still argued about.

Wild chimpanzees use dozens of gestures on purpose. The gesturer looks at the other, waits for an answer, and tries again if nothing happens. Catherine Hobaiter and her team have worked out what many of the gestures ask for. An open hand held out asks for food. A long, loud scratch on one's own arm asks to be groomed there. In 2023 Kirsty Graham and Catherine Hobaiter showed films of chimpanzee and bonobo gestures to 5,656 people online. Each person chose one meaning out of four. By chance they would be right 1 time in 4. They were right more than half the time. So we still understand much of our cousins' sign system. From the 1960s, scientists also tried to teach apes human signs and symbols. A gorilla named Koko and a bonobo named Kanzi learned hundreds. What that shows is argued about.
- e.g. In the Talking Without Words activity, your family guesses messages from hands and body alone.
- e.g. A chimpanzee who finds a snake gives an alarm call, and calls more when others nearby have not seen the snake yet.
- e.g. Human babies point to show things to others before they can talk. Other apes seldom point just to share something interesting.
- **Scientists who taught signs and symbols to apes, such as Francine Patterson and Sue Savage-Rumbaugh:** The apes used hundreds of signs or symbols to ask, answer and name things, and understood many spoken requests. That is the beginning of language.
- **Herbert Terrace, who taught signs to a chimpanzee named Nim:** After studying his own films, he concluded in 1979 that Nim mostly copied his teachers and strung signs together to get rewards, without grammar. Language needs grammar.
- **Scientists who study wild apes, such as Catherine Hobaiter:** Asking whether apes can learn our language is the wrong test. Their own gestures and calls already carry meaning, and that is what should be studied.
### Chimpanzees and bonobos: fighting, hunting, sharing and peace · *defined*
**Definition:** Our two closest relatives live differently. Chimpanzee groups are led by males and can be violent to neighbours. Bonobo groups are led by females and are more peaceful, though not always.

At first Jane Goodall thought chimpanzees were gentler than people. Then she and the Gombe field staff saw two things that shocked her. Chimpanzees hunt monkeys in groups and eat the meat. And from 1974 to 1978, the Gombe chimpanzees split into two groups, and the males of one group killed the males of the other, one at a time. Chimpanzees also share meat, adopt orphans and comfort the hurt. Both are true of the same animals. Bonobos live only south of the Congo River. Among bonobos, females stick together and outrank the males, and neighbouring groups sometimes meet and mix peacefully. Bonobos do fight, but killings like those at Gombe are almost unknown among them. Nobody is sure why the two species differ. We are equally related to both. So nobody can point to chimpanzees and say 'humans are violent by nature', or to bonobos and say 'humans are peaceful by nature'.
- e.g. Chimpanzee males patrol the edge of their range in silent single file.
- e.g. Bonobo females who are not related still groom each other, share food and back each other up against males.
- e.g. After a hunt, a chimpanzee holding meat is surrounded by others holding out open hands. Many are given a piece.
### How we know: watching for years, and who did the watching · *defined*
**Definition:** Almost everything known about wild apes comes from field studies in which people watch the same individuals, by name, for many years.

Jane Goodall arrived at Gombe on 14 July 1960, aged 26, with her mother Vanne. The officials would not let a young woman camp there alone. She had no university degree. For months the chimpanzees ran from her. She kept coming back to the same hillside until they stopped running. She gave them names such as David Greybeard, Flo and Fifi, and wrote about their different characters. When she went to Cambridge University to study for a doctorate, some scientists told her this was wrong. They said animals should have numbers, and that only humans have personalities. She did not agree, and in time most scientists came round to her view. She was not the only one working this way. Japanese scientists had been naming individual monkeys since the 1950s. Dian Fossey with gorillas and Biruté Galdikas with orangutans began long studies soon after. And much of the Gombe record was written by Tanzanians. Hilali Matama was hired in 1968, one of the first Tanzanian research assistants. Today Tanzanian field staff follow the chimpanzees and collect the data every day. Jane Goodall died on 1 October 2025, aged 91. The Gombe study still goes on.
- e.g. A single visit could never have shown the four-year fight between two groups, or a mother's skills passing to her daughter.
- e.g. Naming individuals lets a watcher follow one life for decades: who their mother was, whom they learned from, and whom they trusted.
- e.g. Louis Leakey, who sent Jane Goodall to Gombe, also helped Dian Fossey and Biruté Galdikas begin.
### So are humans special? · *debated*
**Definition:** Humans share tool making, culture, feelings, memory and self-knowledge with other apes. What, if anything, is found only in humans is still argued about.

Look at what has come off the old list: tools, culture, knowing yourself in a mirror, comforting a friend. Each was once called 'only human'. Each was then seen in other apes by someone who watched long enough. Some things have not been seen in other apes. No other ape has a language with grammar that can say anything at all. No other ape's culture builds up and up, so that a child today uses tools no single person could invent. No other ape cooks food, or works together in groups of thousands of strangers. Are these new kinds of things, or the same things grown larger? Scientists disagree, and their main views are below. There is one more thing to notice. 'Special' can mean 'different from the others'. In that sense every species is special. A chimpanzee beats us at glance memory, a gibbon at swinging, an orangutan at knowing a thousand forest plants. 'Special' can also mean 'above the others'. Science has no test for that meaning.
- e.g. In the Copycat Test, you find out whether people copy a step that clearly does nothing. Some scientists think this habit of exact copying is what lets human culture build up.
- e.g. In the game Only Humans?, you sort sixteen claims into 'others do it too', 'still argued about' and 'not seen in other apes so far'.
- e.g. Other kinds of humans, such as Neanderthals, lived until about 40,000 years ago. If they were still here, the gap between us and our nearest relatives would look smaller.
- **Charles Darwin, in The Descent of Man (1871):** The difference in mind between humans and other animals is great, but it is a difference of degree and not of kind. Other animals have the same abilities in smaller amounts.
- **Thomas Suddendorf, a psychologist, in The Gap (2013):** There is a real gap. Two things widen it: humans can imagine endless possible situations and think about them, and humans have a strong urge to share what is in their minds with others.
- **Michael Tomasello, who has compared young children and other apes for many years:** Other apes are clever alone. What humans add is doing things together with a shared goal: pointing things out, teaching, and helping each other on a common task from a very young age.
- **Frans de Waal, who studied chimpanzees and bonobos for most of his life:** Each time a line is drawn between humans and other animals, later evidence rubs it out. It is better to ask what each species is good at than to look for a line.
### Our cousins are in danger, and so are their cultures · *defined*
**Definition:** Every great ape species except humans is endangered. When a group of apes is lost, the group's learned traditions are lost too.

Great apes have few young. A chimpanzee mother has one baby about every five years, and an orangutan mother one about every seven or eight. So a group that loses a few adults takes many years to recover. Forests are cut for farms, roads and mines. Apes are hunted, and babies are caught to be sold. There is a second kind of loss. In 2019 Hjalmar Kühl and a large team compared 144 chimpanzee communities and 31 behaviours. In places where people had changed the forest most, a behaviour was 88 per cent less likely to be found. A tradition needs enough chimpanzees, living close enough together, for the young to watch. When groups shrink and scatter, the watching stops. A tradition that is lost may not be invented again. This is why some scientists now say that ape cultures need protecting, and not only ape bodies. Some people also say that great apes should have a few basic rights in law, such as not being kept alone in a cage.
- e.g. The western hoolock gibbon, India's only ape, lives in forest patches cut apart by tea gardens, roads and railway lines. Gibbons seldom come down to the ground, so a gap in the trees can trap a family.
- e.g. In 1999, New Zealand made a law that great apes may be used in research only when the research is for the good of the apes.
- e.g. In 2016, a judge in Mendoza in Argentina ruled that a chimpanzee named Cecilia, who had lived in a zoo for more than 30 years, had some basic rights. Cecilia was moved to a sanctuary.

## Mostly agreed
- Humans, chimpanzees and bonobos descend from common ancestors, and chimpanzees and bonobos are our closest living relatives. *How we know:* Their DNA is more like ours than any other species' DNA is. Human chromosome 2 carries the marks of two ape chromosomes joined end to end. Our bones, teeth, hands and blood are built on the same plan.
- Chimpanzees make and use tools. *How we know:* Jane Goodall saw it at Gombe in 1960. Since then it has been filmed at many sites, with stems, brush tips, sponges, stone hammers and sharpened sticks.
- Groups of chimpanzees and orangutans have learned traditions that differ from group to group. *How we know:* Records from seven long chimpanzee studies showed 39 behaviours that were common in some groups and missing in others, where the place could not explain the difference. In 2011 a new habit was filmed spreading among chimpanzees who had watched each other.
- Great apes know their own bodies in a mirror. *How we know:* Chimpanzees with a dye mark on the face touch the mark on their own face when they see a mirror. Gordon Gallup first showed this in 1970, and it has been repeated with orangutans, bonobos and some gorillas.
- Other apes form bonds that last for years, and they act towards friends and family in ways that show feeling. *How we know:* Long field studies have recorded mothers carrying dead babies, friends making up after fights, adults adopting orphans, and apes caring for wounds.
- Every great ape species except humans is endangered. *How we know:* The Red List of the International Union for Conservation of Nature, which counts wild populations, lists all of them as endangered or critically endangered.

## How we found out
**Jane Goodall, an Englishwoman, with the chimpanzee David Greybeard. Louis Leakey sent her to Gombe, and her mother, Vanne Morris-Goodall, went with her., She arrived on 14 July 1960. She saw the termite fishing in the autumn of that year..** In 1960 scientists defined humans as 'Man the Toolmaker'. They held that only humans make tools. Nobody had watched wild apes for long enough to check. On 14 July 1960 Jane Goodall, aged 26, arrived at Gombe in Tanzania with her mother, Vanne. She had no university degree. For months the chimpanzees ran from her. She kept returning to the same hillside until they let her stay. That autumn she watched a male she called David Greybeard push a grass stem into a termite mound and eat the termites holding on to it. Soon she saw chimpanzees pick leafy twigs and strip the leaves off before fishing. They were making a tool. She sent word to Louis Leakey, the scientist who had sent her to Gombe. He replied: 'Now we must redefine tool, redefine man, or accept chimpanzees as humans.' Some scientists doubted a young woman with no training. Then the fishing was photographed and filmed, and watchers at other sites saw tool making too. The old definition was dropped.  
*What people believed before:* That only humans make tools. It was shown wrong when someone watched wild chimpanzees every day for months and saw them strip twigs to fish for termites.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only humans make tools.** (Most scientists before 1960, who defined humans as 'Man the Toolmaker'; *rejected*)  
  For: Nobody had reported a wild animal changing an object to fit a job. Human tools were found with the oldest human fossils known then.  
  Against: In 1960 Jane Goodall watched chimpanzees strip leaves from twigs to fish for termites. Tool making has since been seen at many chimpanzee sites, and in orangutans and some birds.
- **Humans are the top of evolution, and other apes are less evolved.** (A very common picture, drawn as a line of figures walking from a bent ape to an upright man; *rejected*)  
  For: Humans have the largest brains for their body size, and have built cities, writing and machines.  
  Against: Evolution is a branching tree and not a ladder. The chimpanzee line and the human line have been changing for exactly the same length of time since they split. Each fits their own way of living.
- **A scientist should give animals numbers and should never write about their feelings or characters.** (Many scientists who studied animal behaviour in the 1960s; *minority*)  
  For: It is easy to imagine human feelings in an animal when they are not there. This mistake is called anthropomorphism. Numbers and plain descriptions guard against it.  
  Against: Individual apes do differ in character, and those differences explain what happens in a group. Leaving them out makes the record less accurate. Most ape scientists now use names.
- **What chimpanzee groups have should not be called culture.** (Some scientists, such as Claudio Tennie; *debated*)  
  For: Each chimpanzee might work the habit out alone, once the right things are around. Ape habits also stay simple. They do not build up over generations as human skills do.  
  Against: At Budongo in 2011, a new moss sponge spread almost only to chimpanzees who had watched it being made. Groups living in similar forests keep different habits for decades.
- **Apes can learn human language.** (Scientists who taught signs or symbols to apes from the 1960s to the 1990s; *debated*)  
  For: Koko the gorilla and Kanzi the bonobo used hundreds of signs or symbols. Kanzi carried out many spoken requests he had never heard before.  
  Against: Herbert Terrace found that the chimpanzee Nim mostly copied his teachers and repeated signs to get rewards. No taught ape has shown the grammar of even a three-year-old child.
- **Human minds differ from other apes' minds in kind, and not only in amount.** (Thomas Suddendorf and some other psychologists; *debated*)  
  For: No other ape has open-ended language, culture that builds up, or stories about the far past and future. These look like new abilities.  
  Against: Charles Darwin and Frans de Waal argue that each of these grows out of something other apes have in a smaller amount, such as gestures, traditions and planning for tomorrow.

## Roots
We have learned what humans share with the other apes: DNA, bodies, tools, traditions and feelings. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

## Story: Who Was Watching Nick?
*This is a true story. The forest, the date and time, the pool between the roots, the moss sponge, the six days, the second new habit and the count of sixty-eight chimpanzees are from the scientific report of 2014 by Catherine Hobaiter and her team. The sentence in quotation marks near the end is her own, from an interview. What Catherine thought and felt, and her evening with the films, are imagined. The names Nick and Nambi match the short codes NK and NB in the report. One radio report gives the alpha male's name as Mick.*

The rains of 2011 were heavy in the Budongo Forest in Uganda. A river spilled over part of the forest floor. When the water went down, it left a small pool between the roots of two big trees.

Catherine Hobaiter knew every chimpanzee who came to drink there. She had followed this community, called Sonso, for years. Sixty-eight of them had names. She carried a notebook and a small video camera, and the chimpanzees took about as much notice of her as of a tree stump.

Sonso chimpanzees have one way of drinking from a pool like this. They pick a few leaves, fold them into the mouth and chew them into a wad. They dip the wad in the water and suck it like a sponge. Every grown chimpanzee at Sonso does this. Their mothers did it before them. In about twenty years of records, nobody had seen them do anything else.

On the morning of the fourteenth of November, at five minutes past nine, Nick walked up to the pool. Nick was the alpha male, the highest in rank. The others shifted out of his way. Catherine lifted the camera, as she did a hundred times a day.

Nick did not pick leaves. He reached to the trunk beside him and pulled off a handful of green moss. He rolled the moss loosely, put it in his mouth for a moment, took it out and pushed it into the water. Then he lifted it, dripping, and sucked.

Catherine held the camera very still. A sponge made of moss. In all the Sonso records, those words had never been written.

She was not the only one watching. Nambi, the highest-ranking female, sat an arm's length from Nick. Her eyes were on his hands. A little later Nambi pulled moss from the same trunk, rolled it and dipped it in the pool.

That evening Catherine watched the film again and again. One chimpanzee doing a new thing was interesting. Two was something else. If the moss sponge spread, she would be seeing what scientists had argued about for years.

The argument went like this. Chimpanzees in one forest crack nuts, and chimpanzees in another do not. Many scientists said this was culture: the young learn the habit by watching the old. Others were not sure. Perhaps each chimpanzee works the habit out alone, they said, when the right things happen to be lying around. To settle it, someone would have to catch a habit on the day it began. And for every chimpanzee who picked it up, that someone would have to know one thing. Had they watched another chimpanzee do it first?

Nobody had ever caught the first day in the wild. Catherine had the first day on film.

So from the next morning she wrote down more than who drank. For every visit to the pool she noted who was using what kind of sponge. And she noted who was sitting near enough to see.

The pool was a busy place that week. A young female named Karibu sat beside her mother, Kwera, while Kwera worked a lump of moss in the water. Karibu watched. Then Karibu reached for the trunk. One after another, over six days, seven more chimpanzees made moss sponges.

Each night Catherine went back through the films and asked her question. Before this chimpanzee's first moss sponge, had they watched someone make one? Yes. Yes. Yes. For six of the seven, the film showed them watching first.

Something else was new at the pool that week. Old leaf sponges lay on the ground all around it, dropped by earlier drinkers. A young male picked one up, chewed it and used it again. Soon others were doing the same. That was a new habit too. But when Catherine checked the films, these chimpanzees had not needed to watch anybody. Used sponges were everywhere. Anyone could come across one and try it.

So two new habits had started at one pool in one week. One of them turned up here and there, wherever a chimpanzee happened on an old sponge. The other moved like a message passed along a line, from the one who made it to the one who was looking.

And the chimpanzees who came to the pool that week but never saw a moss sponge being made? They went on folding leaves, as their mothers had.

Later Catherine and her team put every visit into a table and worked through the numbers. A chimpanzee who watched a moss sponge being made was about fifteen times more likely to start making one. Catherine said it more simply. 'Basically, if you saw it done, you learned how to do it, and if you didn't you didn't.'

Scientists checked again three years later. Moss sponges were still being made at Sonso.

Nick did not know he had begun anything. Nambi did not know she had passed anything on. Catherine knew, because she had written down who was drinking, and also who was watching.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the hidden idea is how scientists can tell that a habit is culture. It is not enough to see that a new habit appears. You must know, for each learner, whether they watched someone first. The moss sponge spread only along the lines of who watched whom. The reuse of old leaf sponges did not, because any chimpanzee could find an old sponge alone. That contrast, at one pool in one week, is the evidence. It also shows why patient watching and careful notes matter, as they did for Jane Goodall.

Every stalk is dipped in water in the same way and held in the seeds for a count of three. Each stalk gets five dips, because one dip can be a fluke. The dips go in turn, leafy, stripped, brush, again and again, so that no stalk gets all the practice. Only one thing changes: the shape of the stalk.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for each stalk: leafy, stripped and brush tip. Write the number of seeds for each of the five dips, then the total.
- **results table:** Stalk | Seeds on dip 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 | Total for 5 dips
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the three leafy stalks and the tumbler of seeds. 2. Explain the three tools they will make. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* The seeds are termites in a mound. You will fish with three stalks: leafy, stripped, and stripped with a brush tip. Which will bring out the most seeds? Which the fewest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the stalks and the seeds. Guess which tool will catch the most. Say why.
  - Ask: What do you think the leaves will do when the stalk goes into the hole? (good: They will get stuck.; They might help by holding more seeds.; They will fold back and block the hole.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Some children think the leafy stalk will catch most because it is biggest. The test will answer it.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave the first stalk as it is. 2. Pull every leaf off the second stalk. 3. Pull every leaf off the third stalk. 4. Lay the last 2 cm of the third stalk on the board. Press and roll the back of the spoon on it ten times. 5. Split the squashed end into thin fibres with a fingernail.  
  *Parent says:* Leave one stalk leafy. Strip the leaves off the other two. Now squash the tip of the third and split it into a little brush.  
  *Child mode:* Keep one stalk leafy. Strip the other two. Squash one tip and split it into a brush.
  - Note: A fresh green stalk frays best. If the tip will not split, tie five short pieces of thread round it, with 2 cm of each hanging free. Chimpanzees fray a stem by pulling it through their teeth. Your child may try that too, since curry-leaf stalks are safe to chew.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put 3 tablespoons of sesame seeds in the dry steel tumbler. 2. Stretch the paper over the top and hold it with the rubber band. 3. Push a pencil through the middle of the paper to make one hole. 4. Draw the results table.  
  *Parent says:* This is the termite mound. The hole is the only way in, and you cannot see inside. A chimpanzee cannot see inside a mound either.  
  *Child mode:* Put the seeds in the tumbler. Cover it with paper. Make one pencil hole. Draw your table.
  - Note: Keep the hole no wider than a pencil. A real termite tunnel is narrow, and the narrow hole is what makes the leaves a problem.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Dip the bottom 3 cm of the leafy stalk in water and tap off the drip. 2. Push it through the hole to the bottom, without tearing the paper. 3. Count to three and pull it out slowly. 4. Brush the seeds onto the plate, count them and write the number. 5. Do the same with the stripped stalk, then the brush tip. 6. Repeat until each stalk has had five dips.  
  *Parent says:* Wet the tip, push it in, count to three, and pull out slowly. Count the seeds. Now the next stalk. We go round five times.  
  *Child mode:* Wet the tip. Push it in and count to three. Pull out slowly. Count the seeds.
  - Note: If the leafy stalk will not go through, that dip scores 0. Do not force it. Wet the tip before every dip, because the seeds stick to the water. If almost no seeds stick to any stalk, the tips are too dry. Clear the plate after each count.
  - Check: In the Termite Fishing Test, why do you fish in turn and not five times with one stalk first? → **So no stalk gets all your practice**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Add up the five dips for each stalk. 2. Write the three totals. 3. Draw a bar for each total.  
  *Parent says:* Add up each row. Now draw three bars, one for each stalk. Which bar is tallest?  
  *Child mode:* Add up each row. Draw a bar for each stalk. Which is tallest?
  - Note: A bar chart suits these results, because the three stalks are separate things to compare.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table and the chart together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened. What did you do to the stalk to make it work better?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Why did stripping the leaves help? (good: The leaves caught on the hole.; A bare stalk could reach the seeds.)
  - Ask: Why might a brush tip hold more seeds than a plain tip? (good: It has more wet surface for seeds to stick to.; Seeds get caught between the fibres.)
  - Note: In most homes the brush tip brings out the most seeds and the leafy stalk the fewest. Write down what you really found. If the brush did not win, look at its tip. Fibres that have stuck together into one point act like a plain tip. The reason: a stalk only works if it fits the hole, and a frayed tip has more surface. Real termites bite on to anything pushed into their nest, and a brush gives them more to bite. In a real test in the Republic of Congo in 2009, brush tips brought out about 5 termites each try. Plain stems brought out about 1 termite in every 4 tries. Your child changed a stalk twice to fit a job. That is tool making, and chimpanzees do both of those steps.
  - Think: You knew what the tool was for before you made it. What must a chimpanzee know before stripping a stem?
### e2 · The Copycat Test: do people copy a step that does nothing? (30 min)
- **question:** You show someone how to get a sweet out of a clear box. Before you open the lid, you tap the lid three times and turn the box round. Anyone can see these steps do nothing. Will people copy them?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many of the people you test will tap the lid, and how many will turn the box? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every person sees the same four steps, done in the same order at the same speed. You say the same words to each person and never say 'copy me'. Each person is tested alone, so nobody watches anyone else's turn. The box, the spoon and the sweet are the same each time. Only the person changes.
- **record:** Draw a table with a row for each person. Write their age, whether they tapped the lid, whether they turned the box, and what they said when you asked why.
- **results table:** Person and age | Tapped the lid? | Turned the box? | What they said when asked why
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the question with your child. 2. Make a list of at least three people to test. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason for each person, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* The box is clear, so anyone can see that tapping does nothing. Who on your list will tap anyway? Who will go straight for the lid? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* List three people to test. Guess who will tap the lid. Say why.
  - Ask: Do you think grown-ups or small children will copy more? Why? (good: Small children, because they copy everything.; Grown-ups, because they want to do it properly.; Nobody, because they can see it is useless.)
  - Note: You can be tested only if you have not read this page. If you have, help with the list. Grandparents, neighbours, cousins and friends all count.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child practises the four steps until they are smooth. Tap the lid three times with the spoon. Turn the box round once on the table. Open the lid. Take the sweet. 2. They practise the words too.  
  *Parent says:* Practise until you do it the same way every time. Say only this: 'Watch how I get the sweet.' Afterwards say: 'Your turn to get the sweet.'  
  *Child mode:* Practise: tap three times, turn the box, open, take the sweet. Learn your two sentences.
  - Note: The words matter. 'Copy me' would tell people what to do. 'Your turn to get the sweet' names only the goal.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Bring in the first person alone. 2. Your child shows the four steps once. 3. They put in a new sweet, close the lid and hand over the spoon. 4. They watch without speaking, and write what the person did after the person leaves. 5. Do the same with each person.  
  *Parent says:* Show the steps once. Then give them the spoon and say your sentence. Do not help, and do not smile at the tapping.  
  *Child mode:* Show each person the steps once. Give them the spoon. Watch quietly. Write what they did.
  - Note: Keep the others out of the room, so nobody sees a turn before their own. Each person keeps the sweet they get.
  - Check: In the Copycat Test, why is each person tested alone? → **So they copy only what you showed**
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count how many people tapped the lid and how many turned the box. 2. Go back to each person and ask why they did, or did not. 3. Write their answers in the last column.  
  *Parent says:* Count the taps and the turns. Now go and ask each person one thing. Why did you tap, or why did you not?  
  *Child mode:* Count who tapped and who turned. Ask each person why. Write what they say.
  - Note: The answers are often the best part. People say 'because you did' or 'I thought it was part of it'. Some cannot say why at all.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table together. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it might have happened. 3. After they have answered, read them the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you guessed, what you saw, and why you think people did that. When could copying a step you do not understand be useful?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed. Say what you saw. Say why people might copy.
  - Ask: When is it useful to copy a step you do not understand? (good: When the job is too complicated to see why each step matters.; In cooking, or tying a sari, or a science method.; When the person showing you knows more than you.)
  - Note: With three or four people, your result may go either way. Write down what you really found. In 2005 Victoria Horner and Andrew Whiten ran a test like this with a puzzle box. Young chimpanzees who could see through the box left out the useless step. Children of three and four copied every step, even with the clear box. Some scientists think this habit is what lets human skills build up. Many human skills are too complicated for a learner to see why each step matters. Think of soaking, grinding and fermenting for idli batter. Copying exactly keeps the steps that work, even when nobody remembers why. This is an idea and not a settled fact. The chimpanzees in the test were being sensible.
  - Think: The chimpanzees skipped the useless step and the children did not. Who was being clever?

### a2 · The Hundred Square: what does 'almost 99 per cent the same' mean? (12 min)
- **record:** A square of 100 boxes with 1 red box and 3 blue boxes, and your two sums written under it.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child outlines a square of 10 boxes by 10 boxes. 2. Tell them each box stands for one DNA letter in every hundred. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* These 100 boxes stand for your DNA. How many boxes do you think are different in a chimpanzee? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Outline 100 boxes. Guess how many are different in a chimpanzee. Say why.
  - Note: Many children guess 20 or 30, because a chimpanzee looks so different from a person. Write the guess beside the square.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child colours 1 box red. This is for single letters that differ. 2. They colour 3 boxes blue. These are for stretches that one species has and the other lacks. 3. They count the boxes left white.  
  *Parent says:* Colour one box red. Those are letters that differ when the two are lined up. Now three blue, for stretches that are added or missing. How many are left white?  
  *Child mode:* Colour 1 box red. Colour 3 boxes blue. Count the white boxes.
  - Note: The exact figure for single letters is about 1.2 in 100, so the red box should really be a little bigger than one box. With the blue boxes, 96 stay white. Counting only red gives 'almost 99 per cent'. Counting red and blue gives '96 per cent'. Both are honest. They count different things.
  - Check: One book says humans and chimpanzees are 99 per cent alike. Another says 96. What is the likeliest reason? → **They counted different kinds of difference**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Tell your child that one set of human DNA has about 3,000 million letters. 2. They work out how many letters one box stands for. 3. They work out how many letters 1.2 boxes stand for. 4. Tell them two humans differ by about one tenth of a box.  
  *Parent says:* Your DNA has about 3,000 million letters. So how many letters is one box? How many is 1.2 boxes? Is that a small number?  
  *Child mode:* Your DNA has 3,000 million letters. How many is one box? How many is 1.2 boxes?
  - Ask: The red box looks tiny. Is the difference between you and a chimpanzee small or large? (good: Small as a share, but 36 million letters is a lot.; It depends whether you look at the share or the count.)
  - Note: 3,000 million ÷ 100 = 30 million letters for one box. 30 million × 1.2 = 36 million letters. The 2005 study counted about 35 million. Two humans differ at about 1 letter in 1,000, which is one tenth of a box, or about 3 million letters.
  - Think: Would you say 'only 1 in 100' or 'as many as 36 million'? Both are true. Why might someone choose one or the other?

  - Check: Why is finding end letters in the middle of chromosome 2 strong evidence? → **The join idea predicted it before anyone looked**

  - Wait: Do this on day 2

  - Ask: How far back could you trace one habit? What happens to the map before that? (good: Only to my great-grandmother.; Nobody remembers who started it.; It goes back further than anyone can say.)
  - Note: Most rows will be 'learned'. A few may be 'the place', such as sleeping on a mat in a hot town. Almost none will be 'genes'. ### c1 · The Nut in the Bottle: get it out without tipping (20 min)
- **problem:** A groundnut in the shell is at the bottom of an empty 500 millilitre bottle. Get the groundnut out. The bottle must stay standing on the tray the whole time.
- **your turn:** Now set a puzzle for a grown-up. Change one thing so that water no longer works, such as using a raisin, which sinks. Which of your other solutions still works? Design one more.
- **constraints:** The bottle stays upright on the tray. No tipping, lifting, squeezing or cutting.; You may use anything that is on the table.; You have 8 minutes for your first try.; Nobody may tell you the answer during those 8 minutes.
- **general:** Pour water into the bottle. A groundnut in the shell floats, so it rises with the water until you can pick it out at the neck.; This is what orangutans did in 2007. All five carried water from a drinker in their mouths and spat it into the tube, on their first try.
- **creative:** Roll a loop of sticky tape, sticky side out, round the end of a skewer. Press it on the groundnut and lift.; Use two skewers as long chopsticks.; Make a noose of string at the end of a skewer and loop it round the middle of the shell.

  *Child mode:* Get the groundnut out. Keep the bottle standing. Use anything on the table.
  - Note: Do not point at the jug or look at it. In the study, the children had just used the water to water plants, and most still did not think of it.

  - Note: Each failed try is useful. If they reach the answer early, go on to the next step. If the 8 minutes run out, that is what happened to most children in the study too.

### Apes, Tools and Watching (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these Indian primates is an ape?  
   Options: Bonnet macaque / Hoolock gibbon / Grey langur / Slender loris  
   **Answer:** Hoolock gibbon. *Why:* A hoolock gibbon has no tail and swings by the arms, as apes do. Macaques and langurs are monkeys with tails. A loris is a small night primate. Choosing a monkey usually means using 'ape' for any primate.
2. (fill) Humans, chimpanzees and bonobos share a common ____ who lived about 6 to 8 million years ago.  
   **Answer:** ['ancestor']. *Why:* A common ancestor is a being from long ago from whom two kinds today both descend.
3. (truefalse) True or false: humans evolved from chimpanzees.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Humans and chimpanzees both descend from ancestors who were neither. Since the split, both lines have changed for the same length of time.
4. (match) Match each chimpanzee tool to its job.  
   **Answer:** A stem stripped of leaves → fishing termites from a mound; A stone hammer → cracking hard nuts; A wad of chewed leaves → soaking up water; A sharpened stick → hunting in tree holes; A thick, strong stick → opening a termite nest. *Why:* Each tool is shaped for one job. A strong answer can say what would go wrong with the wrong tool.
5. (sort) Sort these primates into great apes, small apes and monkeys.  
   **Answer:** orangutan → Great apes; hoolock gibbon → Small apes; bonnet macaque → Monkeys; gorilla → Great apes; bonobo → Great apes; grey langur → Monkeys; human → Great apes; lion-tailed macaque → Monkeys. *Why:* Great apes are the large apes, including humans. Gibbons are small apes. Macaques and langurs have tails and are monkeys.
6. (write) In a fishing test, a leafy stalk brought almost no seeds through a narrow hole. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** The leaves caught on the edge of the narrow hole, so the stalk could not reach the seeds. Stripping the leaves lets the stalk pass through.. *Why:* A strong answer links the shape of the tool to the shape of the hole. A real termite tunnel is narrow too.
7. (order) Put in order the steps a chimpanzee takes to fish with a brush-tipped stem.  
   **Answer:** ['Pick a long stem', 'Strip off the leaves', 'Fray the tip with the teeth', 'Push the stem into the nest', 'Pull it out and eat the termites']. *Why:* The tool is made before it is used. Stripping and fraying both change the stem to fit the job.
8. (mcq) A chimpanzee strips a twig before fishing. Why do scientists call this making a tool?  
   Options: The twig is long / The twig was changed to fit a job / The twig lay near a mound / A chimpanzee held the twig  
   **Answer:** The twig was changed to fit a job. *Why:* Making a tool means changing an object so that it does a job better. Choosing 'held the twig' describes using a thing, which is less than making one.
9. (odd) Three of these are great apes. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Gorilla / Orangutan / Gibbon / Bonobo  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* A gibbon is an ape, but a small ape. Gorillas, orangutans, bonobos, chimpanzees and humans are the great apes.
10. (draw) Draw a family tree for gibbons, orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos and humans.  
   **Answer:** A tree where gibbons branch off first, then orangutans, then gorillas. The last fork leads to humans on one side and to chimpanzees and bonobos on the other.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts humans inside the tree and not at the top. Chimpanzees and bonobos share the fork nearest to humans.
11. (truefalse) True or false: a chimpanzee is more closely related to a human than to a gorilla.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The gorilla line split off first. The human line and the chimpanzee line shared ancestors for longer.
12. (mcq) When do scientists say that a group of animals has culture?  
   Options: Its learned habits differ from other groups' / It uses stone tools / It lives in a forest / Its members look alike  
   **Answer:** Its learned habits differ from other groups'. *Why:* Culture is a set of traditions learned from others, which differs from group to group. Choosing 'stone tools' mixes up one kind of tradition with the whole idea.
13. (perspective) Two scientists argue about how to record wild chimpanzees.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives a reason, such as: names help you follow one life for decades. It also says what the other has right: we must be careful not to imagine feelings that are not there.. *Why:* Most ape scientists now use names. The first scientist's worry about imagining too much is still a fair one.
14. (label) Label a drawing of termite fishing: termite mound, tunnel, stripped stem, brush tip, termites.  
   **Answer:** ['Termite mound', 'Narrow tunnel into the mound', 'Stripped stem', 'Brush tip', 'Termites holding on']. *Why:* The stem must be bare to fit the tunnel. The brush tip gives the termites more to hold.
15. (write) For months the Gombe chimpanzees ran from Jane Goodall. What did she do, and why did it work?  
   **Answer:** She kept coming back to the same place every day and stayed quiet. Over months the chimpanzees learned that she was harmless and let her stay near.. *Why:* Wild animals behave normally only when they no longer fear the watcher. That takes patience, which is why field studies take years.
### DNA, Culture and Minds (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Lined up letter by letter, about how many DNA letters in 100 differ between you and a chimpanzee?  
   Options: About 10 / About 25 / About 50 / About 1  
   **Answer:** About 1. *Why:* About 1.2 letters in every 100 differ. Choosing a large number usually comes from how different our bodies look. Small DNA changes can have large effects.
2. (truefalse) True or false: a human cell has 46 chromosomes and a chimpanzee cell has 48.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Two chromosomes joined end to end on the human line, so we have one pair fewer.
3. (write) Which two marks in human chromosome 2 show that it was once two chromosomes?  
   **Answer:** End letters in the middle of the chromosome, where no ends belong. And the faded remains of a second pinched-in place.. *Why:* A joined chromosome keeps traces of the two old ends and of both pinched-in places. Both were found.
4. (fill) Learning something by watching others is called ____ learning.  
   **Answer:** ['social']. *Why:* Social learning is how traditions pass from one member of a group to another.
5. (order) Put in order how scientists decide that a habit is culture.  
   **Answer:** ['List habits common in one group and missing in another', 'Remove the ones the place can explain', 'Remove the ones genes can explain', 'Call what is left learned culture']. *Why:* Culture is what remains after the place and genes are ruled out.
6. (mcq) One chimpanzee group cracks no nuts. Their forest has no nut trees. What causes this difference?  
   Options: Culture / The place / Genes / Chance  
   **Answer:** The place. *Why:* They cannot crack nuts that are not there. Choosing 'culture' skips the first check: could the place explain it?
7. (sort) Sort each difference by its cause: learned, the place, or genes.  
   **Answer:** eating with chopsticks or with the hand → Learned; thick wool in Ladakh, cotton in Chennai → The place; blood group B or O → Genes; clasping hands while grooming → Learned; a bonobo's long legs → Genes; no termite fishing where there are no mounds → The place. *Why:* Ask two questions. Does the place offer the same things? Is the family the same? If both are yes, the difference is learned.
8. (mcq) In the mirror test, what shows that an ape knows whose reflection it is?  
   Options: Touching the mirror / Looking behind the mirror / Making a threat face / Touching the mark on their own face  
   **Answer:** Touching the mark on their own face. *Why:* The mark can be seen only in the mirror. Touching it on their own face shows the ape links the reflection to their own body. Looking behind the mirror suggests the ape expects another animal there.
9. (write) A chimpanzee beat students at a glance memory test. Give one reason the comparison was not fully fair.  
   **Answer:** The chimpanzee had practised the task for years and the students had not. When people practised a lot, they matched him.. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. Here both the species and the amount of practice differed.
10. (truefalse) True or false: '99 per cent the same DNA' counts every kind of difference.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It counts only single letters that differ where the two can be lined up. Stretches that are added or missing are left out.
11. (draw) Draw two chromosomes joined end to end. Mark the old ends and both pinched-in places.  
   **Answer:** One long chromosome with two end marks side by side in the middle, an end mark at each tip, and two pinched-in places, one in each half.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows marks in the middle that a normal chromosome would not have.
12. (match) Match each piece of evidence to what it shows.  
   **Answer:** End letters in the middle of a chromosome → two chromosomes joined; A habit spreads only to those who watched → social learning; Touching a dye mark seen in a mirror → knowing one's own body; A stem stripped before use → tool making; Looking where someone wrongly expects a thing → tracking another's belief. *Why:* Each finding is something a watcher could see. The idea on the right is what the finding points to.
13. (perspective) Two children argue about how different humans and chimpanzees are.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says both are right, because one gives the share and the other the count. It may add that small changes in DNA switches can have large effects.. *Why:* 1.2 in 100 is a small share. It is also about 35 million letters. Which one you say depends on what you want to show.
14. (mcq) Why does a group of great apes recover slowly after losing some adults?  
   Options: Apes cannot find food / Mothers have one baby every several years / Apes do not live in groups / Babies are born only in winter  
   **Answer:** Mothers have one baby every several years. *Why:* A chimpanzee mother has a baby about every five years, and an orangutan about every seven or eight. Few young are born, so numbers rise slowly.
15. (write) One new habit spread only to watchers. Another appeared in animals who watched nobody. Which shows social learning? Why?  
   **Answer:** The first. If only those who watched picked it up, then watching was needed. The second could be worked out alone.. *Why:* To show social learning you must know who watched whom, and not only that a habit became common.
### Are Humans Special? (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) 'Only humans make tools' was believed until 1960. Why had nobody shown it wrong before then?  
   **Answer:** Nobody had watched wild apes closely for long enough. Wild chimpanzees ran from people, so short visits showed very little.. *Why:* Not seeing something is weak evidence when few people have looked. The claim rested on a lack of watching.
2. (mcq) A scientist writes 'not seen in other apes so far'. What does she mean?  
   Options: Apes can never do it / Nobody has observed it yet / Apes were tested and failed / Apes do it only in secret  
   **Answer:** Nobody has observed it yet. *Why:* It reports what has been found up to now. Choosing 'can never' turns a gap in the evidence into a fact about apes.
3. (perspective) Two scientists argue about the minds of humans and other apes.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer picks a side and gives evidence. For the first: tools, traditions and the mirror test were once called human-only. For the second: no other ape has grammar or culture that builds up.. *Why:* This is still argued about. What matters is using evidence for your side and seeing the best point on the other.
4. (truefalse) True or false: chimpanzees are an earlier, less evolved form of humans.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Both lines have been changing for the same length of time since they split. Evolution is a branching tree and not a ladder.
5. (write) Children copy a step they can see is useless. Why might that habit be useful for humans?  
   **Answer:** Many human skills are too complicated to see why each step matters. Copying exactly keeps the steps that work, so skills can be passed on and built up.. *Why:* This is one idea about how human culture builds up. It is not settled.
6. (mcq) Young chimpanzees left out a step when they could see it did nothing. What does that show?  
   Options: They could not copy / They were not watching / They understood what the step did / They disliked the reward  
   **Answer:** They understood what the step did. *Why:* With a box they could not see into, the chimpanzees did copy the step. With a clear box they skipped it. So they could copy, and chose not to.
7. (sort) Sort each claim about 'only humans' by what the evidence says.  
   **Answer:** making tools → Others do it too; laughing → Others do it too; cooking food → Not seen so far; using grammar → Still argued about; writing → Not seen so far; teaching on purpose → Still argued about. *Why:* Tools and laughter have been seen in other apes. Grammar and teaching are argued about. Cooking and writing have not been seen.
8. (write) We are equally related to chimpanzees and bonobos. Why can neither show what humans are 'naturally' like?  
   **Answer:** The two live very differently, one more violent and one more peaceful. Since we are as close to one as to the other, neither can be picked as the model for us.. *Why:* Two equally close relatives who differ show that close relatives can go different ways.
9. (mcq) What is cumulative culture?  
   Options: Skills that build up over generations / Any habit learned from a mother / A habit caused by the place / Culture found only in forests  
   **Answer:** Skills that build up over generations. *Why:* In cumulative culture each generation adds to what the last one knew. Choosing 'any habit learned' describes culture in general, which other apes have.
10. (truefalse) True or false: where people have changed forests most, chimpanzee groups show fewer learned traditions.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A 2019 study of 144 chimpanzee communities found this. Small, scattered groups give the young fewer chances to watch.
11. (draw) Draw a test of whether a crow will use a stick to reach food. Label what stays the same.  
   **Answer:** A drawing with food out of beak reach, such as in a tube, and sticks nearby. Labels say what stays the same each time: the tube, the food, the distance and the time allowed.. *Why:* A strong design lets the crow show the skill without being taught, and keeps everything else the same.
12. (fill) Imagining human feelings in an animal when they may not be there is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['anthropomorphism']. *Why:* Scientists guard against it by writing down what the animal does. They must also guard against the opposite mistake, which is refusing to see a feeling that is there.
13. (odd) Three of these have been seen in wild chimpanzees. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Fishing with stems / Cracking nuts with stones / Keeping a fire going / Sponging up water with leaves  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* No wild ape has been seen making or keeping fire. The other three are well-known chimpanzee tool uses.
14. (mcq) Which is the best kind of guess in science?  
   Options: One that fits whatever is found / One that most people like / One that uses long words / One that evidence could show wrong  
   **Answer:** One that evidence could show wrong. *Why:* The joined chromosome idea said what marks should be there. If they had been missing, the idea would have failed. A guess that fits anything tells us nothing.
15. (write) 'Special' can mean 'different from others' or 'above others'. Which meaning can science test? Explain.  
   **Answer:** Science can test 'different', by comparing what each species does. It has no test for 'above', because that depends on what you choose to value.. *Why:* A chimpanzee is better at glance memory and a human at language. Which matters more is a choice, not a measurement.

## Applications
- People who protect apes now plan to save whole groups and their traditions. An ape raised alone by people misses the years of watching that wild young apes get.
- Good zoos and sanctuaries give apes puzzles, such as food hidden in tubes and logs, because a mind that makes tools needs problems to solve.
- Because apes are so like us, people once used chimpanzees to test medicines. For the same reason, many countries have now stopped.
- Scientists study ape gestures to work out how human language might have begun.
- Rope and tree bridges across roads and railway lines let gibbons and other tree-living primates cross gaps in the forest.
- Teachers use what is known about learning by watching. Showing a skill slowly, and letting the learner use your tool, is how many crafts are still taught.
- Courts in some countries have had to decide whether an ape can have rights. The evidence in this lesson is what the judges were shown.

## Spotted around us
- Monkeys sit on temple walls and bus stands. They are monkeys and not apes: look for the tail.
- A neem twig chewed at one end makes a toothbrush. The chewed end is a brush tip, made the same way a chimpanzee frays a stem.
- A small child at a wedding copies exactly how the grown-ups fold their hands. That is social learning.
- A baby points at a crow to show you. Sharing something just because it is interesting is rare in other apes.
- Your grandmother draws a kolam one way and your neighbour's another. Each learned from their own mother.
- Gibbons in a zoo or in a video, singing loud duets. Gibbons are apes, and they have no tail.
- Gorillas and orangutans in a zoo or in a video. Watch their hands.
- A monkey with a tail crossing a road. Monkeys have tails, and apes do not.
- Look at your own hand: nails, fingerprints and a thumb that meets each finger. Every ape has these.

## Open questions
- **Do other apes teach their young on purpose?** There are only a few clear cases, and scientists do not agree on what should count as teaching.
- **Why do chimpanzees and bonobos, who are so closely related, live so differently?** Food, the absence of gorillas south of the Congo River and the bonds between females have all been suggested. Nobody has been able to test these ideas properly.
- **Which DNA differences between humans and chimpanzees matter most?** Most of the 35 million differing letters seem to change nothing. Finding the few that change how a brain or a body grows is slow work.
- **Did human language begin with gestures like those of other apes?** Gestures and words leave no fossils. Scientists compare living apes and babies, and the evidence can be read in more than one way.
- **Did Rakus the orangutan work out his wound medicine alone, or learn it from others?** It has been seen once, in one orangutan who was born somewhere else. Nobody has yet seen another orangutan do it.
- **Is there anything in the human mind that is truly a new kind of thing?** Every proposed difference has someone arguing that other apes have a small amount of it. The argument has gone on since Darwin.

## Connected maths
- In the Termite Fishing Test, a brush tip brought out 55 seeds in 5 dips. A stripped stalk brought out 25 in 5 dips. Find the average for each. How many times as many did the brush catch? **11 seeds a dip for the brush and 5 for the stripped stalk. The brush caught 2.2 times as many.**. 55 ÷ 5 = 11 seeds for each dip. 25 ÷ 5 = 5 seeds for each dip. 11 ÷ 5 = 2.2. So the brush tip caught a little more than twice as many. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- At real termite nests, brush tips brought out about 4.9 termites a try. Plain stems brought out about 0.27. About how many times better was the brush? Why might your seed test give a smaller number? **About 18 times better. Seeds only stick, but termites bite and hold on, so fibres matter more for termites.**. 4.9 ÷ 0.27 is about 18. In the seed test the brush was about 2 times better. A model leaves things out. Sesame seeds stick to any wet surface. Live termites grip with their jaws, and a smooth stem gives them little to grip. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- One set of human DNA has about 3,000 million letters. About 1.2 letters in every 100 differ from a chimpanzee's. About how many letters is that? **About 36 million letters.**. 3,000 million ÷ 100 = 30 million. That is 1 in every 100. 30 million × 1.2 = 36 million. The 2005 study counted about 35 million. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- People watched 20 films of ape gestures and chose 1 meaning out of 4 each time. How many would pure guessing get right? They got more than 10 right. What does that tell you? **Guessing would get about 5 right. More than 10 is twice that, so people were not just guessing.**. With 4 choices, a guess is right 1 time in 4. 20 ÷ 4 = 5. A score above 10 is more than double what luck gives. So people really do understand many ape gestures. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- In the floating nut puzzle, 24 children of each age were tested. 8 per cent of four-year-olds, 42 per cent of six-year-olds and 58 per cent of eight-year-olds solved it. About how many children is that in each group? What pattern do you see? **About 2, 10 and 14 children. More children solve it as they get older, but even at eight, 10 of the 24 did not.**. 8 per cent of 24 is about 2. 42 per cent of 24 is about 10. 58 per cent of 24 is about 14. The number rises with age. All 5 orangutans solved it, which is 100 per cent. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In a Copycat Test, 3 of 4 people tapped the lid. What fraction is that? Could you now say that three quarters of all people copy useless steps? Why or why not? **Three quarters. No, because 4 people are far too few to speak for everyone.**. 3 out of 4 is three quarters, or 75 per cent. But if one person had acted differently, the result would be 50 or 100 per cent. With so few people, one person changes everything. Scientists test many people before they say what most people do. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Name the great apes. → Orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos and humans.
- Did humans come from chimpanzees? → No. Both descend from common ancestors who were neither.
- What is the difference between using a tool and making one? → Making a tool means changing an object so that it does the job better.
- What is culture? → A group's set of traditions, learned from each other.
- How many chromosomes do humans have, and how many do chimpanzees have? → Humans have 46 and chimpanzees have 48. Two joined into one on the human line.
- What does the mirror test check? → Whether an animal knows the reflection is their own body.
- What did Jane Goodall do that let her see so much? → She watched the same wild chimpanzees patiently for years, and knew each one by name.
- Name India's only ape. → The hoolock gibbon.
