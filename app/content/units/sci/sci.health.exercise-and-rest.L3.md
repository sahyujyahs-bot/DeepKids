# Exercise and rest: why muscles get tired, and what sleep does
`sci.health.exercise-and-rest.L3`

**Summary.** Squeeze a clothes peg again and again, and your fingers slow down and start to ache. Rest for two minutes, and much of their strength comes back. A working muscle uses up its quick store of fuel. Your blood brings oxygen so the muscle can refill that store, and carries waste away. Regular exercise makes muscles, the heart and bones stronger. Yoga, an old Indian practice of poses and slow breathing, keeps the body bendy and steady. Sleep is the longest rest of all. While you sleep, your body grows and repairs itself, and your brain stays busy, sorting what you learned and making dreams. A child of 8 needs about 9 to 12 hours of sleep every night.

**Intro.** *Try this. Hold a full one-litre water bottle in one hand. Stretch your arm straight out to the side, at shoulder height. At first it feels light. Keep holding. After a minute your arm shakes and burns. The bottle did not get any heavier. So what changed?* What changed was inside your arm. The muscles holding the bottle were working hard without a break. In this unit you find out what a muscle uses up when it works, and what rest gives back. You also find out what exercise does to your body over weeks, and what your body and brain do while you sleep.

## Terms
- **Muscle**: A part of the body that moves you by pulling. Muscles are made of long, thin cells joined in bundles. *e.g.* The muscles in your forearm pull on your fingers when you squeeze a clothes peg.
- **Contract**: When a muscle contracts, it gets shorter and fatter, and pulls on a bone. *e.g.* Bend your arm and feel the muscle at the front of your upper arm get hard. It has contracted.
- **Relax**: When a muscle relaxes, it stops pulling and goes soft and long again. *e.g.* Let your arm hang down. The muscle at the front of your upper arm is soft. It has relaxed.
- **Fatigue**: Fatigue is tiredness. A muscle with fatigue works more slowly and more weakly, and may ache. *e.g.* After two minutes of squeezing a peg, your fingers slow down. That is muscle fatigue.
- **Oxygen**: A gas in the air. You breathe it in, and your blood carries it to every part of your body. *e.g.* When you run, you breathe faster to take in more oxygen.
- **Stamina**: How long you can keep going at a hard activity before you need to stop. *e.g.* Skipping with a rope for five minutes without stopping takes stamina.
- **Strength**: How hard your muscles can push or pull. *e.g.* Carrying a bucket of water up the stairs takes strength.
- **Flexibility**: How far your body can bend and stretch without pain. *e.g.* Sitting on the floor with straight legs and touching your toes takes flexibility.
- **Yoga**: An old Indian practice of body poses, slow breathing and paying attention. A yoga pose is called an asana. *e.g.* Standing on one leg in tree pose, called vrikshasana, is yoga.
- **Pranayama**: The breathing part of yoga. You breathe in and out slowly, in a set way. *e.g.* Breathing in for a count of four and out for a count of six is a simple pranayama.
- **Sleep cycle**: During a night, sleep goes round in cycles. Each cycle has light sleep, deep sleep and dreaming sleep, and lasts about one and a half hours in older children and adults. *e.g.* In ten hours of sleep, you go round the cycle about six or seven times.
- **Dreaming sleep (REM sleep)**: The part of sleep when most vivid dreams happen. The eyes dart about under the closed eyelids. REM stands for rapid eye movement. *e.g.* If you wake from dreaming sleep, you can often remember a dream.
- **Spinal disc**: A soft, springy pad between two bones of your backbone. It holds water like a sponge. *e.g.* The discs in your back get slightly squashed during the day and fill up again at night, while you lie down.

## Explanations
### What a working muscle uses up · *defined*
**Definition:** A muscle needs energy to contract. It keeps a small, quick store of fuel inside its cells. To refill that store, the muscle needs oxygen and food, which the blood brings.

When you squeeze a peg fast, your finger muscles contract again and again. Each contraction uses some of the quick fuel store. If you keep going without a break, the muscle uses the fuel faster than the blood can help refill it. Waste also builds up inside the muscle. So the muscle slows down and starts to ache. When you stop, blood keeps flowing through the muscle. It brings oxygen, and the muscle uses it to refill its store. It also carries the waste away. After a short rest the muscle can work hard again. The quick fuel is called phosphocreatine. It was found in 1927 by Cyrus Fiske and Yellapragada Subbarow, who grew up in Andhra Pradesh, and at the same time by Grace and Philip Eggleton in England.
- e.g. Squeezing a peg for two and a half minutes without stopping, your count falls in every round.
- e.g. After a short rest, a fast bowler can run in and bowl hard again.
- e.g. Carrying a heavy bag up three floors, your legs ache by the top. After a minute of standing, they feel better.
### Why muscles get tired: two answers · *debated*
Everyone agrees that a muscle that works hard without rest slows down. Scientists still argue about what causes most of the tiredness. One answer is that it happens inside the muscle. The other answer is that the brain makes you slow down early, to keep you safe. Most scientists now think both happen.
- e.g. In the peg test, the fingers slow down even though you try your hardest.
- e.g. Runners near the end of a race can often sprint the last 100 metres. Their muscles still had fuel left.
- **Most muscle scientists:** Tiredness comes mainly from inside the muscle. The quick fuel store runs low, and waste builds up inside the cells. This makes each contraction weaker.
- **Tim Noakes, a South African sports scientist, and others:** Much of the tiredness is made by the brain. The brain feels the warning signs and makes the body slow down long before the muscle is harmed. That is why people can speed up at the finish line.
### How exercise makes a body stronger · *defined*
**Definition:** Exercise is moving your body hard enough to breathe faster and get warm. Done often, over weeks, it makes muscles stronger, the heart and lungs better at their work, and bones harder.

A muscle that works hard often grows a little bigger and stronger. A heart that is often made to beat fast grows stronger too, so it can pump more blood in each beat. Bones get harder when they carry weight and get jolted, as in running, jumping and climbing. Astronauts in space float, so their bones and muscles carry no weight. They get weaker unless the astronauts exercise for about two hours every day. There are three main kinds of exercise. Stamina exercise, such as skipping, cycling, swimming and kho-kho, works the heart and lungs. Strength exercise, such as climbing, carrying and pushing, works the muscles and bones. Flexibility exercise, such as yoga and stretching, keeps the body bending easily. Many games, such as kabaddi, use all three.
- e.g. Climbing the stairs of your building every day builds stamina and strength.
- e.g. A child who skips every day for a month can usually skip for longer without stopping than at the start.
- e.g. Astronauts on the International Space Station run on a treadmill strapped down with belts, so their bones keep getting jolts.
### Yoga · *defined*
**Definition:** Yoga is an old Indian practice. It has three main parts: poses called asanas, slow breathing called pranayama, and paying close attention to the body and the mind.

The Yoga Sutras of Patanjali is an old book about yoga. It was written in Sanskrit between about 1,600 and 2,200 years ago. Nobody knows the exact date. It says a pose should be steady and comfortable. Many of the yoga poses taught around the world today come from Tirumalai Krishnamacharya, who taught yoga in Mysuru from the 1930s. One of his first women students was Indra Devi. She had been born in Latvia, and she later taught yoga in China, the United States and Argentina. Today, studies find that regular yoga helps people bend further and balance better. Since 2015, 21 June has been the International Day of Yoga.
- e.g. Tree pose, vrikshasana, trains balance.
- e.g. Cobra pose, bhujangasana, bends the back gently backwards.
- e.g. Child's pose, balasana, is a resting pose with the forehead on the floor.
### What happens while you sleep · *defined*
**Definition:** Sleep is a long, regular rest in which you lie still and your senses notice much less. Every night it goes round in cycles of light sleep, deep sleep and dreaming sleep.

In deep sleep, your breathing and heartbeat slow down. This is when the body releases most of its growth hormone, a chemical messenger that helps children grow and helps the body repair itself. In dreaming sleep, also called REM sleep, the brain is nearly as busy as when you are awake. Your eyes dart about under your eyelids, and most vivid dreams happen. Your arm and leg muscles go floppy, so you do not act out your dreams. Sleep also helps you remember. People who sleep after learning something new remember it better the next day than people who stay awake. A child aged 6 to 12 needs about 9 to 12 hours of sleep each night.
- e.g. A baby sleeps for most of the day and night.
- e.g. After a good night's sleep, a dance step practised yesterday often feels easier.
- e.g. A dog asleep may twitch their paws and whimper during dreaming sleep.
### Why do we sleep at all? · *idea*
Nearly every animal that has been studied sleeps or rests in a sleep-like way, even fruit flies and tiny worms. Sleep must matter a great deal, because a sleeping animal is easier to catch. Scientists have several ideas about why sleep is needed, and nobody has the full answer yet. Sleep may give the body time to repair and grow. It may help the brain sort and store memories. It may help the brain clear away waste made during the day. That last idea comes from studies on mice, and scientists are still testing it.
- e.g. Dolphins sleep with one half of their brain at a time, so they can keep swimming up to breathe.
- e.g. Frigatebirds can sleep for a few seconds at a time while they fly over the sea.
- **Scientists who study growth and repair:** Sleep is when the body rebuilds. Growth hormone is released mostly in deep sleep.
- **Scientists who study memory:** Sleep is when the brain replays and stores what was learned during the day.
- **Scientists who study brain cleaning:** During sleep, fluid may wash waste out of the brain. This was seen in mice in 2013, and newer studies disagree about it.
### Why you are taller in the morning · *defined*
**Definition:** Between the bones of your backbone are soft pads called discs. They hold water. Standing and sitting all day squeezes some water out, so you get a little shorter. Lying down at night lets them soak it back up.

Most people are about half a centimetre to one centimetre taller just after waking up than at bedtime. Most of the height is lost in the first hour or two after getting up. Astronauts in space have no weight pressing down on their backs. Their discs fill up and their backbones straighten a little, so they grow up to about 5 centimetres taller. They shrink back after they return to Earth.
- e.g. Measure yourself at bedtime and again just after waking. The morning mark is usually a little higher.
- e.g. A sponge squeezed in your hand gets thinner. Put it in water and it swells again.

## Mostly agreed
- A muscle that works hard without a break gets slower and weaker, and rest lets it recover. *How we know:* Anyone can see it with the peg test at home. Scientists measure it with machines that record muscle force.
- Working muscles need oxygen, which the blood brings. When less blood reaches a muscle, it tires faster. *How we know:* Squeezing a peg with the arm held up, so less blood reaches the hand, tires the fingers faster. Lab studies that squeeze the blood supply with a cuff show the same.
- Regular exercise makes muscles, bones, the heart and the lungs stronger. *How we know:* Studies follow children and adults for months and measure their fitness and bone strength. Astronauts lose bone and muscle without exercise.
- Children aged 6 to 12 need about 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night. *How we know:* In 2016, a group of sleep doctors in the United States studied hundreds of research papers and agreed on these numbers.
- The brain is very busy during sleep, especially in dreaming sleep. *How we know:* Machines that record the brain's electrical signals show it. This was first seen clearly in 1953.

## How we found out
**Eugene Aserinsky, a student, and Nathaniel Kleitman, a sleep scientist, at the University of Chicago, with Aserinsky's son Armond as the first sleeper, 1951 to 1953 (published in the journal Science in 1953).** For a long time, many scientists thought sleep was the brain switching off, like a lamp at night. In 1951 Eugene Aserinsky was a student at the University of Chicago, working for the sleep scientist Nathaniel Kleitman. Aserinsky used a machine that recorded the brain's tiny electrical signals, and the movements of the eyes, with wires taped to the head. His first sleeper was his own son, Armond, who was 8 years old. All night the pens of the machine scratched lines on paper. Several times in the night, the lines went wild. Armond's eyes were darting about under his closed lids, and his brain signals looked almost like those of someone awake. At first Aserinsky thought the old machine was broken. But it happened again and again, in other sleepers too, at regular times each night. When sleepers were woken during these times, they usually said they had been dreaming. In 1953 Aserinsky and Kleitman published what they found. They had discovered dreaming sleep, called REM sleep. The brain does not switch off at night.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought the brain switches off during sleep, like a lamp. The recordings showed that several times every night the brain becomes almost as active as when awake, and that this is when most vivid dreams happen.

## Challenging perspectives
- **The ache the day after hard exercise is caused by lactic acid left in the muscles.** (A very common idea, among sports coaches and parents; *rejected*)  
  For: Working muscles do make lactic acid, and lactic acid was for a long time blamed for tiredness.  
  Against: Lactic acid is cleared from the muscles within about an hour after exercise. The ache the next day comes from tiny injuries in the muscle, which heal and make it stronger.
- **Most tiredness during exercise is made by the brain, not by the muscle.** (Tim Noakes, a South African sports scientist, and others; *debated*)  
  For: People can often speed up at the end of a race, so their muscles had more left.  
  Against: The peg test shows the muscle itself slowing down. Lab tests show the muscle's fuel store falling.
- **You can make up for lost sleep in the week by sleeping late on Sunday.** (A common idea; *debated*)  
  For: Sleeping longer after a short night does help you feel less sleepy.  
  Against: Studies find that one long sleep does not undo all the effects of a week of short nights. A steady bedtime works better.
- **Resting is a waste of time.** (A common feeling, among busy children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: While you rest, you are not getting anything visible done.  
  Against: In the peg test, two minutes of rest brought back much of the strength. In deep sleep, the body releases growth hormone. Rest is when the body refills, repairs and grows.

## Roots
We have learned how muscles get tired and recover, what exercise builds and what happens in sleep. Now we ask what rest tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In the peg test, your squeezes slowed each round you did not rest. After two minutes of rest, your count went up again. In those two minutes, your blood brought oxygen and helped the muscle refill its fuel. So a resting muscle is busy, getting ready to work again.  
*Habit it breaks:* We split the day into working and resting, and think of resting as doing nothing. But the peg test shows that strength comes back during rest, because the body is busy refilling and clearing the muscle.  
*Evidence:* The peg count fell from round to round without a break, then rose after two minutes of rest. With the arm held up, so less blood reached the hand, the fingers tired faster.

- *who am i:* You are a body that works and rests in turns, all day and all night. Even your heart rests for a moment between beats.
- *what is life:* Every living thing has times of activity and times of rest. A tamarind tree folds their leaves at night, and a dog sleeps and dreams.
- *what is this world:* The Earth turns from day to night, and the sea comes in and goes out. Much of the world moves in rhythms of more and less.

The peg test showed a rhythm: work uses the fuel up, and rest fills it again. The same rhythm runs through many things. Your heart contracts and then relaxes, every second of your life. Your lungs fill and empty. The Earth turns its face to the Sun and away again. A tamarind tree folds their leaves at night. Your whole body sleeps and wakes. One rhythm of using and refilling shows up in muscles, hearts, trees and planets.

- **Bhagavad Gita 6.16–6.17:** Yoga is not for someone who eats too much or too little, or sleeps too much or too little. It is for someone who keeps a balance in eating, playing, working, sleeping and waking. (The peg test and the sleep diary both show that the body needs a balance of work and rest, not only one of them.)
- **Yoga Sutras of Patanjali 2.46:** A pose should be steady and comfortable. (In tree pose, your muscles work and relax in tiny amounts, all the time, to keep you steady. Steadiness itself needs a balance of effort and ease.)
- **Mandukya Upanishad, verses 3 to 7:** There are four states of awareness: waking, dreaming, deep sleep, and a fourth that is aware of all three. (Sleep scientists also find different states in one night: light sleep, deep sleep and dreaming sleep. The Upanishad asks who is aware through all of them.)
- Science (settled): After hard work, a muscle refills most of its quick fuel within a few minutes, using oxygen brought by the blood.
- Science (settled): The brain is very active in dreaming sleep, and sleep helps people remember what they learned.
- Science (open): Why animals need sleep at all has no full answer yet.
- Other view, Tim Noakes, a South African sports scientist, and others: Much of what we feel as tiredness is made by the brain, which slows us down early to keep us safe. So the peg test may show the brain's caution as much as the muscle's empty store.
- Practice, Feel the rest arrive (4 min): Let's lie down flat on our backs, with our arms by our sides. Close your eyes. Squeeze both fists as tight as you can and count to five. Now let go. Feel your hands go soft and heavy. Now squeeze your toes and count to five. Let go. Now breathe slowly, in for four counts and out for six counts. Do this five times. Notice the warmth in your hands. Your blood is flowing there right now, even though you are doing nothing.
- Ask: When you rest, your body keeps refilling, repairing and growing. Is resting a kind of work?
- Ask: In a dream, you can run and fly while your body lies still. Which one is really you?
- Ask: Your heart rests between every beat, all your life. What else in the world works in turns like that?

## Story: The Night Before Pongal
*Lakshmi, her family and her dance teacher are made up. What the story shows is true: tired muscles need rest to work well, and sleep helps people remember a new skill.*

Lakshmi was nine, and she lived on the fourth floor of a block of flats in Chennai. In three days, her dance class would dance at the Pongal function in the building's courtyard. Lakshmi had a new part, right at the front. It was a quick, stamping step called a jathi, and she could not get it right.

'Feet together, then out, stamp, stamp, turn,' said Revathi Miss at the evening class. 'Your feet must land exactly on the beat, Lakshmi. Practise slowly at home. Then sleep well.'

Lakshmi did not practise slowly. She practised fast, and she practised a lot. After dinner she stamped up and down the hall until the downstairs aunty knocked on the ceiling with a broom. Then she practised on the soft rug in her room, where the stamping made no sound. Her little brother Arjun sat on the bed and copied her with his hands.

At ten o'clock, Amma looked in. 'Lights off, Lakshmi.'

'Five more minutes,' said Lakshmi.

At eleven, Amma looked in again. Lakshmi was still stamping. Her legs were shaking now. The front of her thighs burned. Every time she tried the turn, she wobbled. The more she practised, the worse it got.

'I am getting worse,' said Lakshmi, and she felt like crying. 'I practised for two hours, and I am getting worse.'

Paati, her grandmother, was sitting in the doorway, folding clothes. She was seventy-two, and she still did yoga every morning on the balcony.

'Come here,' said Paati. 'Squeeze my hand as hard as you can.' Lakshmi squeezed. 'Again. Again. Faster.' After twenty squeezes, Lakshmi's hand was slow and aching. 'Now put your hand on your knee and wait,' said Paati. She folded two more saris. Then she held out her hand. 'Squeeze.' Lakshmi squeezed, and this time it was strong again.

'Your hand did nothing for two minutes,' said Paati. 'So why is it strong again?'

Lakshmi frowned. 'Because it rested?'

'Because while it rested, it was busy,' said Paati. 'Your blood was flowing through it all the time, filling it up again. Your legs are like your hand. They are empty now. Stamping more will not fill them.'

'But I still don't know the step,' said Lakshmi.

'Then go to sleep,' said Paati. 'While you sleep, your brain goes over what you practised today. Many times, I have learned a hard pose in the evening and found it easier in the morning.'

Lakshmi did not believe her. But she was too tired to argue. She lay down. She thought she would lie awake worrying. The next thing she knew, the sun was coming through the window and the crows were calling.

She stood up. Her legs felt a little stiff, but they were not shaking any more. She went to the rug, slowly, and tried the step. Feet together, then out, stamp, stamp, turn. Her feet knew where to go. She did it again, a little faster. She did not wobble on the turn.

'Paati!' she shouted. 'It's in my feet!'

Arjun woke up and came to look. 'Teach me,' he said.

So Lakshmi taught him, slowly, the way Revathi Miss had said. Feet together, then out, stamp, stamp, turn. 'Not fast,' she told him. 'Get it right slowly. Then stop and rest. Then sleep on it.'

That evening at class, Revathi Miss watched Lakshmi do the step three times. 'What happened?' she asked. 'Yesterday your feet were everywhere.'

'I slept,' said Lakshmi.

Revathi Miss laughed. 'That is the hardest practice of all for some people.'

On Pongal day, the courtyard was full. The pongal pot bubbled over, and everyone shouted 'Pongalo pongal!' Then the dancers came out. Lakshmi was at the front. Her feet landed exactly on the beat. In the second row of the audience, Arjun was doing the step with his hands, and Paati was keeping time with her fingers on her knee.

That night, Lakshmi went to bed at nine o'clock without being asked. Amma was so surprised that she checked whether Lakshmi had a fever.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Lakshmi's legs were tired because her muscles had used up their quick fuel faster than her blood could help refill it. More practice in that state only made her wobble more. When Paati made her squeeze, rest and squeeze again, Lakshmi felt the strength come back after two minutes of rest. Sleep did two jobs. Her muscles recovered, and her brain went over the new movement, so the step felt easier in the morning. Studies find that sleeping after practising a new movement helps people do it better the next day.

- Ask: Why did Lakshmi get worse the longer she practised?
- Ask: Why was Lakshmi's hand strong again after resting on her knee for two minutes?
- Ask: What did Paati say Lakshmi's brain would do while she slept?
- Ask: Lakshmi taught Arjun to practise slowly, then rest, then sleep. Why is that good advice?
- Ask: Paati is seventy-two and still does yoga every morning. What do you think yoga gives her?

  *Child mode:* Put your hand on your upper arm. Bend your elbow hard. Feel it go hard. Now let go. Feel it go soft.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play through the cards. Mix in a few calls without 'Muscle says'.  
  *Parent says:* Muscle says, stand on tiptoe! Is your calf hard or soft?  
  *Child mode:* Listen for 'Muscle says'. Do it, and feel the muscle. Say hard or soft.
  - Check: When you stand on tiptoe, what does your calf muscle do? → **It contracts and gets hard**
- **s3 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* End by asking about pairs.  
  *Parent says:* When you bend your arm, the front muscle contracts. What does the back of your arm do?  
  *Child mode:* Bend your arm. The front muscle is hard. Feel the back of your arm. Is it hard or soft?
  - Note: The muscle at the back of the upper arm relaxes when the arm bends, and contracts to straighten it. Muscles work in pairs, because each one can only pull.
  - Think: Muscles can only pull, never push. So how does your arm straighten again?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Tired Peg: does rest bring back strength? (30 min)
- **question:** If you squeeze a clothes peg again and again without a break, what happens to how many squeezes you can do? And what happens after a short rest?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will squeeze a peg for five rounds of 30 seconds with no break, then rest for two minutes, then do one more round. Will your count stay the same, go up or go down each round? What will happen after the rest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same peg, the same hand and the same two fingers every round. Count only full squeezes, where the two handle ends of the peg touch. The same person times and counts every round. Then a grown-up does the whole test too, because one person's result can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Plastic pegs can snap, and a spring can pinch. If the fingers really hurt, with a sharp pain rather than an ache, stop at once.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw one bar for each round, as tall as the number of squeezes.
- **results table:** Round | Rest before this round | Number of squeezes in 30 seconds | How my fingers feel
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the peg. 2. Show a full squeeze: pinch the two handle ends with the thumb and first finger until they touch. 3. Let your child try three squeezes.  
  *Parent says:* This is our test machine, a clothes peg. A full squeeze is when the two ends touch. Try three.  
  *Child mode:* Hold the peg between your thumb and first finger. Squeeze until the two ends touch. That is one squeeze. Try three.
  - Note: Pick a peg with a firm spring. A very soft peg is too easy, and the fingers may not tire. If a peg feels too easy, use two pegs held together.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the plan: five rounds of 30 seconds with no break, then two minutes of rest, then round 6. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write the guess at the top of the table.  
  *Parent says:* You will squeeze as fast as you can for 30 seconds, five rounds in a row with no break. Then you rest for two minutes. Then one more round. What do you think will happen to your count? Why?  
  *Child mode:* You will squeeze for five rounds with no break. Then rest. Then one more round. What will happen to your count? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen to your count each round, and why? (good: It will go down, because my fingers will get tired.; It will go up, because I will get faster with practice.; After the rest it will go up again, because my fingers will have rested.)
  - Note: Any guess with a reason is good. Do not say what you expect. Write the guess down so you can compare it with the result.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child sits with the hand resting on the table. 2. Say 'go' and start the timer. 3. Count every full squeeze out loud, quietly. 4. Say 'stop' at 30 seconds and write the number.  
  *Parent says:* Round 1. Ready, go! Squeeze as fast as you can.  
  *Child mode:* Round 1. When your helper says go, squeeze as fast as you can. Stop when they say stop.
  - Note: Counting under your breath works better than making your child count, so they can put all their effort into squeezing. Most children of 8 manage somewhere between 30 and 70 squeezes in the first round.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Go straight on to round 2, with no break. Write the number in 2 or 3 seconds. 2. Do rounds 3, 4 and 5 the same way. 3. After each round, ask how the fingers feel, and write one word.  
  *Parent says:* No break! Round 2, go! Keep squeezing as hard as you can. How do your fingers feel now?  
  *Child mode:* No break. Keep going, round after round. Squeeze as fast as you can. How do your fingers feel?
  - Note: The count usually falls from round to round, and the child says the hand burns, aches or feels slow. If your child slows down on purpose to save strength, remind them to go as fast as they can every round. The test only works if every round is full effort.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child lays the hand on the table and rests for two minutes. 2. Then do round 6, timed the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now rest for two minutes. Let your hand lie still on the table. Then round 6.  
  *Child mode:* Rest your hand on the table. Wait two minutes. Then do round 6, as fast as you can.
  - Check: Riya, a girl in Class 3, did five rounds with no break. Her counts were 50, 44, 39, 35 and 31. After two minutes of rest, what do you think round 6 was? → **About 45, because the rest let her fingers recover**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up does the same six rounds while the child times and counts. 2. Compare the pattern of the two sets of numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Now it's my turn, and you are the timer and counter. Let's see if my numbers make the same pattern.  
  *Child mode:* Now you are the timer. Your grown-up squeezes. Count their squeezes. Is their pattern like yours?
  - Note: The grown-up's numbers may be bigger or smaller. What matters is the pattern: falling with no rest, then rising after the rest. If both of you show it, the result is less likely to be a fluke.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child draws a bar chart, one bar per round. 2. Then ask for three sentences: what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a bar for each round. Then tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a bar for each round. Then say three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a muscle gets tired when it works without a break, and rest brings its strength back. What I saw: my count went from 48 to 29 in five rounds, then up to 43 after resting. Why: the muscle used up its fuel, and while I rested my blood helped refill it. Now read the card 'What a working muscle uses up' together.
### e2 · Arm Up, Arm Down: does blood help a muscle keep going? (25 min)
- **question:** Do your fingers tire faster when your hand is held up above your head, where less blood reaches it?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will squeeze a peg for one minute with your arm held straight up. Later, after a long rest, you will squeeze for one minute with your arm hanging down. Which way will give more squeezes? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same peg, the same hand and the same counter both times. Rest 10 minutes between the two tests, so the fingers start fresh. Do the arm-up test first. That way, if arm-down still wins, it cannot be because you had more practice the second time.
- **safety:** Plastic pegs can snap, and a spring can pinch. If the fingers really hurt, with a sharp pain rather than an ache, stop at once. Holding the arm up is tiring for the shoulder too, so rest the back of the arm against a wall.
- **record:** Fill in the table, and draw two bars, one for each arm position.
- **results table:** Arm position | Squeezes in the first 30 seconds | Squeezes in the second 30 seconds | Total | When did the ache start?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to hold one hand above the head for one minute, and the other hanging down. 2. Then look at the two hands side by side.  
  *Parent says:* Hold one hand up high and let the other hang down, for one minute. Now put them side by side. What do you see?  
  *Child mode:* Hold one hand up high. Let the other hang down. Wait one minute. Now look at both hands together. What is different?
  - Ask: Why is the raised hand paler? (good: Less blood is in it.; Blood has to go uphill to reach it, so less gets there.)
  - Note: The raised hand usually looks paler and the hanging hand looks pinker, with fatter veins. Blood has to be pumped uphill to reach a raised hand, so less of it flows there.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the test. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* First, you squeeze with your arm up for one minute. Then a long rest. Then one minute with your arm down. Which will give more squeezes? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Arm up first. Then rest. Then arm down. Which will give more squeezes? Why do you think so?
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands with their back to a wall and raises the squeezing arm straight up, resting it against the wall. 2. Time one minute. Write the count at 30 seconds and at 60 seconds. 3. Ask your child to say 'now' when the ache starts, and write the time.  
  *Parent says:* Arm up against the wall. Ready, go! Squeeze as fast as you can. Tell me when it starts to ache.  
  *Child mode:* Lean your back on the wall. Raise your arm straight up. Squeeze fast for one minute. Say 'now' when it starts to ache.
  - Note: Count quietly. At 30 seconds, note the count without stopping. Most children feel the ache sooner with the arm up.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Rest for 10 minutes. Read the Pongal story, or play a game. 2. Then do one minute with the arm hanging down at the side, timed and counted the same way.  
  *Parent says:* Now a long rest, so your fingers start fresh. Then the same test with your arm hanging down.  
  *Child mode:* Rest for 10 minutes. Then do it again with your arm hanging down. Squeeze fast for one minute. Say 'now' when it aches.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Compare the totals and the ache times. 2. Ask for what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* Which way gave more squeezes? When did the ache start each time? Tell me what you think, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Which way gave more squeezes? Which started aching sooner? Say what you think, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: Usually the arm-down count is higher and the ache comes later. If your child's results are the other way round, ask what might have been different, such as tired fingers from earlier or counting mistakes. Try again another day.
  - Check: Why do fingers usually tire faster when the arm is held up? → **Less blood reaches the hand, so less oxygen arrives to refill the muscle**
### e3 · Taller in the Morning? A bedtime and wake-up measure (25 min, 2 days)
- **question:** Is your height the same at bedtime and just after you wake up?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will you be taller, shorter or the same height in the morning as at bedtime? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Measure at the same spot on the same wall, barefoot, standing the same way, with the same book and the same tape. Measure three times each time, because one mark can be a little off. In the morning, measure as soon as you get up, before walking about much.
- **safety:** None, but a grown-up should hold the book, so it does not slip onto your child's face.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Work out how many millimetres taller or shorter you were in the morning.
- **results table:** When | Mark 1 (cm) | Mark 2 (cm) | Mark 3 (cm) | Middle mark (cm)
- **schedule:** day 1: At bedtime: measure your height three times against the wall and write the numbers. (12 min); day 2: As soon as you wake up: measure three times again in the same way. Compare. (13 min)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. At bedtime, ask the question. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* Do you think you are the same height at bedtime as in the morning? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Are you the same height at night and in the morning? Guess. Why do you think so?
- **s2 (9 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child stands barefoot with heels, bottom and shoulders touching the wall, looking straight ahead. 2. Lay the book flat on the head, pressed against the wall. 3. Draw a thin line under the book. 4. Step away and measure from the floor to the line. 5. Do it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Heels, bottom and shoulders on the wall. Look straight ahead. I'll put the book on your head and draw a line.  
  *Child mode:* Stand with your heels, bottom and shoulders on the wall. Look straight ahead. Stand still while your grown-up marks the wall.
  - Note: Press the book down gently so it rests on the top of the head, not on hair. Use the middle of the three numbers. A tailor's tape is fine. Read it to the nearest millimetre, the small lines between the centimetres.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. In the morning, measure as soon as your child gets out of bed, in the same way, three times. 2. Write the numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Good morning! Before anything else, let's measure you, the same way as last night.  
  *Child mode:* Good morning! Go straight to the wall. Stand the same way as last night. Your grown-up measures you three times.
  - Note: Most people are taller in the morning, often by about half a centimetre to one centimetre. If the difference is only 1 or 2 millimetres, it could be a measuring mistake. Measure a grown-up too: adults often show a bigger change.
  - Wait: Do this step the next morning, as soon as your child wakes up.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Work out the difference. 2. Ask for what I think, what I saw, why.  
  *Parent says:* How much taller or shorter were you this morning? Tell me what you think, what you saw, and why.  
  *Child mode:* Take away the night number from the morning number. That is the difference. What do you think made it?
  - Check: Why are most people a little taller in the morning? → **The soft discs in the backbone soak up water while you lie down, and get squashed again during the day**

### c1 · The Monsoon Workout: exercise in a small flat (30 min)
- **problem:** It has rained for a week, and nobody can go out to play. Design a 10-minute workout for a small flat that builds stamina, strength and flexibility, without disturbing the neighbours downstairs.
- **your turn:** Now plan your own monsoon workout. Write the moves and the rest times on a card. Do it with your family, and time it. Did it work your breath, your muscles and your stretch?
- **constraints:** It must fit in a space the size of a single bed.; No jumping or stamping, because the neighbours below will hear.; It must include at least one stamina move, one strength move and one stretch.; It must last 10 minutes, with short rests.
- **general:** March fast on the spot, or dance on the spot, for stamina.; Wall push-ups, or sitting against a wall as if on a chair, for strength.; Cobra pose and a toe-touch stretch for flexibility.; Work for one minute, then rest for 30 seconds, then the next move.
- **creative:** Make a dice where each number is a move, and roll for the order.; Climb the building stairs slowly, one floor at a time, if your building has stairs.; Make it a dance to your favourite song, with each verse a different kind of move.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem and the rules together.  
  *Parent says:* It's been raining for a week. Plan a 10-minute workout for our flat, with no jumping.  
  *Child mode:* Rain all week! Plan a 10-minute workout in a small space. No jumping. Include stamina, strength and stretch.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child writes the plan: each move, how long, and the rests.  
  *Parent says:* Write each move, how long it lasts, and where the rests go. Why do you need rests?  
  *Child mode:* Write your moves. How long is each one? Put in rests. Why do you need them?
  - Ask: Why put rests between the moves? (good: So the muscles can recover, like in the peg test.; So the blood can refill the muscles before the next move.)
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the workout together, timed. Then talk about what to change.  
  *Parent says:* Let's do it! Afterwards, tell me what worked and what you would change.  
  *Child mode:* Do your workout with your family. Time it. What would you change next time?
### c2 · The Sleep Den: help someone fall asleep in a noisy city (30 min)
- **problem:** Kavya, a girl in Class 3, lives beside a busy road in Hyderabad. A street light shines into her room, autos honk late at night, and her room gets hot. She keeps waking up. Design changes to her room and her bedtime that would help her sleep through the night.
- **your turn:** Now design your own sleep den for Kavya. Build it as a model in a shoebox, or draw it. Then write a bedtime routine for her with five steps.
- **constraints:** You may only use things that most homes have, or that are cheap to buy.; The family cannot move house or change rooms.; The plan must deal with light, noise and heat.
- **general:** Hang a dark curtain or a thick bedsheet over the window to block the street light.; Use a fan, which also covers small noises with a steady hum.; Stop screens an hour before bed, because bright screens make it harder to feel sleepy.; Go to bed and wake up at the same time every day.
- **creative:** Put the head of the bed on the wall furthest from the road.; Fold a soft cloth into an eye mask.; Make a bedtime routine card with pictures: bath, brush, story, lights out.; Open the window on the far side of the flat in the evening to let cooler air through.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read Kavya's problem together. List the three troubles: light, noise and heat.  
  *Parent says:* What are the three things keeping Kavya awake? Let's list them.  
  *Child mode:* Read about Kavya. What keeps her awake? Find three things.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child builds a model room in a shoebox, or draws one, showing a fix for each trouble.  
  *Parent says:* Build or draw Kavya's room. Show how you stop the light, the noise and the heat.  
  *Child mode:* Make Kavya's room in a shoebox, or draw it. Fix the light. Fix the noise. Fix the heat.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write a five-step bedtime routine for Kavya, and test one idea in your own room tonight.  
  *Parent says:* Now write Kavya a bedtime routine. Which idea will you try in your own room tonight?  
  *Child mode:* Write five bedtime steps for Kavya. Pick one idea to try in your room tonight.
  - Note: Bright light in the evening, especially from screens, delays the body's sleep signal, a hormone called melatonin. A dark, cool, quiet room and a steady bedtime help most children sleep better.
  - Think: Why do you think bright light at night makes it harder to fall asleep?

### Tired, Rested, Strong (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) When does a muscle get tired fastest?  
   Options: When it works hard without any rest / When it works a little and then rests / When it is asleep  
   **Answer:** When it works hard without any rest. *Why:* Working hard without rest uses up the muscle's quick fuel faster than blood can help refill it.
2. (mcq) Which word means a muscle getting shorter and pulling?  
   Options: Relax / Contract / Stretch  
   **Answer:** Contract. *Why:* A muscle contracts to pull. When it stops pulling and goes soft, it relaxes.
3. (mcq) Why do you breathe faster when you run?  
   Options: To take in more oxygen for your working muscles / To cool your teeth / To make your voice louder  
   **Answer:** To take in more oxygen for your working muscles. *Why:* Working muscles need more oxygen, so you breathe faster, and your heart pumps faster to carry it to them.
4. (mcq) Which of these is a flexibility exercise?  
   Options: Cobra pose / Running up the stairs / Carrying a bag of rice  
   **Answer:** Cobra pose. *Why:* Cobra pose bends the back gently and stretches the front of the body. Running builds stamina, and carrying builds strength.
5. (mcq) Which helps a child sleep well?  
   Options: A dark, quiet, cool room / A bright phone screen in bed / A big, sugary drink at bedtime  
   **Answer:** A dark, quiet, cool room. *Why:* Darkness, quiet and coolness help the body feel sleepy. Bright screens delay the body's sleep signal.
6. (truefalse) The brain switches off completely while you sleep.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The brain stays busy, especially in dreaming sleep, when it is nearly as active as when you are awake.
7. (truefalse) Astronauts must exercise every day in space to keep their bones and muscles strong.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In space, bones and muscles carry no weight, so they get weaker unless astronauts exercise for about two hours a day.
8. (fill) The breathing part of yoga is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pranayama']. *Why:* Pranayama means slow breathing in a set way, such as the bee breath.
9. (fill) Most vivid dreams happen in ____ sleep, also called REM sleep.  
   **Answer:** ['dreaming']. *Why:* REM stands for rapid eye movement, because the eyes dart about in this part of sleep.
10. (match) Match each exercise to the kind it mostly builds.  
   **Answer:** Skipping for five minutes → stamina; Wall push-ups → strength; Touching your toes → flexibility; Tree pose → balance. *Why:* Stamina keeps you going, strength pushes and pulls hard, flexibility bends far, and balance keeps you steady.
11. (sort) Sort these into what helps your body rest and what makes it work.  
   **Answer:** sleeping → Rest; child's pose → Rest; slow breathing → Rest; kabaddi → Work; climbing stairs → Work; skipping → Work. *Why:* Rest gives the body time to refill and repair. Work uses up its fuel.

## Open questions
- **Why do animals need to sleep at all?** Sleep may help repair the body, store memories and clean the brain, but nobody knows which of these is the main reason, or whether there is one.
- **Why do we dream?** Some scientists think dreams help the brain sort memories and feelings. Others think they are a side effect of a busy brain. Nobody can test it fully yet.
- **How much of tiredness comes from the muscle, and how much from the brain?** Both seem to play a part, and scientists still argue about how much each matters.
- **Why is yawning catching?** Seeing someone yawn makes many people yawn too, and some animals do it. Scientists have several ideas about why, and none is agreed.

She got back 14 of the 19 squeezes she had lost.**. She lost 48 − 29 = 19 squeezes. The rest brought her up 43 − 29 = 14. 19 − 14 = 5, so she was still 5 short of round 1. A longer rest would probably bring back the rest. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Kavya goes to sleep at 9 at night and wakes up at 6:30 in the morning. How long did she sleep? Is it enough for a child of 8? **9 and a half hours. Yes, it is within the 9 to 12 hours a child of 8 needs.**. From 9 at night to midnight is 3 hours. From midnight to 6 in the morning is 6 hours. Then 30 more minutes to 6:30. 3 + 6 = 9 hours, plus half an hour. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- In a 24-hour day, a child sleeps for 10 hours. How many hours are left for everything else? **14 hours.**. 24 − 10 = 14. A day has 24 hours, and 10 of them are used for sleep. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Lakshmi measured her height at bedtime: 128.3 cm. In the morning she measured 129.0 cm. How many millimetres taller was she in the morning? **7 millimetres.**. 129.0 − 128.3 = 0.7 centimetres. One centimetre is 10 millimetres, so 0.7 centimetres is 7 millimetres. (queues `math.length.standard-units-length.L3`)

Did both show the same pattern? **Yes. Both did more squeezes with the arm down: Divya 19 more, her brother 20 more.**. 81 − 62 = 19. 90 − 70 = 20. The numbers are different, but both did better with the arm down by about the same amount. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
