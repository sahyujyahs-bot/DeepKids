# Cloth: how thread is woven, knitted and stitched, and how old cloth is used again
`sci.making-things.cloth.L5`

**Summary.** Pull a T-shirt sideways and it stretches. Pull a handkerchief and it hardly moves, though both may be cotton. The difference is in how the thread is arranged: a handkerchief is woven from crossing threads, and a T-shirt is knitted from one thread in loops. Your child measures that stretch, finds where it comes from, and tests how small a stitch must be to hold mustard seeds. They also meet the baya weaver and the tailorbird, the silk moth, and the weavers, dyers and embroiderers of many parts of India. Last, they find out how old cloth is mended, remade and recycled.

**Intro.** *Take a cotton T-shirt and a cotton handkerchief. Pull each one sideways. The T-shirt stretches a lot, and the handkerchief hardly at all. Both are made of cotton thread. So why does only one of them stretch?* The answer is not in the cotton. It is in the path the thread takes. In the handkerchief, hundreds of threads cross each other, over and under. This is weaving. In the T-shirt, one long thread is pulled into row after row of loops. This is knitting. Humans are not the only animals who do this kind of work. A male baya weaver knots and weaves a hanging nest from strips of grass, using only his beak and feet. A tailorbird stitches a leaf into a cup with plant fibre. In this unit you take cloth apart to see how it is made, and you make and test some yourself.

## Terms
- **Fibre**: A very thin strand, like a hair. Cloth begins as fibres from a plant, an animal or a factory. *e.g.* Pull a lamp wick apart and you find cotton fibres, each about 2 or 3 centimetres long.
- **Yarn**: A long thread made by twisting many fibres together. Sewing thread, knitting wool and the threads in cloth are all yarns. *e.g.* One yarn pulled out of a towel is many times longer than any fibre in it.
- **Spinning**: Drawing fibres out and twisting them into yarn. The twist presses the fibres together, and friction stops them sliding apart. *e.g.* A charkha is a spinning wheel. Its spindle turns fast and twists cotton into yarn.
- **Ply**: One of the thinner yarns that are twisted together to make a thicker, steadier yarn. *e.g.* Untwist a piece of sewing thread and it opens into 2 or 3 plies.
- **Staple fibre and filament**: A staple fibre is short, like cotton or wool, and must be spun. A filament is one very long fibre, like silk or nylon. *e.g.* One cotton fibre is about 3 centimetres long. The filament from one silk cocoon can be many hundreds of metres long.
- **Weaving**: Making cloth by crossing two sets of threads, over and under each other, at right angles. *e.g.* A handkerchief, a bedsheet, a sari and a gunny sack are all woven.
- **Warp**: The threads that run along the length of a woven cloth. On the loom they are stretched tight before weaving starts. *e.g.* On the Chindi Coaster loom, the string wound up and down the card is the warp.
- **Weft**: The thread that runs across a woven cloth. The weaver passes it over and under the warp threads, row after row. *e.g.* On the Chindi Coaster loom, the cloth strips you weave from side to side are the weft.
- **Loom**: A frame or machine that holds the warp threads tight, so that the weft can be woven through them. *e.g.* A handloom is worked by a person's hands and feet. A power loom is driven by an electric motor.
- **Selvedge**: The firm side edge of a woven cloth, where the weft thread turns back. It has no cut ends, so it does not fray. *e.g.* The long edges of a sari, a dupatta or a thorthu towel are selvedges.
- **Knitting**: Making cloth from one long yarn by pulling it into rows of loops. Each loop hangs from another loop. *e.g.* T-shirts, banians, socks and sweaters are knitted.
- **Fray**: To come apart at a cut edge, as loose threads slide out of a woven cloth. *e.g.* The cut edge of a torn dupatta soon grows a fringe of loose threads.
- **Seam**: A line of stitches that joins two pieces of cloth. *e.g.* Turn a shirt inside out and you can see the seams along the sides and around the sleeves.
- **Hem**: An edge of cloth folded over and stitched down. It hides the cut ends, so the edge does not fray. *e.g.* The bottom edge of a school skirt, a kurta or a curtain is hemmed.
- **Running stitch**: The simplest stitch. The needle goes up and down through the cloth along a line, leaving a dashed line of thread. *e.g.* Kantha quilts are covered with rows of running stitch through layers of old cloth.
- **Backstitch**: A stitch in which each new stitch goes back to meet the end of the last one, so the thread makes one unbroken line. *e.g.* A backstitch seam is stronger than a running stitch seam, and uses about three times as much thread.
- **Natural fibre**: A fibre made by a living being. Cotton, jute, coir and linen come from plants. Wool and silk come from animals. *e.g.* A cotton kurta, a jute bag, a coir doormat and a woollen shawl are made of natural fibres.
- **Synthetic fibre**: A fibre made in a factory from chemicals, mostly from petroleum. Nylon, polyester and acrylic are synthetic fibres. *e.g.* Most school bags, raincoats and sports jerseys are made of polyester or nylon.
- **Blend**: A yarn or cloth made of two or more kinds of fibre mixed together. *e.g.* A school shirt whose label says '65% polyester, 35% cotton' is a blend.
- **Cocoon**: The case that a caterpillar spins around their own body before changing into a moth. A silk moth's cocoon is one long silk filament. *e.g.* A mulberry silk cocoon is about the size of a groundnut shell.
- **Khadi**: Cloth that is spun by hand and woven by hand. *e.g.* During the freedom struggle, Gandhi asked people to spin on the charkha and wear khadi.
- **Ikat**: A way of making a pattern by tying and dyeing the threads before they are woven. The pattern has soft, feathered edges. *e.g.* Pochampally in Telangana, Patan in Gujarat and Sambalpur in Odisha are known for ikat.
- **Embroidery**: Decorating cloth with stitches of thread after the cloth has been woven. *e.g.* Toda women of the Nilgiris embroider red and black patterns on cream cotton shawls.
- **Microfibre**: A tiny piece of fibre, too small to see easily, that breaks off cloth when it is worn and washed. *e.g.* Scientists have found microfibres of polyester in beach sand on six continents.

## Explanations
### From fibre to yarn: spinning and plies · *defined*
**Definition:** A fibre is a very thin strand. Spinning draws fibres out and twists them together into a long yarn.

A cotton fibre is only about 2 or 3 centimetres long. A yarn can be kilometres long, because the fibres overlap and the twist presses them together. Friction between the pressed fibres stops them sliding apart. Short fibres such as cotton, wool and jute are called staple fibres, and they must be spun. Silk, nylon and polyester come as filaments, which are single fibres of great length, and they need only a light twist. A single spun yarn tends to untwist and curl. So two or three single yarns are twisted together in the opposite direction. Each one is called a ply. The plies try to untwist one way and the whole yarn tries to untwist the other way, so the yarn stays still. A takli is a small hand spindle. A charkha turns the spindle with a wheel, so one hand turns and the other draws out the cotton. A spinning mill does the same job on thousands of spindles at once.
- e.g. In Take a Thread Apart, a piece of thread opens into 2 or 3 plies, and each ply opens into short fibres.
- e.g. A cotton lamp wick is fibres with very little twist, so it pulls apart easily. Kite string is tightly twisted, so it does not.
- e.g. A coir rope from Kerala is made of two or three twisted strands of coconut fibre, twisted together the other way.
### Weaving: warp, weft and the loom · *defined*
**Definition:** Weaving makes cloth by crossing two sets of threads at right angles. The warp threads run along the length of the cloth. The weft thread runs across, over and under the warp.

A loom does four jobs. It holds the warp threads tight. It lifts some of them, so a gap opens between the lifted threads and the rest. A shuttle carries the weft thread through that gap. Then a comb-like bar pushes the new weft thread tight against the cloth. In plain weave the weft goes over one warp thread and under the next. It has the most crossings, so it is firm. In twill the weft goes over two or three threads and under one, and each row steps one thread sideways. That makes slanting lines, as in denim. In satin weave a thread floats over many threads before going under one, so the cloth is smooth and shiny but catches easily. Nothing in woven cloth is glued or knotted. Each thread is bent over and under the others, and friction at every crossing holds it in place. At the side edges the weft turns back, so the cloth has a firm edge called the selvedge.
- e.g. In Thread Paths, the handkerchief on the phone screen shows threads crossing like a tiny mat.
- e.g. Jeans are blue outside and pale inside. In denim, the blue warp threads pass over three white weft threads and under one.
- e.g. A gunny sack is plain weave in thick jute yarn, so the over and under pattern is easy to see without a lens.
- e.g. A Pattamadai mat from Tirunelveli is woven the same way, with fine korai grass as the weft and cotton as the warp.
### Knitting: one thread in loops · *defined*
**Definition:** Knitting makes cloth from one long yarn pulled into rows of loops. Each loop passes through a loop in the row before it.

T-shirts, banians, socks, leggings and sweaters are knitted. Because the yarn runs in loops, a row of knitting uses much more yarn than the cloth is wide, often 3 or 4 times as much. When you pull knitted cloth, the loops change shape and the cloth grows longer. When you let go, the loops spring back. A woven thread is already nearly straight, so woven cloth has almost no length to spare. Knitted cloth stretches more across its rows than along its length. The stretch has a cost. If one loop breaks, the loops in its column can slip out one after another. This line of undone loops is called a ladder. The same fact has a use. A knitted sweater can be pulled back into one long yarn and knitted again. Most T-shirt cloth is knitted as a tube on round machines with hundreds of needles. Tiruppur in Tamil Nadu makes a large share of India's knitted clothes. Knitting is much younger than weaving. The oldest known knitted socks, from Egypt, are about 1,000 years old.
- e.g. In Woven or Knitted, the knitted strip stretches many times more than the woven strip under the same bottle.
- e.g. In the same test, one row of thread pulled out of the knitted piece is far longer than the piece is wide.
- e.g. A ladder in a sock or a pair of tights starts from a single broken loop.
- e.g. Many families unravel an outgrown sweater, steam the crinkled wool straight and knit a new one.
### Why a cut edge frays, and what stops it · *defined*
**Definition:** Fraying is the loss of threads from a cut edge of woven cloth. A thread near the edge slides out because nothing beyond it holds it in.

Inside a woven cloth, every thread is held by the threads that cross it. At a straight cut edge, the last thread that runs along the edge has crossing threads on one side only. Rubbing and washing push it off the ends of those threads, and then the next thread is the last one. The edge becomes a fringe. Three things slow this down. A selvedge has no cut ends, because the weft turns back at the edge. A hem folds the cut edge inside and stitches it down. A cut on the slant leaves no thread running along the edge, so no whole thread can slide off. Tailors also cut edges in zigzags with special scissors, so only short pieces can come away. Knitted cloth does not fray in this way. Its cut edge curls, and its loops can run into a ladder.
- e.g. In the Fray Test, the straight cut edge grows the widest fringe, and the selvedge grows none.
- e.g. The fringe at the end of a towel or a dupatta is fraying done on purpose, then knotted so that it stops.
- e.g. The inside seams of a ready-made shirt are wrapped in loops of thread by a machine, to stop the cut edges fraying.
### Stitches and seams · *defined*
**Definition:** A stitch is one pass of thread through cloth. A seam is a line of stitches that joins two pieces.

In a running stitch the needle goes down and up along a line. The thread lies on the top for one stitch and underneath for the next. It is quick, and it lets the cloth gather and ripple. But the two layers are pinned together only at the needle holes, so they can open between the holes. The longer the stitch, the wider the gap. In a backstitch the needle goes back to the end of the last stitch each time. The thread makes an unbroken line on top and a double line underneath. It holds better, takes longer and uses about three times as much thread. A sewing machine makes a lockstitch. One thread comes down with the needle, a second thread comes from a small spool underneath, and the two lock together inside the cloth at every stitch. A machine can make more than a thousand such stitches in a minute, each only 2 or 3 millimetres long. Tailoring is choosing: small stitches where a seam must hold, long stitches where it must be quick or must come out again.
- e.g. In the Leaky Seam Test, the seam with the longest stitches lets the most mustard seeds through.
- e.g. A tailor tacks a blouse together with long running stitches for a fitting, then pulls them out after machine stitching.
- e.g. A kantha quilt is held together by thousands of small running stitches through three or more layers of old cloth.
### Natural fibres from plants and animals · *defined*
**Definition:** Natural fibres are made by living beings. Plant fibres are made mostly of cellulose. Animal fibres are made of protein.

Cotton fibres are hairs that grow on the seeds of the cotton plant. Each fibre is a single long cell. Cotton takes in water easily, so cotton clothes soak up sweat and feel cool, but they dry slowly and crease. Jute fibres come from the stem of the jute plant. Farmers in West Bengal, Assam and Bihar soak the cut stems in water for two or three weeks, until the fibres come loose. Jute is strong and rough, and India grows more of it than any other country. Coir comes from the husk of the coconut and stands up well to water. Linen comes from the stem of the flax plant. Wool is the hair of sheep. Each wool fibre is wavy and covered in tiny scales, so wool cloth traps a great deal of still air and keeps a body warm. Shepherd families of the Deccan weave coarse black wool into blankets called kambli. Pashmina is the fine under-hair of the Changthangi goat, who lives above 4,000 metres in Ladakh. Herders comb it out in spring, when the goats shed their winter coat.
- e.g. A thorthu towel from Kerala is thin cotton. It soaks up water fast and dries fast on a line.
- e.g. Gunny sacks for rice and potatoes are jute, because jute is strong and cheap and lets air through.
- e.g. A coir doormat stays outside in the monsoon for years without rotting.
- e.g. A woollen sweater squeezed in the fist shrinks to a small lump, because most of the sweater is air.
### Silk and the life of the silk moth · *defined*
**Definition:** Silk is a filament spun by a caterpillar to make a cocoon. The mulberry silk moth has four stages of life: egg, caterpillar, pupa and moth.

A female silk moth lays several hundred eggs. Each egg hatches into a tiny caterpillar, often called a silkworm. The caterpillars eat only mulberry leaves. In about four weeks they shed their skin four times and grow to thousands of times their first weight. Then each caterpillar spins a cocoon around their own body, moving their head in a figure of eight for two or three days. The cocoon is one unbroken filament, many hundreds of metres long. Inside, the caterpillar becomes a pupa, and in about two weeks the pupa becomes a moth. The moth wets one end of the cocoon with a liquid that loosens the silk, and pushes out. That breaks the filament into many short pieces. So in most silk making, the cocoons are heated before the moths come out. The pupa inside dies, and the filament can be unwound whole. India makes four kinds of silk. Mulberry silk comes mostly from Karnataka. Tasar comes from wild moths in the forests of Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh. Golden muga silk comes only from Assam. Eri silk comes from Assam and Meghalaya.
- e.g. Ramanagara in Karnataka has one of the largest silk cocoon markets in Asia.
- e.g. Eri cocoons are open at one end, and the moths usually leave before the silk is used. The silk is spun like cotton and is less shiny.
- e.g. The mulberry silk moth has lived with people for about 5,000 years. The adult moths can no longer fly.
- **A silk reeler in Ramanagara:** Unbroken filament makes the strongest, shiniest silk. Many families earn their living from it, and the cooked pupae are used as feed and as food.
- **Kusuma Rajaiah, a handloom technical officer from Hyderabad:** Around the year 2000 he worked out a way to let the moths come out first and then spin the broken cocoons. He called the cloth ahimsa silk.
- **A critic of ahimsa silk:** The moths who come out cannot fly or feed and live only a few days. The silk costs more and is less shiny. So the gain for the moths is small.
### Synthetic fibres, blends, and what all fibres share · *defined*
**Definition:** A synthetic fibre is made in a factory from chemicals, mostly from petroleum. A blend mixes two or more fibres in one yarn.

Nylon was one of the first fully synthetic fibres. A team led by Wallace Carothers made it in the United States in 1935. Polyester followed, patented in Britain in 1941. To make a filament, the hot liquid is pushed through a plate of very fine holes, the way dough is pushed through an idiyappam press. Polyester and nylon are strong and cheap, dry fast and hardly crease. They take in very little water, so they feel sticky in hot weather. Near a flame they melt and stick to skin, which is why cotton is safer in the kitchen. Viscose, also called rayon, is made from wood or bamboo. The plant cellulose is dissolved and pushed out as new filaments. Elastane is a synthetic fibre that stretches to several times its length. A little of it in socks and leggings makes them cling. Under all these differences, every fibre is built the same way. Its molecules are very long chains, lying mostly along the fibre. Cotton's chains are cellulose, which the plant builds from air, water and sunlight. Wool and silk are chains of protein. Polyester's chains are built in a factory.
- e.g. A label that says 65% polyester and 35% cotton names a blend. The cotton soaks up sweat, and the polyester adds strength and dries fast.
- e.g. A nylon school bag keeps books drier in rain than a cotton bag, because nylon takes in very little water.
- e.g. Most cloth sold as bamboo is viscose made from bamboo, so it is partly natural and partly factory-made.
### Weaving and stitching by other animals · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals make thread or build with it. Birds weave and stitch nests, spiders and caterpillars spin silk, and weaver ants join leaves with silk.

A male baya weaver tears long strips from paddy, grass or palm leaves. He nicks the leaf with his beak and flies off, holding the strip. He knots the first strips around a thin twig, often on a thorny tree or a palm over water. Then he weaves strips in and out to make a ring, a roof and a round chamber. One nest is said to take up to 500 trips and about 18 days. When the nest is half built, females come to look at it. If a female accepts the nest, the male finishes it with a long entrance tube that hangs down. A tailorbird pierces the edges of a large living leaf with their beak. They pull plant fibre, cotton fluff or spider silk through the holes. The ends fluff out like rivets and hold without a knot. Weaver ants, the red ants of mango trees, pull leaves together in living chains. Other workers carry larvae, who give out silk that glues the leaves. Young weaver birds weave badly at first and improve with practice, as careful studies of African weaver birds showed.
- e.g. Baya nests hang from babul trees and palms beside paddy fields in much of India.
- e.g. The common tailorbird lives in gardens in almost every Indian city and calls 'towit, towit' loudly.
- e.g. A leaf nest of weaver ants on a mango or jackfruit tree is white inside with larval silk.
### Five ways to put a pattern into cloth · *defined*
**Definition:** A pattern can be woven in, dyed into the threads before weaving, dyed into the finished cloth, printed or painted on, or stitched on.

Woven in: the weaver uses coloured threads on the loom. In a Kanchipuram silk sari, the border and the body are woven in different colours and locked together. In jamdani from Bengal, the weaver adds each flower by hand with extra weft threads. Dyed threads: in ikat, bundles of thread are tied tightly in places and dyed, so the tied parts stay undyed. When the threads are woven, the pattern appears with soft edges. In the patola of Patan, both the warp and the weft are dyed in this way and must meet exactly. Dyed cloth: in bandhani, thousands of tiny points of the cloth are pinched and tied before dyeing. Printed or painted: in ajrakh from Kutch, carved wooden blocks print the cloth in many stages. In kalamkari from Srikalahasti, the artist draws with a bamboo pen. Stitched: embroidery adds the pattern with a needle, as in chikankari from Lucknow, kasuti from Karnataka and Toda embroidery from the Nilgiris.
- e.g. In Paper Ikat, a shape drawn on the strips before weaving shows up broken into small squares after weaving.
- e.g. Turn a printed cloth over and the pattern is fainter on the back. Turn a woven check over and it looks the same.
- e.g. A kasuti pattern is planned so well that it looks the same on both sides of the cloth.
### Handloom, power loom and khadi · *debated*
**Definition:** A handloom is worked by a person. A power loom is driven by a motor. Khadi is cloth that is both spun and woven by hand.

For many centuries, Indian handwoven cotton was sold across Asia, Africa and Europe. In the 1700s and 1800s, machines for spinning and weaving were built in Britain. Mill cloth became cheap, British rule taxed and blocked Indian cloth, and lakhs of spinners and weavers lost their work. The Swadeshi movement began on 7 August 1905 and asked Indians to buy cloth made in India. Later Gandhi asked people to spin on the charkha and wear khadi. Today most cloth in India comes from power looms and mills. A power loom weaves many metres in the time a handloom weaves one. Still, about 35 lakh people work in handloom weaving, and about 7 in every 10 of them are women. The 7th of August is National Handloom Day. People do not agree on what should happen to handloom, and each side has real evidence.
- e.g. A Kanchipuram silk sari can take a weaver from about ten days to many weeks. A power loom copy takes far less time.
- e.g. The Jacquard loom of 1804 used cards with punched holes to lift the right threads for a pattern. Early computers borrowed the idea.
- e.g. A khadi shop sells cloth whose yarn was spun on a charkha and woven on a handloom.
- **A handloom weaver in Pochampally:** My loom needs no electricity, and I can weave patterns no machine weaves well. But the work is slow, and I earn too little unless buyers pay for the skill.
- **A mill engineer in Coimbatore:** Machines make cloth that poor families can afford. Without mills, a simple shirt would cost many days' wages.
- **A buyer:** I buy mill cloth for everyday wear and handloom for what I will keep for years. Both have a place, if weavers are paid fairly.
### What happens to old cloth · *debated*
**Definition:** Old cloth can be worn longer, mended, passed on, remade into something else, or broken down into fibre and spun again.

Making cloth uses land, water and energy. Growing the cotton for one T-shirt takes a great deal of water. Estimates range from hundreds to thousands of litres. So the best use of a garment is to wear it for a long time. After that comes passing it on, and then remaking it into something new. Many Indian homes do this without calling it recycling. Old saris become kantha quilts in Bengal, godhadi quilts in Maharashtra and kaudi quilts in Karnataka. Strips of old cloth are woven into chindi rugs. Worn banians become dusters, and old cotton becomes lamp wicks. Last comes true recycling. At Panipat in Haryana, old woollen clothes from many countries are sorted by colour, torn back into fibre and spun into yarn for blankets. Each round of tearing shortens the fibres, so recycled yarn is weaker. Polyester can be melted and spun again, and some polyester is made from used plastic bottles. One problem is still being studied. When synthetic clothes are washed, they shed microfibres that pass into rivers and the sea.
- e.g. In The Sweater That Went Back to Wool, a sweater is unravelled and knitted again in a smaller size.
- e.g. In many cities, traders give steel vessels in exchange for old clothes and sell the clothes at second-hand markets.
- e.g. The Chindi Coaster in this unit is woven from strips of an old T-shirt.
- **A marine biologist:** Plastic microfibres are now found in sea water, fish and salt. We should cut them down now, before the harm is fully measured.
- **A textile engineer:** Cotton sheds fibres too, and growing cotton takes much water and pesticide. A polyester shirt that lasts ten years may do less harm than three cotton ones.
- **A woman who sorts old clothes at Panipat:** Cloth that reaches us clean, dry and of one fibre becomes yarn again. Blends and dirty cloth mostly become stuffing or waste.

## Mostly agreed
- Woven cloth is held together only by friction where its threads cross. Nothing in it is glued or tied. *How we know:* A single thread can be pulled out of a handkerchief whole. A thread that crosses more threads needs a harder pull, because friction acts at every crossing.
- The way a thread is arranged changes what the cloth does, even when the fibre is the same. *How we know:* Cotton woven into a handkerchief hardly stretches. The same cotton knitted into a T-shirt stretches by a quarter of its length or more, and springs back.
- People were twisting fibres into string and weaving them tens of thousands of years ago. *How we know:* Fired clay from Pavlov in the Czech Republic, about 26,000 years old, carries the prints of woven cloth and nets. Cloth rots, but a print in baked clay stays.
- People in the Indian subcontinent used cotton thread about 7,000 to 8,000 years ago. *How we know:* In 2002, a team led by Christophe Moulherat studied a copper bead from a grave at Mehrgarh. Metal salts had kept the shape of the thread inside, and under a microscope the fibres were cotton.
- Clothes made of polyester, nylon and acrylic shed tiny fibres when they are washed, and these reach the sea. *How we know:* In 2011, Mark Browne and his team found such fibres in sand from 18 beaches on six continents. In their washing tests, one garment gave off more than 1,900 fibres in one wash.
- A male baya weaver builds the nest, and a female chooses whether to accept it. *How we know:* Watchers from Salim Ali onwards have seen females visit half-built nests, tug at them and sit in them. A male whose nest is refused often leaves it and starts another.

## How we found out
**Olga Soffer and James Adovasio, archaeologists in the United States, and Bohuslav Klíma, a Czech archaeologist, The 1990s. Their first report on woven prints from Pavlov was published in 1996..** For a long time, archaeologists thought weaving began with farming, less than 10,000 years ago. The oldest cloth they had found was about that old, and settled villages seemed necessary for looms. The people of the Ice Age were pictured in animal skins. Cloth and string rot quickly, so nobody expected to find any. Then, in the 1990s, Olga Soffer and James Adovasio studied small lumps of fired clay from Pavlov and Dolní Věstonice, in today's Czech Republic. People had lived there about 26,000 years ago. Bohuslav Klíma had dug up the clay years earlier, and it had been stored as scraps. Under a lens, some lumps showed neat rows of crossing lines. Wet clay had once been pressed against woven cloth, nets and cord, and then baked in a fire. The cloth was gone, but the print stayed. Soffer and Adovasio counted several kinds of weave and knots for nets. So people were weaving about 15,000 years before farming. The work needed no village and no big loom, only skilled hands.  
*What people believed before:* That weaving began with farming, less than 10,000 years ago, and that Ice Age people wore only skins. Prints of woven cloth in clay about 26,000 years old showed that weaving is far older.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Only our own kind of human could make string.** (Most archaeologists until recently; *rejected*)  
  For: String needs planning: fibres must be found, twisted and then twisted together again. Old string almost never survives, so none was known from other humans.  
  Against: In 2020, Bruce Hardy and his team reported a tiny piece of three-ply cord on a stone tool from Abri du Maras in France. It is more than 40,000 years old and was made by Neanderthals.
- **A female baya chooses the male who weaves the neatest nest.** (A common belief, found in many books; *debated*)  
  For: Females do visit nests, tug at the strips and sit inside before they accept. Young males build rough nests and are often refused.  
  Against: In 2006, Suhel Quader studied many baya nests in India. Where a nest hung predicted its success a little better than how it was built. This fits females choosing by place more than by weaving.
- **Natural fibres are always better for the Earth than synthetic ones.** (Many shoppers and some sellers; *debated*)  
  For: Cotton, wool and jute rot back into soil. Polyester comes from petroleum, lasts for centuries and sheds plastic microfibres.  
  Against: Cotton needs a great deal of water and often much pesticide. A polyester garment that is worn for many years may use less land and water than several cotton ones.
- **People began to wear clothes only a few thousand years ago.** (A reasonable guess, because the oldest cloth ever found is only a few thousand years old; *rejected*)  
  For: Cloth, skins and thread rot, so very old clothes are not found.  
  Against: The body louse lives only in clothing. Their DNA shows that they split from the head louse at least 83,000 years ago, so people were wearing clothes by then.
- **Handloom weaving will die out, because machines are faster and cheaper.** (Some economists, for more than a hundred years; *debated*)  
  For: The number of handloom workers in India has fallen over the last thirty years, and many weavers' children choose other work.  
  Against: Handloom has outlived this forecast since the 1800s. Some cloth, such as jamdani and double ikat, is still made well only by hand, and buyers pay for it.
- **Silk can be made without harming any moth.** (Sellers of ahimsa silk, also called peace silk; *debated*)  
  For: The moths are allowed to come out of their cocoons alive, and the broken cocoons are spun afterwards.  
  Against: The moths cannot fly or feed and live only a few days. Far more eggs hatch than a farm can feed, so many caterpillars still die.

## Roots
We have learned how thread is woven, knitted and stitched into cloth, and how cloth is taken apart and used again. Now we ask what this tells us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** In Woven or Knitted, one loose end pulled out a whole row as a single thread. So a knitted sweater can go back to yarn and be knitted again. A woven cloth frays where it is cut, yet it can be cut and stitched into something new. The way a thing was made decides how it can be taken apart and made again.  
*Habit it breaks:* We call old cloth 'waste', as if a thing simply ends when we stop using it. We also sort things for reuse by their material alone. But two cloths of the same cotton have different second lives, because one was looped from a single thread and the other was crossed from many.  
*Evidence:* In Woven or Knitted, a row of the knitted piece came out as one long thread. In the Fray Test, the woven piece lost threads only at its cut edges and stayed whole inside. In The Sweater That Went Back to Wool, the cut pieces were wasted and the unravelled wool was not.

- *who am i:* Your body is also many strands held in an arrangement. The food you eat is taken apart and built into you, and you stay yourself while the material changes.
- *what is life:* Living beings were spinning and weaving long before people. A caterpillar spins, a baya weaves and a tailorbird stitches. Making is part of being alive.
- *what is this world:* The world keeps very little as waste. Cotton was air, water and sunlight. A shirt can become a quilt, then a duster, then soil that grows new cotton.

A cotton plant builds fibre out of air, water and sunlight. People twist that fibre into one thread. From one thread come many cloths: woven, knitted, dyed and stitched in hundreds of ways across India. When a cloth wears out, the same thread and fibre can pass into a quilt, a rug or new yarn. So the many cloths are one kind of thread arranged in different ways, and the thread is one kind of fibre, and the fibre came from the same air and water that made you.

- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.6 and 3.8:** The philosopher Gargi Vachaknavi asks the sage Yajnavalkya a weaver's question. If all this world is woven, like warp and weft, on water, then on what is water woven? She asks again and again, down to the last thing on which everything is woven. (Gargi used the picture of a loom to ask what holds the world together. In this unit your child asks the same kind of question of a cloth, and finds threads, then plies, then fibres, then long molecules.)
- **Kabir, the song 'Jhini jhini bini chadariya':** Kabir was a weaver of Varanasi. In this song, sung in his name, he calls the body a finely woven cloth and asks what its warp and weft are. He says he wore the cloth with care and gave it back as he received it. (A weaver saw his own life in his work. The song treats a cloth as something lent, to be looked after and returned, which is how this unit treats old cloth.)
- **Thich Nhat Hanh, The Heart of Understanding (1988):** Look deeply at a sheet of paper and you see the cloud, the rain, the tree and the logger. Without any one of them, the paper would not be here. (The same is true of a cotton shirt. In it are the cotton plant, the soil and rain, the farmer, the spinner, the weaver or knitter, the dyer and the tailor.)
- Science (settled): The cellulose in every cotton fibre was built by a plant from carbon dioxide and water, using the energy of sunlight.
- Science (debated): A whole, such as a cloth, can do things that none of its parts can do alone. Whether this means the whole is something more than its parts is still argued about by philosophers and scientists.
- Other view, The Buddhist monk Nagasena, in the Milindapanha: Nagasena asks King Milinda whether the chariot is the wheels, the axle or the pole. It is none of them. 'Chariot' is only a name for the parts arranged together. In this view, 'cloth' and 'sweater' are also only names. There are threads arranged in a certain way, and no need to look for one last thing on which everything is woven.
- Practice, Follow one thread (3 min): Hold the hem of your shirt up to the light. Find one thread and follow it slowly with your eyes. Watch it go over and under, or round its loops. Breathe slowly four times as you follow it. Now think of who touched this thread before you: a plant, a spinner, a weaver, a tailor.
- Ask: When a sweater is unravelled, the wool is all still there. Is the sweater still there?
- Ask: If you could not see the threads, would you guess that a cloth is made of only one kind of thing?
- Ask: Whose work are you wearing today? How many of them could you name?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A woven cloth has no glue and no knots. So what holds millions of threads together? (hint: Pull one thread out of an old handkerchief. Feel where the pull resists. Then think about what happens at each crossing.)
- *deep:* When a sweater is unravelled into a ball of wool, nothing is lost. So where has the sweater gone? (hint: Ask what a sweater is: the wool, or the way the wool is arranged. Could it be both?)
- *deep:* In ikat, the pattern is dyed into loose threads before any cloth exists. Is the pattern in the thread or in the cloth? (hint: Think about Paper Ikat. Could you see the shape on one strip alone? What had to happen before it showed?)
- *big:* A young baya weaves a rough nest and an older one weaves a fine nest. Is weaving something a baya knows, or something a baya learns? (hint: Think about your own first running stitches. What did you know before you began, and what came only with practice?)
- *big:* How many living beings and people had a hand in the shirt you are wearing? (hint: Start with the cotton plant and the soil. Count the farmer, the spinner, the weaver or knitter, the dyer, the tailor and the seller.)
- *big:* A machine weaves a sari in hours, and a person takes two weeks. Both saris are cloth. What is different about them? (hint: Think about the cloth, the price, who earns from it and what is lost if no one remembers how.)

## Story: The Sweater That Went Back to Wool
*A made-up story. Thenmozhi, Kathir and Paati are imagined, and so is their house in Kotagiri, a real town in the Nilgiri hills of Tamil Nadu. The cloth facts are real. A cut across knitted cloth gives short pieces of yarn and loose loops that run into a ladder. A knitted sweater can be unravelled into yarn, and many families steam the crinkled yarn straight over a pot and knit a new sweater.*

In Kotagiri, the mist comes up the tea slopes in December and sits in the lanes until ten in the morning. Thenmozhi was eleven that winter. Her brother Kathir was six, and he had no sweater. He had left it on the school bus in November, and the bus had not given it back.

"He can have my red one," Thenmozhi told Paati. The red sweater had been hers since Class 2. It was tight across her shoulders now and stopped above her wrists. On Kathir it was the other way round. The body was nearly right, but the sleeves hung past his fingers like two red socks.

"Leave it on the chair," Paati said. "I will see to it on Sunday." Paati was shelling peas, and she did not look up.

Thenmozhi did not want to wait until Sunday. In October she had watched the tailor near the bus stand shorten her school pinafore. He had cut a strip off the bottom, folded the new edge up and run it under his machine. It had taken him four minutes. A sleeve, she thought, was only a small pinafore.

She measured Kathir's arm with the tape from Paati's tin. She marked each sleeve with chalk, eight centimetres from the end. Then she took the big scissors and cut both sleeves straight across.

The cut edges looked wrong at once. They were not flat, like the pinafore's edge. Each was a row of tiny open loops, and between the loops sat short red crumbs of wool, none longer than her finger. She brushed the crumbs off, and more came. She folded the edge to hem it. The loops slipped out of the fold. She pulled the sleeve gently to make it lie flat, and one loop let go, and then the loop under it, and the one under that. A thin gap ran up the sleeve towards the elbow, with little bars of wool across it like the rungs of a ladder.

Thenmozhi put the sweater down very carefully, the way you put down something that might still be falling.

After a while she looked again. A loose end of wool stuck out at the cut edge. She pulled it. A whole row came away with a soft ticking feeling, loop after loop, from the seam all the way round the sleeve and back to the seam. There the wool turned and went into the next row.

She held the pulled-out wool against the tape. It was crinkled like a tiny noodle, and when she drew it straight, it was sixty-two centimetres long. She put the tape around the sleeve. Twenty centimetres. One row was three times as long as the sleeve was round.

So where did the extra wool go? She looked closely at the open loops. The wool did not run straight round the sleeve. It went up, over, down and under, up, over, down and under, all the way round. And the crumbs on the floor were pieces of that same wool. Her scissors had gone through it again and again.

"Paati," she said. "I have done something to the red sweater."

Paati came and looked at the two cut sleeves, the ladder and the heap of crumbs. She was quiet for a moment.

"A pinafore is woven," Paati said. "Hundreds of threads cross each other, and each one is held by all the rest. Cut some, and the others stay where they are. This is knitted. I knit my sleeves from the shoulder down, so that I can add to them when a child grows. I began this one with one thread and looped it back and forth until I reached the wrist."

"One thread," said Thenmozhi. "And I cut it in a great many places."

"You did," said Paati.

Thenmozhi looked at the end of wool still in her hand. She had pulled out three whole rows by now. The wool lay in her lap, crinkled, red and unbroken, longer than she was tall.

"Paati," she said slowly. "If it is one thread, then I do not need to cut it at all. I can pull all of it out. The whole sweater. Then it is just wool again, and you can knit it small."

Paati smiled then, for the first time. "That is what I was going to do on Sunday," she said. "You have saved me the start."

They worked until dark. Paati snipped the stitches that joined the sleeves to the body, because a sweater is knitted in pieces and sewn together. Then Thenmozhi pulled. Each piece came undone from the end where Paati had finished it, row by row, and Kathir walked backwards across the room holding the end. They wound the wool around the back of a chair. It was as crinkled as instant noodles.

"It remembers being a sweater," said Kathir.

"It will forget in the steam," said Paati. She tied each loop of wool in four places and held it over the idli pot until the kinks went soft. Then she hung the loops from the window bar, with a steel tumbler tied to the bottom of each one to pull it straight.

By morning the wool was smooth. There were four fat balls of it, two short lengths from the cut-off ends, and a small paper bag of red crumbs.

"Those are the price of the scissors," said Paati. "Too short to knit. Keep them. They will stuff a ball for the cat."

Paati knitted for nine evenings, and Thenmozhi knitted the plain rows when Paati's eyes were tired. The new sweater was smaller in every direction, so one ball of wool was left over. Kathir wore it to school on the first day of January, with the sleeves ending exactly at his wrists.

"It is my sweater," he told everybody.

"It is my sweater's wool," said Thenmozhi. "That is not quite the same thing."

**For the parent:** For the parent: the story turns when Thenmozhi measures one row of wool and finds it three times as long as the sleeve is round. A knitted sweater is one thread in loops, so a cut goes through that thread again and again, and the loops beside the cut can run. A woven pinafore is many threads that hold each other, so it can be cut and hemmed. The same fact that spoils the cut also saves the day. Because the sweater is one thread, the whole of it can go back to being wool.

- Ask: Why could the tailor cut and hem the pinafore, when the same cut spoiled the sweater sleeve?
- Ask: One row of wool was 62 centimetres long, but the sleeve was only 20 centimetres round. What did that tell Thenmozhi?
- Ask: At the end, Thenmozhi says it is her sweater's wool, and not quite her sweater. What do you think she means?
- Ask: Which old thing in your home could be taken apart and made into something else?

### e2 · The Leaky Seam Test: how small must a stitch be to hold mustard seeds? (45 min)
- **question:** Does the length of a stitch change how many seeds get through a seam? How short must a stitch be to stop a mustard seed?
- **predict:** Guess first. One seam has stitches 1 cm long. One has stitches half a centimetre long. One has stitches a quarter of a centimetre long. Through which seam will the most mustard seeds fall? Will any seam let none through? Why do you think so? Write your guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** All three seams are 8 cm long, in the same cloth, sewn with the same thread, 1 cm from the edge. Each seam is stretched flat over the same tumbler. The same spoonful of seeds is rubbed across each seam 20 times. Only the length of the stitch changes.
- **safety:** A needle is sharp. Sit at a table to sew, and push the needle away from your other hand. Stick the needle in the bar of soap whenever it is not in use. Count the needles at the start and at the end.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each seam, showing the mustard seeds that fell through. Then write one sentence for your claim, one for your evidence and one for your reasoning.
- **results table:** Seam | Stitch length | Number of stitches | Minutes to sew | Mustard seeds that fell through | Rice grains that fell through
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On three pieces of cloth, draw a line 1 cm from one long edge. Start and end the line 1 cm from the short edges, so it is 8 cm long. 2. On piece A, mark a dot every 1 cm along the line. 3. On pieces B and C, mark a dot every half centimetre. 4. Ask for the guesses and the reasons, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* Seam A will have stitches 1 cm long. Seam B will have half that. Seam C will have a quarter. Which seam will leak the most seeds? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a line on three pieces. Mark the dots. Which seam will leak the most seeds? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: Long stitches leave long gaps between the needle holes.; A seed can only get through a gap that is bigger than the seed.)
- **s2 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay piece A on a plain piece, with the edges matching. 2. Thread the needle, double the thread and knot the end. 3. Start the timer. Sew through both layers: down at one dot and up at the next. 4. Pull the thread until it lies flat, without gathering the cloth. 5. At the end, sew two small stitches on the same spot and cut the thread. 6. Stop the timer and write the minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Go down at one dot and up at the next. Keep the thread flat. I will time you.  
  *Child mode:* Put piece A on a plain piece. Sew down at one dot and up at the next. Finish with two stitches on one spot.
  - Note: If your child has never sewn, do the Stitch Sampler activity first. Seam A has 8 stitches.
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Sew seam B in the same way, through every dot. It has 16 stitches. Time it. 2. Sew seam C through every dot and once more halfway between each pair of dots. It has 32 stitches. Time it. 3. Write both times in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Seam B has twice as many stitches as seam A. Seam C has twice as many again. How long do you think each will take?  
  *Child mode:* Sew seam B through every dot. Sew seam C through every dot and once in between. Time each seam.
  - Note: If time is short, sew seam C yourself while your child sews seam B. Time it all the same. The tiny stitches of seam C are slow, and that slowness is one of the results.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Open the two pieces of seam A like a book. Lay them over the tumbler with the seam across the middle and the two short flaps hanging inside. 2. Put the rubber band round the rim. Pull the cloth edges down gently until the cloth is flat. 3. Look at the seam. Can you see gaps between the stitches? 4. Stand the tumbler on the plate and pour the teaspoon of mustard seeds on the cloth. 5. With one finger, rub the seeds across the seam and back 20 times. 6. Tip the seeds from the top into the bowl. Lift off the cloth and count the seeds inside the tumbler. 7. Do the same for seams B and C.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the seam before the seeds go on. What do you see between the stitches? Now rub the seeds across it 20 times.  
  *Child mode:* Stretch the seam over the tumbler. Look for gaps. Rub the mustard seeds across 20 times. Count the seeds inside the tumbler.
  - Ask: What does seam A look like when it is stretched flat? (good: Like a ladder: the thread makes rungs and there are gaps between them.; The two cloths pull apart between the needle holes.)
  - Note: Pull each cloth equally flat, like a drum skin and no tighter. Seam A usually lets many seeds through and seam C none or almost none. If no seeds fall through seam A, the cloth is too slack: pull it flatter and try again.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to guess what rice will do. 2. Repeat the test on all three seams with 1 level teaspoon of raw rice. 3. Write the counts in the table.  
  *Parent says:* A rice grain is longer than a mustard seed. Will more rice get through, or less? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Now try rice. Guess first: will more get through, or less? Why? Test all three seams.
  - Note: Rice usually gets through far less than mustard, because a long grain must meet the gap end on. Whatever you find is a real result. Write it down as it is.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning. 2. Look at the table together: stitch length, number of stitches, minutes and seeds. 3. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then tell me what the shortest stitches cost you.  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed and what you saw. Then say why it happened. What did short stitches cost you?
  - Check: In the Leaky Seam Test, why did the seam with 1 cm stitches let the most seeds through? → **The cloth can open between needle holes that are far apart**
### e3 · The Fray Test: which cut edge loses the fewest threads? (30 min)
- **question:** Which edge of a cloth frays the most when it is washed: a straight cut, a slant cut, a zigzag cut or a selvedge? Does knitted cloth fray at all?
- **predict:** Guess first. Five kinds of edge go into the same jar of water and are shaken 300 times: a straight cut, a slant cut, a zigzag cut, a selvedge, and a cut edge of T-shirt cloth. Which edge will have the widest fringe of loose threads? Which will have none? Why do you think so? Write your guesses and your reasons.
- **fair test:** The three woven pieces are cut from the same cloth, to the same size. All the pieces are shaken together in one jar, so each gets the same water and the same number of shakes. Only the kind of edge changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps with the cutting. Hold the lid on with one hand while shaking, and shake over a sink or a bucket.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar chart with one bar for the fringe on each kind of edge. Then write one sentence for your claim, one for your evidence and one for your reasoning.
- **results table:** Edge | Fringe before (mm) | Fringe after 300 shakes (mm) | What the edge looks like
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the thin cloth and find its selvedge, the firm edge that was never cut. 2. Explain the three cuts you will make. 3. Ask for the guesses and the reasons, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will cut this cloth straight, on the slant and in zigzags. Which edge will fray most in the wash? Which will not fray at all? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Find the firm edge of the cloth. Which cut edge will fray the most? Which will not fray? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think so? (good: On a straight cut, the last thread has nothing outside it to hold it in.; The selvedge has no cut ends, so nothing can slide out.)
- **s2 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the paper square on the cloth with its sides along the threads and one side on the selvedge. Cut round it. Write 1 on the piece. 2. Turn the paper like a diamond, so its corners point along the threads. Cut round it. Write 2. 3. Cut a third square along the threads. Snip small V-shaped teeth, half a centimetre deep, all round its edges. Write 3. 4. Measure any fringe on each piece now and write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Piece 1 is cut along the threads. Piece 2 is cut on the slant. Piece 3 has zigzag edges. Look closely: where do the threads end on each?  
  *Child mode:* Cut three squares with a grown-up: straight, slant and zigzag. Number them 1, 2 and 3.
  - Note: If the cloth has no selvedge, leave that row of the table empty. Ball pen marks stay on through the shaking.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the three woven pieces and the knitted piece in the jar of water. 2. Close the lid tightly. 3. Shake the jar 300 times, counting aloud. Take turns, 100 shakes each.  
  *Parent says:* A washing machine rubs and tumbles cloth. Our jar does the same. Count the shakes with me.  
  *Child mode:* Put all four pieces in the jar. Close the lid tight. Shake 300 times. Count aloud.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lift each piece out and lay it flat on the plate, without pulling its edges. 2. On each edge, measure the fringe at its widest. The fringe is the part where only loose thread ends are left. 3. Write the numbers in the table. 4. Look in the jar for threads that came right out.  
  *Parent says:* Lay each piece flat without pulling. Which edge has the widest fringe? Is there any edge with no fringe at all?  
  *Child mode:* Lay the pieces flat. Do not pull the edges. Measure the fringe on each edge in millimetres.
  - Ask: What does the cut edge of the knitted piece look like? (good: It has curled up, but it has no fringe.; A few short bits came off, but no long threads.)
  - Note: Expect the widest fringe on the straight cut, clearly less on the slant cut and the zigzag cut, and none on the selvedge. If almost nothing has frayed, the cloth is tightly woven. Shake 300 more times.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child for their claim, evidence and reasoning. 2. Look at a hemmed edge on a shirt or a kurta together. 3. Ask the check question.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Then look at this hem. What job is it doing?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed and what you saw. Then say why it happened. What does a hem do?
  - Check: In the Fray Test, why did the selvedge not fray? → **The cross thread turns back there, so no cut ends exist**

(15 min); day 2: Weave 8 rows of T-shirt strip, over and under, pushing each row down with the comb. (15 min); day 3: Plan a stripe or a pattern on squared paper. Weave 8 more rows. (15 min); day 4: Cut the coaster off the loom, knot the warp ends in pairs and try it under a tumbler. (15 min)
- **s1 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up cuts 10 notches, each 1 cm deep and 1 cm apart, along the top edge of the card. Start 1.5 cm from the side. Cut 10 more along the bottom edge. 2. Tape one end of the string to the back. Bring it through the first bottom notch and up the front to the first top notch. 3. Take it behind the card to the second top notch and down the front to the second bottom notch. 4. Carry on until 10 strings cross the front. Tape the end at the back. 5. Cut rings 1.5 cm wide from the body of the T-shirt. Cut each ring open to make a long strip.  
  *Parent says:* This string is the warp. It must be tight, like the strings of a veena. How many warp threads can you count on the front?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cuts the notches. Wind the string up and down the front. Count 10 warp threads. Cut the T-shirt into strips.
  - Note: Pull each T-shirt strip gently and its edges curl in, so it becomes a soft round yarn. T-shirt cloth is knitted, so the cut edges do not fray.
- **s2 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Fix one end of a strip in the hair pin. 2. Weave the first row: over one string, under the next, right across. Leave a tail of 3 cm at the start. 3. Weave back the other way: under where the last row went over. 4. Push each row down with the comb. 5. Do not pull the strip tight at the edges. 6. To join a new strip, lay its end over the old end for 3 cm and weave them together. 7. Weave 8 rows.  
  *Parent says:* Over, under, over, under. On the way back, do the opposite. What would happen if two rows went the same way?  
  *Child mode:* Weave over one string and under the next. Go back the opposite way. Push each row down. Weave 8 rows.
  - Ask: What would happen if two rows went over and under the same strings? (good: They would slide together into one thick row.; They would not lock each other, so the cloth would be loose there.)
  - Note: If the edges are pulled tight, the coaster grows narrow in the middle. Leave a small loop of strip at each turn.
  - Wait: Carry on tomorrow
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. On squared paper, draw a grid 10 squares wide and 16 squares tall. 2. Colour a square wherever the weft goes over a warp thread. Rows 1 to 8 are done already. 3. Plan rows 9 to 16: change the colour of the strip to make stripes, or try going over two and under two. 4. Weave rows 9 to 16 from the plan.  
  *Parent says:* A weaver plans on squared paper before touching the loom. Each square is one crossing. Colour the squares where your strip will be on top.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a grid 10 wide and 16 tall. Colour where the strip goes over. Plan 8 more rows. Weave them.
  - Note: Over two and under two makes a looser, softer band. If each row steps one string sideways, slanting lines appear, as in denim.
  - Wait: Carry on tomorrow
- **s4 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Turn the loom over. Cut the strings across the middle of the back. 2. Lift the weaving off the card. 3. At each end, knot the strings together in pairs, close to the weaving. 4. Trim the ends. 5. Count the crossings: warp threads times rows. 6. Stand a tumbler on the coaster.  
  *Parent says:* Before I cut the strings, tell me: what holds your coaster together? What will the knots at the ends do?  
  *Child mode:* Cut the strings at the back. Lift the weaving off. Knot the strings in pairs. Count the crossings.
  - Check: Why must the warp ends of the coaster be knotted after it is cut off the loom? → **So the end rows cannot slide off the warp**

### c1 · The No-Leak Seam: hold the mustard seeds with the least sewing (30 min)
- **problem:** Sew a seam 8 cm long that joins two pieces of cloth. In the tumbler test, with 20 rubs, it must let through no more than 2 mustard seeds. Among seams that pass, the best one takes the least time to sew.
- **your turn:** Now design your own seam. Draw it from the side, sew it, time it and test it. Did it pass? Was it faster than seam C in the Leaky Seam Test?
- **constraints:** Use the same cloth, the same thread and the same 8 cm line as in the Leaky Seam Test.; Test it exactly as before: stretch the seam over the tumbler and rub 1 level teaspoon of mustard seeds across it 20 times.; Time the sewing from the first stitch to the last knot.; Measure the thread before you start and after you finish, to find how much you used.
- **general:** Sew a backstitch with stitches half a centimetre long.; Sew two rows of running stitch side by side, with the stitches of one row beside the gaps of the other.; Sew a running stitch with stitches a quarter of a centimetre long, as in seam C.
- **creative:** Fold the two flaps over together and sew them down a second time, like the thick seam on the side of jeans.; Sew a running stitch with one backstitch after every third stitch.; Sew over and over the two edges, so the thread wraps round them like a spring.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the table from the Leaky Seam Test. 2. Write down the minutes that seam C took and the seeds it let through. 3. Those are the numbers to beat.  
  *Parent says:* Seam C held the seeds but was slow. Why was it slow? What else could close the gaps between stitches?  
  *Child mode:* Find the time for seam C in your table. That is the time to beat. How else could you close the gaps?
- **s2 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Read the usual ideas. 2. Choose one and sew it, with the timer running. 3. Test it with the mustard seeds. 4. Write the time, the thread used and the seeds that fell through.  
  *Parent says:* Which idea will you try first? Why do you think it will close the gaps?  
  *Child mode:* Pick one idea. Sew it and time it. Test it with the seeds. Write the numbers.
- **s3 (10 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Design a seam of your own and draw it from the side. 2. Sew it, time it and test it. 3. Compare all your seams in one table.  
  *Parent says:* Which seam passed the test in the least time? What made it work?  
  *Child mode:* Design your own seam. Draw it. Sew it and test it. Which seam is best?
  - Check: Why does a second row of running stitch, set beside the gaps of the first, stop more seeds? → **Each gap in one row is pinned shut by a stitch in the other**
### c2 · Wear It Uncut: dress in one cloth with no scissors and no pins (30 min)
- **problem:** Use one uncut rectangle of cloth to dress yourself over your ordinary clothes. The cloth must stay on through 10 jumps and a walk across the room, with both hands free.
- **your turn:** Now design your own way to wear the cloth. Test it with 10 jumps and a walk across the room. Draw it, and write what holds it up.
- **constraints:** Use one cloth only, and do not cut it.; No pins, clips, rubber bands or belts. Use only folds, pleats, tucks, rolls and knots.; You must be able to walk, sit down and lift both arms.; Wear it over your ordinary clothes, so you can test it without worry.
- **general:** Wrap the cloth round the waist and roll the top edge over itself twice, as a lungi or mundu is worn.; Fold part of the cloth into pleats and tuck the pleats in at the waist, as in a sari or a veshti.; Knot two corners together over one shoulder.
- **creative:** Pass one end between the legs and tuck it in at the back, as a dhoti is worn for work.; Twist one end of the cloth into a rope and use it as the belt for the rest.; Cross two corners over the chest and knot them behind the neck.
- **s1 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Clear the floor. 2. Wrap the cloth round the waist and roll the top edge over twice. 3. Jump 10 times and walk across the room. 4. Count the jumps before the cloth slips.  
  *Parent says:* Start with the simplest way: wrap and roll. How many jumps does it last? What is holding it up?  
  *Child mode:* Wrap the cloth round your waist. Roll the top edge over twice. Jump 10 times. Does it stay?
  - Ask: What holds a rolled waist in place? (good: Friction between the layers of cloth pressed together.; The roll squeezes the layers tight round the body.)
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Try pleats: fold part of the cloth back and forth five times and tuck the pleats in at the waist. 2. Try a knot over one shoulder. 3. Test each with 10 jumps. 4. Write which lasted longest.  
  *Parent says:* Pleats put many layers of cloth in one place. Does that hold better or worse than the roll? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Try pleats tucked in at the waist. Try a knot over one shoulder. Test each with 10 jumps.
  - Note: Pleats hold well because many layers press on each other, so friction is high. They also leave room for the legs to move.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Design your own way. 2. Test it with 10 jumps, a walk, sitting down and lifting both arms. 3. Draw it and label what holds it up.

## Worksheets
### Threads, cloth and stitches (core) (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is weaving?  
   Options: Twisting fibres into yarn / Crossing two sets of threads, over and under / Pulling one thread into loops / Printing colour on cloth  
   **Answer:** Crossing two sets of threads, over and under. *Why:* Weaving crosses warp threads and weft threads at right angles. Twisting fibres is spinning, and pulling one thread into loops is knitting. Choosing one of those means the three words need another look.
2. (fill) The threads that run along the length of a woven cloth are called the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['warp']. *Why:* The warp is stretched tight on the loom first. The weft is then woven across it.
3. (truefalse) True or false: a T-shirt is woven.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A T-shirt is knitted from one thread in rows of loops. A strong answer says how to check: the cloth stretches, and a close look shows loops, not crossing threads.
4. (match) Match each word to what it means.  
   **Answer:** Spinning → twisting fibres into yarn; Knitting → pulling one yarn into rows of loops; Selvedge → the side edge where the weft turns back; Hem → a folded edge stitched down; Seam → a line of stitches joining two pieces. *Why:* These five words are used all through the unit. A strong answer can point to each one on a real shirt.
5. (sort) Sort these fibres by where they come from.  
   **Answer:** cotton → From a plant; wool → From an animal; nylon → Made in a factory; jute → From a plant; silk → From an animal; polyester → Made in a factory; coir → From a plant; pashmina → From an animal. *Why:* Cotton, jute and coir are plant fibres. Wool, silk and pashmina are animal fibres. Nylon and polyester are made in factories, mostly from petroleum.
6. (write) Remember Woven or Knitted, where a bottle hung from strips of cloth. Explain why the knitted strip stretched more.  
   **Answer:** The thread in knitted cloth runs in loops, so each row holds much more thread than the cloth is wide. When the bottle pulled, the loops opened out and the strip grew longer. The woven thread was already nearly straight, so it had no length to spare.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the thread lengths: the knitted thread was 3 or 4 times as long as its cloth was wide, and the woven thread was only a little longer.
7. (order) Put the stages of a silk moth's life in order. Start with the egg.  
   **Answer:** ['Egg', 'Caterpillar, eating mulberry leaves', 'Pupa inside a cocoon', 'Moth']. *Why:* The egg hatches into a caterpillar. The caterpillar spins a cocoon and becomes a pupa inside. The pupa becomes a moth, who lays eggs.
8. (draw) Draw the path of one thread in a woven cloth and in a knitted cloth, side by side.  
   **Answer:** The woven thread goes straight across, over one thread and under the next. The knitted thread goes up and down in a row of loops.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the woven thread almost straight and the knitted thread looping, so it is clear which has spare length.
9. (mcq) Which bird stitches the edges of a leaf together to hold a nest?  
   Options: Baya weaver / Crow / Tailorbird / Kingfisher  
   **Answer:** Tailorbird. *Why:* The tailorbird pierces the leaf edges and pulls plant fibre or spider silk through. The baya weaver weaves a hanging nest from strips of grass.
10. (odd) Three of these are natural fibres. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: Cotton / Jute / Polyester / Wool  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* Cotton and jute come from plants, and wool from sheep. Polyester is made in a factory from petroleum.
11. (truefalse) True or false: twisting short fibres together makes a yarn that can hold a load.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The twist presses the fibres together, and friction stops them sliding apart. A lamp wick with little twist pulls apart easily.
12. (label) Label a drawing of a card loom: warp, weft, notch and comb.  
   **Answer:** ['Warp: the strings running up and down', 'Weft: the strip woven across', 'Notch: the cut that holds a warp string', 'Comb: pushes each row down']. *Why:* The warp is held tight by the notches. The weft goes over and under it, and the comb packs the rows together.
13. (perspective) Two people talk about an outgrown school shirt in good condition.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer sees that passing the shirt on keeps all the spinning, weaving and stitching, while recycling keeps only the fibre and shortens it. It agrees with one person and says when the other is right: a shirt too torn to wear is better recycled than thrown away.. *Why:* Both uses are better than the dustbin. The order matters: wear again first, recycle last.
14. (mcq) In a running stitch, the needle goes:  
   Options: round and round one hole / back one stitch each time / down and up along a line / through the top layer only  
   **Answer:** down and up along a line. *Why:* A running stitch goes down and up along a line and leaves a dashed line of thread. Going back each time makes a backstitch.
15. (write) Why does the cut edge of a bedsheet need a hem? Use the word 'fray'.  
   **Answer:** A cut edge of woven cloth will fray, because the threads along the edge have nothing outside them to hold them in. A hem folds the cut edge inside and stitches it down, so the threads cannot slide out.. *Why:* A strong answer says what fraying is and how the fold stops it, as in the Fray Test.
### Testing cloth and choosing fibres (stretch) (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) One row of thread pulled from 5 cm of T-shirt cloth is 18 cm long. What does this show?  
   Options: The cloth was measured wrongly / The thread is made of rubber / The thread runs in loops / Cotton grows when it is cut  
   **Answer:** The thread runs in loops. *Why:* The thread is more than 3 times as long as the cloth is wide, so it must be folded into loops. That spare length is where the stretch comes from.
2. (write) In the Leaky Seam Test, the longest stitches leaked most. Explain why, using the words 'needle holes' and 'gap'.  
   **Answer:** A running stitch pins the two cloths together only at the needle holes. Between the holes the cloths can pull apart and leave a gap. When the holes are 1 cm apart the gap is wide enough for a mustard seed. Short stitches leave only small gaps.. *Why:* A strong answer links the length of the stitch to the size of the gap, and the size of the gap to the size of the seed.
3. (truefalse) True or false: cloth cut on the slant frays more than cloth cut straight along the threads.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. On a slant cut no thread runs along the edge, so no whole thread can slide off. A straight cut frays most.
4. (fill) A pattern made by tying and dyeing the threads before weaving is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ikat']. *Why:* In ikat the pattern is dyed into the threads first. It appears, with feathered edges, as the cloth is woven.
5. (sort) Sort these cloths by how the pattern is made.  
   **Answer:** Kanchipuram border → Woven in; bandhani → Tied and dyed; kantha → Stitched; jamdani → Woven in; Pochampally ikat → Tied and dyed; Toda embroidery → Stitched; kasuti → Stitched; Paithani → Woven in. *Why:* Kanchipuram borders, jamdani and Paithani patterns are made on the loom with coloured threads. Bandhani and Pochampally ikat are tied and dyed. Kantha, Toda embroidery and kasuti are stitched with a needle.
6. (mcq) Which is the safest to wear beside a gas stove?  
   Options: A polyester jacket / A cotton kurta / A nylon dupatta / An acrylic sweater  
   **Answer:** A cotton kurta. *Why:* Synthetic fibres melt near a flame and stick to the skin. Cotton can burn, but it does not melt and stick.
7. (draw) Draw a seam with long running stitches, pulled open. Mark the place where a seed could pass.  
   **Answer:** Two cloths pulled apart with thread crossing between them like the rungs of a ladder. The gaps between the rungs are marked.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the cloth pinned only at the needle holes and open between them.
8. (perspective) Three people talk about silk.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses the facts: a moth who comes out breaks the filament, so reeled silk needs the pupa to die. Eri silk lets the moth out but is less shiny and costs more. It picks one view, gives a reason, and answers one point from another view fairly.. *Why:* This is a real disagreement about how to treat other animals. No answer is the only right one.
9. (order) Put these steps in order, from the cotton field to a finished shirt.  
   **Answer:** ['Pick the cotton', 'Spin the fibres into yarn', 'Weave or knit the yarn into cloth', 'Cut the cloth and stitch the pieces']. *Why:* Fibre comes first, then yarn, then cloth, then the garment. Dyeing can happen to the yarn or to the cloth.
10. (write) A male baya weaver has no hands. Describe how he starts a nest and what he builds it from.  
   **Answer:** He tears long strips from paddy, grass or palm leaves with his beak. He knots the first strips round a thin twig. Then he weaves more strips in and out with his beak, holding them with his feet.. *Why:* A strong answer names the material, the first knot and the weaving, and says that the beak does the work.
11. (match) Match each fibre to a job it suits.  
   **Answer:** Jute → a sack for rice; Wool → a winter shawl; Cotton → a bath towel; Coir → a doormat for the monsoon; Nylon → a school bag for rainy days. *Why:* Each fibre is chosen for what it does: jute is strong and cheap, wool traps air, cotton takes in water, coir stands up to water, and nylon keeps water out.
12. (mcq) Why is denim blue outside and pale inside?  
   Options: Only the outside is dyed / The inside is washed more often / The blue threads lie mostly on the outside / The inside is a different cloth  
   **Answer:** The blue threads lie mostly on the outside. *Why:* Denim is a twill. Its blue warp threads pass over three white weft threads and under one, so the outside shows mostly blue and the inside mostly white.
13. (odd) Three of these are knitted. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: T-shirt / Sock / Sweater / Sari  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A sari is woven on a loom from warp and weft. T-shirts, socks and sweaters are knitted from one thread in loops.
14. (label) Label a drawing of a hem seen from the side: the fold, the hidden cut edge and the stitches.  
   **Answer:** ['The fold at the bottom', 'The cut edge tucked inside', 'The stitches that hold the fold down']. *Why:* A hem works because the cut edge is shut inside the fold, where its threads cannot slide out.
15. (write) A sock has a ladder running down it. Explain how one broken loop caused it.  
   **Answer:** In knitted cloth each loop is held by the loop next to it in the column. When one loop breaks, the next loop has nothing to hold it and slips out, and then the one after that. The line of slipped loops is the ladder.. *Why:* A strong answer says that every loop depends on another loop, which is the cost of knitting with one thread.
### Weavers, evidence and arguments (challenge) (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (write) Cloth rots in the ground. Explain how a lump of fired clay can show that people wove 26,000 years ago.  
   **Answer:** Wet clay was pressed against woven cloth and took its print. The clay was then baked in a fire and became hard. The cloth rotted, but the print of crossing threads stayed in the clay, and the clay can be dated.. *Why:* A strong answer separates the cloth, which is gone, from the evidence, which is its print.
2. (perspective) Three people talk about handloom cloth.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer uses facts from the unit: a handloom is slow, needs no electricity and can weave patterns machines weave badly, and about 35 lakh people work in handloom. Mill cloth is cheap enough for everyone. It picks a side and gives one fair point from another side.. *Why:* This is a real trade-off between price, skill and people's work. There is no single right answer.
3. (mcq) A thread is pulled out of woven cloth 10 cm wide. About how long is the thread?  
   Options: About 5 cm / A little over 10 cm / About 30 cm / About 1 metre  
   **Answer:** A little over 10 cm. *Why:* A woven thread bends only slightly over and under the others, so it is just a little longer than the cloth is wide. A knitted thread would be about 30 cm.

5. (truefalse) True or false: yarn spun from torn-up old clothes is as strong as yarn spun from new fibre.

8. (fill) A ____ loom is driven by a motor, and a handloom is worked by a person.  
   **Answer:** ['power']. *Why:* A power loom weaves many metres in the time a handloom weaves one, which is why mill cloth is cheaper.

   **Answer:** They are quick, and easy to pull out. *Why:* Long running stitches are fast to sew, and the thread slides out easily afterwards. A strong seam is sewn later with small machine stitches.
11. (order) Put these in order, from the oldest to the newest.  
   **Answer:** ['Prints of woven cloth in clay at Pavlov', 'Cotton thread in a copper bead at Mehrgarh', 'The Jacquard loom with punched cards', 'Nylon, an early fully synthetic fibre']. *Why:* The clay prints are about 26,000 years old and the Mehrgarh thread about 7,000 to 8,000. Another: nothing has been lost, so the sweater is only waiting to be knitted again.. *Why:* A strong answer says clearly what the child thinks a sweater is, the material or the arrangement, and gives a reason.

   Options: It runs along the length, held tight / It runs across, over and under the warp / It loops through itself / It holds the hem down  
   **Answer:** It runs across, over and under the warp. *Why:* The weft runs across the cloth, over and under the warp threads. The warp is the set held tight along the length. If you chose loops, that is knitting.

   Options: It was knitted / It was woven / It was printed / It was embroidered

   Options: Its cotton is softer / Its thread is thinner / It has rubber in it / Its looped thread can open out  
   **Answer:** Its looped thread can open out. *Why:* A knitted row holds 3 or 4 times as much thread as the cloth is wide, bent into loops. A pull opens the loops. The cotton itself is the same.

   Options: Jute / Pashmina / Polyester / Linen  
   **Answer:** Pashmina. *Why:* Pashmina is the under-hair of a goat from Ladakh. Jute and linen come from plant stems, and polyester from a factory.
6. (mcq) What is inside a silk cocoon just before the moth comes out?  
   Options: An egg / A mulberry leaf / A pupa / Nothing  
   **Answer:** A pupa. *Why:* The caterpillar spins the cocoon and becomes a pupa inside. The pupa then becomes the moth. The egg stage came much earlier.
7. (mcq) Two seams join the same cloth, one with long stitches and one with short. Which lets more seeds through?  
   Options: The seam with long stitches / The seam with short stitches / Both let the same number through / Neither lets any through  
   **Answer:** The seam with long stitches. *Why:* The cloths are pinned together only at the needle holes. Long stitches leave long gaps between the holes, so more seeds pass.
8. (truefalse) True or false: the selvedge of a cloth frays faster than a cut edge.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. At the selvedge the weft turns back into the cloth, so there are no cut ends to slide out. A cut edge frays.
9. (mcq) In which of these is the pattern stitched on with a needle?  
   Options: Pochampally ikat / Bandhani / Ajrakh / Kasuti  
   **Answer:** Kasuti. *Why:* Kasuti is embroidery from Karnataka. Ikat and bandhani are tied and dyed, and ajrakh is printed with wooden blocks.
10. (mcq) What does a tailorbird use to stitch a leaf?  
   Options: Wet mud / Plant fibre or spider silk / Strips of grass woven over and under / Their own feathers  
   **Answer:** Plant fibre or spider silk. *Why:* A tailorbird pierces the leaf edge and pulls fibre or spider silk through. Weaving strips of grass is the baya weaver's way.
11. (mcq) An old cotton sari is too thin to wear. Which use keeps it as cloth?  
   Options: Burning it / Tearing it into fibre / Stitching it into a kantha quilt / Throwing it away  
   **Answer:** Stitching it into a kantha quilt. *Why:* In a kantha quilt the layers of old sari are still cloth, joined by running stitch. Tearing it into fibre keeps only the fibre.

## Applications
- Surgeons close wounds with stitches, and choose the stitch and the thread for each part of the body.
- Tyres and conveyor belts have layers of strong cords and woven cloth inside the rubber, which carry the load.
- Cloth woven from carbon fibre or glass fibre is set in resin to make light, strong parts for aircraft, rockets and cricket helmets.
- Mats woven from coir or jute are laid on new slopes beside roads. They hold the soil until grass grows, and then rot away.
- Doctors replace damaged blood vessels with tubes of woven or knitted polyester.
- Parachutes and car airbags are tightly woven nylon, so that very little air passes through.
- Sports clothes are knitted so that they stretch with the body, and many are made of polyester because it dries fast.

## Spotted around us
- The firm side edge of a sari, a veshti or a thorthu towel. That is the selvedge, and it never frays.
- Slanting lines on the outside of a pair of jeans, and the paler inside.
- A ladder in a sock or in a school stocking, running from one broken loop.
- Baya nests hanging from a palm or a babul tree beside a paddy field. In Tamil the bird is called thookkanaanguruvi, the small bird of the hanging nest.
- A ball of leaves stuck together on a mango or jackfruit tree. Weaver ants live inside.
- An old sari tied to a hook in the ceiling as a cradle for a baby.
- Leaf plates and leaf cups at a temple or a wedding, stitched together with tiny sticks.
- A tailor's shop: long loose stitches on a half-made blouse, and zigzag edges inside a seam.

## Open questions
- **How did weaving begin: with baskets, with nets or with mats?** The oldest cloth has rotted away. Only a few prints in clay and scraps of cord are left, and they are too few to show what came first.
- **How much harm do microfibres do to sea animals and to people?** The fibres have been found in water, salt, fish and air. Measuring what they do inside a living body over many years is slow and difficult work.
- **Can a cloth made of cotton and polyester together be turned back into good new yarn?** The two fibres are twisted together in every thread. Ways to separate them exist in laboratories, but they are costly and are only now being tried in factories.
- **How much of a baya's weaving is known from birth, and how much is learned?** Young males weave badly and improve. Nobody has yet followed wild bayas closely enough to say how much comes from practice and how much from watching others.
- **When did people first wear clothes?** The DNA of body lice says at least 83,000 years ago and perhaps 170,000. No clothes that old have survived to check.

## Connected maths
- In Woven or Knitted, a woven strip stretched 0.3 cm. A knitted strip cut across the T-shirt stretched 9 cm. How many times as much did the knitted strip stretch? **30 times as much.**. 0.3 cm is 3 tenths of a centimetre, and 9 cm is 90 tenths. 90 divided by 3 is 30. So the knitted strip stretched 30 times as much as the woven strip. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In Woven or Knitted, one row of thread pulled from 5 cm of T-shirt cloth was 18 cm long. The front of the T-shirt is 40 cm wide. About how long is the thread in one row across the front? **About 144 cm.**. 40 cm is 8 times 5 cm. Each 5 cm of cloth holds 18 cm of thread. 8 times 18 is 144. So one row across the front holds about 144 cm of thread. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L5`)
- One row across a T-shirt front holds about 144 cm of thread. The front is 60 cm long and has about 16 rows in every centimetre. About how many metres of thread are in the front? **About 1,380 metres, which is more than 1 kilometre.**. First find the rows: 60 times 16 is 960 rows. Each row holds 144 cm. 960 times 144 is 138,240 cm. Divide by 100 to change centimetres to metres: about 1,382 metres. That is about 1,380 metres. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L5`)
- In the Leaky Seam Test, a seam 8 cm long needs 8 stitches of 1 cm, or 16 stitches of half a centimetre. How many stitches a quarter of a centimetre long does it need? What is the pattern? **32 stitches. Each time the stitch length is halved, the number of stitches doubles.**. A quarter is half of a half. Halving the stitch length doubles the number of stitches: 8, then 16, then 32. The sewing time roughly doubles too. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- One family did the Leaky Seam Test. Their seam with 1 cm stitches let 12 seeds through. Their seam with half-centimetre stitches let 30 through. This is not the usual pattern. Name two things they should check. **They should check that both cloths were pulled equally flat over the tumbler, and that the second seam has no loose or missed stitch.**. Shorter stitches should leave smaller gaps and let fewer seeds through. So something other than stitch length must have changed. A slack cloth opens less than a tight one. One loose stitch leaves one big gap. They could also check that each seam was rubbed 20 times. (queues `math.logic.problem-solving.L5`)
- The Chindi Coaster has 10 warp threads and 16 rows of weft, so it has 160 crossings. A cotton sari has about 4,800 warp threads and about 22,000 rows of weft. About how many crossings hold the sari together? **About 10 crore 56 lakh crossings (105,600,000).**. Each row of weft crosses every warp thread once. So multiply: 4,800 times 22,000. 48 times 22 is 1,056. Then put back the five zeros: 105,600,000. That is 10 crore 56 lakh. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- A male baya weaver is said to make up to 500 trips to build one nest, in about 18 days. About how many trips is that each day, at most? If he works 10 hours a day, about how often does he arrive with a new strip? **At most about 28 trips a day, so a new strip about every 21 minutes.**. 500 divided by 18 is a little less than 28. Ten hours is 600 minutes. 600 divided by 28 is a little more than 21. So he would bring a new strip about every 21 minutes, all day, for 18 days. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
