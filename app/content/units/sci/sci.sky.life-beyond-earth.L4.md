# Life beyond Earth: how scientists look for signs of life on faraway worlds
`sci.sky.life-beyond-earth.L4`

**Summary.** A bottle of sugar water with yeast blows up a balloon, and a bottle without yeast does nothing. You never saw the yeast. You saw only what they did to the air. Scientists hunting for life on faraway planets work in the same way, because no telescope can see a creature on a planet trillions of kilometres away. They look for the gases that life leaves behind. In this unit your child tests which bottles hold life by their gas, finds how warm curd bacteria like the milk to be, and then asks where in space such signs might be found, and how sure we can ever be.

**Intro.** *Two bottles stand on a table, sealed and opaque. The balloon on one of them is slowly swelling, and the balloon on the other is flat. You cannot open them or look inside. What can you say about what is in each bottle?* You can say that something in the first bottle is making gas, and that something in the second is not. That is exactly the problem of a scientist who studies a planet from very far away. She cannot land, scoop up soil or look at a creature. She can only read the light that comes through a planet's air. In this unit you find out which bottles hold living yeast by the gas they leave, test the warmth that curd bacteria need, and learn how scientists read the air of worlds they will never visit.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain what makes a planet or moon a place where life could live: liquid water, energy, the right chemicals, and a temperature that suits.
1. I can explain why the habitable zone depends on the star, and why distance alone does not decide if a world is warm.
2. I can explain how living things can get energy from chemicals in the dark, and why that matters for Europa and Enceladus.
3. I can run a fair test to show that living yeast changes the gas around them, and explain what a biosignature is.
4. I can explain how scientists find planets and read their air with light, and why a gas alone does not prove life.
5. I can use a chain of guesses to estimate how many civilisations there might be, and say why the answer depends on the guesses.
6. I can say what counts as life, and why scientists disagree about fire, viruses and crystals.
7. I can say what scientists agree on, what they still argue about, and how to check a news story about life in space.

## Terms
- **Habitable zone**: The ring of distances around a star where a planet with an atmosphere could be warm enough to have liquid water on its surface. *e.g.* Earth is in the Sun's habitable zone. Venus is near the hot inner edge and Mars is near the cold outer edge.
- **Atmosphere**: The layer of gases that surrounds a planet or moon, held by its gravity. *e.g.* Earth's atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen. Venus has a thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide.
- **Greenhouse effect**: The warming of a planet when gases in its air hold back some of the heat that tries to escape into space. *e.g.* Venus's thick carbon dioxide makes it the hottest planet, hotter than Mercury, which is closer to the Sun.
- **Magnetic field**: An invisible shield around a planet that bends away fast particles from the Sun. Earth has one because of the moving metal deep inside. *e.g.* Earth's magnetic field helps to keep our air from being blown away by the solar wind.
- **Chemosynthesis**: Making food from chemicals, not from sunlight. Some bacteria do this, for example from hydrogen sulfide gas. Say it like this: kee-moh-SIN-thuh-sis. *e.g.* Bacteria at deep-sea vents make food from chemicals in the hot water, and tube worms live on that food.
- **Hydrothermal vent**: A crack in the sea floor where water heated deep underground rushes out, carrying dissolved chemicals. *e.g.* Life thrives around hydrothermal vents in the dark deep sea, where sunlight never reaches.
- **Biosignature**: A sign that living things have been there. It may be a gas, a chemical, a shape or a pattern that is hard to explain without life. *e.g.* A balloon swelling on a bottle of yeast and sugar is a biosignature of living yeast.
- **Spectrum**: Light spread out into its colours, like a rainbow. A gas takes away its own colours from light, so a spectrum shows which gases the light passed through. *e.g.* Light from a star that passes through a planet's air has dark gaps in its spectrum for each gas in the air.
- **Exoplanet**: A planet that goes round a star other than the Sun. *e.g.* TRAPPIST-1 has seven exoplanets going round it.
- **Transit**: When a planet passes in front of its star as we see it, and blocks a tiny part of the star's light. *e.g.* Astronomers find many exoplanets by noticing that a star dims a tiny bit every few days.
- **Red dwarf**: A small, dim, cool star. Red dwarfs are the most common stars in our galaxy. *e.g.* TRAPPIST-1 is a red dwarf only a little bigger than Jupiter.
- **Drake equation**: A chain of estimates, started by Frank Drake in 1961, for how many civilisations there might be in our galaxy who could send signals. *e.g.* The Drake equation multiplies the number of stars by the share with planets, the share of those with life, and so on.
- **Methane**: A gas made of carbon and hydrogen. On Earth, most methane is made by living things such as microbes in cattle stomachs and in wet paddy fields. *e.g.* Titan has lakes of liquid methane, and Mars has a tiny, puzzling amount of methane in its air.
- **Carbon dioxide**: A gas made of carbon and oxygen. Living things breathe it out, and plants take it in. Venus and Mars have air that is mostly carbon dioxide. *e.g.* Your breath holds about a hundred times more carbon dioxide than the air you breathed in.
- **Yeast**: A tiny living thing, a kind of fungus, that is one cell. Yeast eats sugar and makes carbon dioxide gas. Bakers use yeast to make bread rise. *e.g.* In idli and dosa batter, yeast and bacteria make gas, so the batter swells and gets bubbles.
- **Organic molecule**: A molecule built on a framework of carbon atoms, like the molecules that living things are made of. Organic does not mean alive, because non-living chemistry can make them too. *e.g.* Curiosity, a rover on Mars, found organic molecules in an old rock.
- **Light-year**: The distance that light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion kilometres. Astronomers use it to measure distances between stars. *e.g.* TRAPPIST-1 is about 40 light-years away, so its light left it about 40 years ago.
- **Plume**: A jet of gas, water or ice that shoots out of a world into space. *e.g.* Enceladus has plumes that spray out of cracks near its south pole.

## Explanations
### A habitable zone depends on the star · *defined*
**Definition:** The habitable zone is the ring of distances from a star where a planet could be warm enough to have liquid water on its surface. A bright star has a zone far out, and a dim star has a zone close in.

A planet gets warmer the closer it is to its star, because the light spreads out as it travels. Too close and water boils off. Too far and it freezes. Between them is the habitable zone, sometimes called the Goldilocks zone, after a story of porridge that was not too hot and not too cold. Different stars have different zones. The Sun is bright, so its zone lies at about the distance of Earth. A red dwarf like TRAPPIST-1 is small and dim, so its zone is very close in, even closer than Mercury is to the Sun. Being inside the zone does not make a world habitable. It only means that liquid water on the surface is possible, if the world also has air and the right amount of water.
- e.g. Earth is about 150 million kilometres from the Sun, in the middle of the Sun's zone.
- e.g. Venus is about 108 million kilometres from the Sun, and is far too hot.
- e.g. TRAPPIST-1's habitable zone is only a few million kilometres from the star.
### More than distance: air, size and a magnetic field · *defined*
**Definition:** A world's temperature depends on its distance from the star, and also on its atmosphere, its size and its magnetic field.

Venus is only a little closer to the Sun than Earth, but it is about 460 degrees Celsius on the surface, hot enough to melt lead. The reason is its air. Venus has a very thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide, and that gas holds back heat. This is called the greenhouse effect. Mars is the opposite. It is smaller than Earth, so its gravity is weaker, and it has lost most of its air. Its air is now less than a hundredth as thick as ours, and the average temperature is about minus 60 degrees. Scientists think Mars lost its global magnetic field long ago, which let the solar wind strip away much of its air. Earth has a thick enough atmosphere and a magnetic field, which helps to keep its water.
- e.g. Venus is hotter than Mercury, though Mercury is much closer to the Sun, because of Venus's thick air.
- e.g. Mars's thin air cannot hold heat or water on its surface, so water there is mostly ice.
- e.g. Earth's magnetic field bends away fast particles from the Sun, which helps to keep our air.
### Life without sunlight: chemosynthesis · *defined*
**Definition:** Some bacteria make food from chemicals, not from sunlight. This is called chemosynthesis. It lets life live in places with no light.

Plants make food using sunlight. In 1977, scientists in the submarine Alvin found a crowd of animals around hot vents on the dark sea floor, far below any sunlight. There were giant tube worms, clams and crabs. Their food chain starts with bacteria. The bacteria take hydrogen sulfide, a gas that comes out of the vent, and join it with oxygen from the sea water. The chemical reaction gives out energy, which the bacteria use to make food. A giant tube worm has no mouth and no stomach. Instead, bacteria live inside her body and feed her. So a living world needs an energy supply, but the energy does not have to be light. This means that life could live in the dark oceans under the ice of Europa and Enceladus, if hot rock below gives them chemicals.
- e.g. A giant tube worm lives on sugars made by the bacteria inside her body.
- e.g. Hydrothermal vents were found on the sea floor near the Galapagos Islands in 1977.
- e.g. Microbes in the dark rocks deep under the ground also live on chemicals, with no light.
### Enceladus and Europa: oceans under the ice · *idea*
**Definition:** Enceladus and Europa very probably hold salty oceans under their ice. Spacecraft have found chemicals in the plumes of Enceladus that life could use, but nobody has found life.

The Cassini spacecraft flew through the plumes of Enceladus, a small moon of Saturn, many times. It found water, salt and some organic molecules in the spray. In 2017 scientists reported that it also found hydrogen, a gas that could be food for microbes at hot vents on the moon's sea floor. In 2023 other scientists reported that the grains contained phosphates, which life needs to build DNA. Europa, a moon of Jupiter, is bigger, and its ocean may hold more water than all of Earth's oceans. The Europa Clipper spacecraft was launched in 2024 to study the moon. Both moons are warmed inside by the pull of their planet, which squeezes them a little more and a little less as they go round. These are strong clues, but not proof of life.
- e.g. Cassini flew through Enceladus's plumes and tasted the water for salt, hydrogen and organic molecules.
- e.g. Europa Clipper is planned to reach Jupiter in 2030 and fly past Europa many times.
- e.g. Saturn's pull squeezes Enceladus a little more and a little less as it goes round, which warms the moon inside.
### Mars: old lakes, strange rocks and a puzzling gas · *debated*
Mars has dry river valleys and old lake beds, so it once had liquid water. In 2018, NASA's Curiosity rover reported organic molecules in an old rock. These molecules are the kind that life is built from, but non-living chemistry can make them too. Curiosity also measured a very small amount of methane in the air, which seemed to go up and down with the seasons. On Earth most methane is made by life, but it can also be made by rocks. Another spacecraft later found much less methane. In 2024, the Perseverance rover studied a rock named Cheyava Falls, with small spots like a leopard's. NASA said this was a possible sign of past life, but the spots may also have formed without life. Samples have been collected, but they are still on Mars.
- e.g. Perseverance has been collecting rock samples from Jezero crater, an old lake bed, since 2021.
- e.g. Curiosity found organic molecules in an old rock in Gale crater, another ancient lake.
- e.g. Methane on Mars was reported to rise and fall with the seasons, but another spacecraft found very little.
- **Some scientists on the Perseverance team:** The leopard spots on Cheyava Falls look like marks made by microbes on Earth. This is a possible sign of past life, and the samples should be brought to Earth.
- **Other scientists:** The same spots can form without life, from chemical reactions in the mud. Without a sample in a lab here, we cannot be sure.
- **Scientists today:** Everyone agrees the rock is interesting. Bringing the sample back to labs on Earth would give a much better test, but that mission is uncertain.
### Titan: a world with a different kind of liquid · *idea*
**Definition:** Titan, a moon of Saturn, has lakes and seas of liquid methane and ethane on its surface, and a thick nitrogen atmosphere. It is too cold for water to be liquid there.

Titan is the only moon in our solar system with a thick atmosphere. It is about minus 179 degrees on the surface. At that cold, rock is hard as steel and water is ice. But methane and ethane, gases on Earth, are liquid. Rain falls, rivers run and lakes fill. In 2005 the Huygens probe landed on Titan. NASA plans to send a flying lander, called Dragonfly, in the late 2020s. Some scientists wonder whether a kind of life could use methane as its liquid instead of water. Nobody has found such life, and nobody knows if it could exist. Under the ice, Titan may also have a layer of liquid water. Titan shows us that life as we know it may not be the only kind to look for.
- e.g. Rivers of liquid methane have carved valleys on Titan, like rivers of water on Earth.
- e.g. The Huygens probe sent back pictures from Titan's surface in 2005.
- e.g. Dragonfly is a planned drone-like lander that will fly from place to place on Titan.
### Biosignatures: signs of life in the air of a world · *defined*
**Definition:** A biosignature is a sign that life has been there. The best-known one is a mix of gases in a planet's air that would not last together unless living things kept making them.

Earth's air is about one fifth oxygen and has a little methane. Oxygen and methane react with each other, and in a few years they would disappear. They are still there because living things, plants, algae and microbes, keep making them. In 1965 the scientist James Lovelock pointed out that this is how an observer could tell that Earth has life without landing. A world whose air has gases that react with each other, but which never run out, is probably being refilled by something. This is called an atmosphere out of balance. Yeast in a bottle show the same idea. The sugar and yeast keep making carbon dioxide, and the balloon stays swollen.
- e.g. Without plants and microbes, the oxygen in Earth's air would be used up in a geologically short time.
- e.g. Most of the methane in Earth's air is made by living things such as microbes in wet rice fields and in the stomachs of cattle.
- e.g. A bottle of yeast and sugar swells a balloon, but a bottle of sugar alone does not.
### False alarms: when a gas is not a sign of life · *debated*
Oxygen on its own is not proof of life. On some planets, strong light from a star can split water or carbon dioxide and make oxygen with no living thing involved. In 2020, scientists reported a gas called phosphine in the clouds of Venus. On Earth phosphine is made mostly by microbes. Other teams looked again and found much less, or none. In 2023, a team led by Nikku Madhusudhan, an astrophysicist born in India who works in Cambridge, used the James Webb Space Telescope on a planet called K2-18 b, about 120 light-years away. They found methane and carbon dioxide. There was also a hint of dimethyl sulfide, a gas that on Earth is made mostly by tiny sea plankton. Other teams re-examined the data and did not agree that the hint was real.
- e.g. A hot planet could make oxygen by splitting water in its air, with no life.
- e.g. The Venus phosphine claim of 2020 was questioned by other teams who looked at the same data.
- e.g. The K2-18 b hint of dimethyl sulfide is not accepted by all astronomers.
- **Scientists who reported the K2-18 b hint:** The signal of a gas that on Earth is made by life is worth following. More observations with the James Webb Space Telescope could make it clearer.
- **Other scientists who re-examined the data:** The hint is weak and may be noise. Other chemistry could make the gases, and the planet's nature is not even sure.
- **Scientists today:** One gas will never be enough. A claim of life needs several signs that fit together, repeated by different teams, and no better non-living explanation.
### How we find planets and read their air · *defined*
**Definition:** Astronomers find many exoplanets by the tiny dip in a star's light when a planet passes in front, called a transit. They read a planet's air from the colours missing in the starlight that passes through it.

A planet is far too faint to see next to its bright star. But when it crosses in front of the star, the starlight dims a tiny bit. Jupiter would block about 1 part in 100 of the Sun's light, and Earth only about 1 part in 10,000. If the dip repeats, the planet is real, and the time between dips is the length of its year. When the planet is in front, a little starlight also passes through the thin ring of its air. Each gas takes away its own colours from the light. So the spectrum, the starlight spread into a rainbow, has dark gaps that show which gases are there. In 2022 the James Webb Space Telescope found carbon dioxide in the air of a hot gas planet named WASP-39 b. In 2018, scientists at the Physical Research Laboratory in Ahmedabad reported the first planet found from India, using a telescope at Mount Abu.
- e.g. A star that dims by 1 part in 100 every 6 days has a big planet with a 6-day year.
- e.g. Kepler, a space telescope launched in 2009, found thousands of planets with the transit method.
- e.g. Each gas in a planet's air takes away its own set of colours from the starlight.
### TRAPPIST-1: seven worlds around a red dwarf · *idea*
**Definition:** TRAPPIST-1 is a small red dwarf star with seven Earth-sized planets going round it. Three of them lie in the habitable zone.

In 2017, astronomers led by Michael Gillon in Belgium reported seven planets going round TRAPPIST-1, a dim red star about 40 light-years from Earth. The star is only a little bigger than Jupiter, and all seven planets orbit closer to it than Mercury does to our Sun. Their years last only from 1.5 to about 19 days. Planets e, f and g are often named as lying in the habitable zone. But a red dwarf sends out strong flares, and the planets are probably locked so that one side always faces the star. In 2023 the James Webb Space Telescope looked at the two inner planets and reported that they seem to have no thick atmosphere. Nobody yet knows if any of the outer planets has air, or water. This is a good place to test the methods in this unit.
- e.g. TRAPPIST-1 is so close to its planets that a year on one of them is less than two Earth days.
- e.g. The James Webb Space Telescope has begun to study the air of the TRAPPIST-1 planets.
- e.g. A planet locked to its star has one side in permanent day and one side in permanent night.
### The Drake equation: a chain of guesses · *idea*
**Definition:** The Drake equation multiplies a chain of numbers to estimate how many civilisations in our galaxy might send signals. Some numbers are known and some are guesses, so the answer depends mostly on the guesses.

In 1961, the astronomer Frank Drake wrote a list of the numbers that decide how many civilisations we might hear from. The first numbers are the number of stars in the galaxy, which is at least 100 billion, and the share with planets, which astronomers now think is most. After that, the numbers become guesses. What share of habitable planets get life? What share of life becomes intelligent? How long does a civilisation last and send signals? If you guess that each step is easy, you might get millions of civilisations. If you guess that one step is very rare, you might get only ourselves. This does not tell us the answer. It tells us which numbers we most need to find out, such as how often life begins.
- e.g. Astronomers can now count planets, so one early number of the chain is no longer a guess.
- e.g. Nobody knows how often life begins when conditions are right, because we have only one example.
- e.g. Guessing one step is very rare can reduce the answer from millions of civilisations to almost none.
### What counts as alive? · *debated*
A working definition used by NASA is that life is a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution. That means it keeps itself going using energy, and it passes on changes so that its kind can adapt over many generations. By this idea, yeast, a tardigrade and a banyan tree are alive. A fire grows and uses fuel, but does not pass on a code, so it is not alive. A virus carries a code and evolves, but cannot make more of itself without a host cell, so many scientists say a virus is on the edge. A crystal grows too, but does not use energy to keep itself going. Any definition built from life on Earth may miss life that works in another way, and scientists keep debating it.
- e.g. A fire uses fuel and grows, but does not copy a code, so it is not usually called alive.
- e.g. A virus carries a code and evolves, but needs a host cell to make more virus.
- e.g. A seed can sit for years doing almost nothing and still be alive.
- **Scientists who use the NASA definition:** Life is a chemical system that keeps itself going and can evolve. It is a useful working idea for deciding what to look for.
- **Scientists who think any definition is too narrow:** We have only one example of life. A definition built from it may leave out forms of life that work in other ways.
- **Scientists today:** There is no agreed answer. Most work with a flexible idea and judge each case by how many of the signs of life it shows.
### Listening for signals · *defined*
**Definition:** SETI, the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence, uses radio telescopes to listen for signals that natural things could not make. No such signal has been confirmed.

A civilisation that wanted to be noticed could send a radio signal. Radio waves cross space quickly and are easy to receive. SETI scientists such as Jill Tarter have listened to many stars for decades. They look for a signal that is very narrow, in a single pitch, because nature rarely makes such a signal. On 15 August 1977, the Big Ear radio telescope in Ohio, in the United States, recorded a strong narrow signal that lasted for 72 seconds. Jerry Ehman, who found it in the printout, wrote Wow! next to it. It has never been heard again, and nobody knows what made it. Signals take time. A message from a star 40 light-years away would take 40 years to arrive.
- e.g. The Wow! signal of 1977 was strong and narrow but never repeated.
- e.g. Most strange signals turn out to come from satellites, aircraft or machines on Earth.
- e.g. A reply to a message sent to a star 40 light-years away would take at least 80 years to come back.

## Mostly agreed
- A planet's temperature depends on its air as well as its distance from the star. *How we know:* Venus is about 460 degrees Celsius, hotter than Mercury, which is closer to the Sun. Space probes measured both, and the cause is the thick carbon dioxide air of Venus.
- Europa and Enceladus very probably have salty oceans of liquid water under their ice. *How we know:* The Cassini spacecraft flew through spray from Enceladus and found salt and water. Magnetic measurements by the Galileo spacecraft fit a salty ocean on Europa.
- Some living things get their energy from chemicals, not from sunlight. *How we know:* In 1977 scientists found animals around hot vents on the dark sea floor. Their food starts with bacteria that make food from hydrogen sulfide.
- Organic molecules exist on Mars. *How we know:* In 2018 the Curiosity rover reported organic molecules in an old rock. Organic molecules can also be made without life, so this does not show life.
- There are thousands of planets going round other stars. *How we know:* More than 5,000 exoplanets have been confirmed, found by the dip of starlight in a transit and by the tiny wobble they cause in their stars.
- A gas in a planet's air can be read from its starlight. *How we know:* In 2022 the James Webb Space Telescope found carbon dioxide in the air of the planet WASP-39 b, many light-years away, from the colours missing in the starlight.
- No life has been found anywhere except on Earth. *How we know:* Every claim so far, from canals on Mars to gas on Venus, has had another explanation that most scientists find more likely.

## How we found out
**James Lovelock, a British scientist, and Dian Hitchcock, an American scientist working with NASA, 1965 to 1967.** In the early 1960s, NASA planned to land spacecraft on Mars to look for life. Most scientists thought the only way was to scoop up soil and test it for living things. James Lovelock, a British scientist asked to help, thought of another way. He asked what an observer far away would learn by studying Earth's air. Earth's air holds oxygen and methane together, even though they react and should disappear. They stay because living things keep making them. Mars's air looked like a settled mix, with nothing refilling it. In 1965 Lovelock wrote that Mars was probably lifeless and that a planet's air could show life from far away. In 1967, with Dian Hitchcock, an American scientist who worked at NASA, he published a longer paper on the idea. Today, telescopes read the air of faraway planets in just this way.  
*What people believed before:* Many scientists thought the only way to find out if a planet has life was to land and test its soil. Lovelock and Hitchcock showed that a planet's air, studied from far away, can reveal whether something is keeping it out of balance.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Life can only live in the habitable zone, at the right distance from a star.** (A natural guess, from the Goldilocks idea.; *debated*)  
  For: Liquid water on a surface needs the right warmth, and a planet too near or too far loses it.  
  Against: Europa and Enceladus are far outside the Sun's zone, yet probably have oceans, because their planet's pull warms them inside. Life might live under ice, far from any star's warmth.
- **If a planet's air has oxygen, it must have life.** (Many people, and some early papers.; *rejected*)  
  For: On Earth, nearly all oxygen comes from plants, algae and microbes.  
  Against: On some planets, strong starlight can split water or carbon dioxide and make oxygen with no life. A gas alone is not enough. Scientists look for several signs that fit together.
- **The 2024 leopard-spot rock on Mars shows that microbes once lived there.** (Some commentators and news stories.; *debated*)  
  For: The spots look like marks made by microbes in rocks on Earth, and NASA called them a possible biosignature.  
  Against: The same spots can form from chemistry alone. Scientists say they need to study the sample in laboratories on Earth, and the plan to bring it back is uncertain.
- **The methane on Mars is made by microbes.** (Some scientists, after the Curiosity rover measured methane in 2013 to 2019.; *debated*)  
  For: On Earth most methane is made by life, and Curiosity saw it rise and fall with the seasons.  
  Against: Rocks can make methane without life, and another spacecraft later measured much less. The amount is tiny and its source is unknown.
- **A hint of dimethyl sulfide on the planet K2-18 b means it has life.** (Reports of a team led by Nikku Madhusudhan, 2023 and 2025.; *debated*)  
  For: On Earth, dimethyl sulfide is made mostly by sea plankton, and the team saw a hint of it.  
  Against: Other teams looked at the data again and found the hint was weak or not there. Non-living chemistry may also make it, and the planet itself is not well understood.
- **The Viking landers found life on Mars in 1976.** (Gilbert Levin, who designed one of the Viking tests, and a few others.; *minority*)  
  For: A test that fed nutrients to Martian soil gave off gas, as living microbes would.  
  Against: Another Viking instrument found no organic molecules. Later missions found chemicals in Martian soil that could explain the gas without life. Most scientists do not accept the claim.
- **All life must use liquid water.** (Most biologists, because all known life does.; *debated*)  
  For: Every living thing found so far needs liquid water to carry out the reactions of life.  
  Against: We have only one kind of life to study. Some scientists ask whether other liquids, like methane on Titan, could host other chemistry. Nobody has found such life.
- **If aliens existed, we would have heard from them by now.** (Enrico Fermi's question of 1950 and many who repeat it.; *debated*)  
  For: The galaxy is old and has billions of stars, so a civilisation had a long time to spread or send signals.  
  Against: We have listened to a tiny part of the sky and a tiny set of signal types for a short time. Signals take years to cross space, and a civilisation may not last long or may not send any.

## Roots
We have tested bottles of yeast, cups of curd and the air of other worlds. Now we ask what it means that life shows itself by what it changes, and how sure anyone can be of what they cannot see.

**Deep truth.** Only the bottle with yeast and sugar made gas and blew up the balloon. From outside, you could not see the yeast, only the gas they kept making. On a far planet, life might show as gases that would fade away unless living things kept making them. So we may find life by what it keeps changing, long before we see a creature.  
*Habit it breaks:* We expect to find life by seeing a creature. But from far away, the first sign would be a change in the air.  
*Evidence:* In the Bottle Biosignature test, the yeast and sugar bottle swelled the balloon on top, and the bottles with no yeast, no sugar or dead yeast stayed flat. If your own results differ, write them down as they are and test again.

- *who am i:* Each breath you breathe out holds about a hundred times more carbon dioxide than the air you breathed in. Your body changes the air around you all the time.
- *what is life:* Yeast, curd bacteria and idli batter all show themselves by what they do to the air, bubbles and a lifted lid, long before we see anything.
- *what is this world:* Earth's air is full of oxygen only because plants and microbes keep making it. Without them, it would fade away.

Life is not only the creatures. It is also what they do to the world around them. Yeast change the air in a bottle. Plants and microbes keep Earth's air full of oxygen. Bacteria around a hot vent turn rock chemicals into food. From a distance, only the changes can be seen. This means that a living thing and its surroundings cannot be pulled apart cleanly. When scientists read the air of a distant planet, they are asking whether something there is a part of the planet's chemistry, steadily changing it. The question that remains is how sure we can be of what we cannot see.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.12 (the banyan seed):** Uddalaka asks his son Svetaketu to break open a banyan fruit, and then a seed inside the fruit. 'What do you see?' 'Nothing, sir.' 'That fine thing you do not see, from that this great banyan tree stands.' (We cannot see yeast, but a swollen balloon shows they are there. The unseen shows itself by what it does.)
- **Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 (Buddhist):** Do not accept something only because of report, tradition or hearsay. When you know for yourselves what is good and what is harmful, then act on it. (A news story that says 'signs of life found' should be checked. Scientists ask what was observed and what was only guessed.)
- **Thirukkural 423:** Whatever the matter, and whoever says it, wisdom is to find the true meaning in it. (A claim about life in space might come from a famous person or from a child. The test is the evidence, not the speaker.)
- Science (settled): Living things change the gases around them, and these changes can be measured. On Earth, plants and microbes keep oxygen and methane in the air together, out of balance.
- Science (idea): A mix of gases that would not last together may be a sign of life on a far planet. Scientists are testing how often non-living chemistry can fake it.
- Science (open): Whether life exists on any world beyond Earth is not known.
- Other view, Scientists who are careful about claims of life: Gases alone can never prove that something is alive, because non-living chemistry can make nearly every gas that life makes. Only a living sample, seen to grow and copy itself in a laboratory, would settle it. Until then, every claim should be treated as a question. That is an honest and careful view.
- Practice, Listen for what you cannot see (3 min): Sit still and close your eyes. Put one hand on your chest and feel your heart. Put the other hand in front of your nose and feel your breath. You cannot see your heart or your breath. How do you know they are there? Breathe slowly three times, and notice what your body is doing to the air.
- Ask: How would you know that something is alive if you could never see it?
- Ask: If a gas on a far planet looked exactly like a sign of life, but nobody could be sure, what should we do?
- Ask: Is a planet with life on it more than a planet plus living things, or is it one thing?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* You cannot see yeast, but you can see a balloon swell. Is a balloon good evidence of life? (hint: Think about what else could swell a balloon, and how you could test it.)
- *deep:* Venus is hotter than Mercury, though Mercury is closer to the Sun. What does this tell you about planets? (hint: Think about what the air does to heat.)
- *deep:* Why can bacteria live on chemicals in the dark, and a plant cannot? (hint: Think about where each gets its energy.)
- *deep:* A cup of milk with a spoon of curd does not set in a fridge, but sets later when moved somewhere warm. Were the curd bacteria dead or asleep, and how could you tell? (hint: Think about what you saw when you moved it somewhere warm.)
- *big:* If we find a gas that could be a sign of life, how much proof is enough to say there is life? (hint: Think about who should check the claim, and what would change your mind.)
- *big:* Many news stories say 'signs of life found on another planet'. Who should we trust, and how can a child check? (hint: Think about the difference between what was observed and what was guessed.)
- *big:* Is it better to spend money on looking for life in space, or on caring for life on Earth? (hint: Think about what each could teach us, and whether the two can help each other.)
- *big:* If we found a living microbe on Mars, would that change how you feel about Earth? (hint: Think about what it would mean if life started more than once.)

## Story: The Batter on the Balcony
*A made-up story. The family, the kitchen and the winter morning in Bengaluru are imagined. The science is real. Idli batter rises when yeast and bacteria make gas, cold slows them down, and heat that is too great kills them.*

It was the coldest week in Bengaluru, and the idli batter had not risen.

Thenmozhi lifted the lid of the steel container on the balcony. Last night the batter had been smooth and white. This morning it was smooth and white. It lay in the container like a flat pond.

'It's dead,' said her brother Hari. He was seven, and he liked announcing things. 'The batter has died. No idli.'

Their grandmother, Paati, had come from Madurai for the week, because Amma was away at a conference. She leaned over the container and sniffed it. Then she shook her head slowly.

'Not dead,' said Paati. 'Asleep. The little ones who make the batter rise do not like cold. It was twelve degrees last night.'

'What little ones?' said Hari.

'The yeast, and the bacteria,' said Paati. 'They are too small to see, but they are in every spoon.'

Thenmozhi frowned. 'How do you know they are even alive, if we cannot see them?'

Paati pointed at the spoon in the container. 'When they are awake, three things happen. The batter swells, and it lifts the lid. Tiny bubbles come up through it. And the smell turns a little sour. You never see them. You see what they do to the air.'

Thenmozhi sat down on the floor with a bump. She had read about Mars the night before. 'Paati, that is how scientists look for life on other planets! They cannot see any creature on a planet that far away. They look at the gases.'

'Then you are a scientist,' said Paati, and her eyes were bright. 'Find me the planet where my little ones can live.'

So they held a search. Thenmozhi spooned a little batter into four small steel katoris, and covered each one. She put one on the cold balcony and called it Mars. She put one on the shelf above the fridge, where the back of the machine gave a gentle warmth. She put one inside the oven, which was switched off, with just the little oven light on. And Hari, grinning, put one on top of the hot water geyser.

'That is Venus,' he said. 'The hot planet.'

'Venus will not work,' said Paati. 'But let him. Nothing teaches like a test that fails.'

They waited all afternoon. Every hour, Thenmozhi lifted each lid and looked.

By evening, Mars had not changed at all. It was flat and cold. Venus on the geyser was flat as well, and it smelled like cooked flour. The cup above the fridge had a few bubbles. But the cup in the oven, with its small light, had risen into a soft white dome. Its lid sat crooked, pushed up by the gas. When Thenmozhi smelled it, it was sour and sweet at once.

'That one is awake,' said Hari. 'That is the Earth cup.'

'And Venus is dead,' said Thenmozhi. 'The heat was too much for the little ones.'

'But look at Mars,' said Paati. 'Move him in with Earth.'

Thenmozhi carried the cold katori into the warm oven. She had to wait all night. In the morning, Mars had a few bubbles, and by lunchtime it had a small dome of its own.

'It was only asleep!' said Hari.

Thenmozhi nodded slowly. 'Cold is a pause,' she said. 'Heat that is too great is the end. Venus never woke up.'

Paati poured the good batter into the idli plates, and the kitchen filled with steam. Hari looked at the container with the dome of batter in it. 'If a spaceship came and looked at our kitchen from far away,' he said, 'how would the visitors know there was life in there?'

'They could not see the yeast,' said Thenmozhi. 'But they could see the gas.'

**For the parent:** We cannot see tiny living things from far away, but we can see what they do to their surroundings. A lifted lid, a swollen balloon, or a gas that nothing else explains is a sign that something alive is there.

- Ask: Why can a swelling balloon or a lifted lid show that tiny living things are present, though you cannot see them?
- Ask: Why did the batter in the hot place not rise, though the batter on the cold balcony did later?
- Ask: How is looking at a planet's air like looking at the batter's lid?
- Ask: What other explanation could there be for bubbles, besides living things?

## Worksheets
### Reading the Clues (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which is the best description of a biosignature?  
   Options: A sign that living things have been there, such as a gas that would not last without them / A living creature seen through a telescope / Any gas found in a planet's air  
   **Answer:** A sign that living things have been there, such as a gas that would not last without them. *Why:* A biosignature is a sign of life, not the life itself. It might be a gas, a chemical or a pattern that is hard to explain without life. Picking 'any gas' misses that most gases can be made without life.
2. (mcq) Four bottles each hold warm water and sugar. Only the one with living yeast swells the balloon. Why is the bottle with sugar but no yeast important?  
   Options: It shows that sugar alone does not make the gas / It shows that balloons swell by themselves / It is there to use up the extra sugar  
   **Answer:** It shows that sugar alone does not make the gas. *Why:* The bottle with sugar alone is the control. It stays flat, so the gas in the other bottle must come from the yeast. Picking 'balloons swell by themselves' misses that the same balloon stayed flat.
3. (fill) Yeast eat sugar and give out a gas called carbon ____.  
   **Answer:** ['dioxide']. *Why:* Carbon dioxide is the gas that swells the balloon. It is also the gas in the bubbles in idli batter and in the bread dough that rises.
4. (truefalse) Venus is hotter than Mercury because Venus is closer to the Sun.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Mercury is closer to the Sun. Venus is hotter because it has a thick carbon dioxide atmosphere that traps heat.
5. (sort) Sort these worlds. Around the Sun, is each one too hot, just right or too cold on its surface?  
   **Answer:** Mercury → Too hot; Venus → Too hot; Earth → Just right; Mars → Too cold; Jupiter → Too cold. *Why:* Mercury and Venus are hot, Venus because of its thick air. Earth has liquid water. Mars is cold because it is farther and has lost most of its air. Jupiter is very far from the Sun.

## Applications
- Bread, idli, dosa and curd all depend on tiny living things that change the food and give out gas, which is the same sign scientists look for on other worlds.
- The transit method that finds planets also lets astronomers study stars that pulse or have spots.
- Spectra, the colours missing from light, are used to test the purity of water, the content of foods and the gases in smoke.
- Telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope read the air of faraway planets from the colours missing in starlight.
- Gas sensors for carbon dioxide and methane are used on farms, in landfills and in food packaging to detect living microbes.
- Enzymes from microbes that live in hot springs and cold seas are used in laboratories, in detergents and in medicine.
- Clean rooms for space probes use the same methods as hospitals, so that Earth germs are not carried to other worlds.
- Satellite pictures of Earth show forests turning green in the monsoon, which is how living things can be seen from space.
- Fermi estimates, chains of rough guesses like the Drake chain, are used to count tigers, to plan hospitals and to guess the size of a crowd.
- ISRO's Mars Orbiter Mission, which reached Mars in 2014, carried a sensor to look for methane in the Martian air.
- Planetary protection rules for spacecraft are shared between space agencies of many countries.
- Radio telescopes in India, such as the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope near Pune, listen to the sky for faint natural signals.

## Open questions
- **Is there life anywhere beyond Earth, even a microbe?** No life has been found yet. Scientists have only looked at a few places, and the best places, like the oceans under the ice of Europa and Enceladus, have not been sampled.
- **Did life begin more than once, or only once on Earth?** All life on Earth shares one family tree. We cannot tell if life began many times and only one family survived, because we have only one example.
- **What made the leopard-spot rock on Mars?** The spots may have formed with the help of microbes or by chemistry alone. The sample is still on Mars, and laboratories on Earth have not studied it.
- **Where does the methane on Mars come from?** Spacecraft have measured different amounts at different times. Nobody knows if it comes from rocks, from old ice, or from life.
- **Could life use a liquid other than water, such as the methane of Titan?** Nobody has found such life, and nobody has shown it is impossible. We know only one kind of life.
- **Why have we not heard from anyone, if the galaxy has so many stars?** We have listened to a small part of the sky for a short time. There are many possible answers, including that life is rare, and none has been tested.

Each crossing comes once every 4 days. 60 divided by 4 is 15. You can check it by counting in fours: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 44, 48, 52, 56, 60. That is 15 fours. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A message sent to a star 40 light-years away travels at the speed of light. If someone there replied at once, how many years would pass before you got the answer? **80 years.**. The message takes 40 years to get there. The reply takes 40 years to come back. 40 plus 40 is 80 years. You would need to wait a lifetime for the answer. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.mental-strategies.L4`)
- A Drake chain starts with 100,000,000,000 stars. It divides by 5, then by 10, then by 100. How many are left? **20,000,000.**. 100,000,000,000 divided by 5 is 20,000,000,000. Divided by 10 is 2,000,000,000. Divided by 100 is 20,000,000. That is 20 million. You can check by multiplying back: 20,000,000 times 100 times 10 times 5 is 100,000,000,000. (queues `math.number-sense.place-value.L4`)
- Light travels about 9.5 trillion kilometres in a year. Round that to 10 trillion. About how far is a star 40 light-years away? **About 400 trillion kilometres.**. 40 times 10 trillion is 400 trillion. You can think of it as 4 times 10, which is 40, and then 10 more. The exact answer is a little smaller, about 380 trillion, because 9.5 is a little less than 10. (queues `math.number-sense.estimation.L4`)
- In a made-up curd test, cup C in the warm box set after 5 hours, and cup B on the counter set after 11 hours. How many hours longer did cup B take? Did cup C take more or less than half of cup B's time? **6 hours longer. Cup C took a little less than half.**. 11 take away 5 is 6 hours. Half of 11 hours is 5 and a half hours. Cup C took 5 hours, which is a little less than 5 and a half. So warmth made the curd set in about half the time. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- Venus is about 460 degrees on the surface. Mars is about minus 60 degrees. How many degrees hotter is Venus than Mars? **520 degrees hotter.**. From minus 60 up to 0 is 60 degrees. From 0 up to 460 is another 460 degrees. 60 plus 460 is 520 degrees. So Venus is 520 degrees hotter than Mars. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.written-methods.L4`)
- A spacecraft crosses a plume at about 8 kilometres every second. A made-up plume is 40 kilometres wide. How many seconds does the crossing take? **5 seconds.**. The spacecraft goes 8 kilometres each second. 40 divided by 8 is 5. You can check it: 8 times 5 is 40. So the instruments have only about 5 seconds to collect grains. ## Warm-up
- What do all the living things we know of need? → Liquid water, a supply of energy and the right chemicals.
- Why is Venus hotter than Mercury? → Venus has a thick carbon dioxide air that holds heat in.
- What is chemosynthesis? → Making food from chemicals, not from sunlight.
- What is a biosignature? → A sign that living things have been there, such as a gas that would not last without them.
- What gas do yeast give out when they eat sugar? → Carbon dioxide.
- What does a dip in a star's light, repeating every few days, show? → A planet is crossing in front of the star.
- How do scientists find out which gases are in a planet's air? → From the colours missing in the starlight that passed through it.
- Why is one gas not enough to prove life? → Non-living chemistry can make many gases too.
- Cold or too much heat: which one kills curd bacteria? → Too much heat. Cold only makes them sleep.
- What does the Drake chain show? → That the answer depends mostly on the guesses we know least about.
