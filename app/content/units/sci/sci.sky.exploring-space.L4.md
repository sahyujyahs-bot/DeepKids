# Exploring space: why a satellite never lands, and what it costs to put one up
`sci.sky.exploring-space.L4`

**Summary.** A rocket only has to climb about 400 kilometres to reach the space station, a little further than the road from Chennai to Bengaluru. Yet the rocket is as tall as a ten-storey building and about nine tenths of its weight is fuel. The puzzle is that going up is the easy part. Staying up is hard, because the station is falling all the time and keeps missing the ground, by moving sideways at about 8 kilometres every second. Your child rolls marbles off a table to see how falling and missing fit together, then loads a balloon rocket to see why every kilogram costs so much fuel. Along the way come telescopes that see in radio waves and infrared, the long curved road Chandrayaan-3 took to the Moon, and what space does to a body.

**Intro.** *Throw a ball as hard as you can. It curves down and lands. Throw it harder and it lands farther away. Now imagine a throw so fast that the ground curves away as quickly as the ball falls. Where does the ball land?* It never lands. That ball would circle the Earth, falling all the time and always missing. This is exactly what the space station does. In this unit you roll marbles off a table and watch speed decide where they land. You load a balloon rocket with paper clips and find out why rockets must carry fuel to lift their own fuel. You build a telescope from two lenses. You draw the orbits of satellites to scale on the floor. And you follow the 40-day journey of India's Chandrayaan-3, which went round the Earth again and again before it ever reached the Moon.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain that a satellite is always falling, and stays up because it moves sideways fast enough to keep missing the ground.
1. I can say about how fast a satellite must move to stay in orbit, and why a rocket spends most of its effort on sideways speed and not on height.
2. I can use my balloon rocket results to explain why a lighter rocket gains more speed, and why rockets drop their empty stages.
3. I can explain why a higher orbit is slower than a lower one, and why one special high orbit makes a satellite hang over the same spot.
4. I can explain that a wider telescope both collects more light and shows finer detail, and say how radio and space telescopes see what our eyes cannot.
5. I can say what weightlessness, radiation and space junk do to people and machines, and how each danger is handled.
6. I can describe an Indian mission such as Mangalyaan or Chandrayaan-3 from launch to arrival, and say why the journey took many days.

## Terms
- **Orbit**: The curved path of a thing that is falling around a planet or a star and keeps missing it. *e.g.* The International Space Station orbits the Earth about every 90 minutes.
- **Free fall**: Falling with nothing holding you up. A thing in free fall feels no weight, even though gravity is pulling it. *e.g.* At the top of a jump, for a moment you feel light. Astronauts on the space station are in free fall all the time.
- **Altitude**: How high something is above the ground or the sea. *e.g.* The space station flies at an altitude of about 400 kilometres.
- **Orbital speed**: The sideways speed a thing needs to keep missing the ground and stay in orbit. Close to the Earth it is about 8 kilometres every second. *e.g.* A Vande Bharat train runs at about 160 kilometres an hour. A satellite near the Earth is about 170 times faster.
- **Thrust**: The push that an engine gives to a rocket. It comes from the gas thrown out of the back. *e.g.* A balloon that is let go flies off because its escaping air gives it thrust.
- **Propellant**: Everything a rocket burns and throws out of its engine, which means the fuel and the oxygen together. *e.g.* Most of a rocket's weight at lift-off is propellant.
- **Payload**: The useful thing a rocket carries into space, such as a satellite, a lander or a crew. *e.g.* The payload of Chandrayaan-3's rocket was the spacecraft that carried Vikram and Pragyan.
- **Stage**: One section of a rocket, with its own tanks and engine. It is dropped when its tanks are empty. *e.g.* India's PSLV has four stages.
- **Exhaust speed**: How fast the gas leaves the engine. For the rockets we use now it is about 3 to 4 kilometres a second. *e.g.* A rocket that throws gas out faster gets more speed from the same amount of propellant.
- **Escape speed**: The speed a thing needs to leave a planet for good, with no more push. For the Earth it is about 11 kilometres every second. *e.g.* Mangalyaan had to leave the Earth's pull behind before it could travel to Mars.
- **Geostationary orbit**: A circle about 36,000 kilometres above the equator. A satellite there takes exactly one day to go round, so it stays above the same spot on the ground. *e.g.* The dish on a rooftop points at a satellite in this orbit.
- **Weightlessness**: The floating feeling of having no weight. It happens in free fall, and scientists also call it microgravity. *e.g.* On the space station, a pen let go in the air just stays there.
- **Radiation**: Energy that travels through space as waves or very fast tiny pieces. A lot of the radiation from the Sun and stars can harm living bodies. *e.g.* The Earth's air and its magnetic field protect us from most space radiation.
- **Space debris**: Broken satellites, empty rocket stages and tiny flecks of paint that still circle the Earth. Also called space junk. *e.g.* A fleck of paint moving at orbital speed can crack a window.
- **Light-year**: The distance light travels in one year, about 9.5 trillion kilometres. It measures distance, not time. *e.g.* The nearest star after the Sun is a little more than 4 light-years away.
- **Radio telescope**: A telescope that catches radio waves from space with a big dish. Radio waves pass through clouds, so it works day and night. *e.g.* The Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope near Pune has 30 dishes, each 45 metres wide.
- **Infrared**: Light that our eyes cannot see, which warm things give out. It is the light that feels like heat. *e.g.* The James Webb Space Telescope looks at the sky in infrared.
- **Resolution**: How much fine detail a picture or a telescope can show. A wider telescope opening gives finer detail. *e.g.* The two headlights of a faraway lorry look like one light, until you look through binoculars.
- **Launch window**: The short time when the planets are placed so that a trip needs the least fuel. *e.g.* Mars launch windows come about every 26 months. Mangalyaan used one in late 2013.
- **Trajectory**: The planned path of a rocket or spacecraft through space. *e.g.* Mangalyaan's trajectory curved round the Sun from the Earth to Mars.

## Explanations
### Falling sideways: what an orbit is · *defined*
**Definition:** An orbit is a fall that never ends. A satellite falls towards the Earth all the time, but it moves sideways so fast that the Earth's ground curves away as quickly as the satellite falls.

Drop a ball and it falls straight down. Throw it sideways and it falls in a curve and lands farther away. Throw it faster and it lands even farther. The Earth is round, so the ground curves away below a flying ball. Over every 8 kilometres, the ground curves down by about 5 metres. A ball falls about 5 metres in its first second. So a ball that also moves 8 kilometres sideways in that second would find the ground just as far below as before. It keeps falling and keeps missing. That is an orbit. Eight kilometres a second is far faster than any throw, so only a rocket can reach it. Isaac Newton imagined this more than 300 years ago, with a cannon on a very high mountain.
- e.g. A stone thrown gently lands at your feet. A cricket ball thrown hard lands far down the field. A satellite is thrown so fast that it never lands.
- e.g. The Moon is also falling around the Earth, and it takes about 27 days to go round once.
- e.g. The space station and the astronaut beside her fall together, so she floats next to the wall.
### How fast, and how long a lap · *defined*
**Definition:** A satellite close to the Earth must move sideways at about 8 kilometres a second, which is about 28,000 kilometres an hour, to stay in orbit.

The fastest train in India runs at about 160 kilometres an hour. The space station is about 170 times faster. One lap of the Earth at the station's height is about 42,000 kilometres long. At that speed a lap takes about 90 minutes. So the crew sees about 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day. Here is the surprise about rockets. To rise 400 kilometres needs only about a third of the speed that staying up there needs. So a rocket climbs through the thick air quickly, then tips over and spends most of its effort gaining sideways speed. Height is easy. Speed is the hard part.
- e.g. 28,000 kilometres an hour is about 460 kilometres every minute.
- e.g. The station crosses the whole length of India, about 3,000 kilometres, in about 6 minutes.
- e.g. A rocket from Sriharikota heads out over the sea towards the east, in the same direction as the Earth turns, to borrow some of the Earth's own spin.
### Higher orbits are slower · *defined*
**Definition:** The higher a satellite orbits, the slower it moves and the longer one lap takes, because the Earth's pull is weaker farther away.

The space station is 400 kilometres up and takes 90 minutes for a lap. Navigation satellites such as the American GPS ones are about 20,000 kilometres up and take about 12 hours. At about 36,000 kilometres, a lap takes 24 hours, exactly the time the Earth takes to turn once. A satellite there over the equator seems to hang still above one spot, so it is called geostationary. This is why a TV dish on a roof can stay pointed at one place in the sky. The Moon is 384,000 kilometres away and takes about 27 days. Speeds drop too. The station moves at about 7.7 kilometres a second, a geostationary satellite at about 3, and the Moon at about 1.
- e.g. India's INSAT and GSAT satellites sit in geostationary orbit and carry TV, phone and weather signals.
- e.g. A satellite that is a little lower than another one will slowly overtake it.
- e.g. To move a satellite to a higher orbit, an engine speeds it up at the right moment, and afterwards it settles into a slower, higher circle.
- e.g. Katherine Johnson, an American mathematician, worked out the paths of the first American crewed orbit flights by hand in the early 1960s.
### A rocket carries fuel to lift its fuel · *defined*
**Definition:** A rocket speeds up by throwing its own gas backwards. A heavier rocket needs more push for the same speed, so most of a rocket's weight at lift-off is propellant.

The gas leaves a rocket engine at about 3 to 4 kilometres a second. The rocket needs about 9 kilometres a second in all to reach orbit, because gravity and air slow it on the way up. That is more than twice the speed of the gas. So the rocket must throw away most of its own weight to get there. Every extra kilogram of payload needs more fuel to lift it, and then more fuel to lift that fuel. For a big rocket, around nine tenths of the weight at lift-off is propellant. The satellite on top may be only a few percent of the weight, or less than one percent. This is why rockets drop their empty tanks. A stage that is empty is dead weight, and dropping it makes the rest much easier to speed up.
- e.g. PSLV weighs hundreds of tonnes at lift-off, and the satellites it carries weigh only a few tonnes.
- e.g. A balloon rocket with paper clips taped on goes a shorter distance than one with none.
- e.g. A rocket that dropped nothing would need to be far bigger to reach orbit.
### Climbing to the Moon in small steps · *defined*
**Definition:** A spacecraft can raise its orbit by firing its engine at the right moment to speed up. A series of small burns can lift it from a low orbit all the way to the Moon.

Chandrayaan-3 was launched on 14 July 2023 and landed on 23 August 2023, 40 days later. The rocket did not throw it straight at the Moon. It left the spacecraft in a long oval orbit round the Earth. Over the next days, ISRO fired the spacecraft's engine several times when it was closest to the Earth. Each burn stretched the oval farther out. On 1 August a last burn sent it off towards the Moon. On 5 August the Moon's gravity caught it, and it began to orbit the Moon. More burns lowered that orbit step by step, until the lander separated and came down. A single big push would have needed a much bigger rocket. Many small burns trade time for rocket size. Mangalyaan did the same round the Earth in 2013 before it left for Mars.
- e.g. From 14 July to 23 August is 40 days: 17 days left in July, and 23 more in August.
- e.g. Mangalyaan was launched on 5 November 2013. It reached Mars orbit on 24 September 2014, about 320 days later.
- e.g. Mars launch windows come about every 26 months, so Mangalyaan could not simply wait a week and go.
### Telescopes: light, detail and magnifying · *defined*
**Definition:** A telescope's opening does two jobs. It collects more light, and it shows finer detail. Both get better as the opening gets wider.

In the half-lens test of Class 3, a bigger lens made a brighter picture. A bigger opening also makes a sharper picture. Light spreads out a little as it goes through any opening, and a narrow opening blurs fine detail more than a wide one. A telescope with a wide mirror can split two stars that look like one to a small telescope. Magnifying is a separate thing. A telescope with two lenses magnifies by the long focal length of the big front lens divided by the short focal length of the eyepiece. A lens that bends light to a point 100 centimetres away and an eyepiece that bends it to a point 2 centimetres away gives 50 times. But magnifying a blurry picture only gives a bigger blur. Galileo used lenses in 1609. Isaac Newton built a telescope with a curved mirror in about 1668. Very big lenses sag under their own weight, and a mirror can be supported from behind. So all the biggest telescopes use mirrors.
- e.g. The Hubble Space Telescope has a mirror 2.4 metres wide.
- e.g. The James Webb Space Telescope has a mirror 6.5 metres wide, made of 18 six-sided pieces.
- e.g. No telescope on or near the Earth can show the flags left on the Moon, because they are far too small to see from so far away.
### Seeing light our eyes cannot see · *defined*
**Definition:** Light comes in many kinds. Our eyes see only a narrow band. Radio waves, infrared, ultraviolet and X-rays are also light, and different telescopes catch them.

The air blocks most X-rays and ultraviolet light, and part of the infrared. So telescopes for those kinds of light must go above the air. India's AstroSat, launched in 2015, studies the sky in ultraviolet and X-rays. Radio waves pass through air and clouds easily. So radio telescopes can stand on the ground. The Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope at Khodad near Pune has 30 dishes, each 45 metres wide, spread over a field about 25 kilometres across. They work together as if they were one huge telescope. The James Webb Space Telescope looks in infrared, the light that warm things give out. It can see through some dust clouds and catch light from the earliest galaxies. It sits about 1.5 million kilometres from the Earth, behind a sunshield about the size of a tennis court, to stay very cold.
- e.g. A TV remote control sends infrared light that your eyes cannot see, but a phone camera often can.
- e.g. Radio dishes need no dark sky, so astronomers can use them in the daytime.
- e.g. A hot cup of tea glows in an infrared camera, though it looks plain in ordinary light.
- e.g. In 1967 Jocelyn Bell, a student in England, noticed a strange regular beat on the chart of a radio telescope. It was a pulsar, a spinning star, and she was the first to notice it.
### Light takes time: looking far is looking back · *defined*
**Definition:** Light travels about 300,000 kilometres every second. A light-second is the distance light covers in one second.

Light from the Moon takes about 1.3 seconds to reach us. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds. A radio message to Mars takes between 3 and 22 minutes, depending on where the planets are. Voyager 1, launched in 1977 and now in the space between the stars, is so far that a message takes close to a whole day to reach it. The nearest star after the Sun is a little over 4 light-years away, so the light we see from it left more than 4 years ago. When a telescope looks at a faraway galaxy, it sees light that left billions of years ago. Looking far away is looking back in time.
- e.g. If the Sun vanished, we would see it go dark only 8 minutes and 20 seconds later.
- e.g. Controllers of Voyager 1 send a command and wait about two days for the answer to come back.
- e.g. The light from the Moon you see tonight left it a little over a second ago.
### What space does to a body · *defined*
**Definition:** In weightlessness, an astronaut's bones and muscles get weaker because they no longer carry the body's weight. Space radiation also adds a risk to health.

On Earth, your legs and back work against gravity all day, and that keeps them strong. In orbit there is no weight to carry. Astronauts lose bone, roughly one percent each month in the bones of the hips and legs, and their muscles shrink. Body fluids move up towards the head, which makes faces puffy and can change eyesight. To fight this, astronauts on the space station exercise about two hours every day on a treadmill and other machines. The station is inside the Earth's magnetic field, which turns away much of the radiation. A trip to the Moon or Mars goes beyond that shelter. Peggy Whitson spent more than 600 days in space in all, and showed that a body can manage long stays with the right exercise.
- e.g. After months in orbit, an astronaut may find it hard to stand at first when she returns to Earth.
- e.g. Astronauts strap themselves onto a treadmill with elastic cords, to push their feet against something.
- e.g. A metal wall or a water tank around a sleeping place gives extra cover from radiation.
### Space junk · *defined*
**Definition:** Space junk is the broken and unused things that still orbit the Earth. At orbital speed even a tiny piece can do great damage.

Since 1957, thousands of rockets and satellites have been launched. Dead satellites, empty rocket stages and the pieces from collisions still orbit the Earth. Tens of thousands of pieces bigger than a few centimetres are tracked, and there are millions of smaller flecks. Something moving at 8 kilometres a second carries more energy than a bullet, even if it is only a fleck of paint. In 2009, a working satellite and a dead one collided and made thousands of pieces. The space station has moved out of the way of junk several times. Junk in low orbits slowly falls back, because there is a trace of air even at that height, and it burns up. Junk at 36,000 kilometres stays for ages. So old satellites there are pushed a little higher, into a 'graveyard' orbit, before they die.
- e.g. A speck of paint has cracked a window of the space station.
- e.g. The Moon's orbit has no air to clean it, so junk there would stay.
- e.g. Future satellites may carry a way to drop out of orbit when their job is done.
### Could people ever reach another star? · *idea*
The nearest star after the Sun is Proxima Centauri, a little over 4 light-years away. Voyager 1 moves at about 17 kilometres a second, which is about 17,000 times slower than light. At that speed it would take about 70,000 years. No rocket we can build now could do much better. Scientists have ideas, but none has been built.
- e.g. A light sail is a thin mirror pushed by light itself, with no fuel. A project announced in 2016 imagined pushing tiny chips this way with powerful lasers.
- e.g. A generation ship would carry people who live and die on the journey, so that their great-grandchildren arrive.
- **Some physicists:** Tiny probes pushed by light may reach another star in a few decades, but people could not go this way.
- **Other scientists:** A ship big enough for people would need amounts of energy that nobody knows how to make. They think the stars are for telescopes, not for travel.
### Who owns the Moon and the planets? · *debated*
In 1967, countries including India signed the Outer Space Treaty. It says that no country can claim the Moon or any planet, and that space should be used for peaceful purposes. But it does not clearly say who may own things dug up from the Moon or from asteroids. In 2015 the United States passed a law letting its companies keep resources they take from space. Luxembourg, Japan and some other countries passed similar laws later.
- e.g. A company that digs up Moon ice for water could be allowed to sell it, under some national laws.
- e.g. Antarctica is shared by many countries under a treaty and has no owner. Some people think the Moon should be like that.
- **People who favour mining rights:** Companies will not pay for expensive missions unless they can keep what they find. Without rights, nobody will go.
- **People who favour shared ownership:** The Moon belongs to all humankind. A few rich countries or companies should not take its best places and its resources.
- **Many lawyers:** The old treaty is too vague. The world needs new agreements, made before the first mine is dug.
### India's way: small steps and learning from mistakes · *defined*
**Definition:** India's space agency, ISRO, builds each mission as a careful step beyond the last, and learns from failures.

In 2014, India's Mangalyaan reached Mars orbit on the first try. No other country had done that. It was reported to cost about 450 crore rupees, much less than most Mars missions. Ritu Karidhal, an engineer from Lucknow, was among the team leaders. In September 2019, the lander of Chandrayaan-2 lost contact just before touching down. The orbiter stayed in good health and kept working around the Moon for years. ISRO studied what went wrong, changed the lander and its landing plan, and tried again. Chandrayaan-3 landed on 23 August 2023. Its project director was P. Veeramuthuvel, from Tamil Nadu. India was the fourth country to land on the Moon, and the first near its south pole.
- e.g. Chandrayaan-3 reused the Chandrayaan-2 orbiter, which was still working, to talk to the lander.
- e.g. Engineers chose a bigger landing area, so that a small error would not matter.
- e.g. Many of the engineers and scientists at ISRO are women, and several led teams in these missions.

## Mostly agreed
- An orbit is a fall that keeps missing. A satellite in orbit is in free fall. *How we know:* The crew of the space station float beside everything, as in any fall. Newton's maths of falling and sideways speed predicts the paths of thousands of satellites.
- The lower a satellite orbits, the faster it moves and the shorter its lap. *How we know:* Radar and laser tracking give the heights and lap times of satellites, and they follow this rule from 90 minutes at 400 kilometres to 24 hours at 36,000 kilometres.
- A rocket's final speed depends on how fast its gas leaves and on how much of its weight is propellant. *How we know:* Every rocket launched fits this rule. In the balloon test, adding weight to the same balloon always shortened the distance.
- A wider telescope opening collects more light and shows finer detail. *How we know:* Each time bigger mirrors have been built, astronomers have seen fainter things and split stars that looked single.
- Light travels at the same speed everywhere, about 300,000 kilometres a second. *How we know:* Laser flashes bounced off mirrors left on the Moon by astronauts and by a Soviet rover return after about 2.6 seconds, and the Moon's distance can be measured this way to a few centimetres.
- Radio waves pass through clouds, but the air blocks most X-rays and ultraviolet light. *How we know:* Radio telescopes work on cloudy days. X-ray telescopes see almost nothing from the ground and work well in orbit.
- Weightlessness weakens bones and muscles, and exercise slows the loss. *How we know:* Hundreds of astronauts have been measured before, during and after their stays on space stations.

## How we found out
**Isaac Newton, an English scientist (1643 to 1727), About 1666 to 1687.** More than 2,300 years ago, the Greek thinker Aristotle taught that the sky and the Earth follow different rules. Things on Earth fall to the ground. Things in the sky are made of a perfect stuff and move in circles by their own nature, so they never fall. For about 2,000 years most scholars agreed. Then Isaac Newton asked whether the pull that drops an apple might also reach the Moon. The Moon is about 60 times farther from the Earth's centre than an apple. So he worked out that the pull there should be 60 times 60, or 3,600 times, weaker. It would make the Moon fall only about a millimetre and a half each second. He compared this with the Moon's sideways speed, about one kilometre a second, and the numbers matched the curve of the Moon's path. He also imagined a cannon on a mountain, firing faster and faster. In 1687 he published this in his book, the Principia. The Moon was falling too. The sky followed the same rule as the apple.  
*What people believed before:* Aristotle taught that the heavens follow different rules from the Earth, so the Moon and the stars never fall. Newton showed that the Moon falls like an apple, only it moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Satellites stay up because they are so high that Earth's gravity cannot reach them.** (A very common idea, even among grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Astronauts float, and things seem to weigh nothing up there.  
  Against: At the height of the space station, gravity is still about nine tenths as strong as on the ground. The station would fall straight back if it stopped moving sideways. Gravity is what bends its path into a circle.
- **To reach orbit, a rocket just has to go high enough.** (Many people before they learn about orbits; *rejected*)  
  For: A rocket does go up, and space is up.  
  Against: A rocket that reached 400 kilometres and then stopped would fall straight back down. What keeps a satellite up is its sideways speed of about 8 kilometres a second, which takes most of the rocket's effort.
- **A strong telescope on the Earth can see the flag that astronauts left on the Moon.** (Many people, and some websites; *rejected*)  
  For: Telescopes show craters on the Moon, and a flag seems like a small step down from a crater.  
  Against: The flag is about a metre wide. The Moon is 384,000 kilometres away. The finest detail even the Hubble Space Telescope can show is far bigger than a flag. The landing sites have been photographed by spacecraft that circle the Moon low down.
- **Space is freezing cold everywhere, so anything left there freezes at once.** (A common idea; *rejected*)  
  For: Space is mostly empty and dark, and the shadows on the Moon are extremely cold.  
  Against: Empty space has almost no matter to carry heat. A surface in sunlight on the space station can reach well over 100 degrees Celsius, and the shaded side can fall below minus 100. Spacecraft need both heaters and ways to shed heat.
- **The Apollo Moon landings were faked in a studio.** (Some people since the 1970s; *rejected*)  
  For: The photographs have odd shadows and no stars, and the flag seems to wave.  
  Against: Cameras set for bright ground cannot show faint stars. The flag had a rod across the top, and it moved only when touched. About 380 kilograms of rock came back and has been studied by labs in many countries. The Soviet Union, a rival that tracked the flights, never said they were fake. Mirrors the astronauts left on the Moon still bounce lasers back today.
- **Space is so big that space junk is nothing to worry about.** (Some people, and some companies in the past; *debated*)  
  For: Space has a huge volume, and most satellites have never been hit.  
  Against: Satellites use a few popular orbits, so the junk is crowded in a thin region near the Earth. A collision in 2009 produced thousands of pieces. More satellites are launched every year, and each collision makes more junk.

## Roots
We rolled marbles, loaded a rocket and followed satellites round the Earth. Now we ask what falling, orbits and distance tell us about the world we live in.

**Deep truth.** In the marble test, faster marbles landed farther away. A marble fast enough to miss the whole floor would never land. A satellite is exactly that, always falling and always missing. So staying up and falling turn out to be one motion.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture staying up as being held up, like a lamp on a hook. We think falling is the opposite of staying up. A satellite shows that one motion can be both.  
*Evidence:* In Falling Sideways, every marble spent about the same time in the air, but the faster ones landed farther along. The Earth's ground curves away by about 5 metres over 8 kilometres, and the space station moves 8 kilometres every second. If your own marbles landed differently, write down exactly what they did and test again.

- *who am i:* You are standing on a planet that is falling around the Sun all the time. You ride along without feeling a thing, as an astronaut rides along with her station.
- *what is life:* Our bones and muscles are built for working against gravity. Take away the load and they shrink within weeks. A living body keeps its shape by using it.
- *what is this world:* A thrown stone, the Moon and the space station follow one rule of falling, and differ only in their sideways speed.

A dropped marble, the Moon and the space station are all falling, and the only difference is how fast they move sideways. A slow throw lands near. A faster throw lands far. A throw of about 8 kilometres a second misses the Earth for ever. So 'up there' is not a place where the rules stop. The same gravity that pulls a pebble out of your hand also holds the Moon in its path and the Earth in its path round the Sun.

- **Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta:** An ancient hymn asks who really knows how the world began and where it all came from. It ends by saying that perhaps the one who watches from the highest heaven knows, or perhaps even that one does not. (The farther we look, the older the light we see. A telescope shows us how far our knowing goes, and the hymn reminds us to say where it stops.)
- **Frank White, The Overview Effect, 1987:** Many astronauts who have seen the whole Earth from orbit say that it changed how they felt. They saw one round world with no lines between countries and a very thin layer of air. Frank White gave this feeling the name 'the overview effect'. (From far enough away, the thin skin of air you measured on your lime becomes plain to see. A far view can show something that is hard to notice from the ground.)
- Science (settled): A satellite in orbit is in free fall around the Earth, and the same gravity holds the Moon and the planets in their paths.
- Science (open): Seeing the whole Earth from space changes how many astronauts feel. Researchers are still working out how common this is and why it happens.
- Other view, Many physicists: 'Falling' and 'flying' are only words. What matters is the path, and the path comes from a few numbers: the speed, the height and the pull of the Earth. Asking whether a satellite is 'really' falling adds nothing to the maths.
- Practice, Feel the floor push up (3 min): Stand with your feet apart and close your eyes. Feel the floor pushing up on the soles of your feet. That push is what you call your weight. Now imagine the floor is gone and you are falling, and the push is gone too. That is how an astronaut feels. Breathe in slowly three times. Then open your eyes and feel the floor again.
- Ask: If you were falling for ever and the ground kept curving away, would you feel like you were falling?
- Ask: The Earth is falling round the Sun, and you feel nothing. What else might be moving that we do not notice?
- Ask: From far away the Earth looks like one round world. What would you notice about it that you cannot notice from the ground?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A satellite is falling all the time and never lands. Is it falling, or is it flying? (hint: Think about the marble in the test: while it was in the air, what was gravity doing to it?)
- *deep:* Gravity still pulls on an astronaut in orbit, yet she feels no weight. What do we really mean by feeling weight? (hint: Think about what you feel when you stand on the floor. Is it gravity, or the floor pushing back?)
- *big:* A message to Voyager 1 takes close to a day to arrive. In what sense is Voyager 'there' while we are talking to it? (hint: Think about what we know of Voyager now, and how old that news is.)
- *big:* Hubble sees galaxies as they were billions of years ago. Is a telescope also a clock that looks backward? (hint: Light from a faraway thing carries news from the time it left.)
- *big:* Space junk comes from our own rockets. Who should clean it up, and who should pay? (hint: Think about who launched what, and who is harmed if a satellite fails.)
- *big:* The Moon belongs to no country by law. Should anything on the Moon belong to anybody? (hint: Think about the air, the sea and Antarctica, and how people have shared them or failed to.)

## Story: The Stone That Missed the Sea
*Thamarai, her aunt Chithi and the village are made up. The science is real. A ship does vanish hull first beyond the horizon. India does have a rocket engine testing centre at Mahendragiri in Tirunelveli district. Fisher families in India are given small satellite alert devices, and weather offices send them warnings about the sea. The details of this rescue are imagined.*

The sea at Kanyakumari was turning from blue to copper. Thamarai sat on a rock with her feet in the foam. She was nine, and she was watching her father's boat go out to fish, as she did every evening.

The boat grew smaller and smaller. Then something odd happened. The bottom of the boat disappeared first. The sail stayed for a moment. Then the sail went too.

"Chithi," she said, "the sea is eating the boat. Why does the bottom go first?"

Her aunt sat down beside her. Chithi was an engineer who tested rocket engines at Mahendragiri, and she was home for the holidays. She picked up a small stone.

"Throw this," she said. "As far as you can into the sea."

Thamarai threw. The stone made a curve and went splash, a short way out.

"Again, harder," said Chithi.

Thamarai threw harder. The splash was farther. She threw with all her strength, and it was farther still.

"Now think," said Chithi. "What does the stone do the whole time it is in the air?"

"It falls," said Thamarai. "From the moment it leaves my hand."

"Good. So every throw is a fall. A harder throw is a fall that lands farther away. Now, if you were as strong as a cannon, how far could you throw?"

"Very far. But not forever."

"The sea is not flat," said Chithi. "It is a piece of a very big ball. Look at your father's boat. The ball of the Earth curves down between your eyes and the boat. First the bottom is hidden behind the curve, and then the sail. The ground bends away from you."

Thamarai looked at the horizon. For the first time, she saw it as the edge of a curve.

"So if I threw the stone fast enough," she said slowly, "the sea would bend away just as the stone fell."

"Yes," said Chithi. "And the stone would fall all the time, and never reach the water. It would go all the way round. That is how a satellite stays up. It does not hang there. It falls, and the Earth curves away, and so it keeps missing. It needs about eight kilometres every second. Nobody can throw like that. A rocket does the throwing."

"That is what you do at Mahendragiri?"

"I test the engines that make the throw," said Chithi, smiling. "A rocket spends only a short time going up. Then it tips over and spends most of its fuel going sideways."

The sky went dark. A few stars came out. Thamarai noticed that her father's boat had no lamp she could see.

"Chithi, he is late."

A man ran down the beach, waving his phone. "There is a storm warning! The weather office says the wind is rising. All the boats must come in!"

The women of the village gathered at the shore. The wind began to tug at Thamarai's hair. Waves came higher up the rocks.

Then Thamarai's mother held up a small orange box. "This is the alert box the fisheries office gave to every boat. Your father has one. If he is in trouble, he presses the button."

"How does the box talk?" asked Thamarai. "There are no towers in the sea."

"It talks to a satellite," said Chithi. She was looking at the sky. "A very special one. It is 36,000 kilometres up, over the equator. At that height, one lap round the Earth takes exactly one day. The Earth turns once a day too. So the satellite hangs above the same spot in the sky all the time, like a lamp on a hook. The box always knows where to point."

A phone buzzed in Thamarai's mother's hand. She read the message and let out a long breath. "The coast guard has his position. The boat's engine failed, but he is safe, drifting about ten kilometres out. A coast guard boat is on its way."

Everyone cheered. Thamarai felt her knees go weak and she sat down on the sand.

Two hours later, the lights of a big ship came in, with her father's small boat behind it on a rope. He stepped onto the beach, wet and tired and smiling.

That night Thamarai lay on the roof and looked at the stars. She knew now that one of them was not a star at all. It was a box of metal 36,000 kilometres away, falling round the Earth and always missing, and moving so exactly in time with the turning of the world that it stayed above her village.

She took a pebble from her pocket. She held it out over the dark sea and opened her hand. It fell, as it always had. Now she knew why. The pebble had no sideways speed, so it landed. A satellite has so much that it never does.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a satellite is not held up by anything. It is falling around the Earth and missing. A higher orbit is slower, and at 36,000 kilometres one lap takes exactly one day. That matches the Earth's spin, so the satellite stays over the same spot. That is why the alert box could always point to one place in the sky.

- Ask: Why does the bottom of a ship disappear before its sail when it goes out to sea?
- Ask: What would you have to do to a thrown stone so that it never landed in the sea?
- Ask: Why can a satellite 36,000 kilometres up stay above one spot of the ground all the time?
- Ask: Chithi said a rocket spends most of its fuel going sideways. Why?
- Ask: Why did the pebble land in the sea, when a satellite never lands?

### e1 · Falling Sideways: does a faster marble take longer to land? (40 min)
- **question:** A marble rolls off the edge of a table. If it leaves the table faster, does it land farther away? And does it take longer to reach the floor?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will a faster marble land farther away, in the same place, or nearer? Will it take longer to fall? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same marble, the same ramp and the same table every time. Only the height that you let the marble go from changes: 5, 10 and 15 centimetres above the table top. Let go of the marble without pushing it. Roll it three times from each height, and use the middle landing mark, because one try can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Keep marbles away from children under five, who might put them in their mouths. Wipe up any water that drips on the floor, so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw three bars, one for each start height, as long as its middle landing distance. Write one sentence under the bars that says what the pattern is.
- **results table:** Start height above the table (cm) | My guess for the landing distance (cm) | Try 1 (cm) | Try 2 (cm) | Try 3 (cm) | Middle number (cm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the marble, the ramp and the newspaper on the floor. Ask for a guess and a reason. Write down the guess.  
  *Parent says:* A marble will roll off the edge of the table. Does a faster marble land farther away? Does it take longer to fall? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A marble rolls off a table. If it is faster, where does it land? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the faster marble will land there? (good: It has more speed, so it flies farther.; It stays in the air longer.; It lands in the same place because gravity is the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children say a faster marble takes longer in the air. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the ruler so its low end rests on the table, 15 centimetres from the edge. Stack books under the other end. Tape a line on the newspaper right under the table edge. This is the zero line.  
  *Parent says:* The ruler is a ramp. The higher the marble starts, the faster it will be at the bottom. The tape under the edge is zero. We measure from there.  
  *Child mode:* Make a ramp. Tape a zero line on the floor under the table edge.
  - Note: The marble runs off the low end, across 15 centimetres of flat table, and over the edge. Use a ruler with a groove so the marble goes straight. Raise the high end so that the marble starts 5 centimetres above the table. Mark 5, 10 and 15 centimetres on a pencil line on the books if you wish.
  - Check: Why does the marble start from the same ramp each time and change only its starting height? → **So only the speed changes and every other thing stays the same**
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Dip the marble in the water and shake it once. Let it go from the 5 centimetre mark. Look for the wet spot nearest to the table. Mark it with a pencil dot. Measure from the zero line. Do it three times, then at 10 and at 15 centimetres.  
  *Parent says:* Let the marble go. Do not push it. Watch where it first touches the paper. Mark the first wet spot only. It may bounce on after that.  
  *Child mode:* Wet the marble. Let it go. Mark the first spot. Measure from zero.
  - Note: A grown-up can watch the landing, because it is hard to roll and watch at the same time. The marble may bounce. Only the first wet spot counts. If a try goes very differently from the other two, repeat it. If the wet spots are too faint, wet the marble more or use a dry newspaper under a dark pen dot. Your numbers will differ from the example. What matters is that the higher start lands farther.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Find the middle number for each height. Draw the three bars. Look at how the distance grew when the start height grew.  
  *Parent says:* Look at our numbers. Which start height gave the farthest landing? Did the landing double when the height doubled?  
  *Child mode:* Which height landed farthest? Draw a bar for each one.
  - Note: The distance grows with the speed, but not in step with the height. Doubling the height does not double the distance. The marble's speed grows more slowly than the height does. Do not explain this yet. Just notice.
  - Check: The marble that started higher left the table faster. Where did it land? → **Farther from the table**
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for what I think, what I saw and why. Then ask the Earth question.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now? What did you see that shows it? And why? Now imagine the floor curved away downwards. How fast would the marble need to go so that it never landed?  
  *Child mode:* Say what I think, what I saw and why. Then think: a curved floor.
  - Note: This is claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer: what I think: a faster marble lands farther away. What I saw: the marble from 15 centimetres landed about one and a half times as far as the one from 5, for example. Why: all the marbles fell the same height, so they took about the same time to fall, but the faster ones moved farther sideways in that time. Then the Earth question: on the real Earth the ground curves away by about 5 metres over 8 kilometres. A satellite falls and misses all the way round.
  - Check: A cannonball is fired sideways from a very high mountain. What would make it go right round the Earth and never land? → **Being fired fast enough, about 8 kilometres a second**

## Worksheets
### Falling, Rockets and Telescopes (core) (core, 18 questions)
1. (mcq) Why does the space station not fall down to the ground?  
   Options: It is so high that there is no gravity / It moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing the ground as it falls / The air holds it up  
   **Answer:** It moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing the ground as it falls. *Why:* Gravity is still pulling the station, with about nine tenths of its pull on the ground. The station falls all the time, but its sideways speed of about 8 kilometres a second carries it past the curve of the Earth. Picking 'no gravity' is a very common mix-up.
2. (write) Two marbles roll off the same table. One leaves slowly and one leaves fast. Say where each lands, and which one is in the air longer.  
   **Answer:** The fast marble lands farther from the table. Both marbles are in the air for about the same time, because both fall the same height with the same pull. The fast one just moves farther sideways in that time.. *Why:* A strong answer says the landing places differ but the times are about the same, and gives the reason: the fall depends on height and gravity, not on sideways speed.
3. (fill) A satellite is always ____ towards the Earth, but its sideways speed makes it miss the ground.  
   **Answer:** ['falling']. *Why:* An orbit is a fall that keeps missing. The satellite falls all the time, and the Earth's curve drops away below it.
4. (truefalse) To reach orbit, a rocket only has to climb high enough.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A rocket that stopped at 400 kilometres up would fall straight back down. Staying up needs a sideways speed of about 8 kilometres a second, and that takes most of the rocket's effort.
5. (mcq) About how fast must a satellite near the Earth move sideways to stay in orbit?  
   Options: About 8 kilometres every second / About 80 kilometres an hour / About 8 metres every second  
   **Answer:** About 8 kilometres every second. *Why:* At about 8 kilometres a second, which is about 28,000 kilometres an hour, the ground curves away as fast as the satellite falls. It is about 170 times faster than a very fast train.
6. (match) Match each thing in orbit to the time that one lap takes.  
   **Answer:** The space station → About 90 minutes; A navigation satellite such as GPS → About 12 hours; A geostationary satellite → 24 hours; The Moon → About 27 days. *Why:* The higher the orbit, the longer the lap. At 36,000 kilometres a lap takes exactly one day.

## Applications
- Geostationary satellites carry TV, phone and weather signals, and a dish on a roof stays pointed at one spot in the sky.
- India's NavIC navigation satellites help ships, trains and phones find their position.
- Weather satellites track cyclones in the Bay of Bengal, so coastal families can be warned in time.
- Distress alert boxes on fishing boats send a position through a satellite when an engine fails far from the shore.
- Satellite pictures help farmers and officials watch crops, floods, forests and water in lakes and reservoirs.
- Big telescopes on mountains and in space, and radio dishes, help scientists learn how stars and galaxies are born.
- Space debris tracking lets satellites and the space station move away from junk before a collision.
- Exercise machines and bone studies made for astronauts help doctors understand weak bones in older people.
- Water recycling systems made for space stations are used to clean drinking water in places far from pipes.
- Engineers who design a soft landing for a lander use the same ideas as people who design helmets and cycle seats.

## Open questions
- **How can people stay healthy on a trip to Mars and back?** The trip takes many months each way. Nobody yet knows how to protect bones, muscles, eyes and the body from radiation for that long.
- **Could a telescope find signs of life on a planet around another star?** Telescopes such as the James Webb can study the air of some faraway planets. So far, nobody has found clear signs of life.
- **What is the best way to clean up space junk?** Ideas include nets, harpoons and lasers, but none has yet cleaned up much junk. It is hard to catch something moving at 8 kilometres a second.
- **Can people ever travel to another star?** The nearest star is over 4 light-years away. Ideas such as light sails have been imagined, but nobody has built one that could carry anything to another star.
- **Who may own what is dug up on the Moon or on asteroids?** The 1967 treaty says that no country can own the Moon, but it does not clearly say who may own what is dug up. Countries disagree.
- **What is the cheapest safe way to put things into orbit?** Reusable rockets and new engines are being tried. Nobody knows how cheap and safe launching can become.
- **How much water is there on the Moon, and could people really use it?** We know there is ice in dark polar craters, but not how much or how easily it can be dug out.

## Connected maths
- On your orbit map, 1 centimetre stands for 1,000 kilometres. The geostationary circle is 42.2 centimetres from the middle, and the Earth's edge is 6.4 centimetres from the middle. How many kilometres above the ground is a geostationary satellite? **About 35,800 kilometres.**. 42.2 take away 6.4 is 35.8 centimetres on the map. How far from the table does each land? What does this show about doubling the speed? **A lands 20 centimetres away and B lands 40 centimetres away. Doubling the speed doubled the distance.**. Distance is speed times time. 50 times 0.4 is 20. 100 times 0.4 is 40. Both marbles fall for the same 0.4 seconds, so the faster one covers twice as much ground in that time. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)

Was the fall the same each time? **It fell by 110, then 80, then 50 centimetres. The fall got smaller each time.**. 300 take away 190 is 110. 190 take away 110 is 80. 110 take away 60 is 50. The first 10 clips cost the most distance, and later clips cost less because the rocket was already slow. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.multi-digit.L4`)
- The Moon is 384,000 kilometres away, and light travels 300,000 kilometres every second. About how many seconds does moonlight take to reach us? **About 1.3 seconds.**. 384,000 divided by 300,000 is a little more than 1. It is about 1.28, which rounds to 1.3 seconds. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- Chandrayaan-3 was launched on 14 July 2023 and landed on 23 August 2023. How many days was that? Aditya-L1 was launched on 2 September 2023 and reached its place near the Sun on 6 January 2024. How many days was that? **40 days, and 126 days.**. From 14 July to 31 July is 17 days, and then 23 more in August makes 40. For Aditya-L1: 28 days left in September, then 31 in October, 30 in November, 31 in December, and 6 in January. 28 plus 31 is 59, plus 30 is 89, plus 31 is 120, plus 6 is 126. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
- Voyager 1 moves about 17,000 times slower than light. The nearest star is about 4 light-years away, which is how far light goes in 4 years. About how many years would Voyager 1 take to get there? **About 68,000 years, or about 70,000 years.**. Light takes 4 years. A thing 17,000 times slower takes 17,000 times as long. 4 times 17,000 is 68,000 years. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- The Devasthal telescope has a mirror 3.6 metres wide. A small school telescope has a mirror 1.2 metres wide. How many times wider is the big mirror? How many times more light does it gather? **3 times wider, and 9 times more light.**. 3.6 divided by 1.2 is 3. The light gathered depends on the area, and area grows with width times width. 3 times 3 is 9. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- A rocket weighs 200 tonnes at lift-off, and 180 tonnes of that is propellant. Its first stage weighs 130 tonnes in all and is dropped when empty. How much does the rest of the rocket weigh after the drop? What fraction of the lift-off weight is that? **70 tonnes, which is a little more than one third.**. 200 take away 130 is 70. 70 out of 200 is 7 out of 20, which is a little more than one third. The rest of the rocket is now much lighter, so each push speeds it up more. (queues `math.fractions.parts-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A lap of the Earth at the station's height is about 42,000 kilometres. The station moves at 7.7 kilometres every second. About how many minutes does one lap take? **About 91 minutes, or about 90 minutes.**. 42,000 divided by 7.7 is about 5,450 seconds. 5,450 divided by 60 is about 91 minutes. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- A Vande Bharat train runs at about 160 kilometres an hour. A satellite near the Earth moves at about 28,000 kilometres an hour. About how many times faster is the satellite? **About 175 times, or about 170 times faster.**. 28,000 divided by 160 is 175. So the satellite is roughly 170 to 175 times as fast as the train. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)
- In your telescope, the front lens has a focal length of 120 centimetres and the eyepiece has a focal length of 6 centimetres. It magnifies 20 times. You swap the eyepiece for a stronger one, to magnify 30 times with the same front lens. What focal length should the new eyepiece have? **4 centimetres.**. Magnifying is the front focal length divided by the eyepiece focal length. 120 divided by 6 is 20. For 30 times, 120 divided by 30 is 4. A stronger eyepiece has a shorter focal length. (queues `math.division.multi-digit.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What keeps a satellite from falling to the ground? → It falls all the time, but moves sideways so fast that it keeps missing.

- Why does a rocket drop its empty stages? → They are dead weight.
- At what height does a satellite stay over one spot? → About 36,000 kilometres, above the equator.
- What are the two jobs of a wide telescope opening? → Collecting more light and showing finer detail.
- Why can radio telescopes work on cloudy days? → Radio waves pass through clouds.
- Why do astronauts exercise two hours a day? → To keep their bones and muscles strong.
- How many days did Chandrayaan-3 take to reach the Moon? → 40 days, from launch to landing.
