# Keeping food safe: why food goes bad, and how to stop it
`sci.food.food-safety.L3`

**Summary.** Dal left out on a hot day turns sour and frothy by the next morning. Tiny living beings called microbes got into it, ate it and multiplied. Washing and covering keep microbes out of food. Cooking kills most of them, and a cold fridge slows the rest. Drying, salt, sugar and sourness keep food for months, which is how pickles and vadams last.

**Intro.** *Smell a spoon of fresh curd, then a spoon of curd left out for two days. What changed? Nobody added anything to it.* Nothing was added that you could see. But food is never empty of life. Tiny living beings called microbes land on it from the air, from hands and from other food. In warm food they eat and multiply, and that is what you smell. In this unit you find out how families keep food safe, and you test it with three dabbas of dal water.

## Terms
- **Microbe**: A living being too small to see without a microscope. Bacteria, moulds and yeasts are microbes. *e.g.* The bacteria that turn milk into curd are microbes.
- **Bacteria**: Microbes made of just one tiny cell each. One of them is called a bacterium. They live almost everywhere. *e.g.* Bacteria in warm dal can make it sour and frothy by the next morning.
- **Mould**: A kind of fungus that grows as fine threads on food, often making green, white or black fuzzy spots. *e.g.* The green fuzz on an old piece of bread is mould.
- **Spore**: A tiny, tough resting cell made by some bacteria and moulds. Spores survive drying and even boiling, and start growing again when things get better. *e.g.* Mould spores float in the air of every kitchen and land on food.
- **Multiply**: To grow in number. A bacterium multiplies by splitting into two. *e.g.* One bacterium becomes 2, then 4, then 8, in warm food.
- **Spoil**: When food goes bad because microbes are growing in it. Spoiled food may smell sour, froth, go slimy or grow fuzz. *e.g.* Sambar left out overnight in summer can spoil.
- **Food poisoning**: An illness that comes from eating food with harmful microbes, or the poisons they make, in it. It can cause vomiting and loose motions. *e.g.* Eating cut fruit that sat uncovered in the sun all day can give you food poisoning.
- **Contaminate**: To get harmful microbes or dirt into something clean. Unwashed hands, flies and raw meat can contaminate food. *e.g.* A fly that walks on rubbish and then on your idli contaminates it.
- **Pasteurise**: To heat a food like milk for a short time, then cool it quickly, so most of its microbes die. *e.g.* Packet milk in India is pasteurised before it reaches the shop.
- **Preserve**: To keep food from spoiling for a long time, for example by drying it, salting it, or pickling it. *e.g.* Lemon pickle in salt keeps for many months.
- **Ferment**: When microbes we want change a food in a useful way, for example making it sour or making it rise. *e.g.* Idli batter ferments overnight and rises.
- **Nutrient**: A part of food that the body needs to live, grow and stay well. Carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins and minerals are nutrients. *e.g.* Dal gives you protein, and rice gives you carbohydrate.
- **Carbohydrate**: The part of food that gives the body most of its energy. Starch and sugar are carbohydrates. *e.g.* Rice, ragi, chapati and potatoes are full of carbohydrate.
- **Protein**: The part of food the body uses to build and repair itself, such as new skin and muscle. *e.g.* Dal, milk, paneer, eggs and fish are rich in protein.
- **Fibre**: The rough part of plant food that the body does not digest. It helps food move through the gut. *e.g.* The skin of a guava and whole moong are full of fibre.

## Explanations
### Why food goes bad · *defined*
**Definition:** Food spoils when microbes such as bacteria and moulds land on it, feed on it and multiply.

Microbes are everywhere: in the air, on hands, on vegetables and in soil. When some land on food that is warm and wet, they feed on it. As they feed, they make gases and acids. That is why spoiled dal smells sour and froths, and why old bread grows fuzz. Food also changes in other ways, without microbes. A cut apple turns brown in air, and old oil can smell stale.
- e.g. Dal left out overnight in summer smells sour and has bubbles on top.
- e.g. A green fuzzy spot grows on bread kept in a closed bag for a week.
- e.g. Milk left out on a hot day turns sour and lumpy.
### A microbe's own problem: finding a meal · *defined*
**Definition:** Like every living being, a microbe needs food, water and warmth to live and multiply.

Look at life from a microbe's side. They are too small to walk to food, so they must land on it, carried by air, water, hands or flies. Then they need water to soak up food, and warmth to grow fast. Our cooked food has all three, so it is a perfect home for them. When times are hard, some bacteria and moulds make spores. A spore is a tough resting cell that can wait through drying or boiling, and grow again later.
- e.g. Bacteria in a dry packet of poha cannot grow, because there is no water.
- e.g. Mould spores floating in the kitchen air wait until they land on damp bread.
- e.g. Some spores survive boiling, which is why cooked rice and dal can still spoil if left warm.
### From a few to millions: why time matters · *defined*
**Definition:** Bacteria multiply by splitting in two, so their number doubles again and again.

In warm food, some bacteria can split every 20 minutes. One becomes 2, then 4, then 8. After 2 hours there can be 64, and after 7 hours more than 2 million. So a food with a few microbes is usually safe, but the same food left warm for hours may not be. The World Health Organization says cooked food should not sit at room temperature for more than 2 hours.
- e.g. Idli batter rises overnight because its microbes multiply for hours.
- e.g. Cooked food eaten within 2 hours is safer than the same food eaten in the evening.
- e.g. A fridge does not kill bacteria, but it slows their splitting a lot.
### Washing and covering: keeping microbes out · *defined*
**Definition:** Washing moves microbes and dirt off food and hands. Covering stops new microbes landing on food.

Soil on vegetables carries many microbes. Washing in running water and rubbing with your fingers moves much of that soil and its microbes away. Washing does not remove every microbe, so food eaten raw must be washed well. Covering food keeps off dust, flies and drops from a cough. A fly walks on rubbish and then on food, carrying microbes on their feet and mouth.
- e.g. Coriander from the market is washed until the water stays clear.
- e.g. A net cover or a steel plate keeps flies off a plate of hot idli.
- e.g. Hands are washed with soap before cooking and before eating.
### Cooking and cooling: killing microbes or slowing them · *defined*
**Definition:** Heat kills most microbes. Cold slows them down. Neither works if food then sits warm for hours.

Boiling water is 100 degrees Celsius. At that heat most bacteria die within a minute or two. A pressure cooker gets hotter, about 120 degrees, and kills even more. Spores and some poisons that microbes make can survive boiling. So cooking helps most when food is eaten soon, or cooled and put in the fridge. A fridge should be colder than 5 degrees Celsius. Microbes grow fastest between 5 and 60 degrees.
- e.g. Milk is boiled, then covered, to keep it from going sour.
- e.g. Leftover sambar is cooled, covered and put in the fridge.
- e.g. Chicken and eggs are cooked until no pink or runny part is left.
### Keep raw and cooked food apart · *defined*
**Definition:** Raw meat, fish, eggs and unwashed vegetables can carry microbes onto cooked food that is ready to eat.

Cooking kills the microbes in the food being cooked. But if raw fish touches a board, and cooked food is then cut on the same board, microbes ride across. The World Health Organization calls this one of its five keys to safer food. Use a separate board and knife, or wash them with soap and hot water in between.
- e.g. Raw fish is cut on one board, and the cucumber salad on another.
- e.g. A plate that held raw chicken is washed before cooked chicken goes on it.
- e.g. Raw eggs are kept in their own box in the fridge.
### Old ways of keeping food: drying, salt, sugar, oil and sourness · *defined*
**Definition:** Food keeps for a long time when microbes cannot get water, or when it is too salty, too sweet or too sour for most of them.

Microbes need water. Sun-dried vadams, appalams and dried fish keep for months, because there is almost no water left in them. Lots of salt or sugar pulls water out of microbes, so they cannot grow. That is how lemon pickle and mango jam keep. Sour foods, such as tamarind and lemon, slow down many microbes too. In pickles, a layer of oil on top also keeps air and new microbes off. A wet spoon can bring water into a pickle and let mould grow.
- e.g. Tamarind rice was a favourite food for long train journeys.
- e.g. Kerala families dry fish in the sun and store it for the rainy months.
- e.g. Families in the north make aam papad, thin sheets of dried mango pulp.
### Spoiling and fermenting: microbes we do not want, and microbes we do · *defined*
**Definition:** Fermenting is when microbes we want change food in a useful way. Spoiling is when other microbes, or the same ones for too long, make it unpleasant or unsafe.

Idli batter is left out overnight so that bacteria and yeasts can eat and multiply. They make gas, so the batter rises, and acid, so it turns sour. That is fermenting. Curd is made the same way, by curd bacteria. Because the good microbes fill the food first, they leave little room for harmful ones. But batter left out for two or three days goes too sour and may grow mould.
- e.g. Milk with a spoon of curd added sets into curd overnight.
- e.g. Dosa batter rises when it ferments overnight.
- e.g. Batter left out for three days smells too sour and may grow mould.
### Parts of food and their jobs · *defined*
**Definition:** Food is made of nutrients, and each kind has a main job in the body.

Carbohydrates, such as rice and ragi, give most of our energy. Fats, such as ghee and oil, give energy too, and the body stores fat for later. Proteins, such as dal, milk and fish, build and repair the body. Vitamins and minerals, such as those in fruit, leafy greens and milk, keep the body working and help it fight illness. Fibre helps food move through the gut, and water carries everything around. Microbes need many of the same nutrients. That is why the foods that feed us best, like dal and milk, also spoil fast.
- e.g. A ragi ball gives energy.
- e.g. Sambar's dal gives protein for growing.
- e.g. Amla and guava give vitamin C, which helps cuts heal.
### Food poisoning: what happens, and what to do · *defined*
**Definition:** Food poisoning is illness from eating food that has harmful microbes, or the poisons they made, in it.

Most food poisoning gives a tummy ache, vomiting or loose motions for a day or two. The body loses a lot of water, so the most important thing is to drink. ORS, a drink of clean water with the right amounts of salt and sugar, puts back what is lost. A child who cannot keep drinks down, or seems very sleepy, needs a doctor quickly. Some microbes make poisons that survive boiling. So reheating food that has already spoiled does not make it safe.
- e.g. A family at a wedding feast who all ate the same old chicken may all fall ill.
- e.g. A child with loose motions drinks small sips of ORS often.
- e.g. Spoiled dal is thrown away, not boiled again.
### Is the five-second rule true? · *defined*
**Definition:** The five-second rule says food dropped on the floor is safe if you pick it up within five seconds. Tests show microbes can move onto food in less than one second.

In 2016, Robyn Miranda and Donald Schaffner at Rutgers University in the United States tested it. They dropped watermelon, bread, buttered bread and jelly sweets onto steel, tile, wood and carpet spread with harmless bacteria. Some bacteria moved onto the food in under one second. Longer contact moved more. Wet watermelon picked up the most, and the dry sweet the least. So the floor, the food and the time all matter, and no rule of five seconds makes food clean.
- e.g. A wet cucumber slice picks up more floor dirt than a dry biscuit.
- e.g. A clean, dry floor carries fewer microbes than a kitchen floor near the dustbin.
- e.g. Food left on the floor for a minute picks up more than food picked up at once.
### How should we wash fruit and vegetables? · *debated*
Everyone agrees that soil should be washed off, and that soap and washing powder must never be used on food. People disagree on whether soaking in salt water, tamarind water or vinegar water does much more than plain water.
- e.g. Many families soak greens in salt water before washing them.
- e.g. Food safety agencies in many countries advise rubbing produce under clean running water.
- e.g. Peeling removes more from a cucumber or carrot than washing does.
- **Food safety agencies, such as the United States Food and Drug Administration:** Rub fruit and vegetables under clean running water. This removes most soil and many microbes. Soaps and special washes are not needed, and soap can stay on food and upset the stomach.
- **Many Indian families and some researchers:** Soaking in salt water or a weak vinegar solution for a few minutes, then rinsing, removes more dirt and some pesticide from leafy greens. Some studies find this helps more than plain water. Other studies find only a small difference.

## Mostly agreed
- Most food spoils because microbes, such as bacteria and moulds, grow in it. *How we know:* Food that is boiled and sealed, so that no new microbes can get in, stays fresh for months or years. This is how tinned food works. Lazzaro Spallanzani showed this with boiled, sealed broth in the 1760s, and Louis Pasteur showed it again in the 1860s.
- Microbes grow fastest in food that is warm and wet, between about 5 and 60 degrees Celsius. *How we know:* Scientists grow microbes in labs at different temperatures and count them. Food kept in a fridge, or kept very hot, spoils much more slowly.
- Boiling kills most bacteria, but not all spores and not all the poisons some microbes make. *How we know:* Lab tests show that bacterial spores can survive boiling. Some outbreaks of food poisoning came from food that was cooked and then left warm for hours.
- Washing hands with soap, washing produce, cooking well, keeping raw and cooked food apart and keeping food cold all lower food poisoning. *How we know:* The World Health Organization's Five Keys to Safer Food are based on many studies of where food poisoning comes from.
- Drying, salt, sugar and sourness keep many foods safe for a long time. *How we know:* Microbes need water to grow. Dried, salted and sugary foods hold very little water that microbes can use. Pickles and dried fish have been stored this way for thousands of years.

## How we found out
**Alice Catherine Evans, a microbiologist at the United States Department of Agriculture, 1917 to 1930.** In the early 1900s, many people in the United States drank raw milk straight from the farm. Doctors knew a cow illness caused by a bacterium called Bacillus abortus. They believed this germ harmed only cows. Alice Evans was a scientist at the government's dairy laboratory in Washington. She looked closely at Bacillus abortus and at another bacterium that caused a long, coming-and-going fever in people, called Malta fever. Under the microscope and in careful tests, the two were almost the same. She warned that raw milk from cows that looked healthy could make people ill, and that milk should be pasteurised. She reported this in 1917 and published it in 1918. Many scientists and doctors did not believe her, partly because she was a woman without a doctorate. Other scientists then tested her idea and kept finding she was right. In 1922 she caught the illness herself, and it troubled her for about twenty years. In 1928 she became the first woman to lead the Society of American Bacteriologists. Pasteurising milk became the rule in the United States, and the illness became rare.  
*What people believed before:* Doctors believed the bacterium that made cows ill could not harm people, so raw milk from healthy-looking cows was safe. Alice Evans showed the cow germ and the germ of a human fever were almost the same, and others confirmed that raw milk could spread it.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Food dropped on the floor is safe if you pick it up within five seconds.** (A popular saying in many countries; *rejected*)  
  For: Very little dirt can be seen on food picked up at once, and most people who do this do not fall ill.  
  Against: In tests at Rutgers University in 2016, bacteria moved onto food in under one second. Wet foods picked up the most. You cannot see microbes, so 'it looks clean' tells you little.
- **If food smells fine, it is safe to eat.** (Many people; *rejected*)  
  For: Spoiled food often smells sour or bad, and a bad smell is a useful warning.  
  Against: Some harmful bacteria grow in food without changing its smell or taste. Food left warm for many hours can make people ill even if it smells normal.
- **Boiling spoiled food again makes it safe.** (A common idea in many kitchens; *rejected*)  
  For: Boiling kills most bacteria, so reheating seems to start again.  
  Against: Some bacteria make poisons that survive boiling. The poison stays even after the bacteria die, so food that has already spoiled should be thrown away.
- **A fridge kills germs.** (Many children and some grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: Food lasts much longer in the fridge, so it seems the cold must kill microbes.  
  Against: Cold slows most microbes but does not kill them. When the food warms up again, they start multiplying. Food in the fridge still spoils, only more slowly.
- **Packet milk must be boiled before drinking.** (Many Indian families; *debated*)  
  For: Boiling kills microbes, and milk can pick up microbes if it is kept out of the fridge, opened, or poured into an unclean vessel. Boiling is a safe habit when you are not sure.  
  Against: Packet milk is already pasteurised. If it has been kept cold and the packet is fresh and unopened, it is safe to drink without boiling. Boiling for a long time also loses some vitamins.

## Roots
We have watched three dabbas of dal water spoil at different speeds. Now we ask what that tells us about food, time and the living beings we share our kitchen with.

**Deep truth.** The three dabbas of dal water looked the same on the first day. The boiled, closed one stayed fresh longest, because it started with the fewest living microbes. Microbes double again and again, so a few take longer to become a crowd that spoils food. So safe food is food eaten while its microbes are still few.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort food into 'clean' and 'dirty', or 'fresh' and 'spoiled', as if these were fixed labels. In fact every food holds some microbes. What changes is how many there are, and that number grows with time.  
*Evidence:* In the Three Dabbas test, dabba C, with many microbes from the unwashed leaf, spoiled first. Dabba A, boiled and closed, with the fewest, spoiled last. All three got the same dal water.

- *who am i:* You are an eater, like the microbes in the dal. You both need the same food to live.
- *what is life:* Life is always growing in number where there is food, water and warmth. Your food is a meal for many beings, not only for you.
- *what is this world:* The air, your hands and every leaf carry tiny living beings. A kitchen is a shared home, and keeping food safe means sharing it wisely.

One bacterium becomes two, two become four, and soon a dabba of dal holds millions. Each of them is a living being feeding and making copies of themself. The same dal would have become you, if you had eaten it. So the one meal can turn into many different lives, depending on who reaches it first.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2 (Bhrigu Valli):** Bhrigu came to understand that food is Brahman. From food all beings are born, by food they live, and into food they return. (The dal water fed the microbes, and the microbes multiplied from it. Every living being, from a bacterium to a child, is made from food and lives by it.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 17.10:** The Gita describes food that was cooked long before, that has lost its taste, that smells bad and is stale, as food that dulls the mind and body. (Long ago, people noticed that cooked food left for many hours changes and can harm us. The Three Dabbas test shows why: time lets microbes multiply.)
- Science (settled): Bacteria multiply by splitting, so their number can double every 20 minutes in warm food.
- Science (open): How many microbes of each kind it takes to make a person ill depends on the microbe and the person, and for many germs it is still being studied.
- Other view, Some food scientists: Not all spoiling is microbes. A cut apple turns brown because of chemicals in the apple meeting air, and oil goes stale by reacting with oxygen. So 'how many microbes' is only part of the story of food going bad.
- Practice, Where did this meal come from? (3 min): Before your next meal, look at your plate for a moment. Think of the rice growing in a field and the dal in its pod. Then think of all the tiny beings who would like to eat it too. Say thank you to the cook, and eat while it is fresh.
- Ask: If microbes and people both need the same food, who does the food belong to?
- Ask: Is spoiling bad, or is it only bad for us?
- Ask: Can there ever be food with no other life in it at all?

## Story: The Tamarind Rice Tin
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: dry, sour, salty and oily foods keep longer, and cooked food left warm for many hours can make people ill.*

The night before the long train journey, Paati's kitchen in Madurai smelled of tamarind and roasted chilli.

Kavya was nine. She and her little brother Arjun were going to Delhi with Appa, to see their aunt. The train would take almost two days.

"Two days of train food?" said Arjun.

"Two days of my food," said Paati. She was stirring a big pan of puliyodarai, rice mixed with a thick, dark tamarind paste, roasted dal, peanuts and plenty of sesame oil.

Kavya sat on the kitchen step and watched. On the counter were four steel tins. One held the tamarind rice. One held idlis rolled in milagai podi, the red chilli powder, with oil poured over. One held lemon rice. The last one held curd rice.

"Paati, why these four?" asked Kavya. "Why not sambar and rice, like every day?"

Paati smiled. "Good question. Sambar is lovely today. But by tomorrow afternoon, in a hot train, it would turn sour and frothy. These foods keep."

"Why do they keep?"

"You tell me," said Paati. "Look at them. What is the same about them?"

Kavya looked. The tamarind rice was sour and oily. The lemon rice was sour too. The idlis were dry and coated in oil and chilli powder. She tasted a grain of the tamarind rice. It was sour, salty and hot all at once.

"They are all sour, or dry, or salty," she said slowly. "And they all have oil."

"And the curd rice?" asked Paati.

"Curd is sour too," said Kavya. "But it is wet."

"Exactly," said Paati. "So the curd rice we eat tonight, for the first dinner on the train. The tamarind rice can wait until tomorrow."

Kavya remembered something from her science book. "Food goes bad because microbes eat it," she said. "Tiny living things. They need water and warmth. Sambar has lots of water."

"And tamarind?"

"Maybe sour is hard for them," said Kavya. "And salt pulls the water out of them. And oil keeps the air off."

Paati pressed the lid down on the tamarind rice tin. "My mother packed these same foods when she went to Kashi on the train, sixty years ago. She did not know about microbes. But she knew what keeps."

The next morning they climbed into the train at Madurai Junction. The fan turned slowly. Outside, the fields went by, green and then brown and then green again.

That night they ate the curd rice with a little lime pickle. It was cool and good.

The next day it was very hot. By lunchtime, Arjun was hungry and grumpy. Under his seat he found a small box. It was a little curd rice left over from the night before, shut in a bag all day beside the warm window.

"I want this one," he said, opening it.

Kavya held his hand. "Wait. That sat warm since last night. Smell it."

Arjun sniffed. "It smells fine."

"It might still have lots of microbes," said Kavya. "Some of them don't change the smell. Paati said wet food left warm for a long time is the risky one."

Appa looked up from his newspaper. "Kavya is right. Let's not take the chance."

Kavya opened the tamarind rice tin instead. It smelled just as it had in Paati's kitchen. They ate it with the peanuts crunching, and nobody missed the curd rice.

On the second evening, a man selling cut pineapple walked through the coach. The pieces sat on an open tray, and two flies walked on them. Arjun looked at Kavya.

"Uncovered, cut, wet and warm," he said. "Lots of microbes."

Kavya laughed. "Now you sound like Paati."

In Delhi, their aunt asked what they had eaten on the train. Arjun told her about the tamarind rice. Then he explained why it kept: sour, salty, oily and dry. Kavya let him tell it. He had worked it out on the train, and he got it right.

**For the parent:** For the parent: food keeps when microbes find it hard to grow in it. Dry, sour, very salty or oily foods keep for a day or more, while wet, mild foods like sambar or curd rice should be eaten within a few hours if they are not kept cold. Kavya worked it out by looking at what the travel foods had in common.

- Ask: What did the four travel foods have in common?
- Ask: Why did the family eat the curd rice on the first night, and not on the second day?
- Ask: Arjun said the old curd rice smelled fine. Why did Kavya still say no?
- Ask: Why was the cut pineapple on the open tray a risk?
- Ask: Which foods would you pack for a two-day train journey, and why?

### e1 · Three Dabbas of Dal Water: which stays fresh longest? (30 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Dal water is boiled and poured into three clean dabbas. One is closed, one is left open, and one is closed with an unwashed coriander leaf inside. Which one stays fresh longest?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which dabba will go sour and frothy first, and which will stay fresh longest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** To make it fair, all three dabbas get the same dal water, boiled together, in the same amount. They are the same kind of dabba, rinsed the same way, and kept side by side on one shelf. Only one thing is different in each: whether it is covered, and whether an unwashed leaf goes in.
- **safety:** A grown-up does all the boiling and pouring. Never taste the dal water at any stage. Smell it by wafting the air towards your nose with your hand, not by putting your nose in. At the end, a grown-up pours it down the sink or toilet, and washes the dabbas with soap and hot water.
- **record:** Fill in the table with what you see and smell each time: clear or cloudy, bubbles or froth, a skin or fuzz on top, and the smell (fresh, a little sour, very sour). Then write what you think happened, what you saw, and why.
- **results table:** Dabba | What was done | Bedtime, day 1 | Morning, day 2 | Evening, day 2 | Day 3 | Order it spoiled (1st, 2nd, 3rd)
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess, then boil the dal water, fill the three dabbas, and look at them at bedtime. (25 min); day 2: Look and smell, morning and evening, and fill in the table. (5 min); day 3: Look and smell again. Decide the order. Then a grown-up throws the dal water away and washes the dabbas. (5 min)

  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child the three empty dabbas. 2. Explain what will go in each. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write it down.  
  *Parent says:* All three get the same boiled dal water. A gets a lid. B stays open. C gets a lid, but first an unwashed coriander leaf goes in. Which will go sour first? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Three dabbas, the same dal water. A is closed. B is open. C is closed with an unwashed leaf. Which goes sour first? Why?
  - Ask: Which dabba do you think will stay fresh longest, and why? (good: A, because nothing new can get in.; A, because the leaf in C has dirt on it, and B is open to flies and dust.)

  *Parent says:* Boiling kills most of the microbes already in the dal. So all three dabbas start almost clean.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up boils the dal water. Boiling kills most microbes in it. Stand back from the stove.
  - Note: The mixture should be thin, like watery dal. If it is thick, add a little more water. Stir so it does not stick.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. While the dal boils, pour just-boiled water into each dabba and onto each lid. 2. Swirl, and pour it away. 3. Label the dabbas A, B and C.

  *Parent says:* Now only two things are different. B is open to the air. C has a leaf from the market, with whatever was on it.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours. A gets its lid. B stays open. You drop the unwashed leaves into C. Then close C.

  *Child mode:* Put them side by side on a shelf. Not in the fridge. Look again at bedtime.
  - Note: Lift each lid only for a moment when you look, and put it straight back.

### e2 · How Many Washes? The coriander test (20 min)

### e3 · The Five-Second Test: does dropped food pick up dirt? (20 min)

  *Child mode:* Which picked up the most? Was 1 second safe?

  - Check: Anjali dropped a wet slice of cucumber on the kitchen floor and picked it up in one second. What is true? → **Some dirt and microbes can stick to it, even in one second**

## Applications
- Dairies pasteurise milk before it goes into packets.
- Tinned and bottled foods are heated and sealed so no microbes can live or get in.
- Railway and school kitchens follow food-safety rules from FSSAI, India's food safety authority.
- Hospitals cook food for patients with extra care, because illness makes people weaker against germs.
- Ice cream, fish and vaccines travel in cold vans and boxes so they never warm up on the way.
- Factories dry milk into milk powder and fruit into dried fruit so they keep for months.
- Health workers teach families to boil or filter water, wash hands and cover food, to prevent diarrhoea in children.
- Astronauts eat food that is dried or sealed in packets, because there is no fresh food in space.

## Open questions
- **How many of the microbes in the air of a kitchen are alive, and which kinds land on food most?** Scientists have only started counting the microbes in homes. They vary from house to house, with the season, and with who lives there.
- **Does soaking vegetables in salt water or vinegar remove many more microbes than plain running water?** Studies give different answers. It depends on the vegetable, the microbe, the strength of the soak and how long it lasts.
- **Why do some people fall ill from the same food that leaves others well?** Age, the microbes already in the gut, past illnesses and genes all seem to play a part, but nobody can yet say exactly how much each one matters.
- **Can we keep food fresh longer using helpful microbes, the way curd bacteria crowd out harmful ones?** Food scientists are testing helpful bacteria and their natural chemicals on foods. Some work, but how safe and how useful they are for many foods is still being studied.
- **Which old Indian ways of keeping food work best, and why?** Some, like drying and salting, are well understood. Others, like the exact effect of turmeric, tamarind or mustard oil on different microbes, have been tested only a little.

How many could there be at 10 o'clock? **64 bacteria.**. 2 hours is 6 lots of 20 minutes, so the bacteria double 6 times. 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L3`)
- In the Three Dabbas test, Nandini looked at her dabbas at bedtime on day 1, in the morning and evening of day 2, and once on day 3. Dabba C smelled sour at the first morning check, and dabba B at the evening check. Dabba B lasted how many checks longer than C? **1 check longer.**. The checks were: bedtime day 1, morning day 2, evening day 2, day 3. C went sour at check 2 and B at check 3. 3 minus 2 is 1. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- In How Many Washes, Revathi's coriander needed 4 washes. She used 4 tumblers of water for each wash. How many tumblers did she use in all? How many would she save if she could get it clean in 3 washes? **16 tumblers. She would save 4 tumblers.**. 4 washes times 4 tumblers is 16. 3 washes times 4 tumblers is 12. 16 minus 12 is 4. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- The World Health Organization says cooked food should not sit out for more than 2 hours. Lakshmi's family finished cooking lunch at 12:30. By what time should the leftovers go in the fridge? **By 2:30.**. 12:30 plus 2 hours is 2:30 in the afternoon. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
- Farhan's family has 12 food packets. 4 of them say 'Keep refrigerated'. What fraction of the packets must be kept cold? **One third of the packets.**. 4 out of 12 is 4 twelfths. 12 shared into 3 equal groups is 4 in each, so 4 is one of those 3 groups: one third. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What makes food go bad? → Microbes that feed and multiply in it.
- Name the four habits that keep food safe. → Washing, covering, cooking and keeping it cold.
- Does a fridge kill microbes? → No. It slows them down.
- Why do vadams keep so long? → They are dry, and microbes need water.
- Is the five-second rule true? → No. Microbes can move onto food in under a second.
- Which part of food gives most of our energy? → Carbohydrate, as in rice and ragi.
- What is pasteurised milk? → Milk heated for a short time and cooled, so most microbes die.
- What should you drink if you have loose motions? → ORS, or plenty of clean water with a little salt and sugar.
