# The immune system: the defence that learns and remembers
`sci.body.immune-system.L4`

**Summary.** You catch chickenpox once, but you catch a cold again and again. Your body has two defences. The first is quick and the same every time. The second is slow on the first meeting with a germ, but it picks out a cell that fits that one germ, makes many copies, and keeps a memory. Your child plays the Key Shop to see how a body can pick a matching cell from many, and runs a town test to see why vaccinating some people protects others. They learn how a vaccine gives the slow first lesson safely, and how Sarah Nelmes's blistered hand led to the first one.

**Intro.** *Most children in India have a small round scar on the top of one arm. It came from one tiny injection when they were babies, and it never went away. Why would a scratch from one day in your first weeks still matter now?* That injection was a vaccine. It showed your body a harmless piece of a germ, once, long ago. Your body is still ready for that germ today. You have not forgotten it, though you do not remember it. Your body has two defences. One is quick and works the same way every time. The other one learns, and it keeps what it learns for years. In this unit you find out how a body can learn without a teacher, and how doctors found a way to use that.

## Terms
- **Immunity**: Immunity is when your body is ready to stop a germ before it makes you ill. *e.g.* After chickenpox, most people have immunity to chickenpox for life.
- **Innate immunity**: The quick defence you are born with. It reacts to almost any germ in the same way, within minutes or hours. *e.g.* Redness and swelling around a cut, and white blood cells eating germs.
- **Adaptive immunity**: The defence that learns. It takes days on the first meeting with a germ, fits that one germ exactly, and remembers it. *e.g.* After a measles vaccine, your body can stop measles for many years.
- **Antigen**: Any shape on a germ that your immune system can notice and react to. *e.g.* The spiky bits on the outside of a virus are antigens.
- **Antibody**: A tiny Y-shaped protein that fits one antigen, like a key fits one lock. Antibodies stick to germs and mark them to be removed. *e.g.* A measles antibody sticks to measles and does not stick to flu.
- **B cell**: A white blood cell that makes antibodies. Each B cell makes only one kind of antibody. *e.g.* Your body has millions of B cells, each one ready for a different shape.
- **T cell**: A white blood cell that helps run the fight. Helper T cells give orders. Killer T cells destroy body cells that a virus has taken over. *e.g.* Killer T cells find a cell with a virus hiding inside and break it open.
- **Lymph node**: A small bean-shaped knot where immune cells meet and learn about germs. You have hundreds, in your neck, armpits and groin. *e.g.* When you have a sore throat, the lumps under your jaw are lymph nodes.
- **Memory cell**: A long-lived immune cell that stays after a fight. It remembers one germ and can act within days when that germ returns. *e.g.* A memory cell for measles stays in your body for decades.
- **Clone**: A copy of a cell. When a B cell finds a germ it fits, it splits again and again into many copies. *e.g.* One B cell can become thousands of identical copies in a week.
- **Vaccine**: A safe way to show the immune system a germ, so the slow first lesson happens before the real germ arrives. *e.g.* The polio drops and injections given to babies in India.
- **Booster**: An extra dose of a vaccine that reminds the body, so its memory is stronger or lasts longer. *e.g.* The extra tetanus dose for older children.
- **Herd protection**: When enough people in a place are protected, a germ cannot easily find new people to spread to. People who cannot be vaccinated are safer too. *e.g.* A baby too young for the measles vaccine is safer when everyone around is protected.
- **Allergy**: When the immune system reacts strongly to something harmless, such as pollen, dust or a food, as if it were a germ. *e.g.* Sneezing and itchy eyes in a dusty season.
- **Variolation**: An old way to protect against smallpox by giving a small dose of real smallpox, to cause a mild illness. It could kill, but less often than catching smallpox naturally. *e.g.* Lady Mary Wortley Montagu had her children variolated in the 1700s.

## Explanations
### Two defences: the quick one and the one that learns · *defined*
**Definition:** Innate immunity is the quick defence you are born with. Adaptive immunity is the defence that learns each germ.

Your first defence is innate immunity. It includes the walls you learned about in Class 3, such as skin, tears and mucus, and the white blood cells that eat germs. It reacts within minutes or hours, and it reacts in much the same way to almost every germ. That makes it fast, but it cannot tell one germ from another very well. Your second defence is adaptive immunity. It takes about a week or more the first time, because it has to find the right cell for this one germ. But it fits that germ exactly, it grows into a large army, and it keeps a memory. Both defences matter. The quick one holds the line while the slow one gets ready.
- e.g. Redness and swelling around a cut is the quick defence.
- e.g. Antibodies against measles after a measles vaccine is the learning defence.
- e.g. A cold: the quick defence gives you a runny nose, and the learning defence clears the virus a few days later.
### Antigens and antibodies: shapes that fit · *defined*
**Definition:** An antigen is a shape on a germ that the immune system notices. An antibody is a tiny Y-shaped protein that fits one antigen.

Every germ has shapes on the outside. Those shapes are antigens. An antibody is a protein that has a shape on each arm of its Y. That shape fits one antigen, as a key fits one lock. When an antibody fits, it sticks. Germs covered in antibodies cannot easily enter your cells. They also clump together, and white blood cells find them and eat them more easily. An antibody against measles will not stick to flu, because the shapes do not fit. This is why the defence that learns must make a different antibody for each different germ.
- e.g. The spikes of a virus are antigens.
- e.g. An antibody against chickenpox sticks to chickenpox and not to a cold virus.
- e.g. Antibodies sticking to bacteria make them easy for white blood cells to eat.
### B cells and T cells: the two kinds of learning cells · *defined*
**Definition:** B cells make antibodies. T cells help run the fight and destroy body cells that a virus has taken over.

The defence that learns uses two main kinds of white blood cells. B cells make antibodies, and each B cell makes only one kind. Antibodies work well against germs that float around in your blood and fluids. But a virus also hides inside your own cells, where antibodies cannot reach. For this, you have T cells. Helper T cells give orders and wake up the B cells. Killer T cells check your own cells, find the ones with a virus hiding inside, and destroy them. B cells and T cells work as a team. Neither could do the whole job alone.
- e.g. A B cell makes antibodies for one germ shape.
- e.g. A helper T cell tells B cells to start making antibodies.
- e.g. A killer T cell breaks open a cell that has a virus inside it.
### The body chooses a defender. It does not copy the germ. · *defined*
**Definition:** Clonal selection means that the body already has many different B cells, and a germ finds the one that fits and makes it multiply.

How does your body make an antibody for a germ it has never met? The answer is that it makes millions of different B cells ahead of time, each with a different antibody shape, before any germ arrives. Most never meet a matching germ. When a germ arrives, one of those B cells happens to fit its antigen. That B cell is chosen, and it splits again and again into thousands of copies. Each copy makes the same antibody. The germ did not teach the body how to make the antibody. The germ only picked out one that was already there. This idea is called clonal selection. It is natural selection, as in Darwin's idea, happening inside your body in a few days.
- e.g. A body holds a huge range of B cells before any germ arrives.
- e.g. A germ finds the one B cell that fits and makes it multiply.
- e.g. The copies make identical antibodies, and the fight turns.
### First time slow, second time fast: memory cells · *defined*
**Definition:** Memory cells are B cells and T cells that stay in the body after a fight and respond within days to the same germ.

The first time a germ arrives, it takes about a week or more for enough of the right B cells to build up. During that time the germ can make you ill. After the fight, most of the copies die. A few stay as memory cells, sometimes for decades. When the same germ comes back, memory cells start making antibodies within two to four days, and in much larger amounts. The germ is stopped before you feel ill. This is why you usually have chickenpox only once.
- e.g. Most people catch chickenpox once in their lives.
- e.g. A second meeting with measles is usually stopped before it causes illness.
- e.g. Colds keep coming back because there are more than 200 different viruses that cause them.
### Lymph nodes: where cells meet and learn · *defined*
**Definition:** Lymph nodes are small bean-shaped organs where immune cells meet germ pieces and multiply.

Fluid called lymph drains from all over your body, carrying bits of whatever is in your tissues. It flows through lymph nodes before it returns to your blood. Inside the nodes, T cells and B cells wait. Scout cells from the quick defence carry a piece of a germ to a node and show it to them. If a B cell fits, it starts to multiply right there. So when you have a sore throat, the nodes under your jaw fill with dividing cells and swell. They may feel sore. That is a sign the learning defence is at work.
- e.g. Sore, swollen lumps under the jaw during a throat infection.
- e.g. Swollen lumps in the armpit after an infected cut on the hand.
- e.g. Swelling that goes back down after a week or two.
### The two defences talk to each other · *defined*
**Definition:** Scout cells of the quick defence carry germ pieces to T cells and wake the defence that learns.

The quick defence and the defence that learns are not separate. A scout cell, called a dendritic cell, meets a germ at a cut. It eats the germ, cuts it up, and carries a piece of it to a lymph node. There it shows the piece to T cells. When a T cell fits, it wakes up and starts the learning defence. So the quick defence often starts the slow one. If the quick defence stops a germ at the skin, the slow one may never need to wake up.
- e.g. A scout cell carries a piece of a germ from a cut to a lymph node.
- e.g. A T cell that fits wakes up and tells B cells to start.
- e.g. A germ stopped by tears may never reach a lymph node.
### How a vaccine teaches the body · *defined*
**Definition:** A vaccine shows the immune system a safe version of a germ, so memory cells are made before the real germ arrives.

A vaccine does the slow first lesson in advance, and does it safely. There are a few ways to do it. Some vaccines use a germ that has been weakened so that it cannot make you ill. Some use a germ that has been killed. Some use only a small piece of the germ, such as a spike. Tetanus vaccine uses a harmless form of the poison the tetanus bacteria make. Newer vaccines carry a small set of instructions, called mRNA, that tells your own cells to make one harmless spike for a short time. In every case, B cells and T cells find the antigen, the right ones multiply, and memory cells stay. A sore arm or a mild fever afterwards is the immune system at work. Serious reactions are rare.
- e.g. BCG, polio drops and measles vaccine use weakened germs.
- e.g. Injected polio vaccine uses killed polio viruses.
- e.g. Hepatitis B vaccine uses a piece of the virus, and tetanus vaccine uses a harmless form of the poison.
### Why some vaccines need boosters, and why flu changes · *defined*
**Definition:** A booster is an extra dose to strengthen or extend memory. Some germs change their shape, so old memory fits them less well.

Memory does not last the same time for every germ. For some, one or two doses give memory for years. For others, the antibodies slowly fade, and an extra dose a few years later brings them back up. Some germs also change. Flu viruses slowly change the shapes on their outside, so last year's memory fits this year's flu less well. This is why doctors update the flu vaccine. The common cold is caused by hundreds of different viruses. You may have a memory for one of them and meet a different one next month. Germs change by a kind of natural selection too. The ones whose shapes slip past our memory spread best.
- e.g. An extra tetanus dose for older children.
- e.g. A new flu vaccine each year.
- e.g. Many different cold viruses, so many colds.
### Herd protection: protecting people who cannot be protected · *defined*
**Definition:** When enough people in a community are immune, a germ cannot spread easily, and people who are not immune are safer too.

A germ needs a path from person to person. When most people are immune, the germ reaches a protected person and stops. That cuts the path to the few who are not protected. Some people cannot be vaccinated safely, such as tiny babies or people whose immune system is weak from an illness or treatment. They are protected by the people around them. How many people must be immune depends on how easily the germ spreads. Measles spreads very easily, so about nineteen in every twenty people need to be immune. A germ that spreads less easily needs a smaller share.
- e.g. A school where almost every child is vaccinated against measles.
- e.g. A newborn baby kept safe by the people around her.
- e.g. A town where a germ dies out after reaching only a few people.
### Babies borrow memory · *defined*
**Definition:** A mother passes some of her antibodies to her baby before birth and through her first milk.

A newborn baby has not had time to build memory. So a mother lends her baby some of her own antibodies. They cross to the baby before birth. More come in the first milk, called colostrum, and in breast milk afterwards. These borrowed antibodies protect the baby for the first months. Then they slowly fade, and that is when the baby's vaccines start to matter. So the baby is first protected by her mother's memory and later by her own.
- e.g. A newborn who is protected from some illnesses in the first months.
- e.g. Colostrum, the thick first milk, rich in antibodies.
- e.g. The vaccine schedule starting just as borrowed antibodies fade.
### When the defence goes wrong: allergies and more · *defined*
**Definition:** An allergy is when the immune system reacts strongly to something harmless, such as pollen or a food.

The learning defence can make mistakes. In an allergy, it treats a harmless thing as a germ. The body makes a large reaction to pollen, dust, or a food such as groundnuts. That is why a person with an allergy sneezes, itches or gets a rash. A strong allergic reaction can be dangerous, and it needs a doctor. In some illnesses, the defence attacks the body's own cells by mistake. And in some people, parts of the defence are missing or weak. Doctors can help with all of these. Scientists are still working out why the defence goes wrong in some people and not in others.
- e.g. Sneezing in a dusty season.
- e.g. A rash after a food that does not suit one person.
- e.g. A person whose body attacks its own insulin cells, as in type 1 diabetes.
### Down to the genes: how a body makes billions of shapes · *defined*
**Definition:** Your B cells build their antibody genes by picking pieces at random from lists and joining them, so a few hundred pieces can make billions of shapes.

You have only about 20,000 genes, yet your B cells can make billions of different antibodies. How? The gene for an antibody is not read from one place. Each new B cell picks one piece from each of three short lists and joins them. Then the cell adds a few random letters at the joins. Think of making a word by choosing one syllable from each of three bags. With ten syllables in each bag, you can make a thousand different words. Add random letters at the joins, and the number grows much larger. The Japanese scientist Susumu Tonegawa showed this shuffling in the 1970s, and won a Nobel Prize for it in 1987.
- e.g. Three bags of ten syllables give a thousand words.
- e.g. Each B cell shuffles in its own random way.
- e.g. Billions of shapes come from a few hundred pieces.
### What nobody knows yet · *idea*
Scientists have learned a great deal about the immune system, but many big questions are still open. Why do some memories last for life and others fade after a few years? Why does the same germ make one person very ill and another person hardly ill at all? Why does the defence turn on the body's own cells in some people? How do the nervous system and the immune system talk to each other? These questions matter for medicine, because answers could help design better vaccines, and cures for allergies and illnesses where the defence attacks the body.
- e.g. How long a memory lasts for each germ.
- e.g. Why a germ affects people so differently.
- e.g. Why a few people's defences attack their own bodies.
- **Many immunologists:** The big picture is clear, but many details of memory and of misfiring are still being worked out.
- **Researchers on the body's wider messaging:** The immune system may be better understood as one part of a whole body of signals, linked to sleep, stress and the gut, not a separate army.

## Mostly agreed
- The body has two defences: an innate one that reacts quickly and the same way to most germs, and an adaptive one that learns each germ and remembers it. *How we know:* People and animals with a damaged adaptive defence get repeated, serious infections even though their quick defence works.
- Each B cell makes one kind of antibody, and a germ selects the matching B cell, which then multiplies. This is clonal selection. *How we know:* In 1958, Gustav Nossal and Joshua Lederberg tested single cells from a rat and found that each cell made only one kind of antibody.
- Memory cells make a second infection much faster and stronger than the first. *How we know:* After vaccines and infections, blood tests show antibodies rising within days on the second exposure, against more than a week the first time.
- Vaccines train the adaptive defence without the illness, and have saved millions of lives. *How we know:* The World Health Organization declared smallpox gone from the world in 1980, after a worldwide vaccination effort.
- When enough people are immune, a germ spreads much less, and people who are not immune are protected too. *How we know:* Across many countries, measles cases fell sharply as vaccination rose, and rose again where vaccination dropped.
- A mother's antibodies reach her baby before birth and in her first milk, and protect the baby for the first months. *How we know:* Newborns have their mother's antibodies in their blood at birth, and the amounts fall over the first months.
- Some germs, such as flu viruses, change their outer shapes over time, so memory must be updated. *How we know:* Flu vaccines are reviewed and updated every year using samples of the viruses that are going around.

## How we found out
**Linus Pauling, Niels Jerne, Macfarlane Burnet, Gustav Nossal and Joshua Lederberg, 1940 to 1984.** For many years, scientists believed that antibodies were moulded around germs, like clay pressed around a coin. In 1940, the chemist Linus Pauling gave this idea a clear form. It explained how a body could make a fitting antibody for almost anything. But in 1955, Niels Jerne suggested the opposite. He said the body already holds many kinds of antibodies, and a germ only finds the one that fits. In 1957, Macfarlane Burnet added that each cell makes only one kind and copies itself when chosen. In 1958, Gustav Nossal and Joshua Lederberg tested this. They put single cells from a rat into tiny drops. Each cell made only one kind of antibody. The germ had not shaped it. Burnet shared the 1960 Nobel Prize, and Jerne won the Nobel Prize in 1984.  
*What people believed before:* Antibodies are moulded around each germ, like clay around a coin. Nossal and Lederberg's single-cell test showed that each cell makes only one kind before it meets any germ, so the germ picks, and does not shape.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A vaccine gives you the disease.** (Some worried families, and some rumours; *rejected*)  
  For: Some people do get a sore arm or a mild fever after a vaccine, which can look like a weak form of the illness. A few vaccines use a weakened germ, and the idea sounds as if it carries risk.  
  Against: Vaccines use weakened, killed or partial germs, or instructions for one harmless piece. A sore arm or low fever is the immune system at work. Serious harm is rare, and far rarer than harm from the disease.
- **Catching the illness is always better than a vaccine, because it gives stronger memory.** (Some families and some old sayings; *rejected*)  
  For: For some germs, getting the real illness does give strong memory, and some people stayed well after illness in the past.  
  Against: The real illness can kill or leave harm. Measles can damage the brain. Polio can paralyse. A vaccine gives the memory without that risk, and for many germs the memory lasts well.
- **Many vaccines at once overload a baby's immune system.** (Some worried families; *rejected*)  
  For: It is natural to worry when a small baby gets several injections.  
  Against: A baby's immune system meets thousands of germs every day. Scientists estimated, in a paper in 2002, that a baby could in theory respond to many thousands of vaccines at once. The few vaccines in the schedule use only a tiny part of that ability.
- **You can boost your immune system with the right food, drink or pill.** (Many adverts and some wellness books; *debated*)  
  For: Sleep, a varied diet, exercise and not smoking all help the immune system work well.  
  Against: A stronger immune system is not always better. An overactive one causes allergies and attacks the body. There is no pill that safely makes it stronger. Sleep, food and exercise help it stay in balance.
- **Antibodies are shaped around the germ, like clay pressed around a coin.** (Many scientists in the 1930s and 1940s, including Linus Pauling; *rejected*)  
  For: It explained how one body could make an antibody for almost any germ, even a made-in-a-lab chemical never seen before.  
  Against: Single cells were found to make only one kind of antibody before meeting any germ. The germ selects a ready-made cell. It does not shape the antibody.
- **Too much cleanliness in childhood weakens the immune system.** (Some immunologists, since the 1980s; *debated*)  
  For: Allergies are more common in cleaner places, and children who grow up around animals and farms seem to have fewer. The idea is called the hygiene hypothesis.  
  Against: The evidence is mixed. Washing hands still stops serious illnesses. Scientists think what matters is meeting a wide range of friendly microbes, not skipping hygiene.

## Roots
We have seen how a body can answer a germ it has never met. Now we ask what that tells us about learning, about the self, and about the world we live in.

**Deep truth.** Your body does not learn about a germ by being taught. It makes millions of kinds of defenders before any germ arrives. The germ finds the one that fits, and that one multiplies. So some learning is a choosing from what was already there.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think learning means being shown the answer, from a teacher to a pupil. The immune system learns another way: all the possible answers are made first, and the world picks the one that fits.  
*Evidence:* In the Key Shop test, nobody shaped a key for the germ. The germ simply matched one of many random keys. The more shapes were possible, the more keys we needed. In 1958, single cells were found to make only one kind of antibody each before meeting any germ.

- *who am i:* Your body carries defenders for germs that have not been born yet. You are partly a library you have never read.
- *what is life:* A living being holds many answers in advance and lets the world choose among them. This is how a body learns, and how living things change over many lifetimes.
- *what is this world:* Germs and bodies change each other. When a body gets better at stopping a germ, the germs that slip past spread best. Neither side ever finishes.

Before any germ arrives, your body makes billions of different shapes by shuffling a few hundred pieces. Each B cell holds one shape. When a germ fits one of them, that cell splits into a crowd of identical cells. So one set of genes holds endless possibility, and each meeting with the world brings one real thing out of it. This is the One and the many in a body: one source, many forms, and the world choosing among them.

- **Charles Darwin, On the Origin of Species (1859), chapter 4:** Darwin wrote that nature does not design a living thing. Many different living things are born, and nature keeps the ones that fit their surroundings. (Inside your body, clonal selection does what Darwin described for whole species. Many B cells exist, the germ keeps the one that fits, and that one multiplies.)
- **Avatamsaka Sutra, as told in the Huayan tradition (Indra's net):** Imagine a net with a jewel at every knot. Each jewel shows a reflection of every other jewel. Nothing stands alone. (Your B cells together hold a reflection of every shape a germ could have. The body is a net of mirrors for a world it has not yet met.)
- **Isha Upanishad, opening verse:** That is whole, this is whole. From the whole the whole comes out. Take the whole from the whole, and the whole remains. (A chosen B cell splits into two complete cells, each as whole as the first. After the split, nothing is missing from either.)
- Science (settled): Clonal selection is how adaptive immunity works. Each B cell carries one antibody shape, a matching germ selects it, and the cell multiplies.
- Science (open): How long the memory of each germ lasts, and why some memories last for life, is not yet fully understood.
- Other view, Many immunologists: B cells follow chemistry. A shape fits or it does not fit, and the cell divides when it does. They would say there is no knowing or choosing in it, only molecules, and that calling it learning is only a way of speaking.

If you cannot find it, put your hand there anyway. Close your eyes. Breathe in slowly. Imagine the many small cells resting in your body. Imagine one of them holding a memory of a germ from long ago. Breathe out slowly. Stay still for three breaths.

- Ask: When a B cell splits, are there two cells, or one cell in two places?
- Ask: Is choosing from what was already made a kind of learning, or a different thing?

## Story: Blossom's Blisters
*Real: Sarah Nelmes, the dairymaid; her cow Blossom; Dr Edward Jenner of Berkeley, Gloucestershire; the boy James Phipps, aged eight; the date 14 May 1796; the test with smallpox on 1 July 1796; and the saying among dairymaids that cowpox kept off smallpox. Imagined: Sarah's thoughts, her talk with Jenner, her cows' faces, and the idea about a look-alike picture. Nobody recorded what Sarah said. Jenner did not know why it worked. The reason was found about 150 years later.*

In the spring of 1796, Sarah Nelmes was milking cows at a farm near Berkeley, a village in the west of England. She was a dairymaid. She knew every cow by touch, even in the dark.

Her favourite was a brown cow called Blossom. Blossom had a white star on her forehead and a way of kicking the bucket when the flies bothered her. Sarah loved her.

That month, Blossom came down with a sickness called cowpox. A few sores appeared on her udder. Soon, Sarah's own hand had a few angry blisters on it, where she had touched them. They were sore, and she had a little fever for a day. But then the blisters dried, and she was well again.

“Lucky,” said the older dairymaids. “Now you will never get the smallpox.”

Sarah had heard this all her life. Every dairymaid in the valley said it. Smallpox was the worst sickness in England. It left some people dead and many more with pitted faces, or blind. Sarah's own mother had the marks all over her cheeks. Yet the girls who milked cows seemed to be spared.

One morning, the village doctor, Dr Edward Jenner, came to the farm. He was a quiet man with sharp eyes. He had heard the saying many times too, and for years he had wondered whether it was true.

“May I look at your hand, Sarah?” he asked.

He looked at the blisters for a long time. Then he asked if he could take a little of the clear liquid from them. Sarah said yes. It was the fourteenth of May.

“Why?” she asked, as he carefully touched a small glass to the blister.

“I want to see whether the cowpox really keeps off the smallpox,” said Jenner. “I shall give it to a healthy boy, and later, I shall give him the smallpox itself.”

Sarah stared at him. “On purpose?”

“On purpose,” he said slowly. “And I am not sure I should. But if I am right, it could save many lives.”

The boy was James Phipps, who was eight. He was the son of Jenner's gardener. James rolled up his sleeve and held out his arm. Jenner made two small scratches on it and pressed in the liquid from Sarah's hand. James winced and then grinned.

Sarah went back to her cows. But she could not stop thinking about it.

“Why should a cow's sickness stop a person's sickness?” she asked Blossom as she milked. Blossom flicked her tail. “They are two different sicknesses.”

She turned it over all that week. And then, one evening, she thought of something.

There were two cows at the farm that looked almost alike. Blossom and her sister, Primrose, were both brown, and both had a star on their foreheads. Strangers could not tell them apart. Sarah could, but only by the notch in Primrose's ear. If Sarah had only ever met Primrose, she would still stop Blossom at the gate and say, “Hello, Primrose.” She would be nearly right.

Sarah put down the pail. “That's it,” she whispered. “The body must keep a picture of every sickness it has beaten. Then it knows that sickness next time. And cowpox and smallpox must look nearly alike. So the picture of one nearly fits the other.”

She laughed out loud. Nobody was listening but the cows.

A week went by. James grew a little hot, and his arm was sore. Dr Jenner came every day to look at him. By the ninth day, James was running about again.

Then, on the first of July, Jenner did the thing that Sarah dreaded. He gave James real smallpox, from a person who was ill, to see what would happen.

All the village waited. Sarah milked the cows with her heart in her throat.

A day passed. Then two. Then a week. James stayed perfectly well.

Jenner came to the farm, and he was smiling. “Nothing, Sarah. Not a spot. The cowpox kept him safe.”

“Do you know why?” she asked.

He shook his head. “I do not. Nobody does. Perhaps one day someone will.”

Sarah wanted to tell him about the cows. But she said only, “Perhaps it is a kind of memory.”

Jenner looked at her for a long moment. He did not write it down. But he did write down the rest, in a book that two years later made the doctor famous. In it, he called cowpox by a Latin name that includes the word vacca, which means cow. That is where our word vaccine comes from.

Sarah's name was in the book too. It was on the page about the blisters. It was written there as Sarah Nelmes, a dairymaid. That was all it said.

She never learned what the memory was made of. Neither did Jenner. It took people another one hundred and fifty years to find the cells that keep it. But Sarah had been right about the main thing. The body does keep a picture. And a look-alike can borrow it.

**For the parent:** The body keeps a picture of every sickness it beats. Cowpox looks so much like smallpox that the same picture nearly fits both.

- Ask: Sarah thought a look-alike could borrow a body's memory. How could you test that idea with the Key Shop game?
- Ask: Jenner was not sure he should try the test on James. What would you want to know before you said yes?
- Ask: Sarah's name is in the book, but only as a dairymaid. Whose names do we forget when we tell the story of an invention?

### g1 · Quick or Learning? The defence verdict (20 min)
- **rules:** Quick defence means the body reacts fast, to almost any germ, in the same way. Learning defence means B cells, T cells, antibodies or memory.; One player is the reader. The reader takes the top card and reads the front aloud. The reader does not show the back.; Every other player puts one verdict token face down. Then everyone turns the tokens over together.; The reader turns the card around and reads the back. Every player whose token matches the first words on the back scores one point.; After each card, the reader's job passes to the player on the left. With two players, you swap after every card.; If you play alone, put the cards in a pile, guess, then check the back. Score a point for each right guess.; The game ends when all 24 cards have been read. The player with the most points wins. If there is a tie, the tied players share the win.
- **Printable cards: 24 two-sided cards: something that happens in a body on the front, and the verdict with the reason on the back:** Redness and swelling appear around a scraped knee within an hour. (back: Quick defence. The body reacts within minutes to any cut, in the same way every time.); A child vaccinated at nine months has measles antibodies in her blood at five years old. (back: Learning defence. Memory cells and B cells made after the vaccine keep making antibodies against measles only.); Tears wash dust out of your eye. (back: Quick defence. Tears are always ready, and wash away anything, not one particular germ.); A white blood cell eats a bacterium at a cut within minutes. (back: Quick defence. White blood cells that eat germs are on guard all the time.); A newborn baby has her mother's antibodies in her blood. (back: Learning defence, borrowed. Her mother's learning defence made them, and passed them to her baby.); You had chickenpox once and, when you met the virus again, you stayed well. (back: Learning defence. Memory cells recognised the virus and acted fast, before it could make you ill.); A fever starts a few hours after a germ gets in. (back: Quick defence. The brain raises the body's heat soon after the quick defence sounds the alarm.); After a booster dose, memory B cells wake up and multiply. (back: Learning defence. A booster reminds the memory cells, so they respond faster and in greater numbers.); Sticky mucus in the nose catches dust and germs. (back: Quick defence. Mucus is always there, and catches whatever lands on it.); A killer T cell breaks open a body cell that has a virus hidden inside. (back: Learning defence. Killer T cells are trained to fit one virus shape, and find cells that show it.); The lumps under your jaw swell for a week during a sore throat. (back: Learning defence. B cells and T cells are dividing in the lymph nodes.); Stomach acid kills many germs in your food. (back: Quick defence. The acid works on almost every germ that is swallowed.); A scout cell at a cut carries a piece of germ to a lymph node. (back: Quick defence. It is a scout of the quick defence, but its job is to wake up the learning one.); A vaccine leaves memory cells that last for years. (back: Learning defence. Memory cells are made only by the defence that learns.); Your skin keeps germs out of your body. (back: Quick defence. The skin is a wall that is the same against all germs.); The second time a germ arrives, antibodies appear in three days, not ten. (back: Learning defence. Memory cells make the second response faster and bigger.); Pus forms at a cut that has germs in it. (back: Quick defence. Pus is mostly white blood cells that died while eating germs.); Tiny hairs in your windpipe sweep mucus and dust upwards. (back: Quick defence. They work all the time, and clean away whatever lands there.); A germ finds the one B cell that fits and makes it split into thousands of copies. (back: Learning defence. This choosing of a matching cell is clonal selection.); A helper T cell tells B cells to start making antibodies. (back: Learning defence. Helper T cells are part of the team that learns.); A chemical in your spit breaks open the walls of some bacteria. (back: Quick defence. This chemical, called lysozyme, is in saliva, tears and mucus all the time.); A blood test shows a teenager has antibodies against hepatitis B because of a vaccine. (back: Learning defence. The vaccine showed the body a piece of the virus, and B cells made matching antibodies.); A person had measles as a child and is still protected from it fifty years later. (back: Learning defence. Memory cells can last for decades.); A sneeze blows dust and germs out of your nose. (back: Quick defence. A sneeze is a fast reflex that clears the nose of anything that tickles it.)
- **Printable tokens: Verdict tokens, for four players:** Quick defence; Quick defence; Quick defence; Quick defence; Learning defence; Learning defence; Learning defence; Learning defence
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 24 cards and the 8 tokens. Shuffle the cards and put them in a pile. Give each player one token of each kind.  
  *Parent says:* These cards show things that happen in a body. Each player gets two tokens. One says Quick defence and one says Learning defence. We will guess which defence does each thing.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards and tokens. Shuffle the cards. Take two tokens.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the first eight cards. After each one, read the back aloud. Ask the question below after card four.  
  *Parent says:* The reader reads the front. Everyone else picks a token. Turn the tokens over together. Now the reader reads the back. Who got a point?  
  *Child mode:* The reader reads a card. Pick a token. Turn it over. Check the back.
  - Ask: How can you tell whether a card is about the quick defence or the one that learns? (good: If it is the same for every germ, it is quick.; If it fits one germ or remembers, it is learning.; If it takes days, it is learning.)
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the next twelve cards. Swap the reader's job after every card. Keep a tally of points on paper.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going. Remember. The quick defence is the same for any germ and works in minutes. The learning defence fits one germ, takes days the first time, and remembers.  
  *Child mode:* Play on. The quick defence is fast and the same for every germ. The learning defence fits one germ and remembers.
  - Ask: Which cards fooled you? What made them hard? (good: The scout cell one.; The lymph node one.; Some things use both defences.)
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the last four cards. When all cards are read, add up the points. Ask the closing question.  
  *Parent says:* That was the last card. Add up your points. Here is a question. Which defence would you rather have, if you could have only one? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Count your points. Which defence would you keep if you had only one? Why?
  - Ask: Why does your body need both defences? (good: The quick one holds the line while the slow one gets ready.; The learning one is too slow on its own.; The quick one cannot remember.)
  - Note: A good answer says that the quick defence works at once but cannot remember or fit one germ, and the learning defence is exact but slow the first time. They need each other.
  - Think: Some cards had a bit of both. Which one was that?
### g2 · Shield the Town (20 min)
- **rules:** The town is a board of 36 squares. Each square is a person. The germ will start in one square, chosen by dice.; Players work together. They agree on where to place 10 shields before the germ starts. A shielded square can never get sick.; To start the germ, roll two dice. The first is the row and the second is the column. If that square has a shield, roll again.; The starting square is sick. It rolls one die for each of its four neighbours, above, below, left and right. A 1, 2, 3 or 4 makes the neighbour sick. A 5 or 6 keeps the neighbour well.; Every newly sick square rolls the same way for its own neighbours. A square rolls for each neighbour only once.; When no new square gets sick, count the sick squares. The town wins if 8 or fewer squares are sick.; Play it again with a new start. Players can move up to 3 shields between rounds, but they must say why. After three rounds, add up how many rounds the town won.
- **Printable board: A six by six town board, numbered with a row number 1 to 6 down the side and a column number 1 to 6 along the top:** Draw a grid of 6 columns and 6 rows with squares about 3 centimetres wide.; Write the numbers 1 to 6 along the top and down the side.; Colour a square red when it becomes sick.
- **Printable tokens: 12 shield tokens, small green paper squares:** Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield; Shield
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw the board. Put out the shields. Talk together about where ten shields would block the germ best. Place them.  
  *Parent says:* This is our town. We have ten shields and no idea where the germ will start. Where should we put them? Why there?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the board. Talk about where to put ten shields. Place them.
  - Ask: Why did you choose those squares for the shields? (good: To make a wall.; To protect the middle.; To cut the town into small parts.)
  - Note: Let the children argue. A line of shields breaks the town into parts. A scattered set leaves paths. Do not give the answer. They will see it in the first round.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Roll two dice to find the start. Colour that square red. Roll one die for each neighbour, above, below, left and right. A 1, 2, 3 or 4 makes it sick. Keep going until nothing new gets sick. Count the sick squares.  
  *Parent says:* Roll the two dice for the start. Colour it red. Now roll for each neighbour. One to four, and it is sick. Five or six, and it stays well. Keep going.  
  *Child mode:* Roll two dice for the start. Roll for each neighbour. One to four makes it sick. Colour it red.
  - Check: A square next to a sick square has a shield. Can it get sick? → **No, a shield protects it**
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the sick squares. If 8 or fewer squares are sick, the town wins. Look at the board. Where did the germ stop? Which shields did the most good? Move up to three shields and play again.  
  *Parent says:* Count the red squares. Eight or fewer, and the town wins. Look at where the germ stopped. Which shields helped most? You may move three shields. Where will you put them?  
  *Child mode:* Count the red squares. Eight or fewer is a win. Move up to three shields. Play again.
  - Ask: Which shields did the most good, and which did none? (good: The ones that blocked a path.; The ones near where the germ started.; The ones the germ never reached did nothing.)
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play one more round. Add up how many of the three rounds the town won. Then ask the closing question.  
  *Parent says:* That was the last round. How many did the town win? Here is a question. If you could not choose where the shields go, what would that mean for a real town?  
  *Child mode:* Count your wins. What if you could not choose where the shields go?
  - Ask: In a real town, we cannot choose who is protected so carefully. What does that mean for how many people we need to protect? (good: We need to protect more people.; We need many people to be protected.; It is safer if almost everyone is protected.)
  - Think: In your game, a few well-placed shields did a lot. In real life, who are the people near the edge, who are safe only when others are protected?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Key Shop: how many random cells until one fits? (40 min)
- **question:** If a body makes defender cells with random shapes, how many does it need to make before one of them fits a new germ? Does it need more when there are more possible shapes?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. How many random cells will you need before one fits the germ? Will you need more cells when each cell has more flips in its code? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The germ's code is flipped once for each size of code, and it stays the same through all the rounds of that size. Every defender cell is made the same way, by flipping the same number of times. The only thing that changes between the three parts is the number of flips in the code, which is the number of shapes that are possible. That way, any difference in the number of cells needed comes from the number of shapes.
- **record:** Write the number of cells you needed in each round in the table. At the end, add the numbers in each row and divide by the number of rounds to find the average. Then say what you thought, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Flips in each code | Shapes possible | Cells needed in each round | Average cells needed
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read out the rules of the game. The germ has a code made of coin flips. The body makes random cells by flipping the same number of times. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Imagine a germ with a shape. We show the shape as three coin flips, like heads, tails, heads. Your body makes new defender cells with random shapes. How many random cells do you think it needs before one fits? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* A germ has a shape made of three coin flips. Your body makes random cells. How many will you need before one fits? Why?
  - Ask: What do you think will happen if each code has four flips instead of three? Why? (good: There are more shapes, so it will take more cells.; It is harder to match, so more tries.; More possible shapes, so a longer search.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Write the guesses at the top of the sheet. Most children guess between three and ten.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write all eight possible three-flip codes in a list: HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT. Flip the coin three times for the germ, and write the code on a card. Keep the card face up.  
  *Parent says:* Here are all the shapes that a cell with three flips can have. Count them. How many are there? Now flip three times, to give the germ a shape. Write it here.  
  *Child mode:* Write all eight three-flip codes in a list. Flip three times for the germ. Write its code on a card.
  - Ask: How many different shapes are possible with three flips? (good: Eight.; Eight, because each flip has two choices and there are three flips.)
  - Note: If your child asks how to be sure of eight, list them in order, as heads then tails for each place. There are two choices for the first flip, two for the second and two for the third, so two times two times two is eight.
  - Check: How many different shapes are possible if each cell has two flips? → **Four**
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Make a defender cell. Flip the coin three times and write the code. If it is the same as the germ, write down how many cells you have made so far. If not, make another cell. Do this for six rounds. Each round starts again at cell number one.  
  *Parent says:* Now you are the body. Make one cell at a time with three flips. Does it match the germ? If not, throw it away and make another. Count the cells. Do six rounds.  
  *Child mode:* Flip three times. Does the code match the germ? If not, make another cell. Count how many. Do six rounds.
  - Ask: Did any round take far longer than the others? What do you think happened? (good: I had bad luck.; The matching shape came up late.; It is random, so some rounds take longer.)
  - Note: The average should be near eight, but single rounds can take from one cell to twenty or more. That is normal for chance. If your child is tired, share the flipping, one flips and one writes. Do not stop before six rounds.
  - Check: Why do we flip the coin to make each defender cell, instead of choosing the best one? → **A real body does not know a new germ's shape in advance**
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Flip the coin two times and write the germ's new code. Make cells with two flips each. Count how many it takes to match. Do six rounds, and write each number in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now the shapes are simpler. Each cell has only two flips. There are fewer shapes to choose from. Do six rounds and write the numbers. I wonder if you will need fewer cells.  
  *Child mode:* Now use two flips. Make a new germ code. Do six rounds. Write the numbers.
  - Note: Two flips give four shapes. The average should be near four. Rounds will be quick, about half a minute each.
- **s5 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Flip the coin four times and write the germ's new code. Make cells with four flips each. Count how many it takes to match. Do three rounds. Each round may take a few minutes.  
  *Parent says:* Now every code has four flips. That makes more shapes. Do three rounds. It may take a while. Keep counting.  
  *Child mode:* Now use four flips. Make a new germ code. Do three rounds. Be patient.
  - Ask: While you flip, what do you notice about how long this is taking? (good: It is taking much longer.; The matching shape is harder to hit.; There are more shapes to get through.)
  - Note: Four flips give sixteen shapes. The average is near sixteen, but with three rounds, yours may be far from that. Rounds may take thirty or more cells. If time runs short, do two rounds and ask a sibling to share their numbers.
- **s6 (9 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add the numbers in each row and divide by the number of rounds, to find the average. Look at the three averages. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then ask the two questions below.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see in the numbers? Why do you think that happened? Then look at the three averages. What do you notice?  
  *Child mode:* Find the average for each row. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What happened to the average each time we added one more flip to the code? (good: It about doubled.; It went up a lot.; More shapes meant more cells.)
  - Ask: A real body has billions of possible shapes. How could it ever find a match in time? (good: It makes lots and lots of cells.; It makes many at the same time.; It starts with many already made.)
  - Note: A strong answer says that each extra flip doubled the shapes and about doubled the cells needed. The averages should come near four, eight and sixteen. After they have said what they saw, explain: a body does not shape a cell for a germ. It makes huge numbers of random cells, many at once, so that some fit almost any germ. When one fits, it multiplies. If your numbers did not follow the pattern, say so. Chance can hide a pattern, and more rounds would show it.
  - Check: In your results, the average number of cells rose when the code had more flips. What does this show? → **More possible shapes means more cells are needed to find a match**
  - Think: Nobody told the germ which cell to meet. Did the body learn the germ's shape, or did it find a cell that already fitted?
### e2 · Spread and Shield: does protecting some squares protect all? (37 min)
- **question:** In a small town, how many people get sick when a germ starts with one person? Does shielding some people protect the rest?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which town will have the fewest sick squares: no shields, 8 shields or 16 shields? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every town is a grid of 25 squares, and the germ always starts in the middle square. Every sick square rolls the same die in the same way for each of its neighbours. The only thing that changes is how many squares have a shield, and the shields are placed by drawing numbered slips, so nobody can pick the best places. Any difference in the number of sick squares comes from the number of shields.
- **record:** Write the number of shields, the number of sick squares and the number of well but unshielded squares for each town. Then draw a bar for each town to show the sick squares.
- **results table:** Town | Squares with a shield | Squares that got sick | Squares not shielded and still well
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three towns you will test. Town A has no shields, town B has 8, and town C has 16. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We will test three towns. In town A, nobody has a shield. In town B, eight squares have shields. In town C, sixteen have shields. Which town will have the fewest sick squares? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three towns: no shields, eight shields, sixteen shields. Which gets the fewest sick squares? Why?
  - Ask: If the germ cannot pass through a shield, what will it do in town C? (good: It will get stuck.; It will have to go around.; It will stop quickly.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children guess that more shields means fewer sick squares. A good question is whether the unshielded squares are safe too.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a grid of five squares by five squares, each about 3 centimetres wide. Number the squares 1 to 25, row by row. Write the numbers 1 to 25 on slips of paper, but leave out 13. Put the 24 slips in the bowl. Draw the grid three times, for towns A, B and C.  
  *Parent says:* This is your town. Each square is a person. The middle square, number 13, is the first person who is sick. Colour it red. We draw the grid three times, one for each town.  
  *Child mode:* Draw a grid of five by five squares. Number them 1 to 25. Colour square 13 red. Draw it three times.
  - Note: A ruler makes this go faster. Let your child draw one grid and trace the other two, or photocopy a grid.
  - Check: Why do we draw the shields from a bowl, and not choose the squares? → **So nobody can pick the best places to block the germ**
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play town A, which has no shields. Square 13 is sick. For each of its four neighbours, above, below, left and right, roll the die. A 1, 2, 3 or 4 makes that neighbour sick. Colour it red. Then each new red square rolls for its own neighbours, once per neighbour. Skip a neighbour that is already red. Stop when no new square gets sick. Count the red squares.  
  *Parent says:* Square 13 is sick. It rolls the die once for each neighbour. A one, two, three or four makes that neighbour sick. Colour it red. Then the new red squares roll for their neighbours too. Keep going until nothing new gets sick.  
  *Child mode:* Square 13 rolls for each neighbour. A 1, 2, 3 or 4 makes it sick. Colour it red. Keep going.
  - Ask: What path did the germ take through your town? (good: It spread outward from the middle.; It went in all directions.; It followed the squares next to each other.)
  - Note: Each sick square rolls only once for each neighbour, the moment it becomes sick. Keep a list of squares waiting to roll so none is forgotten. With no shields the germ usually reaches most of the town, often fifteen to twenty-five squares. Sometimes it dies early, by chance.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play town B. Draw 8 slips from the bowl. Mark those squares with a green shield. Put the slips back. Square 13 is sick. Roll for the neighbours as before, but shielded squares never get sick. Count the red squares.  
  *Parent says:* Now eight squares get a shield. Draw eight slips and mark them green. A shielded square cannot get sick, so the germ cannot pass through it. Roll as before. How many squares get sick this time?  
  *Child mode:* Draw eight slips. Mark those squares green. Shielded squares never get sick. Roll for the germ. Count.
  - Note: Put every slip back after drawing, so the bowl is full again for town C. Town B usually has about 3 to 14 sick squares. Chance matters a lot with so few squares. If a shield lands right next to square 13, that may block much of the spread.
- **s5 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play town C. Draw 16 slips from the bowl. Mark those squares with a green shield. Square 13 is sick. Roll for the neighbours as before. Count the red squares. Then count the squares that are not shielded and still well.  
  *Parent says:* Now sixteen squares get a shield. Draw sixteen slips and mark them green. Roll as before. Count the sick squares. Then count the squares that have no shield and are still well.  
  *Child mode:* Draw sixteen slips. Mark them green. Roll for the germ. Count the sick squares.
  - Ask: Some squares have no shield and are still well. Why did the germ not reach them? (good: The shields around them blocked the germ.; The germ could not get to them.; They were behind the shielded squares.)
  - Note: Town C usually has only 1 to 5 sick squares. Many unshielded squares stay well. That is the idea of herd protection: the shields protect the squares around them too.
- **s6 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the results in the table. Draw a bar for each town to show the sick squares. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. Then ask about the unshielded squares that stayed well.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see in the three towns? Why do you think that happened? Look at the unshielded squares that stayed well. What protected them?  
  *Child mode:* Write the numbers in the table. Draw a bar for each town. Say what you thought, saw and why.
  - Note: A strong answer says that more shields meant fewer sick squares, and that shields protected some unshielded squares too. Dice are random, so one run can surprise you. If your results do not follow the pattern, run the towns again and add the results together. After they have said what they saw, explain: real germs differ in how easily they spread. Measles spreads very easily, so almost everyone must be protected to stop it. A germ that spreads less easily needs fewer shields.
  - Check: In town C, several unshielded squares stayed well. What protected them? → **The shielded squares around them blocked the germ's paths**
  - Think: In a real town, some people cannot be vaccinated. Who protects them?

## Worksheets
### Two Defences (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which defence reacts within minutes, in much the same way to almost every germ?  
   Options: The quick defence / The defence that learns / Memory cells  
   **Answer:** The quick defence. *Why:* The quick defence is the one you are born with. It includes skin, tears and white blood cells that eat germs. It is fast but does not tell germs apart well. The defence that learns takes days the first time.
2. (truefalse) The quick defence remembers a germ so it can act faster next time.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The quick defence reacts the same way every time. Only the defence that learns, with its memory cells, remembers a germ.
3. (fill) A tiny Y-shaped protein that fits one germ shape is called an ____.  
   **Answer:** ['antibody']. *Why:* An antibody is a Y-shaped protein. Its tips fit one antigen, like a key in a lock. Each kind of antibody fits only one shape.
4. (match) Match each part of the defence that learns to its job.  
   **Answer:** B cell → makes antibodies; Killer T cell → destroys body cells with a virus hidden inside; Lymph node → a small knot where immune cells meet and multiply; Memory cell → stays after a fight and acts fast next time. *Why:* B cells make antibodies. Killer T cells find cells with a virus inside. Lymph nodes are meeting places. Memory cells stay for years and respond fast.
5. (sort) Sort each one by which defence does it.  
   **Answer:** tears washing out dust → Quick defence; antibodies made after a vaccine → Defence that learns; sticky mucus in the nose → Quick defence; memory cells for measles → Defence that learns; redness around a scrape → Quick defence; a killer T cell finding a virus → Defence that learns. *Why:* The quick defence is always ready and the same for every germ. The defence that learns uses antibodies, T cells and memory.
6. (mcq) Why does the defence that learns take about a week the first time a germ arrives?  
   Options: It has to find and multiply the one cell that fits that germ / It waits for the germ to die first / It has to be taught by a doctor  
   **Answer:** It has to find and multiply the one cell that fits that germ. *Why:* The body already holds many cells, but only a few fit a new germ. Those few must divide until there are enough to fight. That takes days. Memory cells make it faster next time.
7. (truefalse) An antibody against measles will also stick to a flu virus.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. An antibody fits one shape. A measles antibody does not fit a flu virus, so a different antibody is needed.
8. (write) A friend asks why most people only get chickenpox once. Explain it in two or three sentences.  
   **Answer:** The first time, the body finds the cells that fit the virus and some stay behind as memory cells. If the virus returns, the memory cells act in a few days, before it can make you ill.. *Why:* A strong answer says that memory cells stay after the first illness and make the second response fast. It may add that the first response is slow.
9. (order) Put these in order, for what happens when a germ meets a body for the first time.  
   **Answer:** ['A germ arrives', 'The germ meets the one B cell whose antibody fits', 'That B cell splits into many copies', 'The copies make lots of antibodies', 'A few cells stay behind as memory cells']. *Why:* The germ comes first. It picks a matching cell. That cell copies itself, the copies fight, and a few remain as memory.
10. (mcq) What does a vaccine do?  
   Options: It shows the body a safe version of a germ, so memory cells are made before the real germ arrives / It kills every germ in the body for ever / It gives the body the antibodies ready-made, to use up  
   **Answer:** It shows the body a safe version of a germ, so memory cells are made before the real germ arrives. *Why:* A vaccine does the slow first lesson in a safe way. B cells and T cells find the safe version, multiply, and leave memory cells behind.
11. (count) Kavya's card shows polio drops 3 times, measles and rubella 2 times and one booster. How many doses are there in all?  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* 3 plus 2 plus 1 is 6. Some vaccines are given in more than one dose so the memory is stronger.
12. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Skin, tears, mucus, memory cell.  
   **Answer:** Memory cell, because it belongs to the defence that learns. The other three are walls that are always there, in the quick defence.. *Why:* Skin, tears and mucus work the same against all germs. A memory cell remembers one germ.
13. (draw) Draw an antibody holding on to the spike of a germ. Show how the shapes fit.  
   **Answer:** A Y-shaped antibody with one tip fitting into a notch or a spike on a round germ.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the tip of the Y fitting the shape on the germ exactly, and not just touching.
14. (perspective) A family is not sure about taking a child for a vaccine.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason is fine. A strong answer asks real questions, such as what is in the vaccine, how safe it is, and what the illness can do.. *Why:* Vaccines are one place where evidence and fear meet. A good decision uses the evidence and also listens to the worry.
15. (mcq) A child has a sore arm and a mild fever the day after a vaccine. What is the best explanation?  
   Options: The immune system is responding, which is expected / The vaccine has given the child the illness / The vaccine has failed  
   **Answer:** The immune system is responding, which is expected. *Why:* A sore arm or a low fever shows the immune system at work. It is usually mild and short. A high fever, trouble breathing or a rash needs a doctor.
16. (truefalse) A fever after a vaccine always means something has gone wrong.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A mild fever can be the immune system at work. A fever that is high or that lasts needs a doctor.
### How the Defence Learns (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) How many kinds of antibody does one B cell make?  
   Options: One kind / As many as there are germs / Three kinds  
   **Answer:** One kind. *Why:* Each B cell makes only one kind of antibody. That is why the body needs millions of different B cells, each ready for a different shape.
2. (mcq) Why does the body need killer T cells as well as antibodies?  
   Options: Viruses hide inside body cells, where antibodies cannot reach / Antibodies are too slow / Killer T cells make antibodies  
   **Answer:** Viruses hide inside body cells, where antibodies cannot reach. *Why:* Antibodies work on germs floating outside cells. A virus inside a cell is out of their reach. Killer T cells find and destroy the cell with the virus hidden in it.
3. (fill) The fluid that drains from your tissues, flows through the nodes and returns to your blood is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['lymph']. *Why:* Lymph carries bits of whatever is in the tissues, including germ pieces, through lymph nodes. In the nodes, immune cells can meet them.
4. (match) Match each kind of vaccine to what it shows the body.  
   **Answer:** Weakened germ vaccine → a germ made too weak to make you ill; Killed germ vaccine → a germ that cannot grow; Piece-of-germ vaccine → a single spike or part; Toxoid vaccine → a harmless form of a germ's poison. *Why:* All four show the immune system an antigen without the real illness. B cells and T cells then make memory cells.
5. (order) Put these in order, for how a vaccine protects someone.  
   **Answer:** ['A vaccine shows the body a safe antigen', 'Scout cells carry a piece to a lymph node', 'B cells and T cells that fit multiply', 'Memory cells stay behind', 'The real germ arrives and is stopped fast']. *Why:* The vaccine comes first, and the immune cells do their work. Memory cells wait for the real germ, and then act quickly.
6. (count) A chosen B cell splits in two every 8 hours. Starting with 1 cell, how many cells are there after 24 hours?  
   **Answer:** 8. *Why:* In 24 hours there are three splits of 8 hours. 1 becomes 2, then 4, then 8.
7. (truefalse) Every B cell is made after a germ arrives.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The body makes B cells with many different shapes before any germ arrives. The germ only picks one that fits.
8. (write) One scientist says a germ teaches the body how to make an antibody. Another says the germ only picks one that is already there. Use the Key Shop test to say which is better supported.  
   **Answer:** The germ picks one that is already there. In the Key Shop test, nobody shaped a key for the germ. We made random keys, and one matched by chance. The more shapes were possible, the more keys we needed.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the test as evidence. It says the cells were made without knowing the germ, so the germ chose, and did not teach.
9. (perspective) A school has some children who cannot be vaccinated because of a health condition.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason is fine. A strong answer weighs the child who cannot be vaccinated against the worry of the unsure parent, and asks for facts.. *Why:* Herd protection means one family's choice affects others. A good answer takes both the evidence and the worry seriously.
10. (mcq) Why is a tiny baby safer when the people around her are all protected?  
   Options: The germ finds fewer people to spread through, so it rarely reaches her / The baby gets their antibodies through the air / Protected people cannot carry any germs at all  
   **Answer:** The germ finds fewer people to spread through, so it rarely reaches her. *Why:* A germ needs a path from person to person. When most people are protected, the germ stops before it gets to the baby. That is herd protection.
11. (odd) Which one is the odd one out, and why? Chickenpox, measles, a cold, mumps.  
   **Answer:** A cold, because hundreds of different viruses cause colds, so memory for one does not protect against the next. The other three are each caused by one virus.. *Why:* Memory helps most when the germ stays the same. Colds change because many viruses cause them.
12. (draw) Draw a graph with two lines. One shows antibodies during a first infection. The other shows antibodies during a second infection with the same germ.  
   **Answer:** The first line rises slowly over about a week or more to a low peak. The second line rises in two to four days to a much higher peak.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the second line rising earlier and higher than the first, because memory cells act at once.
13. (mcq) Why must the flu vaccine be updated each year?  
   Options: Flu viruses slowly change their outer shapes, so last year's memory fits less well / The vaccine wears out in the bottle / Antibodies disappear after a month  
   **Answer:** Flu viruses slowly change their outer shapes, so last year's memory fits less well. *Why:* Flu viruses change the shapes on their surface. An old antibody fits a changed virus less well. A new vaccine shows the body the new shapes.
14. (truefalse) A stronger immune system is always better.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. An overactive immune system causes allergies, and in some people attacks the body's own cells. A healthy immune system is balanced.
15. (fill) A body builds billions of different antibody shapes by shuffling and joining a few hundred gene ____.  
   **Answer:** ['pieces', 'parts', 'segments']. *Why:* A B cell picks one piece from each of three lists and joins them, and adds a few random letters at the joins. A small set of pieces makes billions of shapes.
16. (write) A vaccine protects a child who was vaccinated. Explain how it can also protect a child who was never vaccinated.  
   **Answer:** If many people are protected, the germ cannot find enough people to spread through. A child who was not vaccinated is less likely to meet the germ, because it stops at the protected people around her.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the idea of paths. Protected people block paths, and so the germ rarely reaches unprotected people.
### Test the Idea (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A scientist finds that a single cell makes only one kind of antibody, before meeting any germ. Which idea does this support?  
   Options: The germ picks a ready-made cell and does not shape the antibody / The germ shapes the antibody like clay around a coin / Antibodies are not made by cells  
   **Answer:** The germ picks a ready-made cell and does not shape the antibody. *Why:* If a cell already makes one fixed kind before any germ arrives, the germ cannot have shaped it. The germ can only find the cell that fits.
2. (mcq) A child tests shields in a grid town but chooses the places herself. What is the problem with her test?  
   Options: She might choose places that block the germ well, so the number of shields is not the only change / Choosing is always more fair than drawing / Shields do not matter in a grid  
   **Answer:** She might choose places that block the germ well, so the number of shields is not the only change. *Why:* A fair test changes only one thing. If she picks smart places, then the places and the number both change. Drawing from a bowl keeps the places random.
3. (write) The first vaccine test was done on a healthy eight-year-old boy, and then he was exposed to smallpox. Why would this not be allowed today? Describe how a careful test would be done instead.  
   **Answer:** Today we do not deliberately expose healthy children to a deadly illness. A careful test would first be done in a lab and then on small groups of adults who agree, with doctors watching and the right to leave. Then it would be tested on larger groups, always with checks and agreement.. *Why:* A strong answer names consent and safety, and describes testing in steps from small to large. Jenner did not know how to do this, and the world has learned since.
4. (fill) A germ whose outer shape changes can slip past old ____.  
   **Answer:** ['memory', 'antibodies', 'immunity']. *Why:* Memory cells and antibodies fit a germ's old shape. If the shape changes, the fit is poor, and the germ gets past.
5. (match) Match each problem to what is happening in the body.  
   **Answer:** An allergy → the defence reacts strongly to something harmless; An autoimmune illness → the defence attacks the body's own cells; A weak immune system → some parts of the defence are missing or not working; A new flu each year → the germ's shape changes, so old memory fits less well. *Why:* Each is a different way the defence can fall short. Three come from the body, and one comes from the germ.
6. (sort) Sort each one by whether memory helps.  
   **Answer:** a second meeting with chickenpox → Memory helps; a brand-new type of flu virus → Memory helps little; measles after a measles vaccine → Memory helps; a cold from a different cold virus → Memory helps little; a tetanus booster ten years later → Memory helps; a germ whose shape changes each month → Memory helps little. *Why:* Memory works when the germ stays the same, or when a booster reminds the memory cells. It works less when the germ changes or is a different germ.
7. (count) In an unprotected town, a sick person would pass a germ to 4 other people. Out of every 4 people, how many must be protected so that a sick person passes it on to only 1 person?  
   **Answer:** 3 out of every 4. *Why:* If 3 of the 4 people are protected, only 1 can catch it. So a sick person passes it on to 1 person, and the germ no longer grows. That is 3 out of 4, or 75 out of 100.
8. (truefalse) An unprotected person is safer in a town where most people are protected than in a town where few are.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. In a town with many protected people, a germ finds few paths and rarely reaches the unprotected person.
9. (perspective) A vaccine has a small risk for each person, and protects others in the town.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason is fine. A strong answer weighs the small risk against the larger benefit and says how trust could be built.. *Why:* Herd protection joins one person's choice to everyone's safety. Good decisions explain the evidence and listen.
10. (write) Compare the Key Shop test with natural selection among animals in a forest. What is the same, and what is different?  
   **Answer:** Both begin with many different forms made without knowing what will be needed. In the forest, the surroundings keep the animals that fit. In the body, the germ keeps the cell that fits. The difference is speed and place: in the body it takes days and happens inside one body, and in a forest it takes many lifetimes.. *Why:* A strong answer says that in both cases the variety exists first and something in the surroundings picks. It notes that the body does it in days, not generations.
11. (odd) Which is not a way to make a vaccine? A weakened germ, a killed germ, a harmless form of a poison, a germ made stronger.  
   **Answer:** A germ made stronger, because vaccines use safer versions of a germ, not stronger ones.. *Why:* A vaccine must teach the immune system without causing the illness. It uses weakened or killed germs, a piece of a germ, or a harmless form of a poison.
12. (draw) Draw a town where most houses have a shield and a few do not. Show the germ's path stopping at the shields.  
   **Answer:** A grid of houses, most with shield marks, a few without, and arrows showing the germ spreading from one sick house and stopping at the shields.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows unshielded houses surrounded by shields, with no arrow reaching them.
13. (mcq) In the Spread and Shield test, one group had 16 shields but 9 sick squares. Which is the best next step?  
   Options: Run the town again several times, since chance can change one result / Decide that shields do not work / Choose the shield squares by hand next time  
   **Answer:** Run the town again several times, since chance can change one result. *Why:* Dice are random, so one run can be unusual. Repeating the town and adding up the results shows the pattern. Choosing squares by hand would stop the test being fair.
14. (order) Put these in order, for what happens the second time a known germ arrives.  
   **Answer:** ['The germ arrives', 'Memory cells meet the germ', 'Memory cells multiply very fast', 'Antibodies appear within two to four days', 'The germ is stopped before illness']. *Why:* Memory cells are already waiting, so they act at once. This is much faster than the first meeting, when the matching cell had to be found.
15. (truefalse) A vaccine made and tested in one country works in exactly the same way in every country.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Differences such as nutrition, gut infections and other germs can change how well some vaccines work. This is why scientists, such as Gagandeep Kang, test vaccines in the places where they will be used.
16. (write) There is a vaccine for measles, but not one for the common cold. Give two reasons why a cold vaccine is much harder to make.  
   **Answer:** More than 200 different viruses cause colds, so one vaccine would have to fit many shapes. Cold viruses also change over time, so the memory would fit less well.. *Why:* A strong answer names the number of different viruses and their changing shapes. Both make a single memory hard to use.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which is true of the quick defence?  
   Options: It acts within minutes or hours, and in much the same way against most germs / It makes a different antibody for each germ / It remembers a germ for years  
   **Answer:** It acts within minutes or hours, and in much the same way against most germs. *Why:* The quick defence is always ready and does not tell germs apart well. Antibodies and memory belong to the defence that learns.
2. (mcq) Why does an antibody for one germ not work against a different germ?  
   Options: Its shape fits one antigen only, like a key in one lock / Antibodies are too slow / Different germs do not have shapes  
   **Answer:** Its shape fits one antigen only, like a key in one lock. *Why:* An antibody's tip fits one shape. A different germ has a different shape, so a different antibody is needed.
3. (mcq) In the Key Shop test, the average number of cells rose when each code had more flips. What does this show about a body's defender cells?  
   Options: The more shapes are possible, the more random cells are needed to find a match / More flips make the germ weaker / A matching cell is always found on the first try  
   **Answer:** The more shapes are possible, the more random cells are needed to find a match. *Why:* More flips mean more possible shapes, and the matching one is rarer. A real body deals with this by making huge numbers of cells with many different shapes.
4. (mcq) A child meets measles for the second time. Why is she far less likely to get ill than the first time?  
   Options: Memory cells recognise the germ and act within days / The germ gets weaker each time / Her skin has become thicker  
   **Answer:** Memory cells recognise the germ and act within days. *Why:* After the first meeting, memory cells stay. When the germ returns they multiply and make antibodies very fast, before the germ can make her ill.
5. (mcq) What does a vaccine do for a child who has never met the real germ?  
   Options: It starts the slow first lesson safely, so memory cells are ready / It gives the child the full illness in a mild form / It makes the skin harder for germs to pass  
   **Answer:** It starts the slow first lesson safely, so memory cells are ready. *Why:* A vaccine shows the immune system a safe version of the germ. The matching cells multiply, and memory cells stay for later.
6. (mcq) Why might a child need a booster dose of a vaccine?  
   Options: The memory can fade over time, and an extra dose strengthens it / The first dose was a mistake / A booster makes the vaccine cheaper  
   **Answer:** The memory can fade over time, and an extra dose strengthens it. *Why:* For some germs, the antibodies slowly fall over the years. A booster reminds the memory cells and brings the protection back up.
7. (truefalse) When most people in a town are protected, even a few people who are not protected are safer.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The germ has fewer paths to reach them. This is herd protection.
8. (mcq) What is an allergy?  
   Options: The immune system reacts strongly to something harmless, such as pollen / A germ that lives in the nose / A kind of vaccine  
   **Answer:** The immune system reacts strongly to something harmless, such as pollen. *Why:* In an allergy, the defence treats a harmless thing as if it were a germ. That causes sneezing, itching or a rash.
9. (mcq) Why can a person catch a cold many times in a life, though memory cells exist?  
   Options: Many different viruses cause colds, so memory for one does not fit the next / Memory cells only last a week / Colds are not caused by germs  
   **Answer:** Many different viruses cause colds, so memory for one does not fit the next. *Why:* More than 200 different viruses cause colds. Memory for one does not fit another. Chickenpox is one virus, so memory helps for life.
10. (mcq) How does a body get an antibody for a germ it has never met before?  
   Options: It already holds many B cells with different shapes, and one of them fits / It copies the shape of the germ at once / A doctor adds the antibody to the blood  
   **Answer:** It already holds many B cells with different shapes, and one of them fits. *Why:* B cells with many different shapes are made before any germ arrives. The germ finds the one that fits, and that one multiplies. This is clonal selection.

## Applications
- Vaccines for babies and children, such as polio drops, measles and rubella, protect them before they meet the real germs.
- Booster doses, such as the extra tetanus dose for older children, bring memory back up.
- Each year, scientists update the flu vaccine to match the viruses that are going around.
- Doctors give people with a deep cut an extra tetanus dose, because the bacteria that cause tetanus can live in soil and rust.
- Blood tests that look for antibodies show whether someone has been protected by a vaccine or has had an illness.
- Cold chains of fridges, ice boxes and vaccine carriers keep vaccines cool all the way to remote villages.
- Community health workers, called ASHA workers in India, visit homes to remind families about vaccine days.
- Doctors treat allergies by finding what the body reacts to, and sometimes by giving tiny, careful doses to teach the body to tolerate it.
- Scientists make antibodies in the lab, using the shapes that fit a germ, to treat some illnesses.
- Rotavac, a vaccine against a cause of severe diarrhoea in children, was developed in India and tested here, so that it works for children here.

## Open questions
- **Why do some memories last for life while others fade after a few years?** Scientists see that memory for measles lasts for decades and memory for some other germs fades. They do not fully know why.
- **Why does the same germ make one person very ill and another person hardly ill at all?** Age, genes, earlier illnesses, nutrition and luck all play a part, but nobody can yet predict exactly how one person's body will react.
- **Why does the defence sometimes attack the body's own cells?** In many autoimmune illnesses, scientists know which cells are attacked, but not always what started the mistake.
- **Could we make one vaccine that protects against all flu viruses, or all cold viruses?** Researchers are trying to find shapes that stay the same across many strains. They have not yet succeeded.
- **How do the immune system and the brain talk to each other?** Signals pass between them, and sleep and stress change how the defence works, but the details are still being found.
- **Are there more ways to remember than memory cells?** Some scientists have found that even the quick defence can change after a first meeting with a germ. They call this trained immunity, and are still working out how much it matters.

## Connected maths
- In the Key Shop test, one family needed these numbers of cells in six rounds with three flips: 6, 12, 3, 9, 15 and 5. They also did four more rounds: 4, 10, 7 and 9. What was the average number of cells over all ten rounds? **8 cells.**. Add all ten numbers: 6 plus 12 is 18, plus 3 is 21, plus 9 is 30, plus 15 is 45, plus 5 is 50, plus 4 is 54, plus 10 is 64, plus 7 is 71, plus 9 is 80. Divide 80 by 10 to get 8. Three flips give 8 shapes, so about 8 cells are needed on average. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L4`)
- A code of 2 coin flips gives 4 shapes. A code of 3 flips gives 8 shapes. A code of 4 flips gives 16 shapes. How many shapes would a code of 8 flips give? Explain how you know. **256 shapes.**. Each extra flip doubles the number of shapes. 16 doubled is 32 for 5 flips, then 64, then 128, then 256 for 8 flips. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)
- A code of 10 flips gives 1,024 shapes. A code of 20 flips gives about 1,000,000 shapes. About how many shapes would a code of 30 flips give? **About 1,000,000,000, which is a billion.**. Ten more flips multiplies the shapes by about 1,000, because 10 flips make about 1,000 shapes. So 20 flips is about a million, and 30 flips is about a thousand million, which is a billion. A real body makes billions of antibody shapes. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L4`)
- A germ doubles in number every 4 hours. A chosen B cell doubles every 8 hours. Both start with 1. After 48 hours, how many germs and how many B cells are there? Which is bigger? Why does the body need help from memory cells? **4,096 germs and 64 B cells. The germs are far more. Memory cells start with many cells already made.**. In 48 hours, the germ doubles 12 times, because 48 divided by 4 is 12. Twelve doublings from 1 make 4,096. The B cell doubles 6 times, because 48 divided by 8 is 6. Six doublings make 64. The germs win at first. That is why you can be ill the first time. With memory, the body starts with many more cells, so it can win. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L4`)
- In the Spread and Shield test, a town has 25 squares. In town C, 16 squares have a shield and 3 got sick. What fraction of the town is shielded? What percent is that? **16 out of 25, which is 64 out of 100, or 64 percent.**. 16 out of 25 is the same as 64 out of 100, because multiplying both numbers by 4 gives 64 and 100. So 64 percent of the town was shielded. The 3 sick squares were a small share of the 9 that had no shield. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In the word puzzle, Meenakshi took 150 seconds the first time and 60 seconds the second time. How many seconds faster was the second time? How many times as fast was it? **90 seconds faster. 2 and a half times as fast.**. 150 take away 60 is 90 seconds. To compare, divide 150 by 60, which is 2 and a half. The second time was 2 and a half times as fast, like memory cells acting faster on a second meeting. (queues `math.time.duration.L4`)
