# Reflexes, instincts and imprinting: what animals can do without lessons
`sci.animal-behaviour.born-knowing.L4`

**Summary.** A newly hatched duckling follows the first moving thing it sees, with no lessons. How can an animal know things it was never taught? In this lesson you will find three reflexes in yourself, test what cold water on the face does to your heartbeat, and judge which drawn face is cuter.

**Intro.** *A goose is rolling a stray egg back to her nest with her beak. A scientist takes the egg away halfway. The goose goes on with the rolling movement, all the way to the nest, with no egg under her beak. Why did she not stop?* The goose was born able to make this rolling movement. A round thing lying near her nest sets the movement off. Once the movement starts, it runs to the end. Your body has patterns like this too. A blink, a swallow and a yawn each wait for a trigger. In this lesson you find the triggers for three of your own reflexes. You test whether cold water on your face changes your heartbeat. You also meet the ducklings who follow the first moving thing they see.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a reflex is, and name the stimulus and the response of three reflexes.
1. I can explain what a fixed action pattern is, and give two examples.
2. I can explain what an instinct is, and how it differs from something learned.
3. I can explain what a sign stimulus is, and why a dummy can set off a behaviour.
4. I can test fairly what sets off the diving reflex, and say what my results show.
5. I can explain imprinting and the sensitive period, and tell how Konrad Lorenz tested them.
6. I can explain why a made-up trigger can work better than the real one.
7. I can give reasons why inborn behaviour and learning work together.

## Terms
- **Ethology**: The science of how animals behave, studied by watching them and testing them. *e.g.* Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen were ethologists. They watched geese, gulls and fish for years.
- **Innate**: Present from birth or hatching, without being taught. Another word for it is inborn. *e.g.* A duckling's urge to follow is innate. Nobody teaches it.
- **Stimulus**: The thing that sets off a response. In this lesson we also call it the trigger. *e.g.* A drop of lemon juice on your tongue is a stimulus. It makes your mouth water.
- **Response**: What the body or the animal does after the stimulus. *e.g.* When something flies at your eye, the blink is the response.
- **Reflex**: A fast, automatic response of the body to one particular stimulus. Nobody has to learn it. *e.g.* Your hand pulls away from a hot tumbler before you feel the pain.
- **Diving reflex**: The slowing of the heart when the face is in cold water and the breath is held. *e.g.* A seal's heart slows as soon as she dives. Yours slows a little too.
- **Fixed action pattern**: A set of movements that an animal is born able to do. The movements come in the same order each time and usually run to the end. *e.g.* A goose rolls an egg back to her nest in the same way every time.
- **Instinct**: A whole behaviour that an animal can carry out without being taught. *e.g.* A cuckoo chick pushes the other eggs out of the nest a day or so after hatching.
- **Sign stimulus**: The one feature of a thing that sets off an inborn behaviour. *e.g.* The red spot on a gull's beak is a sign stimulus. The chick pecks at the spot.
- **Dummy**: A simple model that a scientist shows to an animal, to find out which feature the animal responds to. *e.g.* A cardboard gull head with a red spot is a dummy.
- **Supernormal stimulus**: A made-up trigger that works better than the real one, because the key feature is bigger or stronger. *e.g.* An oystercatcher chooses a giant plaster egg over her own egg.
- **Imprinting**: Fast learning that happens in a short time early in life and is very hard to undo. A young bird learns whom to follow. *e.g.* Goslings who first saw Konrad Lorenz followed him everywhere.
- **Sensitive period**: The short time when a particular kind of learning, such as imprinting, can happen. *e.g.* For a mallard duckling, the sensitive period for imprinting is about the first day after hatching.

## Explanations
### A reflex is a fast response to one trigger · *defined*
**Definition:** A reflex is a fast, automatic response of the body to one particular stimulus. Nobody has to learn it.

Something rushes at your eye, and your eyelid shuts. You did not plan to shut it. The thing that sets a reflex off is called the stimulus, or the trigger. What the body then does is called the response. Each reflex has a trigger of its own. A drop of lemon juice makes your mouth water, but a loud clap does not. A reflex works in four steps. Sensors in the skin, the eye or the tongue pick up the trigger. A message runs along a nerve to the spinal cord or to the lowest part of the brain. A second message runs straight back to a muscle. The muscle pulls. The message does not wait for the parts of the brain that plan. That is why a reflex is so fast. When you touch a hot tumbler, your hand is already moving away before you feel the pain. After a tap below the kneecap, the thigh muscle starts to pull in less than a twentieth of a second. A nerve message is a tiny wave of electricity. It is made by charged particles moving in and out of the nerve cell. The fastest nerves carry a message about 100 metres in one second.
- e.g. The blink. When something rushes at the eye, the eyelid shuts.
- e.g. Mouth-watering. When sour juice touches the tongue, saliva flows.
- e.g. Pulling away. When skin touches something hot, the arm muscles pull the hand back.
- e.g. The knee kick. After a tap below the kneecap, the lower leg swings forward.
### The diving reflex: a reflex you share with seals · *defined*
**Definition:** In the diving reflex, the heart slows when the face is in cold water and the breath is held.

Cold water on the skin around your eyes and nose is picked up by a nerve of the face. If you are also holding your breath, a message goes to your heart, and your heart beats more slowly. Blood vessels in your arms and legs become narrower as well. Less blood goes to the limbs, and more is kept for the brain and the heart. In this way the oxygen from one breath lasts longer. In humans the heart usually slows by a tenth to a quarter. In a seal the change is far bigger. A seal's heart can drop from about 100 beats a minute to about 10 during a dive. Ducks, dolphins, otters and whales have the same reflex. So do newborn human babies. Scientists think the reflex is very old, and that all these animals inherited it from shared ancestors. The women divers of Jeju island in Korea, called haenyeo, depend on it every working day. They dive for shellfish on one breath. Chank shell divers have long done the same off Thoothukudi in Tamil Nadu.
- e.g. A seal's heart slows from about 100 beats a minute to about 10 on a dive.
- e.g. A human's heart slows by a tenth to a quarter with the face in cold water.
- e.g. A duck's heart slows when she puts her head under water.
### Fixed action patterns: movements that run to the end · *defined*
**Definition:** A fixed action pattern is a set of movements that an animal is born able to do. The movements come in the same order every time, and once started they usually run to the end.

A greylag goose sees an egg lying outside her nest. She stretches her neck, hooks the egg under her beak and rolls the egg back between her feet. In 1938 Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen took the egg away halfway through. The goose carried on. She drew her beak all the way back to the nest with nothing under it. Every greylag goose rolls an egg in this way, including geese who have never seen this done. That is a fixed action pattern. Your swallow is one too. Once food touches the back of your mouth, more than 20 muscles work in a set order. You cannot stop them halfway. A yawn is another. In Germany, the scientist Irenäus Eibl-Eibesfeldt raised red squirrels alone, with no soil and no nuts. Later he gave each squirrel a nut on a bare floor. The squirrels scraped at the floor, pressed the nut down with their noses and made covering movements over it. A fixed pattern is useful because it works the very first time, with no practice. Scientists today often say 'modal action pattern', because no two runs are exactly alike. A goose steers the egg from side to side when the egg wobbles. So part of the pattern is fixed, and part is adjusted as she goes.
- e.g. A goose rolling an egg back to her nest.
- e.g. A person swallowing a mouthful of water.
- e.g. A yawn, which lasts about six seconds and is hard to stop once begun.
- e.g. A squirrel making digging and covering movements over a nut.
### Instincts: whole behaviours that need no teacher · *defined*
**Definition:** An instinct is a whole behaviour that an animal can carry out without being taught. It is inherited from the parents, as the shape of a beak is.

A common cuckoo chick hatches in the nest of another kind of bird. On the first or second day, the chick is still blind and has no feathers. The chick heaves the other eggs over the edge with their back. The chick has never seen this done. A koel is raised by crows and hears cawing all day. The young koel still grows up to call 'ku-oo' like a koel. A duckling raised by a hen walks into water and swims. These are instincts. An instinct is usually built from several fixed patterns, with steering from the senses and choices along the way. A bird building her first nest uses inborn movements. She also chooses the branch, tests each twig and gets better with every nest. So an instinct gives an animal a strong start, and learning improves on that start. Instinct matters most for things that must go right the first time, with no teacher near. Most insects and spiders never meet their parents, so they depend on instinct for much of what they do. They learn as well. Elephants, crows and humans grow up slowly beside their elders, and learn much more of what they do. They have instincts too.
- e.g. A cuckoo chick pushing eggs out of the nest.
- e.g. A koel raised by crows calling like a koel.
- e.g. A duckling raised by a hen swimming on the first try.
- e.g. A newborn calf standing up and searching for the udder.
### Sign stimuli: one small feature sets off the behaviour · *defined*
**Definition:** A sign stimulus is the one feature of a thing that sets off an inborn behaviour. Scientists find it by showing animals simple models called dummies.

A herring gull has a red spot on the lower part of her yellow beak. Her chick pecks at the spot, and the pecking makes her bring up food. Niko Tinbergen and his student Ab Perdeck showed gull chicks cardboard heads. A head with no spot got few pecks. A head with a red spot got many. The chick was responding mostly to one feature, a red patch on a long thin shape. A male stickleback fish guards his nest in spring, when other males have red undersides. Tinbergen reported that males attacked rough wax lumps with red undersides. They left lifelike models with no red alone. A mother hen runs to a chick she can hear cheeping, even behind a screen. In one old test, a hen could see a chick struggling under a glass cover but could not hear them. She did not go to the chick. A simple trigger is quick to pick up, and in nature it is nearly always right. In a gull colony, a red spot above a chick really is a parent. But a simple trigger can be fooled. In Australia, male jewel beetles were found clinging to brown beer bottles beside the road. The shiny brown glass with its small bumps had the features of a female beetle's back.
- e.g. A gull chick pecks at the red spot on a parent's beak.
- e.g. A male stickleback attacks anything with a red underside.
- e.g. A mother hen comes running when she hears a chick cheeping.
- e.g. A goose rolls in any round thing that lies near her nest.
### Supernormal stimuli: stronger than the real thing · *defined*
**Definition:** A supernormal stimulus is a made-up trigger that works better than the real one. Its key feature has been made bigger or stronger.

Tinbergen offered an oystercatcher, who is a shore bird, a choice of eggs to sit on. One was her own. One was a giant egg, far too big for her to cover. She tried to sit on the giant egg. Herring gull chicks were shown a lifelike model of a parent's head and a thin red stick with white bands. They pecked more at the stick. The stick had more of what the chicks respond to: redness, thinness and contrast. In nature this causes no trouble, because no real egg is ever that big. So a bird never needed an inborn rule that says an egg can be too big. Only a person with paint and plaster can make an egg bigger than any real one. Cuckoo chicks do something similar to their foster parents. The inside of a cuckoo chick's open mouth is bright orange-red. One cuckoo chick's begging call sounds like a whole nest of chicks. A small reed warbler goes on feeding a cuckoo chick who is several times her own size. People make supernormal stimuli for each other all the time. A sweet tastes sweeter than any fruit does. A cartoon animal has eyes bigger than any real baby's eyes.
- e.g. An oystercatcher choosing a giant egg.
- e.g. Gull chicks pecking more at a striped red stick than at a model beak.
- e.g. A reed warbler feeding a cuckoo chick much bigger than herself.
- e.g. A sweet that is sweeter than a ripe mango.
### Why do big eyes look cute? A possible trigger in humans · *idea*
**Definition:** The baby schema is a set of features that babies have: a large head, a high forehead, big eyes low on the face, round cheeks and a small nose and chin. In 1943 Konrad Lorenz proposed that these features set off caring in adults.

Human babies, puppies, kittens and chicks share these features. Lorenz's idea was that the features work as a sign stimulus for humans. An adult who sees them feels an urge to look after the small one. Tests support part of this. Scientists have changed photos of babies to make the eyes bigger and the face rounder. People rated the changed babies as cuter, and said they wanted to look after them more. Children as young as three respond in the same way. Toy makers and cartoon artists use this. Over fifty years, the artists who drew Mickey Mouse gave him a bigger head and bigger eyes. That is a supernormal stimulus made with a pencil. What is not settled is how much of the response is inborn. People also learn what counts as cute from the toys and cartoons around them. So this is an idea with some good evidence. You test part of the idea yourself in the Big Eyes Test.
- e.g. A puppy's round head and big eyes make many people say 'aww'.
- e.g. Soft toys are made with bigger heads and eyes than real animals have.
- e.g. Cartoon babies have foreheads much higher than real babies' foreheads.
- **Konrad Lorenz, in 1943:** The response is inborn. Baby features are a sign stimulus that sets off caring in adults. A red spot sets off pecking in a gull chick in the same way.
- **Scientists who doubt that it is all inborn:** People do respond to baby features. But children grow up surrounded by big-eyed toys and cartoons, so some of the response may be learned.
### Imprinting: a duckling learns whom to follow · *defined*
**Definition:** Imprinting is a fast kind of learning that happens during a short time early in life. A young bird learns whom to follow, and the learning is very hard to undo.

A greylag gosling can walk within a day of hatching. She is born with the urge to follow, and with the movements for following. But she is not born knowing what her mother looks like. In her first day she fixes on a large thing that moves near her and makes sounds. In nature that thing is her mother. Konrad Lorenz let goslings hatch in an incubator, so that the first moving thing they saw was Lorenz. They followed him across fields and into the river, and stayed away from adult geese. Mallard ducklings were harder to lead. They followed Lorenz only when he squatted low and quacked without stopping. The short time when imprinting can happen is called the sensitive period. In 1959 Eckhard Hess reported tests on mallard ducklings with a model duck that moved round a track. Ducklings imprinted most strongly at 13 to 16 hours old. After about 30 hours, hardly any did. Ducklings have also imprinted on moving boxes and balls. Imprinting is found mostly in birds who walk soon after hatching, such as ducks, geese and chickens. A sparrow chick stays in the nest, and the parents come to the chick. So the chick has no need to follow anyone. Other animals have their own kinds of imprinting. A ewe learns the smell of her lamb in the first hours after birth. Young salmon learn the smell of their home stream, and find the stream again years later. Human babies do not imprint as ducklings do. But a newborn baby already prefers their mother's voice, which they heard before birth.
- e.g. Goslings following the person who hatched them.
- e.g. A mallard duckling following a model duck round a track.
- e.g. A ewe who knows her own lamb by smell within hours.
- e.g. A salmon returning to the stream where they hatched.
### Where is an instinct kept? Genes build the wiring · *idea*
**Definition:** Scientists think that inborn behaviour comes from the way nerve cells are linked before birth. Genes guide this linking. The details are known for very few behaviours.

A gene is a stretch of DNA. DNA is the long molecule in every cell that is copied from parents to their young. Genes do not carry drawings of nests or burrows. They carry instructions for building a body, cell by cell, including the nerve cells and the links between them. A reflex is the simplest case. A sensor, a nerve going in, a nerve going out and a muscle are all linked before birth. Bigger behaviours are harder to trace. Hopi Hoekstra and her team studied two kinds of American mice. Oldfield mice dig a long burrow with an escape tunnel. Deer mice dig a short burrow with no escape tunnel. Mice raised in cages had never seen a burrow. They still dug the burrow of their own kind. In 2013 the team found three regions of DNA linked to the length of the tunnel. One more region was linked to the escape tunnel. This shows that a behaviour can be inherited in parts. Nobody can yet read a stretch of DNA and say which behaviour it will build. Imprinting leaves a trace as well. Gabriel Horn, Patrick Bateson and Steven Rose studied chicks. They found a small region of the brain that changes when a chick imprints.
- e.g. A reflex, in which a sensor, two nerves and a muscle are linked together before birth.
- e.g. Oldfield mice raised in cages still dig a long burrow with an escape tunnel.
- e.g. A small region of a chick's brain changes during imprinting.
### Is any behaviour purely inborn? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that some behaviours appear without any teaching. They disagree about whether 'inborn' and 'learned' can ever be fully separated.

A duckling just out of the egg turns towards the call of a mother duck of their own kind. That looks purely inborn. Gilbert Gottlieb tested it. Ducklings call softly inside the egg before hatching, and they hear themselves and their brothers and sisters. Gottlieb stopped some duck embryos from making or hearing these calls. After hatching, those ducklings no longer picked out the right mother's call. So even this instinct needed an experience, though nobody taught it.
- e.g. A duckling picks out the call of their own kind of mother.
- e.g. A koel raised by crows calls like a koel.
- **Konrad Lorenz:** Instinct and learning are two separate things. An inborn pattern is inherited whole, as a bone is. Learning is added at set points, as in imprinting.
- **Daniel Lehrman in 1953, and later Gilbert Gottlieb:** No behaviour is simply in the genes. Every behaviour grows step by step, as genes, body and surroundings act on each other, starting inside the egg. Calling it instinct hides the question of how it grows.
- **Most scientists today:** Both points are right. Some behaviours appear in every healthy animal of a kind, and 'innate' is a short name for those. Even they need the right surroundings while the body grows.
- **A fourth view:** An animal acting by instinct is sensing, feeling and choosing the whole time. A goose who rolls an egg steers the egg, and she chose where to build her nest. Instinct is the starting point for her choices.

## Mostly agreed
- Reflexes are inborn and fast, and they run through the spinal cord or the lowest part of the brain. *How we know:* Doctors find the same reflexes in every healthy newborn baby. The time between a tap on the knee and the pull of the thigh muscle has been measured many times.
- Humans share the diving reflex with seals, ducks and other air-breathing animals. *How we know:* Paul Bert measured the slowing heart in diving ducks in 1870. Per Scholander measured it in seals in 1940. It has been measured in people in many laboratories since.
- Animals can carry out complicated behaviour that they have never seen. *How we know:* Edward Jenner described the cuckoo chick pushing out eggs in 1788. Squirrels raised alone made burying movements. Mice raised in cages dug the burrow of their own kind.
- Many inborn behaviours are set off by one simple feature, the sign stimulus. *How we know:* Tinbergen and others showed dummies to gull chicks, sticklebacks and other animals. David Lack found that a European robin attacked a bunch of red feathers.
- Young ducks, geese and chickens imprint during a short sensitive period after hatching. *How we know:* Douglas Spalding reported it in chicks in 1873, Oskar Heinroth in goslings in 1911 and Konrad Lorenz in 1935. Eckhard Hess measured the timing in ducklings in 1959.
- Inborn behaviour is inherited through DNA. *How we know:* Hopi Hoekstra's team crossed two kinds of mice and linked parts of their burrow shape to particular regions of DNA.

## How we found out
**Douglas Spalding in Britain, Oskar and Magdalena Heinroth in Germany, and Konrad Lorenz in Austria. Niko Tinbergen worked with Lorenz on fixed action patterns., Spalding published in 1873, Oskar Heinroth in 1911 and Lorenz in 1935. Lorenz, Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch shared the Nobel Prize in 1973..** For centuries people thought a chick or a gosling was born knowing their own mother. In 1873 Douglas Spalding, a largely self-taught scientist who grew up in Scotland, tested this. He put small hoods over the eyes of chicks as they hatched. He took the hoods off a day or two later. The chicks followed him, the first moving thing they saw. Chicks unhooded after four days ran away from him instead. Spalding died young, and his work was forgotten. In Berlin, Oskar and Magdalena Heinroth raised hundreds of kinds of birds by hand in their flat. In 1911 Oskar wrote that new goslings took the first person they saw as their parent. Konrad Lorenz, in Austria, made the test that people remember. In 1935 he reported splitting a clutch of greylag goose eggs. The goose hatched half, and he hatched half. Her goslings followed her. His goslings followed him, even after the two groups were mixed. He named this imprinting. In 1973 he shared a Nobel Prize with Niko Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch. Lorenz had also joined the Nazi party in 1938 and written in support of its ideas about race. He later said that he regretted this.  
*What people believed before:* That a young bird is born knowing their own mother. Spalding's chicks and Lorenz's goslings followed a man instead, because he was the first moving thing they saw. The urge to follow is inborn, but whom to follow is learned.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Douglas Spalding described imprinting sixty years before Konrad Lorenz.** (Historians of science, and the biologist J. B. S. Haldane, who had Spalding's paper reprinted in 1954.; *accepted*)  
  For: Spalding's paper of 1873 reports that chicks followed the first moving thing they saw. It also reports that this stopped working after a few days.  
  Against: Lorenz gave imprinting its name, tested it in more ways and made the idea widely known. Both men deserve credit.
- **A male stickleback attacks anything with a red underside.** (Niko Tinbergen, from tests he reported in 1948 and 1951.; *debated*)  
  For: In his tests, males attacked rough models with red undersides and left lifelike models without red alone.  
  Against: Later scientists repeated the tests with more fish. In 1985 William Rowland and Piet Sevenster found that many males did not attack red dummies more. Red matters, but less simply than Tinbergen said.
- **A wasp acting by instinct will repeat the same steps for ever, whatever happens.** (Many books, retelling a test by Jean-Henri Fabre on a digger wasp.; *rejected*)  
  For: Fabre moved a wasp's cricket away from her burrow while she was inside checking it. She dragged it back and checked the burrow again, about forty times in a row.  
  Against: Fabre himself wrote that wasps from another colony stopped after two or three tries and took the cricket straight in. In 2013 Fred Keijzer showed that later books left that part out.
- **Imprinting happens in one moment and can never be changed.** (Konrad Lorenz, in his early writing.; *debated*)  
  For: Birds raised by hand often stayed attached to people for life.  
  Against: Eckhard Hess, Patrick Bateson and others found that imprinting builds up over hours. The sensitive period has soft edges, and a young bird can sometimes come to prefer a second thing.
- **Humans have no instincts. Everything a person does is learned.** (John B. Watson, an American psychologist, in the 1920s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Humans learn far more than most animals, and people in different places live very differently.  
  Against: Every newborn baby sucks, grasps, turns towards a touch on the cheek and looks longer at face-like patterns. William James argued in 1890 that humans have more instincts than other animals, not fewer.

## Roots
In the Cold Face Test, cold water on your face slowed your heart. You also met ducklings who are born ready to follow. Here we ask where know-how comes from when nobody taught it.

**Deep truth.** In the Cold Face Test, cool water on your face slowed your heart. Seals, dolphins and ducks have the same reflex, and it saves their air on a dive. Scientists think we all inherited it from shared ancestors, who lived long before there were humans. So some of what your body knows how to do is older than humankind.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of what we know as what we learned in our own lifetime. A reflex is know-how that was ready before our first lesson, and it reached us from ancestors.  
*Evidence:* In the Cold Face Test the pulse count usually falls by 2 to 5 beats with a cold, wet face. Nobody practised that. The same slowing was measured in ducks by Paul Bert in 1870 and in seals by Per Scholander in 1940.

- *who am i:* You are someone who learns new things. But you also carry abilities that are much older than you. Your blink, your swallow and your slowing heart were ready before your first lesson.
- *what is life:* Every living being begins with abilities that were handed down from their parents, and then adds what they learn themselves. A duckling is given the urge to follow, and finds out for herself whom to follow.
- *what is this world:* A body is not finished without the world around it. A reflex needs something to set it off, such as cold water on the face. A duckling's urge to follow needs something to follow, such as a moving mother.

The diving reflex is in the seal, the duck, the dolphin and you. These are separate bodies, and they carry the same piece of know-how, because they grew from the same ancestors. A gosling is handed the urge to follow, and the world around her supplies the rest. The Upanishads teach that one life looks out through many bodies. Biology does not say that, but it says something close. All animals are one family, and much of what each one can do was inherited from that family.

- **Yoga Sutras of Patanjali 2.9, with the old commentary of Vyasa:** “स्वरसवाही विदुषोऽपि तथारूढोऽभिनिवेशः” The sutra says that the will to go on living flows by itself, even in the wise. Vyasa adds that even a newborn worm shrinks from danger, though nothing in this life has taught them to. (These thinkers noticed what this lesson tests: a newborn animal acts rightly with no lesson. They explained it by earlier lives of the same being. Biology explains it by the lives of ancestors, passed on through DNA. The two answers differ, but the question is the same.)
- **Mencius 2A:6:** “今人乍見孺子將入於井，皆有怵惕惻隱之心。 / 非所以內交於孺子之父母也，非所以要譽於鄉黨朋友也，非惡其聲而然也。” Mencius says that anyone who sees a small child about to fall into a well feels alarm at once. They feel alarm and pity before any thought of praise or reward. (Mencius was arguing that goodness starts from something inborn, and he was not doing biology. Yet he describes a response in every person that one particular sight sets off. Lorenz's idea about baby faces is a later version of the same thought.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 3.33:** “सदृशं चेष्टते स्वस्याः प्रकृतेर्ज्ञानवानपि। / प्रकृतिं यान्ति भूतानि निग्रहः किं करिष्यति।।” The verse says that even a wise person acts according to their own nature. All beings follow their nature, and forcing alone achieves little. (In Find the Trigger you knew the sock ball could not hit you, and you blinked anyway. The next verse of the Gita adds that a person need not be ruled by every pull. Humans can notice an urge and still choose what to do after it.)
- Science (settled): All animals with backbones share ancestors and inherit their bodies through DNA. The diving reflex has been found in every air-breathing kind that has been tested.
- Science (debated): In 1890 William James argued that humans have more instincts than other animals, not fewer. Scientists still argue about how much of what people do starts from instinct.
- Other view, Zing-Yang Kuo, a Chinese psychologist, from 1921: Kuo argued that the word instinct explains nothing. He raised kittens together with rats, and most of those cats never killed a rat. On his view, each behaviour is built up step by step from the first days of the embryo. What we call inherited know-how is growth that nobody has traced yet.
- Practice, The breath that needs no planning (3 min): Sit still together and close your eyes. Breathe out slowly, and then wait without breathing in. Notice the moment when your body starts the next breath by itself. Do this three times. Then breathe as usual for one minute, and notice each breath arrive without any plan from you.
- Ask: In the Cold Face Test your heart slowed, and you did not plan it. Who or what knew how to do that?
- Ask: A duckling is born ready to learn one thing in her first day. What do you think you were born ready to learn?
- Ask: If part of your know-how is older than humankind, where does 'you' begin?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* You never learned to slow your heart in cold water, yet your body knows how. Is that knowledge really yours, even though you did not learn it? (hint: Think about other things you have that you did not make: your eyes, your name, your first language.)
- *deep:* A goose goes on with the rolling movement after the egg is gone. What might she be noticing while she does it? (hint: Nobody can ask her. What could a scientist watch for, to find out?)
- *deep:* A gosling who follows a man is following her inborn rule exactly. The rule is to follow the first big moving thing she sees. So where did the mistake come from? (hint: Her rule is 'follow the first big moving thing'. In nature that thing is always her mother.)
- *big:* A cartoon can look cuter than a real baby. Can made-up things pull at us harder than real things? (hint: Think about sweets and fruit, or a video game and a real game of catch.)
- *big:* Which is the better start in life: to be born knowing a lot, or to be born able to learn a lot? (hint: A wasp never meets her mother. An elephant calf stays beside hers for years.)
- *big:* How much of what you did today did you plan, and how much did your body do without asking you? (hint: Count the blinks, swallows, breaths and yawns that nobody planned.)

## Story: The Blue Chappals
*Ammu, Ammachi, Unni and the duckling Minnu are made up. These things are real. In Kuttanad in Kerala, herders walk large flocks of ducks through the paddy fields after the harvest. Duck eggs are often hatched under hens. A duckling follows the first moving thing she sees on her first day. Ducklings in tests have followed moving boxes and balls. Ducklings swim without being shown how. The pair of chappals is imagined, and fits these findings.*

In Kuttanad, when the paddy has been cut, the fields belong to the ducks. Ammu's grandmother walked three hundred of them out every morning. She carried a long pole with a white cloth tied to the tip. Where the cloth went, the ducks went.

Ammu was nine, and she had come from Kochi for the Onam holidays. Her job was small. In the shed, a brown hen was sitting on the last twelve duck eggs. Ammu had to tell Ammachi when they began to crack.

Eleven cracked on a Tuesday night. By morning, eleven ducklings were peeping under the hen's wings. The twelfth egg had rolled to the edge of the nest. Ammu picked the egg up to throw away. Something inside tapped against her palm.

She put the egg in half a coconut shell lined with an old towel, and set the shell on the warm kitchen step. Then she sat down beside it and waited.

Near noon the shell split round the top, like a lid. A wet head pushed out. The duckling lay still for a long time, breathing. Then she lifted her head and looked about.

"Ammachi!" Ammu jumped up and ran to the shed. Her blue rubber chappals slapped on the stone. The duckling watched them go.

From that hour, the duckling went wherever Ammu went. She went to the well. She went to the gate. When Ammu sat down, the duckling sat on her foot. Ammu named her Minnu.

"Put her with the others," Ammachi said.

Ammu tucked Minnu under the hen, among the eleven. Minnu climbed out and ran back across the yard, crying. Ammu tried three times. Minnu came back three times.

"She saw you first," said Ammachi. "A duckling follows whatever she sees moving on her first day. After that, the door shuts. I once had one who followed a goat."

"So she thinks I am her mother?"

"I don't know what she thinks," said Ammachi. "I know what she does."

Ammu's cousin Unni, who was seven, thought it was wonderful. "She loves you," he said.

Ammu was not sure, and she had a worry. In nine days she would go home to a flat on the fourth floor. A duck could not live there. And a duck who would not stay with the flock would be alone in the fields, where the kites circled.

So she began to watch Minnu the way Ammachi watched ducks, without hurrying.

She noticed two things. One evening she sat on the veranda wall with her feet tucked up under her. Minnu ran about below, peeping loudly, though Ammu's face was right there above her. And when Ammu left her chappals at the kitchen door and went in barefoot, Minnu did not follow her in. Minnu sat down beside the chappals and went quiet.

Ammu thought about that all through dinner.

In the morning she called Unni. "Put on my chappals," she said. "Walk to the well. Don't look back."

"They're too big."

"That doesn't matter. Walk."

Unni shuffled off across the yard. Minnu got up and hurried after him, with her beak a finger's width behind the blue heels. Ammu walked the other way, barefoot, calling softly. Minnu did not turn her head.

"She doesn't know you at all!" Unni shouted from the well.

"She knows what she saw," Ammu said. She held her hand low, at the height of a duckling's eye. "From down here, what was moving? Not my face. These."

It stung a little, and she did not say so.

"Now watch," she told Unni. "We change one thing at a time. Take them off and carry them."

Unni carried the chappals in his hands, high up. Minnu stood still and cried. He put them on the ground and pulled them along with a string. Minnu followed the string.

"On her first day, the first things she saw were blue chappals moving along the ground," Ammu said. "So to her, that is what a mother looks like."

That afternoon Ammachi put on the blue chappals. They pinched, and she grumbled. She walked into the duckling pen and stood among the eleven and the hen. Minnu settled beside her heels. The eleven pressed in around Minnu, because ducklings like a warm heap. That night Minnu slept in the heap.

Every day after that, the chappals went into the pen, with Ammachi in them. Every day Minnu sat a little further from the blue heels and a little deeper among the eleven.

On the eighth day, Ammachi led the young ones down to the canal for the first time. The hen came too, and Minnu trotted behind the chappals. At the edge the eleven slid straight into the water and paddled off. Nobody had shown them how. The hen ran up and down the bank, clucking with alarm.

Minnu stopped beside the chappals. A ripple touched her feet. She dipped her beak. Then she was in the water as well, paddling hard after the others, with her tail wagging.

"There are no chappals in a canal," said Ammachi, and stepped out of them with a sigh.

Ammu went home to Kochi in new green chappals from the shop at the junction. The blue ones stayed in Kuttanad.

A month later Ammachi sent a message. "Your duck walks in the middle of the flock now. She follows the white cloth like the rest. But yesterday the fish seller came by in blue slippers, and she went halfway to the road after him."

**For the parent:** For the parent: Minnu had not fixed on Ammu as a person. She had fixed on the first thing she saw moving at the height of a duckling's eye. That was a pair of blue chappals. Ammu found this out by changing one thing at a time. That is how Lorenz and Hess studied imprinting.

- Ask: Why did Minnu follow Unni when he wore the blue chappals?
- Ask: What did Ammu learn when Unni carried the chappals high in his hands?
- Ask: Nobody showed the ducklings how to swim. What else can a duckling do without being shown?

### g1 · Which One Did They Choose? Sixteen real tests (15 min)
- **rules:** Shuffle the 16 cards and put them in a pile with the fronts up.; One player reads the front of the top card aloud. It is a supernormal stimulus.); A goose is rolling an egg back to her nest. The egg is taken away halfway. Does she stop, or finish the movement? (back: She finishes the movement with nothing under her beak. A fixed action pattern usually runs to the end. Lorenz and Tinbergen, 1938.); An oystercatcher is a shore bird. In nature no egg is ever that big.); A male European robin guards his garden. He is shown a stuffed young robin with a brown breast, and a bunch of red feathers on a wire. Which one does he attack? (back: The bunch of red feathers. David Lack found that the red breast is the trigger for attack.); A mother hen can see one chick struggling under a glass cover, but cannot hear them. The cheeping sound is the trigger for her rescue, more than the sight of the chick.); Goslings hatch in an incubator, and the first moving thing they see is a man. The goslings had imprinted on him.); One mallard duckling first sees a moving model duck at 15 hours old. Another first sees it at 35 hours old. Which duckling follows the model later? The burying pattern is inborn.); A common cuckoo chick hatches in a reed warbler's nest. The chick is one day old, blind and without feathers. What does the chick do to the other eggs? Nobody shows the chick how.); In Australia, a male jewel beetle can go to a female beetle or to a brown beer bottle. Scientists found many males on one of these. Which one? (back: The bottle. The shiny brown glass with small bumps was a supernormal stimulus.); A newborn human baby is a few minutes old. The baby is shown a paddle with a simple drawn face. Then the baby is shown a paddle with the same parts jumbled. Which paddle does the baby follow further with their eyes? (back: The face. Newborn babies turn further to follow a face-like pattern. Humans are born with a pull towards faces.); Ducklings imprint on two shapes that are the same, such as two balls. Later they can follow two new shapes that match, or two that differ. Which pair do most follow? (back: The matching pair. In 2016 Antone Martinho and Alex Kacelnik showed that ducklings imprint on 'same' or 'different' as well as on shape.); Adults watch a five-minute film of people yawning. Other adults watch a film of people smiling. In which group do more people yawn? (back: The yawn group. In Robert Provine's test, about half of them yawned. Seeing a yawn is a trigger for a yawn.); A toad sees a small strip moving lengthwise, like a worm crawling. Then the toad sees the same strip turned upright and moving sideways. Which one does the toad snap at? (back: The strip moving lengthwise. Jörg-Peter Ewert found that the direction of movement is the trigger. The shape is the same.)

## Experiments
### e1 · The Cold Face Test: what does cold water on your face do to your heartbeat? (35 min)
- **question:** When you hold your breath with your face in cold water, does your heart beat faster, slower or the same? Does cold water on your hand do the same thing?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. With your face in cold water and your breath held, will your heart beat faster, slower or the same? Will a cold hand do the same thing as a cold face? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Sit on the same chair for every try, and rest for one minute before each try. Hold your breath for 20 seconds every time, after a normal breath in. The grown-up counts the pulse for the same 15 seconds every time, from second 5 to second 20. Change only one thing between the tests: nothing wet, the hand in the water, or the face in the water. Do each test three times, because one count can be wrong by a beat or two.
- **safety:** A grown-up stays beside the child for the whole test. The child holds their breath for 20 seconds at most and may lift their face at any moment. Use cold water, not painfully cold water. Leave this test out if your child has a cold, an ear infection, breathing trouble or any heart condition.
- **record:** Draw a table with four rows: resting, breath held, breath held with a hand in cold water, and breath held with the face in cold water. Write three pulse counts in each row. Then circle the middle count of each three.
- **results table:** What was done | Pulse count in 15 seconds: try 1, try 2 and try 3 | The middle count of the three
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Sit your child on a chair at a table. Find their pulse with two fingers on the inside of the wrist, below the thumb. Explain the three tests. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* You will hold your breath three ways: with nothing wet, with a hand in cold water and with your face in cold water. Will your heart beat faster, slower or the same each time? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make a guess about your heartbeat in each test.
  - Ask: Why do you think your heart will do that? (good: Cold is a shock, so my heart will race.; Holding my breath is hard work.; My body might save air.; Seals slow down when they dive.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children expect the heart to race. If the wrist pulse is hard to find, press gently on the side of the neck, beside the windpipe. Practise one 15-second count before you go on.
- **s2 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Your child sits still and breathes normally for two minutes. Count the pulse for 15 seconds, three times, with a short pause between counts. Write down the three counts.  
  *Parent says:* Sit still and breathe as usual. I will count your pulse three times. This is your resting count.  
  *Child mode:* Sit still. Breathe as usual. A grown-up counts your pulse three times.
  - Note: A count of 18 to 26 in 15 seconds is usual for a child of nine who is sitting still.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Test 1, breath held with nothing wet. Start the timer. Your child takes a normal breath in and holds it for 20 seconds. Count the pulse from second 5 to second 20. Rest one minute. Do this three times.  
  *Parent says:* Take a normal breath in and hold it. I will count from second 5 to second 20. Then you breathe and rest for one minute.  
  *Child mode:* Breathe in and hold for 20 seconds. Then rest for one minute.
  - Note: Say 'breathe' at 20 seconds. If 20 seconds is too long for your child, use 15 seconds for every test. Count from second 5 to second 15. Then compare only counts of the same length.
- **s4 (3 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Put the bowl on the table. Add the ice cubes or the fridge water and stir. Test the water with your own hand. It should feel as cold as water from the fridge. Your hand should be able to stay in for 20 seconds.  
  *Parent says:* I am making the water cold. Put one finger in. It should feel cold but it should not hurt.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up makes the water cold. Dip one finger in to check.
  - Note: If you have no fridge, use the coolest water you have, such as water from a clay pot. The change in the pulse will then be smaller.
- **s5 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Test 2, hand in cold water. Your child takes a normal breath in, holds it and puts one hand in the water up to the wrist. Count the pulse on the other wrist from second 5 to second 20. Rest one minute. Do this three times.  
  *Parent says:* Breathe in, hold, and put this hand in the water. Keep it there until I say 'breathe'.  
  *Child mode:* Hold your breath. Put one hand in the cold water for 20 seconds.
  - Note: Dry the hand between tries. This test finds out whether cold on any part of the body changes the heartbeat.
- **s6 (7 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Test 3, face in cold water. Your child leans over the bowl, takes a normal breath in and lowers their face. The forehead, closed eyes, nose and cheeks go under. Hold their wrist and count from second 5 to second 20. Touch their shoulder twice at 20 seconds. Rest one minute. Do this three times.  
  *Parent says:* Breathe in, hold, and lower your face until your forehead and nose are under. Lift your face when I touch your shoulder twice. You can lift it sooner if you want to.  
  *Child mode:* Hold your breath. Put your face in the water. Lift it when your shoulder is touched.
  - Note: Keep the towel ready. If your child does not want to put their face in, soak a small towel in the cold water. Squeeze the towel lightly. They hold their breath, and you press the towel over their forehead, eyes and cheeks. The change is usually smaller this way. If the water has warmed up, add more ice first.
  - Check: In the Cold Face Test, why do you also test a hand in cold water? → **To see if cold on any body part works**
- **s7 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put the three counts in each row in order of size and circle the middle one. Compare the four middle counts. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the four middle counts. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Which test changed your heartbeat most?  
  *Child mode:* Circle the middle counts. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: What had to happen for your heart to slow down? (good: My face had to be in cold water.; Holding my breath alone did not do it.; The cold hand did not do it.)
  - Ask: Why might a slower heart help an animal who is under water? (good: The air in the body lasts longer.; A slow heart uses less oxygen.; A seal can stay down longer.)
  - Note: In most children the face-in-water count is 2 to 5 beats lower than the others. The breath-held count and the cold-hand count stay close to the resting count, or rise a little. Write down what you really found, even if it differs. If nothing changed, the water was probably not cold enough, or the count started too soon. The reason: cold water on the skin around the eyes and nose, together with a held breath, sets off the diving reflex. A nerve of the face carries the message, and the heart slows. Blood vessels in the arms and legs become narrower, so more blood stays with the brain and the heart. The oxygen from one breath then lasts longer. Seals, dolphins, ducks and humans all have this reflex. Your child did not learn it and did not plan it.
  - Think: You may never dive for your food. So why does your body have a reflex for diving?
### e2 · The Big Eyes Test: which drawn face do people find cuter? (25 min)
- **question:** Two drawn faces differ in one thing only. Do most people call the face with bigger eyes cuter? Do most people call the face with the eyes lower down cuter?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. In each pair, which face will get more votes for being cuter? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** The two faces in a pair differ in one thing only, so draw everything else the same. Ask every voter the same question in the same words. Swap which card is on the left for each new voter, because some people tend to pick one side. Ask each voter alone, so that nobody copies. Ask at least 8 voters, because two or three people can agree by chance.
- **record:** Draw a table with two rows, one for each pair. For each voter, put one stroke under the card they chose.
- **results table:** Pair | Votes for the first card | Votes for the second card
- **s1 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Trace the tumbler on all four cards. Draw a faint line across the middle of circles A and B. On A, draw two filled dots on the line, each as wide as the flat end of the pencil. On B, trace the coin twice on the line and fill in a dark centre. Add the same dot nose and the same short curved mouth to both. Ask for the guess and the reason.  
  *Parent says:* These two faces differ in one thing only, the size of the eyes. We will ask 8 people which is cuter. Which face do you think will get more votes? Why?  
  *Child mode:* Draw face A with small eyes. Draw face B with big eyes. Which will win? Why?
  - Note: Check that the nose and mouth are in the same place on both cards. Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On C and D, draw eyes 1 centimetre wide. On C, put the eyes 2 centimetres below the top of the circle. Put the nose 2 centimetres lower, and the mouth near the bottom. On D, put the eyes 4 and a half centimetres below the top. Put the nose and mouth close under the eyes.  
  *Parent says:* These two faces have the same eyes. On C the eyes sit high. On D they sit low, and there is a big forehead above them. Which will get more votes, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Draw face C with high eyes. Draw face D with low eyes. Which one will get more votes?
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask at least 8 voters, one at a time. Hold up cards A and B and ask the question. Record the vote. Then hold up C and D and ask again. Swap the left and right cards for each new voter.  
  *Parent says:* Here are two faces. Which face is cuter? Please point to one.  
  *Child mode:* Ask each voter: which face is cuter? Put a stroke for each vote.
  - Note: Voters must not hear each other's answers. A photo of each pair sent by phone also works. If a voter asks what 'cuter' means, say 'whichever you feel like calling cute'. You can spread this step over the day.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Count the votes for each card. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below. Then find one soft toy or cartoon character at home and look at its eyes and forehead.  
  *Parent says:* Count the votes. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Now look at this toy. What has the toy maker done to the eyes and the head?  
  *Child mode:* Count the votes. Say what you thought would happen. Say what you saw. Say why it happened.
  - Ask: Real babies have big eyes that sit low on the face. Why might adults find that cute? (good: It makes adults want to look after babies.; Babies need a lot of care.; It could be a trigger, like the red spot for a gull chick.)
  - Ask: With 8 voters, how many votes for one card would be strong evidence? (good: Seven or eight.; Much more than four, because four is what chance gives.)
  - Note: In most groups, B and D win clearly. If your votes are close to four against four, ask more people. The reason: babies have big eyes, set low on a large head with a high forehead. In 1943 Konrad Lorenz proposed that these features work as a sign stimulus, and set off caring in adults. Toys and cartoons make the eyes bigger still, which makes them a supernormal stimulus. Scientists still argue about how much of this response is inborn and how much is learned from toys and cartoons.
  - Check: In the Big Eyes Test, why do the two faces in a pair differ in one thing only? → **So we know which feature changed the votes**

  *Parent says:* Watch your toes while I stroke your sole. What did they do? Now swallow, and put your tongue out. What happens in your mouth after the lemon drop?  
  *Child mode:* Watch your toes. Then taste one lemon drop. What does your mouth do?
  - Note: The toes usually curl down. A ticklish child may pull the whole foot away, which is another reflex. After the lemon drop the mouth fills with saliva within a few seconds.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill in the table together. For each reflex, write the stimulus and the response. Ask whether your child planned any of the three.  
  *Parent says:* Each reflex had a stimulus of its own. Would the lemon drop make you blink? Would the sock ball make your mouth water? Did you plan any of these?

### a2 · The Swallow Test: a pattern that runs to the end (10 min)
- **record:** Write whether you could stop a swallow halfway. Then write two numbers: swallows in 30 seconds with no water, and swallows in 30 seconds with small sips.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child rests two fingers on the front of the throat and takes a small sip of water. Ask for a guess. Then they start to swallow and try to stop halfway. Try three times.  
  *Parent says:* Feel the bump in your throat go up and down when you swallow. Do you think you can stop a swallow halfway? Why? Try it.  
  *Child mode:* Feel your throat. Start a swallow. Can you stop halfway?
  - Note: Nobody can stop it. Once water reaches the back of the mouth, the rest of the swallow runs by itself.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child empties their mouth. Ask for a guess. Time 30 seconds while they swallow as many times as they can with no water. Count the throat bumps. Rest one minute. Then time 30 seconds with a small sip for every swallow.  
  *Parent says:* How many times can you swallow in 30 seconds with nothing in your mouth? Guess first. Then we try with a sip each time.  
  *Child mode:* Swallow with no water for 30 seconds. Then swallow with sips. Count both.
  - Note: Most people manage only 4 to 8 dry swallows and find that each one gets harder. With sips, 10 or more is easy. Small sips only, so that nobody chokes.
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the two counts. Talk about the questions below. After your child has answered, read them the note.  
  *Parent says:* Why was it so hard to swallow with nothing in your mouth? What does a swallow need before it will start?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your two counts. Which one was bigger?
  - Ask: What is the trigger for a swallow? (good: Something touching the back of the mouth.; Water or food or saliva to swallow.; Without anything there, it will not start.)
  - Note: The trigger for a swallow is food, water or saliva touching the back of the mouth. With no water, the saliva soon runs out and the trigger is missing. Once a swallow starts, more than 20 muscles work in a set order, and the order cannot be stopped halfway. A goose rolling an egg shows the same two features: a trigger, and a pattern that runs to the end.
  - Check: Which two things are true of a fixed action pattern, such as a swallow? → **It needs a trigger and runs to the end**
### a3 · When Does the Window Close? Draw the ducklings' graph (15 min)

  *Child mode:* Look at the table. At which age did most ducklings imprint? Guess first.

- **s2 (8 min)**

### p1 · The Yawn Spread: does seeing a yawn set off a yawn? (30 min, 3 days)
- **record:** Day 1: a log of yawns, with the time and what came before. Days 2 and 3: a table with six rows, one for each volunteer, and a tick if they yawned within three minutes.
- **schedule:** day 1: Watch for yawns at home and log what came just before each one. (10 min); day 2: Yawn in front of six people, one at a time, and count who yawns. (10 min); day 3: Smile in front of the same six people, count yawns, and compare. (10 min)
- **s1 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 1. In the evening, your child watches the family for an hour while doing other things. For every yawn they see, they log the time and what came just before. They also try to stop one of their own yawns halfway.  
  *Parent says:* Today we collect yawns. Each time someone yawns, write the time and what happened just before. Did that person see or hear another yawn?  
  *Child mode:* Log every yawn you see. Write what came just before it.
  - Note: Late evening gives the most yawns. A yawn lasts about six seconds, and most people find that they cannot stop one halfway.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 2. Your child tests six volunteers, one at a time, without saying what the test is about. They sit facing the volunteer and chat. Over one minute they give three big, slow yawns. Then they watch for three minutes and record a tick for a yawn.  
  *Parent says:* Do not tell them what we are testing. Yawn three times, slowly, where they can see you. Then keep chatting and watch for three minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Yawn three times in front of each person. Watch for three minutes.
  - Note: Choose volunteers aged six or older. Children younger than about five rarely catch a yawn. A stifled yawn with the mouth shut counts as a yawn.
  - Wait: Do this the day after the yawn log
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Day 3. Your child tests the same six people at about the same time of day. This time they give three big smiles in one minute and watch for three minutes. Then they compare the two counts and tell the volunteers what the test was.  
  *Parent says:* Today you smile three times, and we count yawns again. If people yawn as often after smiles, the yawns were not caught from you.  
  *Child mode:* Smile three times in front of each person. Count yawns. Compare the days.
  - Ask: Why did you need the day of smiles? (good: People yawn anyway when they are tired.; To check that it was the yawn that did it.; It is the comparison that makes the test fair.)
  - Note: In Robert Provine's tests, about half of adults yawned while watching yawns, and far fewer while watching smiles. With six volunteers your numbers may differ. Two or three yawns after yawning and none or one after smiling is a usual result. Nobody yet knows why yawns spread.
  - Wait: Do this on the third day, at about the same time of day

## Worksheets
### Reflexes, patterns and imprinting (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is a reflex?  
   Options: A fast, automatic response to a stimulus / A skill learned by practice / A kind of muscle / A plan made by the brain  
   **Answer:** A fast, automatic response to a stimulus. *Why:* A reflex is fast and automatic, and nobody has to learn it. A skill learned by practice, such as cycling, is the opposite kind of thing.
2. (mcq) A drop of lemon juice makes your mouth water. Which one is the stimulus?  
   Options: The saliva / The swallow / The tongue / The lemon juice  
   **Answer:** The lemon juice. *Why:* The stimulus is what sets the reflex off. The saliva flowing is the response. The tongue is where the sensors are.
3. (mcq) A goose's egg is taken away halfway through a roll. What does she do?  
   Options: She stops at once / She flies away / She finishes the rolling movement / She rolls much faster  
   **Answer:** She finishes the rolling movement. *Why:* A fixed action pattern usually runs to the end once it has started. Lorenz and Tinbergen saw the goose draw her beak back to the nest with no egg.
4. (mcq) What does a newly hatched gosling imprint on?  
   Options: The biggest egg in the nest / The first food she eats / The colour of the nest / The first big moving thing she sees  
   **Answer:** The first big moving thing she sees. *Why:* A gosling fixes on the first large moving thing near her in her first day. In nature that is her mother.
5. (mcq) What is a sign stimulus?  
   Options: A reward given after a trick / A loud warning call / A mark on a tree / The one feature that sets off a behaviour  
   **Answer:** The one feature that sets off a behaviour. *Why:* A sign stimulus is one feature, such as the red spot on a gull's beak. The animal responds to that feature more than to the whole thing.
6. (fill) The short time early in life when imprinting can happen is called the ____ period.  
   **Answer:** ['sensitive', 'critical']. *Why:* It is called the sensitive period. Some books call it the critical period. For a mallard duckling this period is about the first day after hatching.
7. (fill) A behaviour that an animal can do from birth, without being taught, is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['innate', 'inborn']. *Why:* Innate and inborn mean the same thing: present from birth or hatching, with no teaching.
8. (truefalse) A fixed action pattern has to be practised many times before it works.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A fixed action pattern works the first time. A squirrel raised with no soil and no nuts still made burying movements.
9. (truefalse) Humans have a reflex that is also found in seals and ducks.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The diving reflex slows the heart of a human, a seal and a duck. The trigger is cold water on the face.
10. (match) Match each reflex to its stimulus.  
   **Answer:** Blink → Something rushing at the eye; Mouth-watering → Sour juice on the tongue; Slower heartbeat → Cold water on the face, with the breath held; Hand pulls back → Skin touching something hot. *Why:* Each reflex has a stimulus of its own. A stimulus for one reflex does not set off another.
11. (sort) Sort these. Is each one a reflex, or a fixed action pattern?  
   **Answer:** a blink → Reflex; the knee kick → Reflex; the hand pulling back from heat → Reflex; a goose rolling an egg → Fixed action pattern; a squirrel burying a nut → Fixed action pattern; a swallow → Fixed action pattern. *Why:* A reflex is one quick response of one body part. A fixed action pattern is a longer set of movements in a set order.
12. (order) Put these steps in order, to show how the blink reflex works.  
   **Answer:** ['Something rushes towards the eye', 'Sensors in the eye pick it up', 'A message runs along a nerve to the lowest part of the brain', 'A message runs back to the eyelid muscles', 'The eyelid shuts']. *Why:* A reflex starts with a stimulus and a sensor. The message goes in along one nerve and out along another. A muscle pulls last.
13. (write) In the Cold Face Test, a cold face slowed the heart. A cold hand did not. What does that show?  
   **Answer:** It shows that the trigger for the diving reflex is cold water on the face. Cold on other parts of the body does not set it off.. *Why:* A strong answer says that only one thing differed between the two tests, the body part in the water. So that body part is what mattered.
14. (write) A gosling's rule is 'follow the first big moving thing'. Explain in two sentences why this rule works in nature.  
   **Answer:** In nature the first big moving thing a gosling sees is her mother. The mother is sitting on the nest when the eggs hatch. So the simple rule nearly always leads her to the right bird.. *Why:* A strong answer says who is beside the nest at hatching. It also says that the rule only fails when a person interferes.
15. (draw) Draw the two faces of one pair from the Big Eyes Test. Circle the one thing that differs.  
   **Answer:** Two round faces that are the same except for one feature: either small eyes and big eyes, or eyes high and eyes low. The differing feature is circled.. *Why:* A strong drawing keeps the head, nose and mouth the same on both faces. Only then can the votes be put down to the one feature that changed.
### Triggers and windows (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A hen ignores a chick she can see but cannot hear. What is the trigger for her rescue?  
   Options: The chick's colour / The chick's size / The chick's cheeping / The glass cover  
   **Answer:** The chick's cheeping. *Why:* She runs to a cheeping chick even when she cannot see them. So the sound is the trigger, more than the sight.
2. (mcq) Why can a dummy set off an inborn behaviour?  
   Options: It smells of food / It is bigger than the animal / Animals like new things / It has the one feature that matters  
   **Answer:** It has the one feature that matters. *Why:* The animal responds to the sign stimulus. Any dummy that carries that feature, such as a red spot, can set off the behaviour.
3. (mcq) A duckling first sees a moving thing at 40 hours old. What will most likely happen?  
   Options: She will imprint at once / She will imprint twice as strongly / She will not imprint on it / She will forget how to walk  
   **Answer:** She will not imprint on it. *Why:* The sensitive period for a mallard duckling is nearly over by 30 hours. At 40 hours she is more likely to move away from a new thing.
4. (mcq) Why is a reflex faster than an action you plan?  
   Options: Reflex nerves are outside the body / Planned actions use no nerves / The message skips the brain's planning parts / Reflexes use bigger muscles  
   **Answer:** The message skips the brain's planning parts. *Why:* In a reflex the message goes in to the spinal cord or lower brain. Then it goes straight back out to a muscle. A planned action takes a longer path.
5. (truefalse) A cold hand sets off the diving reflex as well as a cold face does.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The sensors that set off the diving reflex are in the skin of the face, around the eyes and nose.
6. (truefalse) A koel raised by crows grows up cawing like a crow.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Every koel is raised by crows or other foster birds, and every koel still calls like a koel. The call is innate.
7. (sort) Sort these. Is each one innate, or learned?  
   **Answer:** a duckling's urge to follow → Innate; a cuckoo chick pushing eggs out → Innate; a newborn baby turning to a touch on the cheek → Innate; what a gosling's parent looks like → Learned; riding a bicycle → Learned; reading Tamil or Hindi → Learned. *Why:* The urge to follow is innate, but whom to follow is learned by imprinting. Imprinting is learning, though it happens only once and very early.
8. (odd) Three of these are supernormal stimuli. Which one is the odd one out?  
   Options: A giant plaster egg / A red stick with white bands / A cartoon face with huge eyes / A gull parent's real beak  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* The giant egg, the striped stick and the cartoon face are made-up triggers that are stronger than the real thing. A real beak is the normal stimulus.
9. (count) A seal's heart beats 10 times a minute on a dive. How many beats is that in a 12-minute dive?  
   **Answer:** 120. *Why:* 10 beats each minute for 12 minutes is 10 times 12, which is 120 beats. At 100 beats a minute it would have been 1,200.
10. (order) Put the steps of Konrad Lorenz's goose test in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Split one clutch of goose eggs into two groups', 'The goose hatches one group, and Lorenz hatches the other', 'Each gosling sees a different first moving thing', 'Mix all the goslings together', 'Each gosling goes to the one she saw first']. *Why:* The eggs came from one clutch, so the goslings were alike. Only the first thing they saw differed. That makes it a fair test of imprinting.
11. (write) A toy maker wants a soft toy that people want to hug. Which features should she change, and why?  
   **Answer:** She should give the toy a big head, a high forehead, big eyes set low and round cheeks. These are baby features, and people find them cute and want to care for them.. *Why:* A strong answer names at least two baby features. It links them to the caring response, or to the votes in the Big Eyes Test.
12. (write) An oystercatcher chooses a giant egg that she cannot cover. Explain why her rule works in nature but fails here.  
   **Answer:** Her rule is to sit on the biggest egg. In nature no egg is too big, so the rule never goes wrong. A giant egg made by a scientist is bigger than any real egg. Her rule has no upper limit, so she keeps choosing the bigger egg.. *Why:* A strong answer states the bird's rule, and says that nature never offers an egg that big.
13. (write) A swallow cannot be stopped halfway. Why might that be useful?  
   **Answer:** Food or water that stopped halfway could slip into the windpipe and choke the animal. A swallow that always finishes moves it safely past.. *Why:* A strong answer names a danger of stopping in the middle. Any sensible danger is accepted.
14. (perspective) A wetland centre plans to raise crane chicks by hand and release them. People disagree about how.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. One reason is that imprinting happens in the first days and is very hard to undo. So a chick handled by visitors may never join wild cranes. A fair answer to the tourism officer is that visitors could watch through a window.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses the fact that imprinting is early and lasting. It also takes the need for money and the farmer's worry seriously.
15. (label) Label the four parts of a reflex on this drawing of a hand touching a hot tumbler.  
   **Answer:** ['Sensor in the skin: at the fingertip touching the tumbler', 'Nerve carrying the message in: from the fingertip up the arm', 'Spinal cord: inside the backbone', 'Nerve carrying the message out to the muscle: from the spinal cord down to the arm muscle']. *Why:* The message goes in along one nerve and out along another. It turns round in the spinal cord, without waiting for the planning parts of the brain.
### Testing what is inborn (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A stickleback attacks a red wax lump and leaves a lifelike grey model alone. What does this show?  
   Options: Wax smells like a fish / He is hungry / A red underside is the trigger / Grey fish are his friends  
   **Answer:** A red underside is the trigger. *Why:* The lump had only one fish-like feature, the red underside, and the lump was attacked. The lifelike model lacked red and was left alone.
2. (mcq) Duck embryos who hear no calls in the egg later fail to pick their mother's call. What does this suggest?  
   Options: Hearing calls inside the egg matters / Ducklings are born deaf / Mother ducks teach each call / Calls are learned after a year  
   **Answer:** Hearing calls inside the egg matters. *Why:* Gilbert Gottlieb's tests showed that even an instinct can need an experience. Here the experience is the duckling's own voice, heard inside the egg.
3. (mcq) In the Cold Face Test, why is the pulse counted three times for each test?  
   Options: The heart needs to warm up / One count can be wrong by chance / The water gets colder each time / Three counts take less time  
   **Answer:** One count can be wrong by chance. *Why:* A single count can be off by a beat or two. Three counts show whether the result repeats, and the middle count is a fair one to use.
4. (mcq) Mice raised in cages still dig the burrow shape of their own kind. What does this show?  
   Options: Cages are like burrows / The mice copied other mice / Digging has to be taught / Burrow shape is inherited  
   **Answer:** Burrow shape is inherited. *Why:* The mice had never seen a burrow or another mouse digging. So the shape did not come from copying or teaching.
5. (truefalse) A supernormal stimulus works because the animal's rule has no upper limit.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A rule such as 'sit on the biggest egg' never needed a limit. Nature never offers an egg that is too big.
6. (truefalse) Imprinting is the same as learning by practice, only faster.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Imprinting needs no practice and no reward. It happens only in a short sensitive period, and it is very hard to undo.
7. (match) Match each test to what it shows.  
   **Answer:** Take the egg away halfway through a roll → A fixed pattern runs to the end; Offer a bird a giant egg → A stronger trigger can beat the real one; Hatch goslings away from their mother → What a parent looks like is learned; Raise a squirrel with no soil or nuts → Burying movements are innate. *Why:* Each test removes or changes one thing and watches what the animal still does. That is how inborn and learned parts are told apart.
8. (count) A resting pulse is 88 beats a minute. It slows by one quarter. What is the new pulse?  
   **Answer:** 66. *Why:* One quarter of 88 is 22. 88 minus 22 is 66 beats a minute.
9. (fill) A made-up trigger that works better than the real thing is called a ____ stimulus.  
   **Answer:** ['supernormal']. *Why:* Supernormal means 'beyond normal'. A giant egg and a cartoon face with huge eyes are supernormal stimuli.
10. (write) Does a duckling follow colour or movement? Describe the dummies you would use to find out.  
   **Answer:** I would show one group a red ball that moves and another group the same red ball kept still. Then I would show a grey ball that moves. If ducklings follow both moving balls and not the still one, movement matters and colour does not.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one feature at a time and keeps the rest the same. It also says what result would point to each answer.
11. (write) Give one reason why finding baby faces cute might be inborn. Give one reason why this might be learned.  
   **Answer:** Inborn: children as young as three, and people in many countries, respond to baby features. Learned: children grow up with big-eyed toys and cartoons, so they may learn what counts as cute.. *Why:* A strong answer gives evidence on both sides, and does not claim that the question is settled.
12. (write) A reflex cannot be switched off by deciding. Name one way this helps you and one way this causes trouble.  
   **Answer:** It helps because my hand leaves a hot tumbler before I have time to think. It could be a problem when a doctor needs me to keep my eye open and I blink anyway.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a real case of each. Any sensible examples are accepted.
13. (write) You have the diving reflex, though you may never dive for food. Explain how you came to have it.  
   **Answer:** I inherited it from my parents through DNA, as they did from theirs. Seals, ducks and humans all have it, so scientists think it comes from ancestors that all of us share.. *Why:* A strong answer says the reflex is inherited and not learned. It uses the fact that many kinds of animals share this reflex.
14. (perspective) On her first day, a duckling turns towards the call of her own kind of mother. Scientists explain this differently.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that Gottlieb's ducklings failed to pick the call when they heard nothing in the egg. A useful test is to change what the embryo hears and see which call the duckling later prefers.. *Why:* Working scientists hold each of these views. A strong answer uses the egg test, and suggests changing one thing before hatching.
15. (draw) Draw a dummy that a gull chick would peck at more than a real beak. Label what you made stronger.  
   **Answer:** A long thin shape that is redder than a real beak, with strong bands or a large red patch. Labels point to the stronger redness, the thinness or the contrast.. *Why:* A chick responds to a shape that is red, long, thin and high in contrast. A strong drawing makes at least one of these stronger than in a real beak.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is a reflex?  
   Options: Tying a shoelace / Blinking when something flies at your eye / Singing a song / Choosing a ripe mango  
   **Answer:** Blinking when something flies at your eye. *Why:* A blink at something rushing towards the eye is fast, automatic and unlearned. The other three are learned or chosen.
2. (mcq) What sets off a reflex or a fixed action pattern?  
   Options: A stimulus / A reward / A lesson from a parent / A long rest  
   **Answer:** A stimulus. *Why:* Each reflex and each fixed pattern waits for a stimulus of its own, also called a trigger.
3. (mcq) Which sentence is true of a fixed action pattern?  
   Options: It is different every time / It has to be taught / Once started, it usually runs to the end / Only birds have them  
   **Answer:** Once started, it usually runs to the end. *Why:* A goose finishes the rolling movement even when the egg has gone. Humans have fixed patterns too, such as swallowing.
4. (mcq) A cuckoo chick pushes eggs out of the nest a day after hatching. What kind of behaviour is this?  
   Options: A habit copied from others / An instinct / A trick taught by the foster mother / A game  
   **Answer:** An instinct. *Why:* The chick is blind and has never seen this done, so the behaviour cannot be copied or taught. It is an instinct.
5. (mcq) A gull chick pecks more at a model beak that has a red spot. What is the red spot?  
   Options: A sign stimulus / A reward / A reflex / An imprint  
   **Answer:** A sign stimulus. *Why:* The red spot is the one feature that sets off pecking. That makes it the sign stimulus.
6. (mcq) With the breath held and the face in cold water, a person's heart usually:  
   Options: beats faster / stops for a minute / beats more slowly / beats the same as before  
   **Answer:** beats more slowly. *Why:* This is the diving reflex. In humans the heart usually slows by a tenth to a quarter.
7. (mcq) How does the diving reflex help a seal?  
   Options: Her oxygen lasts longer under water / She swims faster / She keeps warmer / She smells fish better  
   **Answer:** Her oxygen lasts longer under water. *Why:* A slow heart and narrow blood vessels in the limbs save oxygen for the brain and heart. So one breath lasts longer.
8. (mcq) Goslings hatched by Konrad Lorenz followed him and not adult geese. Why?  
   Options: He gave them the best food / He looked like a goose / He was the first moving thing they saw / He trained them with rewards  
   **Answer:** He was the first moving thing they saw. *Why:* The goslings imprinted on the first large moving thing near them, and that was Lorenz. No rewards or training were needed.
9. (mcq) When can a mallard duckling imprint?  
   Options: At any age / Only as an adult / Only in winter / Only in a short time after hatching  
   **Answer:** Only in a short time after hatching. *Why:* Imprinting has a sensitive period. For mallard ducklings this period peaks at 13 to 16 hours and is nearly over by 30 hours.
10. (mcq) An oystercatcher chooses a giant egg over her own. What is the giant egg?  
   Options: A fixed action pattern / A reflex / A supernormal stimulus / An instinct  
   **Answer:** A supernormal stimulus. *Why:* The giant egg is a made-up trigger that works better than the real one. That is a supernormal stimulus.
11. (truefalse) An animal who has strong instincts can also learn.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A bird builds her first nest with inborn movements and builds better nests with practice. Instinct gives a start, and learning adds to it.
12. (mcq) A duckling hatches with the urge to follow. What must she still learn?  
   Options: How to walk / How to breathe / How to swallow / What her parent looks like  
   **Answer:** What her parent looks like. *Why:* The urge and the movements are innate. Which bird to follow is learned by imprinting in the first day.

## Applications
- Keepers raise whooping cranes and California condors for release into the wild. They feed the chicks with puppets and hide their own faces, so that the chicks do not imprint on humans.
- Doctors check a newborn baby's reflexes, such as sucking, grasping and turning to a touch on the cheek. The reflexes show that the baby's nerves are working.
- A doctor taps below the kneecap, or shines a light in the eye, to test a person's reflexes.
- Eye doctors treat a weak eye in a young child by covering the strong eye for some hours a day. This works best early, because seeing has a sensitive period too.
- Toy makers and cartoon artists give their characters big heads and big eyes, because people find baby features cute.
- A shepherd sometimes needs a ewe to accept an orphan lamb. The shepherd brings them together soon after the ewe has given birth. She is still learning her own lamb's smell then.

## Spotted around us
- A line of ducklings hurrying after a mother duck beside a pond or a paddy field.
- Chicks following a hen across a yard. When she stops, they stop.
- A koel calling 'ku-oo' from a neem or mango tree. That koel was raised by foster parents, most often crows.
- A newborn calf or kid getting up on shaky legs and pushing their nose under the mother to find milk.
- People on a morning bus or metro yawning one after another.
- Geese on a lake or in a park, following a person or their mother in a line. Lorenz studied how baby geese follow the first moving thing they see.

## Open questions
- **How does DNA build the wiring for a whole behaviour, such as digging a burrow with an escape tunnel?** Scientists have linked a few regions of DNA to parts of some behaviours. Nobody can yet follow every step from a gene to a movement.
- **What is a duckling's experience while she imprints?** Scientists can see which part of the brain changes, and what the duckling does afterwards. Nobody has a way to find out what it is like for her.
- **Why do yawns spread from one person to another?** About half of adults yawn after seeing yawns. Some scientists link this to feeling close to others, and some link it to staying alert together. The evidence does not settle it.
- **Is the human response to baby faces present from birth?** Children of three already respond to baby features. Testing younger babies is hard, and children see big-eyed toys from their first months.
- **What closes a sensitive period, and can one be opened again?** Some changes in the brain that end a sensitive period are known. Whether a closed period can safely be reopened, in birds or in people, is still being studied.

## Connected maths
- In the Cold Face Test, a pulse count was 22 beats in 15 seconds at rest. It was 18 beats in 15 seconds with the face in cold water. How many beats a minute is each count? By how many beats a minute did the heart slow? **88 beats a minute at rest and 72 beats a minute with the face in cold water. The heart slowed by 16 beats a minute.**. One minute is four lots of 15 seconds. 22 times 4 is 88. 18 times 4 is 72. 88 minus 72 is 16. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- Three pulse counts for one test in the Cold Face Test were 18, 17 and 24. One of them is probably a miscount. Which count best describes the test: the middle one, or the biggest one? Why? **The middle count, 18. The count of 24 is far from the other two and is probably a mistake.**. In order, the counts are 17, 18 and 24. Two of the three agree closely. The middle count stays sensible even when one count is far off. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L4`)
- A seal's heart beats about 100 times a minute at the surface. On a dive it beats about 10 times a minute. How many beats does her heart make in a 15-minute dive? How many would it have made at the surface rate? **150 beats during the dive. At the surface rate it would have been 1,500 beats, which is 1,350 more.**. 10 times 15 is 150. 100 times 15 is 1,500. 1,500 minus 150 is 1,350. Every beat uses oxygen, so far fewer beats means the oxygen lasts longer. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- In the Big Eyes Test, 8 voters chose between two faces. If eye size made no difference, about how many votes would the big-eyed face get? Is 7 out of 8 strong evidence that eye size matters? Is 5 out of 8? **About 4 votes if eye size made no difference. 7 out of 8 is strong evidence. 5 out of 8 is weak evidence.**. With two faces and no real preference, each face gets about half of the votes. Half of 8 is 4. A score of 7 is far from 4. A score of 5 is only one vote away from 4, which can easily happen by chance. (queues `math.probability.likely-and-unlikely.L4`)
- On the ducklings' graph, 3 ducklings out of 10 imprinted at 1 to 4 hours. At 13 to 16 hours, 9 out of 10 imprinted. At 29 to 32 hours, none did. A duckling first meets a moving thing at 45 hours. Predict what happens, and say how the pattern tells you. **She will almost certainly not imprint on it.**. The numbers rise to a peak at 13 to 16 hours. Then they fall to 0 by 29 to 32 hours. Past that age the line stays at 0, and 45 hours is well past it. (queues `math.data.line-graphs.L4`)
- In the Yawn Spread, 3 of 6 volunteers yawned after seeing yawns. On the day of smiles, 1 of the same 6 yawned. Write each result as a fraction. Which is bigger, and what does the difference suggest? **Three sixths, which is one half, after yawns. One sixth after smiles. One half is bigger, which suggests that seeing a yawn sets off yawns.**. 3 out of 6 is the same as 1 out of 2. 1 out of 6 is smaller than 3 out of 6. With only 6 volunteers this is a hint, and more volunteers would make it stronger. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a reflex? → A fast, automatic response of the body to one stimulus, which nobody has to learn.
- Name the stimulus and the response when a sock ball flies at your face. → The stimulus is something rushing at the eyes. The response is a blink.
- What does your heart do when your face is in cold water and you hold your breath? → It beats more slowly. This is the diving reflex.
- What is a fixed action pattern? → A set of inborn movements in a set order, which usually runs to the end once started.
- What is a sign stimulus? Give one example. → The one feature that sets off an inborn behaviour, such as the red spot on a gull's beak.
- What is imprinting? → Fast learning in a short time early in life, such as a gosling learning whom to follow.
- What is a sensitive period? → The short time when a kind of learning, such as imprinting, can happen.
- What is a supernormal stimulus? Give one example. → A made-up trigger that works better than the real one. A giant egg and a cartoon face with huge eyes are examples.
