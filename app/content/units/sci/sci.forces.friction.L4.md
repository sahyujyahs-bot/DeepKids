# Friction: how hard is the grip, and what decides it?
`sci.forces.friction.L4`

**Summary.** Load a box with more weight and it needs a harder pull to move. Turn the same box onto its narrow side, so less of it touches the floor, and the pull barely changes. Friction grows with how hard two things press together, not with how much of them you can see. That is because two surfaces really touch only at tiny spots, and pressing harder makes more spots touch. Three turns of thread round a pencil can hold a bottle of water, because each turn makes the grip several times stronger. Scientists still cannot work out friction from atoms alone.

**Intro.** *Try this. Lay your hand flat on a table and press down gently. Slide your hand along. Now press down hard and slide again. When was it harder to slide, and why do you think so?* At a harbour, one person can stop a huge boat with a rope and an iron post. No arm is that strong. In this unit you will find out what the rope is using. First you will test a simpler question. If you put more weight in a box, how much harder is it to pull? And does it matter which side of the box faces the floor? The answers surprise most people, and they explain the rope too.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say that friction grows when two surfaces press together harder, and that it also depends on what the two surfaces are made of.
1. I can run a fair test with a rubber band gauge, repeat each measurement, and show the results in a table and a graph.
2. I can explain why friction on a hard, flat surface hardly changes with the size of the face, using the idea of tiny real contact spots.
3. I can tell starting friction from sliding friction, and explain why things can stick and slip in jerks.
4. I can explain how rollers, wheels, ball bearings and oil lower friction, and why thick liquids drag.
5. I can explain how turns of a rope round a post make the grip several times stronger with every turn.
6. I can say that the work done against friction turns into heat, and work it out as friction force times distance.
7. I can say what scientists agree about friction, and what they still do not know.

## Terms
- **Friction**: The grip between two surfaces that touch. It works against sliding. *e.g.* A chappal does not slip on a dry road because of friction.
- **Pressing force**: How hard two surfaces are pushed together. Scientists also call it the normal force. *e.g.* A box with a heavy load presses on the floor harder than an empty box.
- **Coefficient of friction**: A number that says how grippy two surfaces are together. It is the friction force divided by the pressing force. It has no unit. *e.g.* Rubber on a dry road is about 0.8. Steel on ice is about 0.03.
- **Proportional**: Two things are proportional when one doubles whenever the other doubles. *e.g.* If a box with 1 kilogram needs 3 newtons of pull, a box with 2 kilograms needs about 6 newtons.
- **Bump (asperity)**: A tiny peak on a surface. Every surface has bumps, even one that feels smooth. *e.g.* Under a microscope, a polished steel spoon looks like a mountain range.
- **Apparent area**: The area of a surface that you can see and measure with a ruler. *e.g.* The big face of a shoebox has a larger apparent area than its end.
- **Real contact area**: The tiny total area where two surfaces really touch, at the tips of their bumps. It is much smaller than the apparent area. *e.g.* A steel box on a steel table touches at only a tiny fraction of the face you see.
- **Starting friction**: The friction that holds a thing still until you push hard enough to start it moving. Scientists call it static friction. *e.g.* A heavy almirah stays still until you push hard, and then it moves.
- **Stick-slip**: Motion in jerks. A thing sticks, then slips, then sticks again, over and over. *e.g.* A squeaking chalk, a squeaking door hinge and a violin bow all move by stick-slip.
- **Rolling friction**: The friction on a wheel or ball that rolls without sliding. It is usually much smaller than sliding friction. *e.g.* A suitcase on wheels is much easier to pull than one dragged on the floor.
- **Ball bearing**: A ring of small balls that lets two parts turn past each other by rolling instead of sliding. *e.g.* A cycle wheel hub and a ceiling fan both have ball bearings.
- **Viscosity**: How thick and slow a liquid is, because of the friction between its own parts. Honey has high viscosity and water has low viscosity. *e.g.* Honey pours slowly and water pours fast.
- **Bollard**: A short, strong post on a quay or pier that a rope is wound round to hold a boat. *e.g.* At Fort Kochi, boats are tied to iron bollards on the quay.
- **Adhesion**: The sticking of two different things together by the pull between their atoms. *e.g.* A sticker sticks to a wall by adhesion.

## Explanations
### What decides how big friction is · *defined*
**Definition:** On a flat surface, the friction force is about equal to mu times the pressing force, where mu depends on the two surfaces.

On a flat floor, friction depends on two things. The first is how hard the two surfaces press together. The second is what the two surfaces are made of. Double the pressing force and the friction roughly doubles. Scientists write this as friction = mu times the pressing force. Mu is the coefficient of friction, a number that belongs to a pair of surfaces. A larger mu means a stronger grip. The rule is a good guide for hard, dry things, but it is not exact.
- e.g. A box with 2 kilograms in it needs about twice the pull of the same box with 1 kilogram in it.
- e.g. Rubber on a dry road has a mu of about 0.8. Steel on ice has a mu of about 0.03, which is about 27 times less grip.
- e.g. Pressing a finger hard on a pencil makes it harder to pull the pencil away.
### Why a wide face and a narrow face grip about the same · *defined*
**Definition:** For hard, dry surfaces, friction is almost the same whatever the size of the face that touches. This is called Amontons's second law.

This rule surprises almost everyone. Take a box and lay it on its big face. Then stand it on its narrow side. The weight is the same, and the pull needed to start it is about the same. Many people expect the bigger face to grip more, because more of the box touches the floor. But the weight is the same, and the weight is what presses. A wider face spreads the same weight over more bumps, and each bump is pressed less. A narrower face puts the same weight on fewer bumps, and each bump is pressed more. The total grip comes out about equal. The rule works for hard, dry surfaces. It fails for soft things such as rubber, and for things that stick, like tape.
- e.g. A brick pulled on its big face or on its narrow end needs about the same pull.
- e.g. A wide bicycle tyre and a thin one of the same bicycle brake in nearly the same distance on a dry, clean road. Racing tyres are special, because the rubber is soft and sticky.
- e.g. A tractor on a muddy field needs wide, patterned wheels for a different reason. Mud is soft, and the wide wheel stops the wheel sinking.
### Why surfaces touch only at tiny spots · *defined*
**Definition:** Real contact area is the small total area where the bumps of two surfaces touch. It grows when the surfaces press together harder.

Under a microscope, even a polished surface is a mountain range. When you put two surfaces together, the peaks of one rest on the peaks of the other. They do not lie flat on each other like two sheets of paper. The area where they truly touch, called the real contact area, is a tiny fraction of the area you can see. For hard metals it is often less than one thousandth. Now press harder. The peaks that touch are squashed a little, and more peaks come into contact. Double the press and about double the real contact area. Friction comes from the grip at the touching spots. So doubling the pressing force doubles the real contact, and doubles the grip.
- e.g. Two steel plates pressed together carry electricity only through a few tiny spots, so the current is smaller than a full contact would allow.
- e.g. Pressing your thumb harder on a table makes more of your skin touch the table.
- e.g. Two sheets of paper on a desk touch at the high points of the paper fibres, with gaps everywhere else.
### Where does the grip really come from? · *debated*
Everyone agrees that friction depends on the load and the materials. They do not fully agree on what happens at the touching spots. Three ideas have been put forward over three hundred years, and each seems to be partly right.
- e.g. Sandpaper clearly grips by catching on bumps.
- e.g. Two clean, very smooth metal plates in a vacuum can weld themselves together, which bumps alone cannot explain.
- e.g. A gecko walks up glass without any bumps for catching.
- **Guillaume Amontons, a French scientist, in 1699, and many after him:** Surfaces grip because their bumps catch on each other. To slide, one surface must lift up and over the bumps of the other.
- **Frank Bowden and David Tabor, at the University of Cambridge, in the 1940s and 1950s:** At the touching spots, the surfaces stick to each other, almost as if they were welded. Sliding means tearing these tiny joins apart again and again. Friction is the force needed to break them.
- **Jacqueline Krim, a physicist in the United States, and other scientists who study friction between single layers of atoms, from the 1980s:** At the level of atoms, friction depends on how the atoms of the two surfaces line up, how they shake and how much heat they carry away. The old rules do not always hold at this tiny size.
- **Many scientists today:** All three ideas matter. Which one matters most depends on the materials and on the size we look at. No single theory yet predicts the friction of any pair of materials from atoms upward.
### Starting friction, sliding friction and stick-slip · *defined*
**Definition:** Starting friction holds a thing still up to a limit. Sliding friction is usually a little smaller. Stick-slip is the jerky movement that results.

Push a heavy box gently. It does not move, because friction pushes back just as hard as you push. Push a little harder and friction pushes back harder too. This goes on up to a limit. Past the limit, the box starts to move. The friction of a moving box is usually a little smaller than the limit. So the box jumps forward a little when it starts. Now imagine a thing being pulled slowly by a spring. It sticks, the spring stretches, the pull passes the limit, the thing slips forward, the spring relaxes and the thing sticks again. This repeats many times a second or just once in a while. It is called stick-slip.
- e.g. A chalk squeaks on a blackboard because it sticks and slips again and again.
- e.g. A violin or sarangi bow makes a string vibrate by sticking to the string and slipping off it, thousands of times each second.
- e.g. A rusty door hinge squeaks for the same reason.
### Rolling and ball bearings · *defined*
**Definition:** Rolling friction is the friction on a wheel or ball that rolls instead of sliding. It is usually much smaller than sliding friction.

A wheel does not slide along the ground. It rolls. The patch of the wheel that touches the ground is for a moment still, so it does not rub. Because of this, rolling friction is usually many times smaller than sliding friction. This is why a wheeled suitcase is easy to pull and a dragged one is hard. A ball bearing puts rolling balls between two parts that turn, so even the axle can roll instead of slide. Rolling friction is not zero. Wheels and the road squash a little as they roll, and this takes energy.
- e.g. Pencils laid under a heavy book let you push the book with one finger.
- e.g. Ball bearings sit inside bicycle hubs, ceiling fans and skateboard wheels.
- e.g. A railway wheel on a steel rail has very low rolling friction, so a train can pull a very heavy load.
### Friction inside liquids · *defined*
**Definition:** Fluid friction is the friction inside a liquid or gas and on things that move through it. Viscosity is how large it is.

A liquid has friction too. Its layers rub against each other, and against anything that moves through it. This is called fluid friction. How strong it is depends on the liquid. The word for how thick and slow a liquid is, is viscosity. Honey is thousands of times more viscous than water. A ball falls through honey slowly and through water quickly. Warm honey flows more easily than cold honey, because heat makes the molecules move faster and slip past each other more easily. Engine oil is made to thin out in a controlled way as the engine warms, so it keeps working from a cold morning to a hot road.
- e.g. Honey, ghee and coconut oil all flow faster after they are warmed.
- e.g. A boat feels the friction of water as it moves, and a cyclist feels the friction of air.
- e.g. Engine oil and the oil in a bicycle chain keep metal parts from rubbing directly.
### The rope rule: why turns multiply the grip · *defined*
**Definition:** When a rope is wound round a post, each extra turn multiplies the load that a light pull can hold by the same number.

A rope wound round a post holds a heavy load with a light pull. Every part of the rope that touches the post adds a little friction. The more rope touches the post, the more friction there is. Here is the surprising part. Each extra turn does not add the same amount. It multiplies the grip by the same number. If the first turn makes the hold six times easier, the second turn makes it six times easier again, so two turns make it thirty-six times easier. The number depends on the rope and the post. Leonhard Euler worked out this rule in the 1700s. Sailors and boatmen had been using it for thousands of years before anyone wrote it down.
- e.g. A boat is held on a bollard with three or four turns of rope and a light hold on the tail.
- e.g. A knot in a shoelace holds because each crossing of the lace is a turn with friction.
- e.g. Rock climbers wind a rope through a device so that a light hold can catch a fall.
### Grip in living bodies · *debated*
Living beings are experts in friction. A gecko's toes carry millions of tiny hairs. Each hair is split into even tinier ends that get so close to a surface that the pull between atoms holds them on. A tree frog has toe pads that are wet and patterned, and they grip wet leaves. Your own fingertips have ridges called fingerprints. Skin with a little moisture grips best. Scientists still disagree about why fingers wrinkle in water. Some think the wrinkles are channels that help grip wet things, like the tread on a tyre. Other experiments found little or no help.
- e.g. A gecko can walk across a glass ceiling.
- e.g. A tree frog's toes grip a wet leaf in the rain.
- e.g. A mountain climber's chalk dries the hands, and a surgeon's glove keeps a grip.
- **Mark Changizi and colleagues, 2011:** Wet fingers wrinkle into channels, like the tread of a tyre, so that water can drain away and the fingers grip better in the rain.
- **Kyriacos Kareklas, Daniel Nettle and Tom Smulders, 2013:** In a test, people with wrinkled fingers picked up wet objects faster than people with smooth fingers. They found no difference with dry objects.
- **Other researchers who repeated the test:** Some other experiments found no clear help from wrinkles, so scientists are still arguing about it.
### Friction costs energy · *defined*
**Definition:** The work done against friction is the friction force times the distance moved. It ends up as heat.

To drag a box across a floor, you must keep pushing, and you do work. Scientists measure the work as the pushing force times the distance. This work does not disappear. It turns into heat, as the surfaces and the air warm up a little. If the friction force is 5 newtons and you drag the box 10 metres, you do 5 times 10, which is 50 joules of work, and all 50 joules end up as heat. Wheels and oil lower the friction force, so the same journey costs less energy. All over the world, a large share of the fuel used by machines goes into overcoming friction.
- e.g. A cyclist's legs do work against friction in the chain, the tyres and the air.
- e.g. Brake pads get hot, because the movement of the vehicle turns into heat in the brakes.
- e.g. Rubbing your hands warms them, because your push does work against friction.

## Mostly agreed
- On a flat, dry surface, friction is roughly proportional to how hard the two surfaces press together. *How we know:* People have pulled loaded blocks along tables with spring scales since the 1600s. The pull needed grows close to evenly with the load for hard, dry surfaces.
- On hard, dry surfaces, friction hardly changes with the size of the face that touches. *How we know:* Amontons found this in 1699 and Coulomb confirmed it in 1785. It is easy to test with a brick on its big face and its narrow end.
- Two surfaces touch only at the tips of their bumps, a tiny part of the area you can see. *How we know:* Bowden and Tabor measured how well electricity passed between pressed metal surfaces. It passed through only tiny spots.
- Rolling friction is usually much smaller than sliding friction. *How we know:* Loads can be moved on rollers or wheels with a fraction of the force needed to drag them. Engineers have measured the difference for centuries.
- The work done against friction turns into heat. *How we know:* Rumford (1798) and Joule (1840s) showed that rubbing and stirring make heat in exact amounts.

## How we found out
**Frank Bowden and David Tabor, physicists at the University of Cambridge, The 1930s to 1950.** For hundreds of years, many people believed that friction came from bumps locking together. Guillaume Amontons, a French scientist, suggested in 1699 that surfaces grip because their bumps catch on each other, and many people agreed for over two hundred years. It followed that very smooth surfaces should always slide easily. But smooth, clean metal surfaces were found to stick, not slide. In the 1930s and 1940s, Frank Bowden and David Tabor, working at the University of Cambridge, tried a new test. They pressed two metal surfaces together and measured how well electricity passed between them. If the surfaces touched all over, a lot of electricity would pass. Only a little did. The surfaces were touching at tiny spots, and at these spots the metal was sticking, as if it were welded. Their book, The Friction and Lubrication of Solids, came out in 1950.  
*What people believed before:* Friction is only the catching of rough bumps, and smoother always means more slippery. Bowden and Tabor found that electricity passed through only tiny spots, so the surfaces touched at only a few places. At those places the metals stuck together, and smooth, clean surfaces could stick very hard.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Two flat things pressed together touch each other all over.** (Almost everyone, because the surfaces look and feel smooth.; *rejected*)  
  For: A mirror, a glass tumbler and a polished tile look perfectly flat and smooth.  
  Against: Under a microscope, every surface is full of peaks. Electricity passes between two pressed metal plates through only tiny spots. The real touching area is much smaller than it looks.
- **A bigger face always has more friction than a smaller face.** (Many students, and many adults.; *rejected*)  
  For: More of the box is touching the floor, so it seems it should grip more.  
  Against: For hard, dry things, the same weight spreads over more bumps, each pressed less. The total grip is nearly the same. Soft rubber and sticky tape are exceptions.
- **Friction is a fixed number that is always at its maximum.** (Many students.; *rejected*)  
  For: Friction always seems to be 'in the way'.  
  Against: When you push a heavy cupboard gently and it does not move, friction pushes back exactly as hard as you push. It rises as you push, up to a limit. After the limit, the cupboard moves.
- **To hold a heavy boat, you need a strong pull.** (Anyone who has not used a rope round a post.; *rejected*)  
  For: The boat is heavy, so it feels as if it needs a big force to hold it.  
  Against: A rope round a post multiplies the grip with every turn. A child can hold a boat with a light pull after a few turns.
- **Wrinkled fingers are made to give a better grip in the rain.** (Mark Changizi, a scientist, and others, from 2011.; *debated*)  
  For: The wrinkles look like tyre tread, and one experiment found that wrinkled fingers pick up wet things faster.  
  Against: Some other experiments found no clear help. Scientists do not agree.

## Roots
We have pulled boxes, wrapped thread round pencils and watched things stick and slip. Now we ask what the grip of friction tells us about touching, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** Two surfaces that look like they touch all over really touch at only a few tiny spots. Press harder, and more spots touch. The grip of friction is the sum of all those tiny meetings. So touching is not a yes or no, it is a how much.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say two things 'touch' as if the whole face meets the whole face. Every surface is a mountain range, and only the peaks meet. 'Touching' has been a label for 'looks like it touches'.  
*Evidence:* In the Press Harder test, doubling the load roughly doubled the pull, and a wide face gripped about the same as a narrow face. Both fit a grip made at tiny touching spots that grow with the press, and do not fit a grip made by the whole face.

- *who am i:* You hold a cup, grip a rope and stand on a slope because your skin meets the world at countless tiny spots, and each spot holds on a little.
- *what is life:* A gecko's toes, a frog's wet pads and your own fingertips are all built to make more tiny meetings with a surface. A gecko clings with millions of hairs, so she can walk on glass.
- *what is this world:* A table, a floor and a rope look smooth and whole. Close up, they are mountain ranges meeting at their peaks, and a boat can be held by the meetings.

The grip of friction is made by the same force that holds atoms together, the electric pull and push between charged particles. At a touching spot, atoms of one surface and atoms of the other come so close that they pull on each other. One force, shown in countless tiny places. Each place is weak, but together they hold a boat. Every turn of a rope round a post adds a whole new ring of these meetings, and the grip multiplies. The one force becomes the many small grips, and the many hold the heavy thing.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** Thirty spokes meet in the hub of a wheel, but it is the empty space in the middle that makes the wheel useful. Clay is shaped into a pot, but it is the hollow that makes the pot useful. (A wheel works because only a small spot of it touches the road, and the rest is hollow space. A grip also works at a few small spots, with much empty space in between.)
- **Udana 6.4, the blind men and the elephant:** In this old Buddhist story, blind men each feel one part of an elephant. One feels the leg and says it is a pillar. One feels the tail and says it is a rope. Each is right about the part and wrong about the whole. (Friction is a grip that saves a boat and a burn on a hand. It is a drag on a cyclist and the reason the brakes work. Each view is true about one side.)
- Science (settled): The real area where two hard surfaces touch is often less than one thousandth of the area we see (Bowden and Tabor, 1950).
- Science (open): No single theory yet predicts friction for any pair of materials from the atoms upward.
- Other view, Many engineers: An engineer needs the number mu and the rules that predict it. What touching really means is an interesting question, but it does not change the answer. The rules work, and that is enough.
- Practice, Press, slide, notice (3 min): Rub your palms together very lightly for five seconds. Now press them together hard, and rub again. Which was harder to slide? Rest your palms together and breathe slowly three times. Think about all the tiny places where your palms are meeting.
- Ask: If two surfaces touch at only a few tiny spots, what is in the places between?
- Ask: Is a sticker touching a wall, or part of it? Where would you draw the line?
- Ask: Who knew the rope rule first, the boatman who used it or the scientist who wrote it down?

## Story: The Fourth Turn
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. A rope wound round a post holds a heavy load with a light pull, and each extra turn makes the hold several times stronger. The boat, the quay and the people are imagined.*

On the evening the rain came early, Anjana sat on the warm steps of the old quay at Fort Kochi with a bobbin of cotton thread in her lap. She was ten, and she had a problem. That morning her teacher had asked the class a question. How many turns of thread round a pencil would hold up a bottle of water? Anjana had tried one turn, and the bottle slid down and bumped her toes. She had tried two turns, and it slid again, only more slowly.

Her grandmother, Kalyani Ammamma, sat beside her, shelling tamarind pods. Ammamma was seventy-three. When she was young, she and her husband had run a cargo boat up and down the backwaters, and her hands were as brown and tough as old rope. 'Your thread is slippery,' she said, without looking up. 'So you must ask it for more.' 'More what?' asked Anjana. Ammamma only smiled and went on shelling.

Anjana wound the thread round the pencil again. This time she did not stop at two turns. She went on to three. She hung the little bottle from one end and held the other end between two fingers, hardly pinching at all. The bottle did not fall. Anjana stared at it. Three turns held it. Two turns had not. She did not understand how one more turn could change so much.

Then the sky turned the colour of wet slate, and the wind came across the water in a long, cold push. Out in the harbour, a big cargo boat called the Lakshmi was coming in, loaded to the rim with coconuts. She was meant to stop at the iron post at the end of the quay. But the wind was behind her, and she kept coming, faster than the boatman had planned.

A rope flew from the deck. Rashid, who worked on the quay, caught it. Thomas, the young man beside him, grabbed the rope behind him. They leaned back with their heels in the wet stone and pulled with all their weight. The boat did not slow. The rope hissed through their hands. 'Let go!' shouted Rashid. 'It's burning!' They let go. The rope skittered across the stones, and the Lakshmi began to swing towards the stone arch of the old bridge.

Ammamma stood up. She did not run. She walked to the iron post, picked up the rope as it slid by, and made one loop round the post. Then a second. Then a third. She held the loose end in her right hand, as lightly as if she were holding a flower. The rope groaned against the iron. Anjana watched her grandmother's hand. It was hardly pulling at all. But the rope did not run free. It slid, slowly, a hand's width at a time, and every hand's width it slid, the boat slowed down.

'It is still too fast,' Anjana whispered. The boat was huge, and the post was shuddering. The rope began to slide faster, and the loose end tugged hard in her grandmother's hand. Anjana jumped to her feet. She thought of her bottle. One turn had slid. Two had slid, more slowly. Three had held. Each turn had made the holding easier, not by a little, but by a lot. 'Ammamma!' she shouted. 'One more turn!'

Ammamma did not ask why. She slipped a fourth loop round the post. The rope bit. The loose end went quiet in her hand. The Lakshmi slid on for the length of two bullock carts, slower and slower, and then she stopped with a soft bump, one arm's length from the arch of the bridge. Coconuts rolled in the hold, and then everything was still. The first fat drops of rain began to fall on the stones.

Rashid and Thomas stood with their palms open. They were red as chillies. 'How?' said Rashid. 'Two of us could not hold her, and you did it with one hand.' Ammamma shook out her fingers. 'I did not hold the boat,' she said. 'The post held the boat. I only held the tail of the rope, and the turns did the rest.' She looked at Anjana. 'My hands knew three turns. She knew why a fourth would work.'

Rashid shook his head, but he was smiling. 'Show me,' he said. Ammamma took his wrist. 'Never pull,' she said. 'Wind the rope, and then ease. Too few turns, and the rope runs. Too many, and the boat stops with a jerk and breaks the line. A little slip is a friend.' Thomas knelt and put his hand on the wet iron post. It was warm.

That night, Anjana wound her thread round the pencil again. Four turns. She hung the bottle from one end, and she held the other end with just one finger. The bottle hung in the lamplight, perfectly still. She did not need the lesson to be any bigger than that. The thread was holding a bottle, and the pencil was holding the thread, and neither was doing very much at all.

**For the parent:** A rope wound round a post holds a heavy boat with a light pull, because every turn multiplies the grip by the same number. One more turn can make the hold several times easier.

- Ask: Why did the fourth turn help more than a fourth person pulling would have?
- Ask: Why did Ammamma let the rope slip a little, instead of locking it with many turns right away?
- Ask: What would have happened if the post had been wet with oil?

## Experiments
### e1 · Press Harder: how does the load change the pull? (40 min)
- **question:** If you put more weight in a box, how much harder is it to pull? And does it matter whether the box lies on its big face or stands on its narrow end?
- **predict:** Before you start, make two guesses. First, how much harder will the box be to pull with two packets in it than with one? Second, will the big face or the narrow end need the bigger pull? Say why you think so for each guess.
- **fair test:** Use the same box, the same floor and the same rubber band every time. Pull level and very slowly, and read the band just before the box moves. For the load test, only the number of packets changes. For the area test, the load stays at two packets and only the face on the floor changes. Do every measurement three times, because one pull can be a fluke.
- **safety:** Keep the pull low and slow so the rubber band cannot snap back at a face. Pull away from your body and keep your face to the side. Put the packets on a table afterwards, not on your feet.
- **record:** Write the gap between the dots for each pull in the table. For each load, find the typical stretch. Draw a line graph with the number of packets along the bottom and the stretch up the side. Then write your claim, your evidence and your reason.
- **results table:** Load in the box | Gap between the dots, try 1 (cm) | Gap between the dots, try 2 (cm) | Gap between the dots, try 3 (cm) | Typical stretch: middle gap minus 4 (cm)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the box, the packets and the band. Ask for both guesses, and for a reason for each. Write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will add weight to a box and pull it along the floor. How much harder will two packets be than one? Why do you think so? Then I will ask a second question.  
  *Child mode:* Guess two things. How much harder is the box to pull with two packets? Which side needs a bigger pull? Why?
  - Ask: Which side of the box will need the bigger pull, the big face or the narrow end? Why do you think so? (good: The big face, because more of it touches the floor.; The narrow end, because it is pressed harder.; They are the same.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Any guess with a reason is a good start. Most people think the big face will grip more. Write the guesses at the top of the table.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw two dots 4 centimetres apart on the flat band. Loop the band round the end of the box. Tape the lid shut. Put the box on the floor.  
  *Parent says:* Mark two dots on the band, 4 centimetres apart. When the band stretches, the dots move apart. The gap tells us how hard we pull.  
  *Child mode:* Draw two dots on the band, 4 cm apart. Loop the band round the box.
  - Note: A wide band works best. If the band stretches more than about 15 centimetres with three packets, use two bands side by side. If it stretches less than 1 centimetre with one packet, use a thinner band. A forcemeter from Class 3 also works. Read newtons instead of centimetres.
  - Check: Why do we mark two dots on the band? → **The gap between the dots shows how hard we pull**
- **s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Start with the empty box. Pull the band level and very slowly. A helper reads the gap just before the box moves. Do this three times. Then repeat with 1, 2 and 3 packets.  
  *Parent says:* Pull as slowly as a snail. The helper watches the dots and calls out the biggest gap just before the box starts to slide. Write it down. Do three pulls for each load.  
  *Child mode:* Pull the band slowly and level. Read the gap just before the box moves. Do it three times for each load.
  - Ask: What do you see in the numbers as you add packets? (good: The stretch gets bigger each time.; Each packet adds about the same extra stretch.; The stretch roughly doubles when the packets double.)
  - Note: Pull level with the floor, not upwards. Stick-slip can make the box jump. This is normal. Read the biggest gap just before the first jump. If you are alone, take a phone video and pause it. Do not worry if the numbers are not perfect. A rubber band is not an exact gauge. Look for the pattern: each packet adds about the same extra stretch.
  - Check: One packet needs a stretch of 4 cm. Two packets need 8 cm. What would you expect for three packets? → **About 12 cm**
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Put two packets in the box. Pull it on its big face, three times. Then stand the box on its narrow end, tape the lid, and pull it three times.  
  *Parent says:* Now keep two packets in the box. First pull it lying flat. Then stand it on its narrow end and pull again. Which one needs the bigger pull?  
  *Child mode:* Two packets in the box. Pull it flat three times. Then pull it on its end three times.
  - Ask: Which face needed the bigger pull? Were you surprised? (good: They were nearly the same.; The big face was a little bigger.; I thought the big face would be much bigger.)
  - Note: For a cardboard box, the two results are usually within about 10 per cent of each other. Differences of one or two millimetres are noise. If one face has a rougher cardboard surface, the result can differ, so tape a piece of smooth paper under both faces if you can.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a line graph. Put the packets along the bottom and the stretch up the side. Ask your child to say their claim, their evidence and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think would happen? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Draw your graph. Say what you thought, what you saw and why.
  - Note: Wait for the child's own reason first. Then you can say: friction grows with how hard the surfaces press together, which is the weight on the floor. A wide face spreads the same weight over more of the tiny bumps on the floor, and a narrow face puts it on fewer. The total grip stays about the same. Surfaces touch only at the tops of their bumps, and pressing harder makes more of them touch.
  - Think: If the face on the floor does not matter, what does the grip depend on?
### e2 · Wrap the Thread: how many turns hold the bottle? (40 min)
- **question:** A bottle of water hangs from a thread that goes round a pencil. How many paper clips must hang from the other end to hold the bottle, after half a turn, one turn, two turns and three turns?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess how many clips will be needed after one turn. Then guess whether each extra turn will take away the same number of clips, or the same share. Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same bottle, the same thread, the same pencil and the same bag are used every time. Only the number of turns changes. Add or take away clips one at a time and decide when the bottle just holds. Do each test twice.
- **safety:** Never use kite thread coated with glass powder, called manja, because it cuts skin. Put a cushion on the floor under the bottle, so it cannot land on a foot. Hold the thread with your fingertips and never wind it round your fingers.
- **record:** Write the clips needed for each number of turns. Look at the numbers, and find how many times fewer clips each turn needs. Then write your claim, your evidence and your reason.
- **results table:** Turns round the pencil | Clips needed: try 1 | Clips needed: try 2 | Typical number of clips
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the pencil, the bottle and the clips. Ask how many clips one turn will need, and ask for the reason. Write the guess down.  
  *Parent says:* A bottle hangs from this thread. After the thread goes round the pencil once, how many clips do you think will hold the bottle? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how many clips one turn needs. Say why you think so.
  - Ask: Will each extra turn take away the same number of clips, or the same share of the clips? Why do you think so? (good: The same number, because each turn does the same job.; The same share, because each turn multiplies the grip.; I do not know, so I will test it.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Few people guess a number close to the real one. Write the guess at the top of the table.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape the pencil so 8 centimetres stick out past the table edge. Put a heavy book on the taped end. Tie the thread round the bottle's neck and a small bag to the other end.  
  *Parent says:* Tape the pencil to the table so it sticks out past the edge. Put a heavy book on the taped end. Tie the bottle to one end of the thread and the bag to the other.  
  *Child mode:* Tape the pencil so it sticks out. Tie the bottle to the thread. Tie the bag to the other end.
  - Note: The pencil must not roll. Use a round pencil, because a six-sided pencil has corners that grip like gears. Put the cushion on the floor under the bottle. If the thread keeps slipping off the end of the pencil, tape a small stopper of paper at the tip.
  - Check: Why do we use the same bottle, thread and pencil each time? → **So only the number of turns changes**
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Drape the thread over the pencil for half a turn. Add clips to the bag until the bottle holds when you let go. Then take clips out one at a time until it starts to slip. Count. Repeat with one full turn.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the bottle. Add clips to the bag, ten at a time at first. Let go gently. If the bottle slides, add more. When it holds, take clips out one by one. Count the clips that only just hold.  
  *Child mode:* Add clips until the bottle stays. Then take them out one at a time. Count.
  - Ask: How many clips did half a turn need? How many did one full turn need? (good: Half a turn needed many more clips.; One full turn needed far fewer clips.; The numbers dropped a lot.)
  - Note: Half a turn needs the most clips, often 60 to 100. One turn often needs about 25 to 40. Add clips in tens at the start to save time. Always let go slowly. A sudden let-go can make the bottle drop faster than it should. Numbers vary with the thread and the pencil, and any numbers are fine. The pattern matters, not the exact count.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Wind two full turns round the pencil. Find how many clips just hold the bottle. Then wind three full turns and find the number again. Try each twice.  
  *Parent says:* Now two turns. Add clips until the bottle holds. Take them away one by one. Then three turns. Try each of them twice.  
  *Child mode:* Now try two turns, then three turns. Count the clips each time.
  - Ask: What happened to the number of clips as you added turns? (good: It dropped very quickly.; At three turns, the empty bag nearly held the bottle.; Each turn needed much fewer clips than the one before.)
  - Note: Two turns usually need only a few clips, and three turns often need none, so the bag alone holds the bottle. Write 0 if the empty bag holds it. If it slips even with many clips, the thread or the pencil is too slippery. Try a rougher string.
  - Check: One turn needs 30 clips. Two turns need 5 clips. About how many clips would three turns need? → **About 1**
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Write the clip numbers in the table. Divide the number for one turn by the number for two turns, then two turns by three turns. Ask for claim, evidence and reason.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the numbers. How many times fewer clips did two turns need than one turn? Tell me what you thought, what you saw and why.  
  *Child mode:* Divide the clips for one turn by the clips for two turns. Say what you found.
  - Note: Wait for the child's own reason first. Then you can say: every part of the thread that touches the pencil adds friction, and every turn multiplies the grip by about the same number. For smooth thread on a pencil, one turn often makes the hold about six times easier. Two turns make it about six times six, which is thirty-six, and three turns make it about two hundred and sixteen times easier. This is why a boat can be held with a light hand.
  - Think: If one turn makes the hold about six times easier, how many times easier is three turns?

### a1 · The Walking Ruler: two fingers that take turns (15 min)
- **safety:** Keep the ruler low over a table, so that it cannot fall on a foot.
- **record:** Draw the ruler and mark where your fingers met each time. Write which finger slid first.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Rest the ruler flat on your two index fingers, near its ends. Ask your child where the fingers will meet when they slide together. Ask why.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the ruler on two fingers, far apart. Slide your fingers slowly together. Where do you think they will meet? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Rest the ruler on your fingers. Guess where they will meet. Why?
  - Note: Most people guess that one finger will win and reach the other. Do not correct this. Write the guess down.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Slide both fingers slowly together. Do not look away. Watch which finger slides and which finger stays still. Mark the meeting point.  
  *Parent says:* Slide your fingers together slowly. Only one finger moves at a time. Which one is it? Do they ever slide together? Where do they meet?  
  *Child mode:* Slide your fingers together slowly. Which finger moves first? Where do they meet?
  - Ask: What did your fingers do? (good: They took turns sliding.; They met in the middle.; One finger stuck while the other slid.)
  - Note: The fingers take turns because of stick-slip. The finger nearer the middle carries more of the ruler's weight, so it presses harder and its friction is bigger. The other finger slides first, until it gets near the middle and its friction becomes bigger than the first one's limit. Then the roles swap. They always meet at the balance point, which is the middle of a plain ruler.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stick the clay on one end of the ruler. Guess where the fingers will meet now. Slide them together again, and mark the new meeting point.  
  *Parent says:* Now stick clay on one end. Where will your fingers meet this time? Why? Try it and see.  
  *Child mode:* Stick clay on one end. Guess where you will meet. Then try.
  - Ask: Where did the fingers meet this time? (good: Closer to the clay.; At the new balance point, nearer the heavy end.)
  - Note: The fingers meet at the new balance point, nearer the heavy end. The finger near the heavy end carries more weight, so it presses harder, and its friction is larger. The lighter finger slides until they meet. This is the same rule as in the Press Harder test: more pressing force, more friction.
  - Check: Why does the finger near the clay slide less? → **It carries more weight, so it presses harder and has more friction**
### a2 · Rollers and Marbles: sliding against rolling (20 min)
- **record:** Write the dot gap for sliding, for rolling on pencils and for rolling on marbles. Write which was the easiest to pull.

  *Parent does:* Show your child the box, the pencils and the marbles. Ask which way will be easiest to pull the box, and why. Write down the guess.  
  *Parent says:* We will pull the same box three ways. First sliding. Then on pencils. Then on marbles. Which way do you think will be easiest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess which way is easiest: sliding, pencils or marbles. Why?
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Most children think the pencils will help but guess too little.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pull the box slowly along the floor, with the band level. Once it moves steadily, read the gap between the dots. Do this three times.  
  *Parent says:* Pull the box steadily. Do not stop or jerk. Read the gap while the box is moving. Do it three times.  
  *Child mode:* Pull the box steadily. Read the gap while it moves. Do it three times.

### a3 · The Slow Drop: friction inside liquids (25 min)
- **safety:** Do the warm-water step only with a grown-up. Wipe up any oil at once, because it makes the floor slippery.
- **record:** Write the three times. Write which liquid had the most friction on the falling ball, and what warming the honey did.
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show the three glasses. Ask your child to rank them from fastest fall to slowest fall. Ask why.  
  *Parent says:* We will drop the same nut into three liquids. Which liquid will let it fall fastest? Which will be slowest? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess the order of the three liquids. Fastest to slowest. Why?
  - Note: Do not correct the guesses. Most children guess that honey is slowest, but some guess that oil is slower than water.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill each glass to the same height. Hold the nut just above the liquid and let it go. Start the stopwatch at the drop. Stop it when the nut touches the bottom. Do each liquid twice.  
  *Parent says:* Hold the nut just above the top. Let go and start the timer. Stop it when the nut hits the bottom. Do each glass twice.  
  *Child mode:* Drop the nut. Time the fall. Do each liquid twice.
  - Ask: Which liquid was slowest? Which two were hardest to tell apart? (good: Honey was much slower.; Water and oil were close.; The nut took many seconds in the honey.)
  - Note: Honey is clearly the slowest, often by 5 seconds or more. Water and oil often fall so quickly that a phone stopwatch cannot separate them. This is fine. Write down what you see. Wipe any spills, because oil and honey make the floor slippery.
- **s3 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up should do this step. Stand the honey glass in a bowl of warm tap water for five minutes. Test it again with the nut.  
  *Parent says:* Warm the honey in a bowl of warm tap water for five minutes. Then drop the nut again. Is it faster or slower?  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to warm the honey. Drop the nut again.
  - Ask: What did warming do to the fall? (good: The nut fell faster.; The honey flowed more easily.)
  - Note: Use warm tap water, not hot, so that no one is burned and the glass does not crack. The warm honey should be noticeably faster. Heat makes the molecules of the honey move faster, so they slide past each other more easily.
  - Check: Honey is warmed. What happens to its friction on a falling nut? → **It gets smaller, so the nut falls faster**
### a4 · Wrinkled Fingers: do they grip wet things better? (35 min)
- **safety:** Use warm tap water, not hot. Wipe up all splashes so that nobody slips.
- **record:** Write the times for the smooth hand and the wrinkled hand, in the order you tried them. Write what you think the wrinkles do.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child why fingers wrinkle in water. Ask whether wrinkled fingers will move wet marbles faster, slower or the same. Ask why.  
  *Parent says:* Why do you think your fingers wrinkle in the bath? Will wrinkled fingers pick up wet marbles faster, slower, or the same? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Why do fingers wrinkle? Will they pick up wet marbles faster? Why?
  - Note: Scientists disagree about what wrinkles are for. Do not tell your child the answer. Any guess with a reason is good.
- **s2 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Soak one hand in warm water for 20 minutes. Do something else with the other hand, such as drawing or reading, while you wait.  
  *Parent says:* Soak one hand for twenty minutes. Draw or read with the other hand while you wait.  
  *Child mode:* Soak one hand for 20 minutes. Draw while you wait.
  - Note: If the fingers have not wrinkled after 20 minutes, add some warm water. Some people wrinkle slowly. Wrinkling is controlled by the nerves, and it can fail in people with some nerve injuries.
  - Wait: Wait 20 minutes for the fingers to wrinkle.
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour the wet marbles into a bowl. Take 10 marbles, one at a time, from the bowl to the cup with the wrinkled hand. Time it. Then do the same with the smooth hand. Then swap the order and repeat.  
  *Parent says:* Move ten wet marbles into the cup, one at a time, using only a finger and thumb. Time yourself with the wrinkled hand and then the smooth hand. Then swap the order.  
  *Child mode:* Move ten wet marbles. Use a finger and thumb. Time each hand.
  - Ask: Which hand was faster? Was it the same both times you tried? (good: The wrinkled hand, a little.; They were nearly the same.; The second try was faster for both, because of practice.)
  - Note: Results are often mixed, and this is why scientists still argue. A second try is usually faster because of practice, so swap the order and do the test twice. Dry the hands, and test dry marbles too. A scientific study found wrinkled fingers helped with wet objects but not dry ones. Other studies found no clear help.
  - Check: Why do we swap the order of the hands for the second try? → **Practice would make the second try faster, whichever hand it was**

### c1 · Lower the Load: hold a heavy bottle with one finger (40 min)
- **problem:** Lower a 500 millilitre bottle of water from a table to the floor at a slow, steady speed, taking about 10 seconds. You may hold the thread with only one finger and thumb. Find a way of winding the thread that works with the fewest turns.
- **your turn:** Sketch your design and say how many turns you expect to need. Try it. Change one thing at a time. Which design lowered the bottle most smoothly? How do you know?
- **constraints:** Use only cotton thread or string, and things you can find in the kitchen or on your desk as posts, such as pencils, spoons and tumblers.; You may hold the loose end with one finger and thumb only. You may not wind it round your fingers.; The bottle must go down slowly and steadily, with no sudden drops.; Count the number of turns you use.
- **general:** Wind the string round a single pencil, as in the Wrap the Thread test, with three or four turns.; Wind the string round a thicker post, such as a steel tumbler laid on its side.; Use a rough string to give more friction.
- **creative:** Weave the string over and under three pencils in a zigzag, like a shoelace, so each pencil adds its own grip.; Wind the string round the post in a figure-of-eight, which makes the string cross itself.; Tie one knot round a pencil as a brake, and open it a little at a time.

  *Parent says:* You have to lower the bottle slowly, using one finger. How could you wind the string? Draw some ideas first.  
  *Child mode:* You must lower a bottle slowly with one finger. Draw some ideas.
  - Ask: What will happen if you use too few turns? What will happen if you use too many? (good: Too few, and the bottle will drop fast.; Too many, and the bottle will not move at all.; We need the right number.)
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Build your first design. Put a cushion under the bottle. Hold the loose end and ease it gently. Count how many seconds the bottle takes to reach the floor.  
  *Parent says:* Build your first design. Hold the end gently. Ease it out. Count the seconds as the bottle goes down.  
  *Child mode:* Build your design. Ease the string out. Count the seconds.
  - Note: Put the table in a place where nobody walks. Keep your child's feet clear of the bottle. If the bottle drops, it will bump the cushion and that is fine.
- **s3 (15 min)**

## Applications
- Boats, ships and ferries are tied to bollards with a few turns of rope, and held with a light pull on the loose end.
- Rock climbers wind a rope through a device so that a light hold can catch a fall.
- Tyres are made of rubber mixes chosen for a high mu on dry and wet roads.
- Ball bearings in ceiling fans, cycle hubs and washing machines turn sliding into rolling.
- Engine oil is made to flow when cold and to stay thick enough when hot, so engines run on a cold morning and on a hot highway.
- Brake pads press on a disc or rim. The pressing force sets the friction that stops the vehicle, and the movement becomes heat.
- Knots, plaited hair, woven cloth and thread hold together because of the friction where the strands cross and touch.
- Hinges, drawers and sliding doors are oiled or waxed to stop stick-slip squeaks.
- Engineers choose materials by their mu, such as rubber for shoe soles and Teflon for non-stick pans.
- Geologists study stick-slip on faults to understand how and where earthquakes may start.
- Machines are checked and oiled, because a worn, dry part wastes energy as heat.

## Open questions
- **Can we predict friction for any pair of materials from the atoms upward?** Scientists can predict friction in a few simple cases. For most materials, they still measure it, because the full atomic story is too complicated.
- **Why do fingers wrinkle in water, and do the wrinkles help us grip?** Some experiments found a benefit and others did not. The question is still being tested.
- **Can we make a material with almost no friction at all?** Scientists have measured almost no friction between layers of graphite in tiny samples. Making it work for large, everyday things is not yet solved.
- **Can we predict when a fault in the Earth will slip in an earthquake?** Faults stick and slip like a block on a spring. But the rocks, the cracks and the water underground are so complicated that nobody can name the day.
- **How does friction change at very small sizes, a few atoms across?** The old rules, such as friction being proportional to load, may stop working at this size. Scientists are still finding out which rules replace them.
- **Why does a thing slide with a smaller friction than the friction it started with?** Scientists describe it well, but they are still working out what the atoms do at the moment a surface lets go.

So you would expect a stretch of about 15 centimetres. A real band may stretch a little differently, because rubber bands are not perfect gauges. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A box presses on the floor with a force of 20 newtons and needs 8 newtons of pull to keep sliding. Divide the pull by the pressing force to find mu, the coefficient of friction. **0.4.**. 8 divided by 20 is 0.4. Mu is the friction force divided by the pressing force. It tells you how grippy the two surfaces are together. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- In the Wrap the Thread test, each extra turn makes the hold 6 times easier. How many times easier do three turns make it? **216 times.**. Three turns multiply the grip by 6 three times. 6 times 6 is 36. 36 times 6 is 216. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- A boat pulls a rope with 1,296 newtons. Three turns make the hold 216 times easier. How many newtons must the boatwoman's hand hold? **6 newtons.**. 1,296 divided by 216 is 6. A hand can easily hold 6 newtons, which is about the weight of a small bottle of water. You can check: 216 times 6 is 1,296. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- Look at the bar chart of made-up clips needed in the Wrap the Thread test. How many times fewer clips did two turns need than one turn? **6 times fewer.**. One turn needed 30 clips and two turns needed 5 clips. 30 divided by 5 is 6. Each extra turn needs about 6 times fewer clips. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- Look at the ruler picture. In a Press Harder test, the two dots started 4 centimetres apart. The ruler shows the gap just before the box moved. What was the stretch of the band? **5 centimetres.**. The pointer is at 9 centimetres. The dots started 4 centimetres apart. 9 take away 4 is 5. So the band stretched by 5 centimetres. (queues `math.measurement-basics.reading-scales.L4`)
- You drag a box 15 metres along the floor. The friction force is 12 newtons. How many joules of work do you do against the friction? **180 joules.**. Work is force times distance. 12 times 15 is 180. Ten 12s make 120, and five more make 60, so 120 plus 60 is 180. All of it ends up as heat. (queues `math.multiplication.multi-digit.L4`)
- The number of clips needed to hold a bottle is 216, 36, 6 and 1 for half a turn, one turn, two turns and three turns in an ideal test. What rule connects the numbers, and what would four turns need? **Divide by 6 each time. Four turns need a sixth of a clip, which is almost nothing.**. 216 divided by 6 is 36. 36 divided by 6 is 6. 6 divided by 6 is 1. So the rule is to divide by 6. The next number is 1 divided by 6, which is a sixth of a clip. In real life, the empty bag alone would hold the bottle. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What happens to friction if you double the weight on a box? → It about doubles.
- Does a bigger face on a hard box give more friction? → No. The weight is the same, so the grip is about the same.
- What is mu? → A number for how grippy two surfaces are together.
- Where do two hard surfaces really touch? → Only at the tips of their tiny bumps.
- What is stick-slip? → Movement in jerks, sticking and slipping again and again.
- Which has less friction, rolling or sliding? → Rolling.
- What does warming honey do to its friction? → It lowers it, so the honey flows more easily.
- What does each extra turn of rope round a post do? → It multiplies the grip by the same number.
- Where does the movement of a sliding box go? → Into heat.
