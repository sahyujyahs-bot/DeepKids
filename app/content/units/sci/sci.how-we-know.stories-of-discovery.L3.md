# Lucky accidents and missing names: how discoveries really happen
`sci.how-we-know.stories-of-discovery.L3`

**Summary.** Spencer Silver tried to make a very strong glue and made a weak one instead. Years later, his weak glue became the glue on sticky notes. Many discoveries began like this, with a result nobody wanted, and a person who stopped to ask why. You will make your own 'failed' tape and find a job it does better than good tape. You will also meet the people who did the work behind famous discoveries but were left out when the credit was handed out.

**Intro.** *In 1968, a chemist named Spencer Silver tried to make a glue strong enough to help build aeroplanes. His glue came out so weak that paper stuck to it peeled straight off again. Was that a failure?* For aeroplanes, yes. But six years later his co-worker Art Fry needed a bookmark that would stay in his song book without tearing the pages. Silver's weak glue was just right. A result is not good or bad by itself. It depends on what you need it for. In this unit you will test this with tape, and meet people whose 'mistakes' became discoveries.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a discovery is, and how it is different from an invention.
1. I can tell the true story of a discovery that began with an accident or a mistake, and say what the person did that turned it into a discovery.
2. I can explain why an unexpected result must be checked and repeated before anyone can call it a discovery.
3. I can test a 'failed' material fairly, and use my results to say which job it does well.
4. I can name people who did important work on a discovery but were left out of the credit, and give reasons why this happens.
5. I can argue for one side of a question about credit, use evidence, and say what the other side has right.

## Terms
- **Discovery**: Finding out something true about the world that people did not know before, and showing others that it is true. *e.g.* Jocelyn Bell Burnell discovered pulsars, stars that send out radio pulses as regular as a clock.
- **Invention**: Something new that a person makes or designs, such as a tool, a machine or a material. *e.g.* The sticky note is an invention. Finding out how glue grips paper is a discovery.
- **Accident**: Something that happens without anyone planning it. *e.g.* Alexander Fleming did not plan for mould to land on his dish of bacteria. It was an accident.
- **Mistake**: Something done wrongly, or a result that is not what you were trying to get. *e.g.* William Perkin was trying to make a medicine for malaria. His mistake made a bright purple dye instead.
- **Serendipity**: Finding something good that you were not looking for, by luck and by noticing. The word comes from Serendip, an old name for Sri Lanka. *e.g.* Finding a forgotten 100-rupee note in an old school bag is a small piece of serendipity.
- **Observation**: Looking at something carefully, with any of your senses, and noting what you see. *e.g.* Jocelyn Bell Burnell's observation was a tiny wobble on a long paper chart, a mark most people would have missed.
- **Result**: What actually happens in a test or an experiment. *e.g.* In the tape test, one result was that fresh tape tore the newspaper.
- **Evidence**: What you saw or measured that shows whether an idea is true. *e.g.* The mice that got penicillin lived and the mice that did not get it died. That was evidence that penicillin worked inside a body.
- **Adhesive**: Any material that sticks things together, such as glue, gum or the sticky layer on tape. *e.g.* The sticky layer on a bindi is an adhesive. So is the rice paste some families use to close envelopes.
- **Fibre**: A very thin thread. Paper is made of tiny plant fibres pressed into a flat mat. *e.g.* Tear a newspaper slowly and look at the edge. You can see the furry fibres sticking out.
- **Credit**: Saying, out loud or in writing, who did a piece of work, so that people know and thank them. *e.g.* At the end of a film, the credits list everyone who made it, from the actors to the cooks.
- **Research student**: A student at a university who does real scientific research under a teacher, called a supervisor, to earn a degree. *e.g.* Jocelyn Bell Burnell was a research student when she found the first pulsar. Antony Hewish was her supervisor.
- **Nobel Prize**: A famous prize given each year for great work in physics, chemistry, medicine, writing and peace. At most three people can share one prize. *e.g.* C. V. Raman won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930 for the Raman effect.
- **Mould**: A fungus that grows as soft, fuzzy patches on old food and damp walls. *e.g.* Bread left in a damp box for a week grows green or black mould.
- **Antibiotic**: A medicine that kills bacteria or stops them growing inside the body. Penicillin was the first one used widely. *e.g.* A doctor may give an antibiotic for a bad throat infection caused by bacteria.
- **Pulsar**: A dead star, smaller than a city, that spins very fast and sends out a beam of radio waves that sweeps past the Earth like a lighthouse. *e.g.* The first pulsar sends a radio pulse to Earth about every one and a third seconds.

## Explanations
### What a discovery is · *defined*
**Definition:** A discovery is finding out something true about the world that people did not know before, and showing others that it is true. An invention is something new that a person makes.

Pulsars spun in space for millions of years before anyone knew about them. Jocelyn Bell Burnell did not make them. She found them, so that is a discovery. A sticky note did not exist until people made it, so that is an invention. The two often go together. Finding out how a weak glue behaves is a discovery. Turning it into sticky notes is an invention. A discovery also needs other people to check it. If only one person has seen something and nobody else can see it again, it is not yet a discovery.
- e.g. Finding that the mould Penicillium makes a substance that kills bacteria was a discovery. Making penicillin into a medicine in bottles was an invention.
- e.g. C. V. Raman discovered that light changes colour very slightly when it bounces off molecules. Nobody invented that. It was always happening.
### Accidents and mistakes that became discoveries · *defined*
**Definition:** Many discoveries began with something nobody planned, or with an experiment that went wrong. The accident gave a surprising result. A person then noticed it and asked why.

In September 1928, Alexander Fleming came back from a holiday to his laboratory at St Mary's Hospital in London. On one glass dish of bacteria, a mould had grown by accident. Around the mould was a clear ring where the bacteria had died. Fleming named the substance from the mould penicillin. In 1856, William Perkin, aged 18, tried to make quinine, a medicine for malaria, in a laboratory at his home in London. He got a dark sludge. When he cleaned it out with alcohol, it turned a bright purple. That was mauve, the first dye made from chemicals rather than plants or insects. In 1896, Henri Becquerel in Paris planned to put uranium salts in sunlight, but the sky stayed cloudy. He left them in a drawer on top of a wrapped photographic plate, and developed the plate anyway. It was fogged. The uranium was giving out rays all by itself, with no sunlight at all. Marie Curie later named this radioactivity.
- e.g. Fleming's dish of bacteria was 'spoilt' by mould. The spoilt dish led to penicillin.
- e.g. Perkin failed to make quinine and made the first chemical dye instead. Mauve soon became a very popular colour for clothes.
- e.g. Spencer Silver's glue was too weak for aeroplanes. It became the glue on sticky notes.
### Why only some people notice: the prepared mind · *idea*
Mould must have landed on other people's dishes before 1928. Most of them threw the dish away. In 1854, the French scientist Louis Pasteur said, 'In the fields of observation, chance favours only the prepared mind.' He meant that luck helps only people who already know enough to see that something is strange. Fleming had studied bacteria-killing substances for years, so a clear ring meant something to him. Bell Burnell knew exactly what her chart should look like, so she noticed a mark that did not belong. This is an idea about how people think, not a law of nature. Some discoveries were made by beginners who looked with fresh eyes.
- e.g. A cook who has made dosa batter a hundred times notices at once when it smells different. A new cook may not.
- e.g. Bell Burnell checked many metres of paper chart by hand every week. She knew its usual wiggles so well that a tiny strange mark stood out.
### A surprise is only the start · *defined*
**Definition:** An unexpected result becomes a discovery only after it has been checked, repeated and explained. Most surprising results turn out to be errors.

When something odd happens, the first question is: did I make a mistake? Bell Burnell's strange mark could have been a fault in the machine, or a signal from a car or a radio on Earth. She and her supervisor checked. The mark came back about four minutes earlier each day, the same way the stars do. Signals from Earth do not do that. So it came from out in space. Fleming grew the mould again in fresh dishes, and the bacteria died again. Penicillin worked in a dish. It still had to be shown to work inside a living body. In May 1940, at Oxford, Howard Florey's team gave eight mice a deadly infection. Four mice also got penicillin. By the next morning the four without penicillin had died. The four with penicillin were alive.
- e.g. Bell Burnell checked whether the signal kept time with the stars. It did, so it was not coming from Earth.
- e.g. The Oxford team compared mice with penicillin and mice without it. This is a fair test, like the ones you have done.
### Why strong tape tears paper and weak tape does not · *defined*
**Definition:** Tape sticks because its glue presses very close to the surface and grips it. If the glue grips the paper's fibres harder than the fibres grip each other, the paper tears when you peel the tape.

Paper is a mat of tiny plant fibres pressed together. Fresh cello tape has a smooth, even layer of glue. It touches the paper almost everywhere and grips the fibres tightly. When you peel it, the top fibres hold on to the glue and leave the rest of the paper behind. That is the grey, furry patch you saw. When you dab tape on a cloth, tiny cotton fibres stick to the glue and cover parts of it. Less glue can touch the paper, so it grips more gently. It can come off without tearing anything. Spencer Silver's glue worked in a similar way. It was made of tiny balls of sticky material. The balls touched the paper only at some points, so the glue held lightly and let go cleanly.
- e.g. Fresh cello tape on a newspaper usually tears the paper when you peel it.
- e.g. A sticky note can be stuck and moved many times, because its glue touches the page only in some places.
- e.g. A bindi can be peeled off the forehead and stuck again, because its glue is a gentle one.
### How credit is given in science · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists get credit when their names are on the report of the work, called a paper, when a discovery is named after them, and when they win prizes.

When scientists find something, they write it up in a paper and send it to a science journal. The names of the people who did the work go at the top. These people are the authors. The first name is often the person who did the most, but different fields use different rules. Sometimes a discovery is named after one person, such as the Raman effect. Big prizes add even more credit. A Nobel Prize can be shared by at most three people. Today many discoveries are made by large teams. The 2016 paper that announced the first gravitational waves, ripples in space, had more than a thousand authors. No prize can name them all.
- e.g. The 1968 paper announcing pulsars had five authors. Antony Hewish was first and Jocelyn Bell was second.
- e.g. In 1928, C. V. Raman and K. S. Krishnan wrote the paper about the new kind of light scattering together. It came to be called the Raman effect.
### Who gets left out, and why · *debated*
Many people who did important work were left out of the credit. Norman Heatley worked out how to grow and clean enough penicillin for the Oxford tests. The 1945 Nobel Prize went to Fleming, Florey and Chain. Heatley was not named, and his work was honoured properly only in 1990. Rosalind Franklin took the X-ray photograph that showed the shape of DNA. Her co-worker Maurice Wilkins showed it to James Watson without asking her. Watson, Francis Crick and Wilkins won the Nobel Prize in 1962. Franklin had died in 1958, and the prize is not given after death. People argue about why this happens so often. Common reasons are that the person was young, a woman, an assistant, or worked far from famous universities.
- e.g. Jocelyn Bell Burnell found the first pulsar as a research student. The 1974 Nobel Prize went to her supervisor Antony Hewish and to Martin Ryle.
- e.g. K. S. Krishnan did the Raman effect experiments with C. V. Raman. Only Raman won the 1930 Nobel Prize.
- **Fred Hoyle, a famous astronomer, at the time:** Hoyle said that Bell Burnell's discovery was the key step, and that the prize should have included her.
- **Jocelyn Bell Burnell herself, in 1977:** She said that a supervisor is responsible for a project's success or failure, and that Nobel Prizes should not usually go to research students. Much later she also said that being a student and a woman lowered her standing.
- **People who defend the prize rules:** They say that the person who designed the project and built the telescope deserves the credit, and that a prize must draw a line somewhere.
### Credit stories from South India · *defined*
**Definition:** Two scientists from South India did key work on famous discoveries, and are much less well known than their partners.

K. S. Krishnan was born in 1898 near Watrap, in what is now Virudhunagar district of Tamil Nadu. In Kolkata, he worked with C. V. Raman on how light scatters, or bounces off, the tiny particles of liquids. In 1928 they found that a little of the scattered light comes back with a changed colour. They wrote the paper together. The discovery was named after Raman, and only Raman won the Nobel Prize in 1930. Yellapragada Subbarow was born in 1895 in Bhimavaram, in Andhra Pradesh. In the 1920s at Harvard University in the USA, he and Cyrus Fiske found two substances that muscles use for energy: phosphocreatine and ATP. Later his team made folic acid, a vitamin, in the laboratory, and early medicines for cancer. Harvard never made him a permanent professor, and few people today know his name.
- e.g. India celebrates National Science Day on 28 February, the day in 1928 when the Raman effect was announced.
- e.g. ATP is the energy carrier in every cell of your body. Subbarow was one of the first to find out what it does in muscles.
### Famous stories often get simplified · *idea*
When a discovery story is told again and again, it often shrinks to one person and one magic moment. Fleming's mould is usually told without the Oxford team who turned it into a medicine. The story that Archimedes jumped out of his bath shouting 'Eureka!' was first written down about 200 years after he died. Nobody knows if it happened. There is also a popular story that a laboratory worker named Mary Hunt found a mouldy melon in a market in Peoria, USA, in 1943, and that its mould made far more penicillin than Fleming's. The melon mould was real and did make far more. Whether Mary Hunt was the one who found it is not certain. Short stories are easy to remember. Real discoveries usually take many people and many years.
- e.g. Fleming saw the clear ring in 1928. Penicillin was first given to patients in 1941. That took 13 years and many people.
- e.g. The best penicillin mould came from a melon sold in a market in Peoria, USA, in 1943.
### Does hot water freeze faster than cold water? · *debated*
In 1963, Erasto Mpemba, a schoolboy in Tanzania, was making ice cream at school. There was not much space in the freezer, so he put his hot milk mixture in without waiting for it to cool. It froze before his friends' cooler mixtures. His teacher told him he must be confused. Later, a visiting physics professor, Denis Osborne, tried it in his own laboratory and got the same result. In 1969 the two of them wrote a paper together, and the idea is now called the Mpemba effect. But many careful tests since then have not found it. It seems to depend on the container, the freezer and how you decide when water has 'frozen'. Scientists still argue about whether it is real.
- e.g. Mpemba was laughed at for saying hot milk froze first. He kept asking until a scientist tested it.
- e.g. In some home tests the hot water freezes first, and in others the cold water does. Try it and see what yours does.
- **Scientists who have seen the effect:** Hot water loses some water as steam, so there is less left to freeze. Hot water may also set up stronger currents inside the cup that carry heat away faster.
- **Scientists who doubt the effect:** In tests where everything is kept exactly the same, cold water almost always freezes first. They think the effect comes from small differences that are hard to control, such as frost under one cup.

## Mostly agreed
- Many important discoveries began with an accident or an unexpected result. *How we know:* The stories of penicillin (1928), mauve dye (1856), radioactivity (1896) and pulsars (1967) were written down at the time by the people involved.
- An unexpected result must be checked and repeated before it counts as a discovery. *How we know:* Fleming grew the mould again, Bell Burnell checked that the signal kept time with the stars, and the Oxford team tested penicillin on mice before giving it to people.
- Most discoveries need many people, not one. *How we know:* Penicillin needed Fleming, Florey, Chain, Heatley, Dorothy Hodgkin who worked out its shape in 1945, and many factory workers. Science papers today often have dozens or even thousands of authors.
- Some people who did key work were left out of prizes and famous stories. *How we know:* Norman Heatley, Jocelyn Bell Burnell, Rosalind Franklin and K. S. Krishnan were not included in Nobel Prizes for discoveries they worked on. The records of the prizes and papers show who did what.
- Paper tears when a glue grips its fibres harder than the fibres grip each other. *How we know:* You can see torn fibres stuck to the peeled tape with a magnifying glass. Scientists who study adhesives measure how hard glue grips with special machines.

## How we found out
**Jocelyn Bell (now Jocelyn Bell Burnell), a research student, with her supervisor Antony Hewish, at Cambridge University, August to December 1967 (the signals), February 1968 (the paper in the journal Nature), 1974 (the Nobel Prize).** In 1967, Jocelyn Bell was a research student at Cambridge University in England. She had helped to build a radio telescope, a field of wooden posts and wires bigger than two football grounds. It recorded radio waves from space as a wiggly ink line on long rolls of paper. Bell checked the paper by hand, many metres every week. Among the wiggles she noticed a small mark that she called 'a bit of scruff'. It kept coming back from the same patch of sky. On 28 November 1967 a faster recording showed what it was: a pulse every one and a third seconds, as regular as a clock. At first the team wondered if it was a fault, or radio noise from people on Earth. But the signal came back four minutes earlier each day, keeping time with the stars. Half as a joke, they named it LGM-1, for 'little green men'. Then, in December, Bell found a second source pulsing in another part of the sky. Two sets of aliens signalling at the same time was very unlikely. The pulses came from spinning dead stars, now called pulsars. The 1974 Nobel Prize went to her supervisor, Antony Hewish, and to Martin Ryle, not to her.  
*What people believed before:* At first the team thought the mark might be a fault in the machine or radio noise from Earth, and joked that it might be aliens. The signal kept time with the stars, so it came from space. A second pulsar elsewhere in the sky showed it was something natural, not a message.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin all by himself, and that is why we have the medicine.** (A very common way the story is told; *rejected*)  
  For: Fleming really did notice the mould, name penicillin and write the first paper about it in 1929.  
  Against: Fleming could not make enough pure penicillin to treat anyone. Florey, Chain, Heatley and their Oxford team did that from 1939 to 1941. American laboratories then found ways to make it in huge amounts.
- **Most great discoveries are lucky accidents.** (A popular idea, because accident stories are the ones people remember; *rejected*)  
  For: Penicillin, mauve, radioactivity, X-rays and pulsars all began with something unexpected.  
  Against: Most discoveries come from long, planned work. Even the lucky ones needed years of knowledge to be noticed, and years of checking afterwards.
- **Jocelyn Bell Burnell should have shared the 1974 Nobel Prize.** (Fred Hoyle and many astronomers since; *debated*)  
  For: She built part of the telescope, found the signal, and insisted it was real when others thought it was a fault.  
  Against: Her supervisor designed the project and the telescope, and she herself said in 1977 that the prize should not usually go to research students.
- **Hot water freezes faster than cold water.** (Erasto Mpemba and Denis Osborne (1969), and some later scientists; *debated*)  
  For: Mpemba saw it with ice cream, and Osborne saw it again in his laboratory.  
  Against: Many careful tests since then find that cold water freezes first when everything else is kept the same.
- **A failed experiment is wasted time.** (A common feeling when a test does not work; *rejected*)  
  For: A failed experiment does not answer the question you asked, and it costs time and materials.  
  Against: A failure tells you what does not work, and sometimes gives a result that is useful for a different question, like Silver's weak glue.

## Roots
We have made tape weaker on purpose and heard true stories of mistakes that became discoveries. Now we ask what a 'mistake' really is, and who a discovery belongs to.

**Deep truth.** Your fresh tape held best, but it tore the newspaper when you peeled it. The tape you dabbed on cloth held less, and came off clean. Which tape was best depended on the job you gave it. A result is called a mistake only when it misses what someone wanted.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort results into successes and failures as if that were part of the result itself. But the same weak glue was a failure for building aeroplanes and a success for sticky notes. The glue stayed the same. Only the question asked of it changed.  
*Evidence:* In the Too-Weak Tape Test, strips from one roll were changed only by the number of dabs on a cloth. The fresh strip won at holding and lost at peeling clean. A lightly dabbed strip lost at holding and won at peeling clean and sticking again.

- *who am i:* You are someone who can notice a surprise and ask why. That is where every discovery starts.
- *what is life:* Penicillium mould was making penicillin long before any person noticed. Living things had found it first.
- *what is this world:* Pulsars spun in space for millions of years before 1967. The world is full of things that are there before anyone finds them.

The tape behaved in one way, whoever was watching. Its glue gripped the paper fibres harder than they gripped each other. That one happening was a failure to someone who wanted a clean page and a success to someone who wanted a firm hold. The many meanings come from the many people asking different questions. The happening underneath stays one and the same.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 2:** The Tao Te Ching says that hard and easy, long and short, high and low make each other. We know one only by comparing it with the other. (Your tape was 'strong' or 'weak' only compared with the job and with the other strips. Success and failure are named the same way, by comparing a result with what someone wanted.)
- **Udana 6.4 (the Tittha Sutta):** The Buddha told of a king who asked men who could not see to touch an elephant. Each touched one part, the trunk, an ear, a leg, and each described a different animal. Each was partly right. (A discovery has many parts, like the elephant. The noticer, the checker, the maker and the explainer each held one part. A story that names only one of them describes only one part.)
- Science (settled): Many major discoveries, including penicillin, radioactivity and pulsars, began with unexpected results that the people involved recorded at the time.
- Science (settled): Large discoveries today are made by big teams. The 2016 paper announcing gravitational waves listed more than a thousand authors.
- Other view, Many working scientists: They say a failed experiment really is a failure: it was built to answer one question and did not answer it. Most failures never turn into anything useful. Calling every mistake a hidden success can make people careless about getting things right.
- Practice, The other job (3 min): Pick up one 'failed' thing: the tape that would not hold, a broken crayon or a scrap of torn paper. Hold it in your open hand and take three slow breaths. Look at it as if you have never seen it before. Now think of three jobs it would be perfect for. Say them slowly, one with each breath.
- Ask: Penicillium mould made penicillin long before 1928. Who discovered it first: Fleming, or the mould?
- Ask: If a result is a mistake for one person and a gift for another, what is it really?
- Ask: When you learn something, how many people helped you learn it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Penicillium mould was killing bacteria long before any person noticed. Was penicillin discovered in 1928, or only noticed? (hint: Think about the difference between a thing being there and a person knowing it is there.)
- *deep:* Your weak tape was a failure for one job and a success for another. Is anything a failure for every job? (hint: Think of a broken pencil, a torn sheet of paper, or a burnt chapati. Can you find a use for each?)

- *big:* Why do we remember one name for a discovery, even when many people did the work? (hint: Think about how a story changes when it is told many times. What is easy to remember?)

## Story: The Curd on Top of the Fridge
*The story is made up. The science in it is true: milk sets into curd faster when it is kept warm, and the top of many fridges is warm, because a fridge pushes heat out at the back and warm air rises.*

Divya was ten and lived on the fourth floor of a block of flats in Chennai. Her school science fair was on Monday. Her question was written at the top of a big chart paper: 'Where in our flat does curd set best?'

On Saturday evening, Amma warmed a pan of milk until it felt just warm on Divya's wrist. Divya poured it into three small steel bowls. Into each bowl she stirred one spoon of yesterday's curd.

She put one bowl on the kitchen shelf. She put one on the balcony, in the shade. She put the third bowl inside the fridge. Then she wrote the time on her chart: 7 o'clock.

Her brother Arjun was seven. He wanted a bowl too. So Divya filled a fourth bowl for him, the same way.

"Where shall I keep mine?" he asked.

"Anywhere," said Divya. She was busy drawing her table.

Arjun looked around. Their cat, Mittai, loved milk. Arjun climbed on a stool and pushed his bowl right to the back of the top of the fridge, where Mittai could not reach. Then he forgot all about it.

On Sunday morning, Divya checked her three bowls. She tipped each one a little, carefully.

The bowl from the fridge was still milk. It sloshed. The bowl from the balcony was thick at the bottom but runny on top. The bowl from the kitchen shelf was soft curd that wobbled.

"The kitchen shelf wins," said Divya, and began to write it down.

Then Arjun remembered. He climbed up on the stool and brought down his bowl. He tipped it right over. Nothing moved. The curd was firm and thick. It was the best curd of all.

Divya stared at it. "Where was that?"

"On top of the fridge," said Arjun. "To keep it from Mittai."

"But the top of the fridge isn't in my test," said Divya. She felt cross. Her chart was nearly finished. Now this bowl did not fit.

Selvi akka, who came every morning to help Amma cook, was rolling out chapatis. She laughed. "Put your hand up there," she said.

Divya climbed onto the stool and put her hand on top of the fridge. It was warm, like a sunny wall.

"A fridge is cold inside," said Selvi akka, "but it pushes the heat out at the back. Warm air rises, so the top gets warm. In my village, my mother kept the curd pot near the stove, where the ashes stayed warm all night. Curd likes to be kept warm."

"So that's why the fridge bowl never set," said Divya slowly. "It was too cold. And the balcony was cooler than the shelf in the night."

Arjun grinned. "So I won."

"You didn't plan it," said Divya. "It was an accident."

"It was still the best curd," said Arjun.

Divya thought about that. Arjun's bowl had not been part of her plan. But it was the most interesting result she had. If she left it out, her chart would be tidier. It would also be less true.

"We have to check it," she said. "One bowl could be a fluke."

So on Sunday evening they did it all again, with four bowls this time. Divya put the fourth bowl on top of the fridge on purpose. On Monday morning, before school, she tipped them one by one. The top of the fridge won again.

At the science fair, her teacher, Mrs Rao, read the chart carefully. "This is a very good discovery, Divya," she said. "Well done."

Divya pointed to the bottom of the chart. She had added a box there, in her neatest writing. It said: 'Found by accident by Arjun Kumar, aged 7. Explained by Selvi akka. Checked again by Divya.'

Mrs Rao smiled. "Most discoveries should have a box like that," she said. "Very few of them do."

That evening, Arjun asked if they could test the top of the television next.

**For the parent:** For the parent: milk sets into curd because bacteria from the spoon of old curd grow in the milk and turn its sugar into an acid, which thickens it. They work fastest when it is warm, and very slowly in the cold. The top of a fridge is often warm because the fridge pushes heat out through the coils at the back, and warm air rises. Divya's planned test missed the best place. Arjun's accident found it, Selvi akka explained it, and Divya checked it by repeating the test. A discovery usually needs all three: a surprise, an explanation and a check. Divya's box at the bottom of the chart gives credit to all three people.

- Ask: Why did the curd inside the fridge not set?
- Ask: Arjun did not plan his result. Why did Divya still include it?
- Ask: Why did Divya test the top of the fridge a second time?
- Ask: Who do you think should get the most credit for the discovery: Arjun, Selvi akka or Divya? Why?
- Ask: Mrs Rao said that very few discoveries have a box like Divya's. Why do you think that is?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Too-Weak Tape Test (30 min)
- **question:** If we make tape weaker by dabbing it on cloth, is it worse at every job, or better at some?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will test four strips of tape: one fresh, and three dabbed on a cloth 3, 8 and 15 times. Which strip will hold a paper note best? Which one will come off the newspaper without tearing it? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** All four strips come from the same roll, are cut the same length, and go on the same newspaper page. Each is pressed down the same way, with one thumb rub, and peeled the same way, slowly. Only the number of dabs on the cloth is different.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps with the scissors. Nothing else in this test is dangerous.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then circle the best strip for a note you want to move around, and the best strip for a note that must never fall off.
- **results table:** Strip | Dabs on the cloth | Did the note stay on when shaken? (yes or no) | Did the newspaper tear when you peeled it? (yes, a little, or no) | How many times did it stick again? (0 to 5)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child a strip of tape. 2. Tell them the true story of Spencer Silver's weak glue in two sentences.  
  *Parent says:* In 1968, Spencer Silver tried to make a very strong glue. It came out so weak that paper peeled off it easily. Today we will make our own weak tape and test it.  
  *Child mode:* In 1968, Spencer Silver wanted a very strong glue. His glue came out weak. Paper peeled off it easily. Today you will make weak tape too. Then you will test it.
  - Note: You only need the outline of the story here. The full story comes later, after the test, so the result is still a surprise.
- **s2 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut four strips of tape, each 6 cm long. 2. Fold over 1 cm at one end of each, sticky side to sticky side, to make a handle. 3. Write A, B, C and D on the handles. 4. Stick each one lightly by its far end to the table edge.  
  *Parent says:* A folded end gives you a handle. Then the glue on your fingers won't change the test.  
  *Child mode:* Cut four strips of tape. Each one is 6 cm long. Fold over one end to make a handle. Write A, B, C and D on the handles.
  - Note: Help with the scissors and the measuring. Keep the strips the same length, because a longer strip has more glue and would hold better anyway.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave strip A as it is. 2. Press strip B onto the cotton cloth and lift it off, 3 times. 3. Do the same with strip C 8 times, and strip D 15 times. 4. Use a new part of the cloth each time.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the sticky side after dabbing. Can you see tiny threads stuck on it? Each dab covers a bit more of the glue.  
  *Child mode:* Leave strip A alone. Press strip B on the cloth and lift it. Do it 3 times. Do strip C 8 times. Do strip D 15 times. Look at the sticky side. What do you see?
  - Ask: What is stuck on the tape after dabbing? (good: Tiny threads from the cloth.; Fluff, cotton fibres.)
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table in a notebook. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason for each strip, and write the guesses beside the table.  
  *Parent says:* Which strip do you think will hold a note best? Which one will come off without tearing the paper? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the table. Guess which strip holds a note best. Guess which one won't tear the paper. Say why.
  - Note: Wait for a reason as well as a guess. A common guess is 'A is best at everything, because it is the stickiest.' Don't correct it. The test will answer it.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the newspaper flat. 2. Use each strip to tape one paper note across its top edge onto the newspaper. 3. Rub each strip once with your thumb. 4. Lift the newspaper by one corner and shake it gently 3 times.  
  *Parent says:* Every strip gets one thumb rub and the same three shakes. That keeps the test fair. Which notes stayed on?  
  *Child mode:* Tape one note onto the newspaper with each strip. Rub each strip once with your thumb. Lift the page by a corner. Shake it gently 3 times. Which notes stayed on?
  - Note: Strip A will almost always hold. Strip D often falls off. Write yes or no for each one in the table.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the page flat again. 2. Peel each strip off slowly, one at a time. 3. Look at the newspaper under each one. 4. Write yes, a little, or no for tearing.  
  *Parent says:* Peel slowly. Now look at the paper where the tape was. Is there a grey furry patch? That means some paper came away with the tape.  
  *Child mode:* Peel each strip off slowly. Look at the paper under it. Is there a furry torn patch? Write yes, a little, or no.
  - Note: Fresh tape on newspaper usually lifts a grey, furry patch or tears a strip. The dabbed strips usually peel off clean. If even strip A comes off clean, press it down again, rub it hard with a fingernail, wait 5 minutes and peel again.
  - Check: Fresh tape tore the newspaper when you peeled it. Why? → **The glue gripped the paper's fibres harder than the fibres gripped each other**
- **s7 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take each strip that still has some stick, with its note. 2. Stick it on a fresh part of the newspaper, rub once, and shake. 3. Peel and stick it again, up to 5 times. 4. Count how many times it still held.  
  *Parent says:* A good sticky note can be moved again and again. Let's count how many times each strip sticks again and still holds its note.  
  *Child mode:* Stick each strip again in a new place. Rub once. Shake. Did it hold? Peel it and try again. Count up to 5.
  - Note: Strip A often sticks again poorly, because torn paper fibres now cover its glue. A lightly dabbed strip, often B or C, may stick again several times. Results differ with the cloth and the tape, and that is fine. What matters is what your child saw.
- **s8 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to read the table and say which strip is best for each job.  
  *Parent says:* Which strip would you use to hold a poster up for a month? Which one for a note you want to move around a book? Was A the best at everything?  
  *Child mode:* Look at your table. Which strip is best for a poster that must not fall? Which is best for a note you can move? Was A best at everything?
  - Ask: Was the strongest tape best at every job? (good: No. It held best, but it tore the paper.; The weak tape was better for moving a note around.)
  - Think: Spencer Silver's weak glue failed as aeroplane glue. Which job was it good at?
### e2 · Hot or Cold: Which Freezes First? (30 min)
- **question:** If we put hot water and cold water in the freezer at the same time, which one starts to freeze first?
- **predict:** Guess first. Will the hot water or the cold water get ice on top first? Or will they freeze at the same time? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use two cups that are exactly the same, with the same amount of water, put in the same freezer at the same moment, side by side on the same shelf. Only the starting warmth of the water is different. Check both at the same times.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water and carries the hot cup. Use water that is hot but not boiling. Close the freezer quickly each time, so the food inside stays frozen.
- **record:** Fill in the table at each check. Write which cup got a skin of ice first, and how many minutes it took.
- **results table:** Time after going in the freezer | Hot water cup: what do you see? | Cold water cup: what do you see?
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell the story of Erasto Mpemba in a few sentences.  
  *Parent says:* In 1963, a schoolboy in Tanzania named Erasto Mpemba made ice cream. He put his mixture in the freezer while it was still hot. It froze before his friends' cooler ones. His teacher said he must be confused. What do you think?  
  *Child mode:* In 1963, Erasto Mpemba made ice cream at school in Tanzania. His mixture was still hot. It froze before his friends' cool ones. His teacher said he was confused. What do you think?
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Draw the results table. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Which cup will get ice on top first, the hot one or the cold one? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Draw the table. Guess which cup gets ice first. Hot or cold? Say why.
  - Note: Most children guess the cold one, because it has less cooling to do. That is a good reason. Write the guess down.
- **s3 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Measure 100 ml of hot water into one cup. 2. Measure 100 ml of cold water into the other. 3. Put both cups on the same freezer shelf, side by side, at the same moment. 4. Start the timer.  
  *Parent says:* Same cups, same amount, same shelf, same moment. The only difference is how warm the water is at the start.  
  *Child mode:* The grown-up pours hot water into one cup. You pour cold water into the other. Same amount in both. Put them in the freezer together. Start the timer.
  - Note: Pour the hot water yourself. Use water that is hot to touch but not boiling, from a flask or a kettle left for a few minutes. Don't put a hot cup on a glass shelf or straight onto frozen food packets. Stand both cups on the same kind of surface.
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every 15 minutes, open the freezer, look at both cups, and close it quickly. 2. Write what you see. 3. Between checks, play the card game or read the explanation cards.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the top of each cup. Is there a thin white skin of ice yet? Close the door quickly, so the freezer stays cold.  
  *Child mode:* Every 15 minutes, look at both cups. Is there a skin of ice on top? Close the door fast. Write what you see.
  - Note: In most home freezers, the cold water gets ice first. Sometimes the hot one does. If both freeze at about the same check, write 'same time'. A skin of ice is the easiest thing to judge. Don't wait for the whole cup to turn solid.
  - Wait: Check every 15 minutes. It usually takes 1 to 2 hours for a skin of ice to form.
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the table together and compare the result with the guess.  
  *Parent says:* Which one got ice first? Was your guess right? Mpemba's result was real for his ice cream. Why do you think many tests since then got a different answer?  
  *Child mode:* Which cup got ice first? Was your guess right? Many scientists got a different answer from Mpemba. Why might that be?
  - Ask: Why might different people get different answers in this test? (good: Different freezers and cups.; The hot water loses some water as steam.; It is hard to decide exactly when water has frozen.)
  - Check: Mpemba's teacher said he was confused. What was the best thing to do about his result? → **Test it again, fairly, and see**
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Repeat the test another day, if your child wants, swapping which side of the shelf each cup goes on.  
  *Parent says:* Scientists repeat a test before they trust it. If we try again with the cups swapped, will we get the same answer?  
  *Child mode:* Scientists always test again. Shall we try again another day? This time swap the cups' places.
  - Think: If your answer was different from Mpemba's, does that mean he was wrong? Or that his freezer and cups were different?

## Applications
- Antibiotics such as penicillin, found from a 'spoilt' dish, now cure infections that once killed many people.
- Low-tack glues, like the one on sticky notes, are used for removable labels, painters' masking tape and medical dressings that come off the skin gently.
- Radio astronomers use pulsars as very exact clocks in space, to study gravity and to look for ripples in space called gravitational waves.
- Chemical dyes, starting from Perkin's mauve, colour most of the clothes we wear.
- The Raman effect is used to find out what a material is made of without touching it, for example to check medicines and gems.
- Scientists use credit lists and author rules to decide whose names go on papers. Some journals now ask every author to say what they did.

## Spotted around us
- Sticky notes on a fridge, a desk or a shop counter: Spencer Silver's 'failed' glue at work.
- A strip of medicine tablets in the cupboard. Many antibiotics today grew out of the penicillin story.
- A bright purple or magenta sari in a shop window. Its colour comes from a chemical dye, like Perkin's mauve.
- National Science Day on 28 February, when schools in India hold science fairs to mark the Raman effect.
- A bindi on a card. Its gentle glue lets you peel it off and stick it again, like a sticky note.

## Open questions
- **Is the Mpemba effect real?** Some careful tests find that hot water can freeze first. Many others find it never does when everything else is kept the same. Scientists still argue about what to count as 'frozen' and which differences matter.
- **How many discoveries are sitting unnoticed in old records?** Scientists keep finding new things in old photographs of the sky and old notebooks. Nobody knows how much is still hidden there, because nobody has looked at it all.
- **What is the fairest way to give credit when a thousand people make one discovery?** Prizes, author lists and named discoveries were set up when teams were small. Scientists are still trying out new ways, such as lists of who did which job.
- **How exactly does a pulsar make its radio beam?** Scientists know pulsars are spinning dead stars with very strong magnetic fields. Exactly how the beam of radio waves is made is still being worked out.

## Connected maths
- Bell Burnell's pulsar sends one pulse every one and a third seconds. That is 3 pulses every 4 seconds. How many pulses arrive in 12 seconds? **9 pulses.**. 3 pulses come every 4 seconds. 12 seconds is 3 lots of 4 seconds, because 4 + 4 + 4 = 12. So there are 3 lots of 3 pulses. 3 × 3 = 9. (queues `math.multiplication.equal-groups.L3`)
- In the Too-Weak Tape Test, Kavya's strips stuck again this many times: fresh strip 1, dabbed 3 times 5, dabbed 8 times 3, dabbed 15 times 0. Which strip stuck again most, and how many more times than the fresh strip? **The strip dabbed 3 times. It stuck again 4 more times than the fresh strip.**. The strip dabbed 3 times stuck again 5 times. The fresh strip stuck again 1 time. 5 − 1 = 4. So it stuck 4 more times. The pattern is not 'more dabs, fewer sticks' all the way: the fresh strip did badly because torn paper covered its glue. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)
- Fleming saw the clear ring in 1928. Penicillin was first given to a patient in 1941. How many years did it take to turn the accident into a medicine? **13 years.**. 1941 − 1928 = 13. Count on from 1928: 2 years to 1930, then 11 more to 1941. 2 + 11 = 13. (queues `math.addition-subtraction.word-problems.L3`)
- Kavya's freezer test: the cold cup got ice at 60 minutes, and the hot cup at 90 minutes. She checked every 15 minutes. How many checks did she make before the hot cup got ice? **6 checks.**. She checked at 15, 30, 45, 60, 75 and 90 minutes. That is 6 checks. Or: 90 ÷ 15 = 6. (queues `math.time.duration.L3`)
