# The digestive system: what teeth, enzymes and gut microbes each do to your food
`sci.body.digestion.L5`

**Summary.** A quarter of a teaspoon of saliva turns a spoonful of thick starch paste runny in ten minutes. The same saliva does nothing to a thick gel made from isabgol. Saliva holds an enzyme, and an enzyme cuts one kind of food molecule and no other. Each part of the digestive system adds its own enzymes, and each enzyme works only at the right warmth and acidity. Human enzymes cannot cut fibre. Bacteria in the large intestine cut fibre. Your child races four pastes down a tilted plate to find what stops saliva working. They also time how fast the mouth makes saliva, dissolve sugar whole and crushed, and listen to a gut before and after lunch.

**Intro.** *A cow lives on grass. If you ate only grass, you would starve. You and the cow both have teeth, a stomach and intestines. So what does the cow have that you do not have?* Grass is mostly fibre, and no enzyme made by a cow or a human can cut fibre. An enzyme is a substance made by living cells that cuts one kind of food molecule. The cow keeps a huge store of microbes in her stomach, and the microbes cut the fibre. You keep microbes too, in your large intestine, but far fewer than she does. In this unit you find what each part of your digestive system does to food. You find out why a crushed lump dissolves faster than a whole one, and what that means for chewing. You test what stops the enzyme in your saliva. You find a food that your saliva cannot change at all.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can name the organs of the digestive system in order, and say the job of each.
1. I can name the four kinds of teeth and the three layers of a tooth, and say what each does.
2. I can explain what an enzyme is, and why each enzyme cuts only one kind of food molecule.
3. I can explain, using my Run Test, how heat and acid stop the enzyme in saliva.
4. I can say which juice works on starch, protein and fat, and where each juice is added.
5. I can explain how digested food and water pass into the blood and reach every cell.
6. I can explain what gut microbes do with fibre, and why plant-eating animals need them most.
7. I can plan a fair test with a control, measure the result and explain what I found.

## Terms
- **Digestive system**: The food tube from the mouth to the anus, with the organs that pour juices into it. *e.g.* The mouth, food pipe, stomach, small intestine and large intestine form the tube. The liver and the pancreas send juices in.
- **Incisor**: A flat front tooth with a thin edge, used for cutting. A grown-up has eight incisors. *e.g.* Your incisors cut a neat curve out of a slice of apple.
- **Canine**: A pointed tooth beside the incisors, used for gripping and tearing. People have four canines. *e.g.* You use a canine to tear a tough piece of roti or sugarcane.
- **Premolar and molar**: The broad back teeth, with bumpy tops, used for crushing and grinding. Premolars sit in front of the molars. *e.g.* Your molars grind roasted chana into a paste.
- **Enamel**: The white outer layer of a tooth. Enamel is the hardest substance in the body, and it has no living cells. *e.g.* A chip in the enamel never heals, because no cells are there to mend it.
- **Dentine and pulp**: The two inner layers of a tooth. Dentine is the hard yellowish layer under the enamel. The pulp is the soft centre, with nerves and blood vessels. *e.g.* A tooth hurts when decay reaches the dentine, because the dentine passes signals to nerves in the pulp.
- **Saliva**: The watery juice made by three pairs of glands around the mouth. Saliva wets food, protects teeth and starts to digest starch. *e.g.* Your mouth makes about 1 litre of saliva every day.
- **Enzyme**: A substance made by living cells that speeds up one kind of chemical change. A digestive enzyme cuts one kind of food molecule into smaller pieces. *e.g.* The enzyme in saliva cuts starch. It does nothing to protein, fat or fibre.
- **Amylase**: The enzyme that cuts starch into sugar. Saliva holds amylase, and so does the juice of the pancreas. *e.g.* Amylase in your saliva turns thick starch paste runny in the Run Test.
- **Starch**: A long chain of sugar units that plants make to store food. Rice, wheat, ragi and potato are rich in starch. *e.g.* One starch molecule can hold thousands of sugar units joined in a row.
- **Gastric juice**: The juice made by the stomach wall. Gastric juice holds hydrochloric acid and an enzyme called pepsin. *e.g.* A grown-up's stomach makes about 1.5 litres of gastric juice a day.
- **Pepsin**: The enzyme in gastric juice that starts to cut proteins. Pepsin works only in acid. *e.g.* Pepsin starts to cut the protein in dal, egg and paneer into shorter chains.
- **Bile**: A yellow-green liquid made by the liver and stored in the gall bladder. Bile breaks fat into tiny droplets. Bile is not an enzyme. *e.g.* Bile breaks up the ghee on your rice so that an enzyme can reach the fat.
- **Pancreatic juice**: The juice made by the pancreas. It holds enzymes for starch, protein and fat, and a base that cancels stomach acid. *e.g.* Pancreatic juice finishes cutting the starch that saliva began to cut.
- **Villi**: Tiny finger-shaped bumps that cover the inside of the small intestine. Digested food passes through them into the blood. One of them is called a villus. *e.g.* Each villus is up to about 1 millimetre tall and has fine blood vessels inside.
- **Absorption**: The passing of digested food and water through the wall of the intestine into the blood. *e.g.* Sugar from your rice is absorbed in the small intestine and reaches your muscles in the blood.
- **Fibre**: The parts of plant food that human enzymes cannot cut. Most fibre is made of chains of sugar units. *e.g.* The skin of rajma, the strings in drumstick and the bran of wheat are rich in fibre.
- **Gut microbes**: The bacteria and other tiny living beings who live in the food tube. Most of them live in the large intestine. *e.g.* A grown-up carries tens of trillions of gut microbes, weighing about 200 grams together.
- **Fermentation**: The way microbes break down food without using oxygen. Fermentation makes acids and gases. *e.g.* Bacteria in your large intestine ferment fibre. Bacteria in a pot of milk ferment milk sugar and make curd.
- **Lactose and lactase**: Lactose is the sugar in milk. Lactase is the enzyme in the small intestine that cuts lactose in two. *e.g.* A baby makes plenty of lactase. Most grown-ups in the world make much less.
- **Control**: The part of a fair test where the thing you are testing is left out. You compare everything else with the control. *e.g.* In the Run Test, the cup with water and no saliva is the control.

## Explanations
### The digestive system: one tube, many stations · *defined*
**Definition:** The digestive system breaks food into molecules small enough to pass into the blood, and sends out what cannot be used.

Food is made of molecules, which are particles far too small to see. Many food molecules are long chains. Starch is a chain of sugar units. A protein is a chain of amino acids. A fat is built from smaller units too. Only single units, or pairs of them, can cross the wall of the intestine. So the body must cut the chains. It does this along one tube, 5 to 9 metres long in a grown-up. The stations come in a fixed order. The mouth breaks and wets the food and starts on starch. The food pipe, or oesophagus, squeezes it down to the stomach. The stomach churns it with acid and starts on protein. The small intestine finishes the cutting and absorbs the pieces. The large intestine takes back water and houses the gut microbes. The rectum stores what is left, and it leaves by the anus. Two organs help from outside the tube. The liver sends in bile, and the pancreas sends in pancreatic juice.
- e.g. Food passes through the mouth, the food pipe, the stomach, the small intestine, the large intestine, the rectum and the anus, in that order.
- e.g. Breaking by force is called mechanical digestion. Teeth and the stomach wall do it.
- e.g. Cutting molecules is called chemical digestion. Enzymes do it.
- e.g. Water, salt and vitamins are already small. They need no digesting.
### Teeth: four kinds, two sets and three layers · *defined*
**Definition:** Teeth are the hard tools of the mouth. Each kind of tooth has a shape that fits one job: cutting, tearing or grinding.

A grown-up has 32 teeth. Count one quarter of the mouth, from the middle to the back. There are 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 premolars and 3 molars. Incisors cut. Canines grip and tear. Premolars and molars crush and grind. A small child has only 20 milk teeth, with no premolars. From about the age of six, milk teeth fall out and permanent teeth replace them. At six, the first permanent molars also come in behind the milk teeth. A child of ten usually has about 24 teeth, some milk and some permanent. The last four molars are called wisdom teeth. They usually come in during the late teens or early twenties, and in some people they never come. A tooth has three layers. The enamel on the outside is the hardest substance in the body. Under it is dentine, which is hard but living. In the centre is the pulp, with nerves and blood vessels. The root holds the tooth in the jaw bone. Enamel has no cells, so the body cannot mend it. Acid made by mouth bacteria from sugar slowly dissolves enamel. Chewing matters for digestion because small pieces give enzymes far more surface to work on.
- e.g. One quarter of a grown-up's mouth: 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 premolars, 3 molars. Four quarters make 32.
- e.g. One quarter of a small child's mouth: 2 incisors, 1 canine, 2 molars. Four quarters make 20.
- e.g. A cow has no upper front teeth. A tiger's canines are as long as your finger.
### Saliva: much more than water · *defined*
**Definition:** Saliva is the juice of the mouth. It wets food, starts to digest starch and protects the teeth.

Three pairs of glands make saliva. One pair sits in front of the ears, one pair under the jaw and one pair under the tongue. Together they make about 1 litre a day. Saliva is 99 parts water in 100. The rest does most of the work. A slippery substance called mucus coats the food so that it slides down. The enzyme amylase starts to cut starch into sugar. Other substances in saliva kill some bacteria. Saliva also holds a weak base, which cancels the acid that mouth bacteria make. That is how saliva protects enamel between meals. At night the glands make very little saliva. So food left on the teeth at bedtime does the most harm. Your glands start before you eat. The sight, the smell or even the thought of food sends a signal from the brain to the glands. People do not all make the same amount of amylase. In 2007 a team led by George Perry counted the copies of the amylase gene in people from several groups around the world. The number of copies differed from person to person. Groups whose food is rich in starch had more copies on average.
- e.g. Chew plain rice for two minutes and it tastes faintly sweet, because amylase has made some sugar.
- e.g. Iodine turns starch blue-black. Bread mixed with saliva for ten minutes turns a paler colour, because some of the starch has gone.
- e.g. A dry mouth makes dry food very hard to swallow.
### Enzymes: one enzyme, one job · *defined*
**Definition:** An enzyme is a substance made by living cells that speeds up one kind of chemical change. Each digestive enzyme cuts one kind of food molecule.

An enzyme is a large molecule with a special shape. One part of it is a pocket. Only one kind of food molecule fits the pocket, as one key fits one lock. When the right molecule sits in the pocket, the enzyme cuts it at one join and lets the pieces go. The enzyme itself is not changed, so it can cut again and again. That is why a tiny amount of saliva can thin a whole spoonful of paste. An enzyme that cuts starch is called an amylase. An enzyme that cuts protein is called a protease. An enzyme that cuts fat is called a lipase. Many enzyme names end in '-ase'. The shape of an enzyme matters a great deal, because the shape decides what can fit in the pocket. Enzymes are proteins, and heat changes the shape of a protein for good. You have seen this when the clear part of an egg turns white in a hot pan. A strong acid also changes the shape. An enzyme with the wrong shape cannot hold its food molecule, so it stops working. In the Run Test you find this out with your own saliva.
- e.g. Amylase cuts starch. It cannot cut the protein in dal.
- e.g. Pepsin cuts protein. It cannot cut starch.
- e.g. No human enzyme fits the joins in cellulose, the fibre of plant cell walls.
- e.g. Washing powders with enzymes use the same idea to cut food stains out of clothes.
### Every station keeps the conditions its enzymes need · *defined*
**Definition:** Each part of the food tube is kept at the acidity that suits the enzymes working there.

The whole tube is at body warmth, about 37 degrees Celsius. Digestive enzymes work fast at this warmth. The acidity changes from station to station. Scientists measure acidity on the pH scale. A pH of 7 is neutral, like pure water. Lower numbers are more acid, and higher numbers are more basic. The mouth is close to neutral, and amylase works well there. The stomach is strongly acid, with a pH of about 2. Amylase stops working in acid. Pepsin is the opposite. Pepsin works only in acid. So starch digestion stops in the stomach and protein digestion starts. A swallowed lump of food is not sour in the middle at first. The amylase inside the lump keeps working for some minutes, until the acid soaks in. When food leaves the stomach, the pancreas adds a base that cancels the acid. The small intestine is close to neutral again. Pepsin stops, and the enzymes of the pancreas take over.
- e.g. In the mouth, the acidity is about pH 7, which is close to neutral. This is where amylase works.
- e.g. In the stomach, the acidity is about pH 2, which is strongly acid. Pepsin works there, and amylase stops.
- e.g. In the small intestine, the acidity is about pH 7 again. The enzymes of the pancreas work there, and pepsin stops.
- e.g. Lemon juice has a pH of about 2, which is why it can stand in for stomach acid in the Run Test.
### The stomach: acid, pepsin and a wall that protects itself · *defined*
**Definition:** The stomach stores and churns food, kills many swallowed microbes with acid, and starts to digest protein.

The stomach wall has millions of tiny pits. Cells in the pits make hydrochloric acid, and other cells make pepsin. The acid does three jobs. It kills many of the microbes that come in with food. It loosens the folded shape of proteins. It also switches pepsin on. The cells release pepsin in a form that cannot cut anything. Only when it meets acid, out in the hollow of the stomach, does it become active. This keeps the cells that make pepsin safe from their own enzyme. The stomach wall is made of protein too. A thick coat of mucus, with a base in it, covers the wall and keeps the acid away. The cells of the lining are also replaced every few days. The stomach absorbs almost no food. After some hours of churning, it passes a thick liquid on to the small intestine, a spoonful or so at a time. In 1823 the English doctor William Prout showed that the acid in the stomach is hydrochloric acid.
- e.g. Acid alone does not digest a meal. It prepares protein for pepsin.
- e.g. Pepsin cuts long protein chains into shorter chains. Other enzymes finish the job later.
- e.g. A few kinds of bacteria can live in the stomach. One kind makes a base around themselves to cancel the acid.
### The small intestine: three juices finish the cutting · *defined*
**Definition:** In the small intestine, bile, pancreatic juice and the enzymes of the intestine wall cut food into units small enough to absorb.

The small intestine is 3 to 6 metres long in a grown-up, and most digestion happens here. Three juices work on the food. Bile comes from the liver. It breaks fat into tiny droplets, as dish soap does to grease. Bile is not an enzyme and cuts no molecules. Pancreatic juice comes from the pancreas. It holds amylase for starch, several proteases for protein and lipase for fat. It also holds the base that cancels stomach acid. The third set of enzymes sits on the surface of the villi. These enzymes make the last cuts. One of them cuts malt sugar into two units of glucose. Another cuts table sugar. Another, called lactase, cuts milk sugar. At the end, starch has become glucose. Protein has become single amino acids. Fat has become fatty acids and smaller parts. The pancreas releases its proteases switched off, as the stomach does with pepsin. They become active only inside the intestine.
- e.g. Starch is cut by amylase, first in the mouth and then in the small intestine. The last cut, made on the villi, gives glucose.
- e.g. Protein is cut first by pepsin in the stomach, and then by proteases in the small intestine. It ends up as single amino acids.
- e.g. Fat is first broken into tiny droplets by bile. Then lipase cuts the fat in the small intestine.
- e.g. A person whose gall bladder has been removed still makes bile. The bile drips in slowly, so very fatty meals are harder to digest.
### Absorption: from the tube to every cell · *defined*
**Definition:** Absorption is the passing of digested food and water through the wall of the intestine into the blood.

The lining of the small intestine is folded and covered with villi. This gives it a surface of about 30 square metres. Each villus holds fine blood vessels. Glucose and amino acids pass through the wall of the villus into this blood. The blood goes first to the liver. The liver stores some glucose and makes many harmful substances safer. Then the blood flows to the heart, and the heart pumps it to every cell of the body. The cells use glucose for energy and amino acids for building. Fats take another route. They pass into fine tubes called lymph vessels and join the blood later, near the heart. Water is absorbed too. Textbooks say about 2 litres of water come in with food and drink each day. The body adds about 7 litres of juices. So about 9 litres enter the tube. The small intestine takes back most of it. The large intestine takes back most of the rest. Only about 100 millilitres leave the body in the faeces.
- e.g. A glucose molecule crosses a villus into the blood. The blood carries it to the liver, then to the heart, and then to a muscle in your leg.
- e.g. A fat molecule crosses a villus into a lymph vessel. The lymph joins the blood later, and then the fat reaches a cell that stores or uses it.
- e.g. Of 9 litres of water that enter the tube, the body takes back about 8.9 litres.
### Milk sugar: why many grown-ups cannot digest milk · *defined*
**Definition:** Lactose, the sugar in milk, can be absorbed only after the enzyme lactase cuts it in two. Most grown-ups make little lactase.

Every baby mammal lives on milk, and the baby's small intestine makes plenty of lactase. In most mammals, the body makes less and less lactase after the young stop drinking milk. Humans are the same. In about two in three grown-ups in the world, lactase falls to a low level after early childhood. When such a person drinks a lot of milk, the lactose is not cut. It passes on to the large intestine. The bacteria there ferment it and make gas and acids. This can cause a stomach ache and loose motions. It is not an illness. In 1965 doctors tested healthy grown-ups and found that low lactase was common. In 1981 Rakesh Tandon and his team tested healthy grown-ups in India. About two in three of the people from South India had low lactase. About one in four of the people from North India had it. Some groups of people keep making lactase all their lives. Their ancestors herded cattle, goats or camels, and this change spread among them in the last 10,000 years. Curd is easier to digest than milk. The curd bacteria have used up part of the lactose, and they carry lactase of their own.
- e.g. A person with low lactase can often drink a small tumbler of milk with no trouble, but not a large one.
- e.g. Curd, buttermilk and hard cheese hold less lactose than milk.
- e.g. Lactose-free milk has had lactase added at the dairy, so the cutting is already done.
### Fibre and gut microbes: the digesting you cannot do yourself · *defined*
**Definition:** Gut microbes are the bacteria and other tiny living beings in the food tube. They ferment the fibre that human enzymes cannot cut.

Fibre is made mostly of chains of sugar units, as starch is. The joins between the units are different. The human body makes only a few enzymes for cutting such chains, and none of them fits the joins in fibre. So fibre reaches the large intestine uncut. Tens of trillions of bacteria live there, of many kinds. Together they weigh about 200 grams. They make thousands of enzymes that the human body does not make. One kind of gut bacterium, called Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, has more than 100 enzymes for cutting sugar chains. The bacteria ferment the fibre and make small acids. The cells of the large intestine absorb these acids and get most of their own energy from them. A small part of a person's energy, up to about a tenth, comes this way. The bacteria also make vitamin K and some B vitamins. They take up space and food, so harmful microbes find it hard to settle. They make gases too, and that is normal. A baby gets their first gut microbes at birth, and more from milk and from the people around them. Human milk holds special sugars that the baby cannot digest. These sugars feed the baby's gut bacteria.
- e.g. Foods rich in fibre: rajma, chana, whole wheat, ragi, guava, greens and most vegetables.
- e.g. Foods with almost no fibre: milk, egg, fish, meat, sugar and maida biscuits.
- e.g. A meal of rajma makes more gas than a meal of white rice, because more of it reaches the bacteria.
- e.g. A course of antibiotics kills many gut bacteria as well as harmful ones. The numbers usually recover over some weeks.
### Other animals: the same tube, with different lengths and chambers · *defined*
**Definition:** Humans and other animals with backbones share the same plan of food tube. What changes most with the kind of food is not the small intestine. It is the large intestine and the chambers where microbes work.

Meat is easy to digest, because an animal's own enzymes can cut it. Plants are hard to digest, because so much of a plant is fibre. No animal with a backbone is known to make an enzyme that cuts cellulose. So plant-eaters depend on microbes, and they give the microbes room and time. Many books say that plant-eaters simply have longer guts. In 2021 a team led by Maria Duque-Correa gathered published measurements from 519 kinds of mammal and tested this. They found that the old rule is weak. The small intestine follows body size more than food. Food changes the large intestine most, and the caecum, the side pouch where microbes work. Some plant-eaters also keep a huge chamber in the stomach. The measured lengths below are rounded, and they vary from animal to animal. A cat of about 2.5 kilograms, a meat-eater, has about 1.4 metres of intestine, and only about 25 centimetres of it is large intestine. A rabbit of about 3 kilograms, a plant-eater, has about 3.4 metres, and about 1.5 metres of it is large intestine, with a caecum about 40 centimetres long. A person of about 65 kilograms has about 8 metres of intestine, measured in people who had died, and about 1.6 metres of it is large intestine. Our caecum is small. A thin tube called the appendix hangs from it. Cattle of about 470 kilograms have about 51 metres of intestine, and about 10 metres of it is large intestine. A cow also has a stomach of four chambers. The first chamber, the rumen, holds many litres of grass, water and microbes. Teeth fit the food too. A cat's back teeth slice like scissors. A cow's back teeth are broad and flat for grinding. A crow has no teeth. A muscular part of her stomach, the gizzard, grinds the food.
- e.g. Cat, a meat-eater: about 1.4 metres of intestine, only about 25 centimetres of it large intestine, with sharp slicing teeth.
- e.g. Human, who eats both: about 8 metres of intestine, about 1.6 metres of it large intestine, with four kinds of teeth.
- e.g. Rabbit, a plant-eater: about 3.4 metres of intestine, about 1.5 metres of it large intestine, with a large caecum.
- e.g. Cow, a plant-eater: about 51 metres of intestine, about 10 metres of it large intestine, with a four-chambered stomach.
### Did cooking shrink the human gut? · *idea*
Humans have smaller teeth, weaker jaws and shorter intestines than other apes of our size. We also have much larger brains. In 1995 Leslie Aiello and Peter Wheeler pointed out that a gut and a brain both use a great deal of energy. They suggested that our ancestors could afford a large brain only because the gut became smaller. A smaller gut needs food that is easier to digest. The scientist Richard Wrangham argues that cooking provided it. Heat softens food, bursts starch grains and loosens proteins, so that enzymes work faster. Pounding and grinding help in the same way. On this idea, some of our digesting is done in the kitchen, before the first bite. Wrangham thinks cooking began almost 2 million years ago. The oldest fireplaces that everyone accepts are much younger than that. So scientists still argue about when cooking began and how much it changed our bodies.
- e.g. Cooked rice is digested far more easily than raw rice, because heat and water have burst the starch grains.
- e.g. Idli batter is ground and fermented, so part of the breaking down is done before the meal.
- e.g. Chimpanzees spend several hours a day chewing. People spend less than one hour.
- **Richard Wrangham and those who agree:** Cooking gave more energy from the same food. Over a very long time, our ancestors' teeth and guts became smaller and their brains larger.
- **Scientists who doubt it:** There is no firm sign of fire as old as the changes in teeth and gut. Cutting and pounding raw food, or eating more meat, could explain them.
### Is the appendix a useless leftover? · *debated*
The appendix is a thin tube, about as long as a finger, joined to the first part of the large intestine. People live well after a surgeon removes it. For over a century, many books called it a useless leftover from plant-eating ancestors. In 2007 William Parker's team in the United States proposed another idea. They suggested that the appendix is a safe store for helpful gut bacteria. After an illness has emptied the gut, bacteria from the appendix could fill it again. In 2013 Heather Smith and her team compared the guts of many kinds of mammal. They found that an appendix has appeared separately more than 30 times in the history of mammals. It has almost never been lost again. A part that keeps appearing is unlikely to be useless. But nobody has yet shown clearly what the appendix does in people.
- e.g. Rabbits, some monkeys and humans have an appendix. Dogs and cats do not.
- e.g. In one study, people without an appendix got one serious gut infection again more often. Other studies did not find this.
- **Scientists who think it is useful:** The appendix is rich in defence cells and holds a film of helpful bacteria. It has appeared many times in mammals, which suggests it does a job.
- **Scientists who doubt it:** People without an appendix stay healthy. Until a clear job is shown in people, the safe store is only an idea.

## Mostly agreed
- Digestion is done by enzymes, and each enzyme cuts one kind of food molecule. *How we know:* In 1833 Anselme Payen and Jean-François Persoz separated a substance from sprouted barley that turned starch into sugar. In 1836 Theodor Schwann found pepsin in stomach juice. Hundreds of enzymes have since been separated and their shapes mapped.
- Acid alone does not digest protein. The stomach needs pepsin, and pepsin needs acid. *How we know:* Schwann showed that acid of the same strength as stomach juice hardly changed meat. Stomach juice dissolved it. Pepsin loses its power when the acid is cancelled.
- The enzyme in saliva stops working in acid and after strong heating. *How we know:* Chemists measure how fast amylase cuts starch at each pH and temperature. You can check it in the Run Test with your own saliva.
- Human enzymes cannot cut fibre, and bacteria in the large intestine ferment it. *How we know:* The human genome holds only a few genes for enzymes that cut sugar chains in food. One kind of gut bacterium holds more than 100. Animals raised with no gut microbes need more food to stay the same weight.
- Most grown-ups in the world make little lactase, and this is normal. *How we know:* Tests since 1965 on healthy grown-ups in many countries show low lactase in about two in three. A 1981 study found it in about two in three South Indians tested.
- Plant-eating animals have larger spaces for microbes, in the large intestine, the caecum or an extra stomach chamber, where microbes cut the fibre. *How we know:* Anatomists have measured the guts of hundreds of kinds of animal. In 2021 a study of 519 kinds of mammal found that the small intestine follows body size more than food, and that food changes the large intestine most. A cow's rumen holds a great deal, and scientists can take samples of its microbes.

## How we found out
**Louis Pasteur, Wilhelm Kühne, Eduard Buchner, Maud Menten with Leonor Michaelis, and James Sumner, Pasteur in the 1850s and 1860s, the name 'enzyme' in 1877, Buchner's pressed juice in 1897, Menten and Michaelis's measurements in 1913, and Sumner's crystals in 1926.** In the 1850s and 1860s, the French scientist Louis Pasteur showed that yeast turn sugar into alcohol. Many scientists then held that only a living cell could cause such a change. In 1877 the German scientist Wilhelm Kühne named the substances that do the work 'enzymes', from Greek words meaning 'in yeast'. But nobody had shown one working with no living cell. In 1897 the German chemist Eduard Buchner ground yeast with sand and pressed out a juice. The juice held no living yeast cells. He stirred in a great deal of sugar. The juice began to bubble. The sugar was turning into alcohol and gas. A juice with no living cell could still do the work. Buchner won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1907. In 1913 Maud Menten, a doctor from Canada, and Leonor Michaelis measured how fast an enzyme from yeast works, and the rule still carries both names. In 1926 James Sumner made crystals of one enzyme and showed that it is a protein.  
*What people believed before:* That the work of digesting and fermenting can be done only by a living cell. Buchner showed that a juice with no living cells could still turn sugar into alcohol and gas.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Stomach acid does the digesting.** (A common belief, and the view of many scientists in the 1820s; *rejected*)  
  For: Strong acid does dissolve many things, and the stomach is where we feel a meal.  
  Against: Schwann showed in 1836 that acid alone hardly changes meat. The stomach's enzyme, pepsin, does the cutting. Most digestion happens later, in the small intestine, which is not acid at all.
- **A grown-up who gets a stomach ache from milk is ill.** (Many doctors in Europe and America before the 1960s; *rejected*)  
  For: In their own countries, most grown-ups drank milk comfortably, so those who could not seemed unwell.  
  Against: Tests around the world showed that low lactase is the usual state of a grown-up mammal. Making lactase for life is the newer and rarer condition.
- **Fibre is useless, because we cannot digest it.** (Many food scientists before the 1970s, who called fibre 'roughage'; *rejected*)  
  For: No human enzyme cuts fibre, and it gives little energy.  
  Against: Gut bacteria ferment fibre into acids that feed the cells of the large intestine. Fibre also holds water, which keeps the contents of the intestine soft and moving.
- **One chewing rule is right for every food: chew each mouthful a very long time.** (Horace Fletcher, an American writer on food, around 1900; *rejected*)  
  For: Chewing well does break food small and mix it with saliva, and eating slowly helps people notice when they are full.  
  Against: No test has shown that one rule suits every food. A banana is ready after a few chews, and a piece of coconut needs many more.
- **Cooking is what gave humans small guts and large brains.** (Richard Wrangham, a scientist who studies apes and early humans; *debated*)  
  For: Cooked food gives more energy for less chewing and digesting. Human teeth, jaws and guts are small for an ape of our size.  
  Against: The oldest agreed fireplaces are far younger than the changes in teeth and gut. Other causes, such as pounding food or eating meat, are possible.
- **The appendix is a safe store for helpful bacteria.** (William Parker and his team, from 2007; *debated*)  
  For: The appendix holds a film of helpful bacteria, and it has appeared more than 30 times among mammals.  
  Against: People without an appendix live healthy lives. The job has not been shown clearly in people.

## Roots
In this unit you found out that each enzyme cuts one kind of molecule, and that gut microbes cut the fibre that you cannot. Here we ask what this tells us about food and about the one who eats it.

**Deep truth.** In Starch or Fibre, saliva made the starch paste runny and left the fibre gel thick. Both are chains of sugar units, and an enzyme cuts only the joins it fits. We have no enzyme for the joins in fibre, but gut microbes have. So whether a plant counts as food depends on the enzymes the eater has or can borrow from microbes.  
*Habit it breaks:* We talk about food as if being food were a property of the thing itself, like its colour. But grass is food for a cow and her microbes together, and it is not food for a person alone. Whether something is food depends on whether the eater has the right enzymes for it.  
*Evidence:* In the Run Test and Starch or Fibre, saliva cut one chain of sugar units and left the other. In Lock and Key Chains, only the tooth of the right shape fitted a notch in the strip. In Right Juice, Right Place, the fibre cards ended up with the gut bacteria. In a cow's rumen, the microbes have a great deal of room, and they cut the fibre in grass. That is what makes grass food for her.

- *who am i:* You carry about as many bacteria in your gut as you have human cells in your body. Some of the food you live on is cut by those bacteria, and not by you.
- *what is life:* Every animal takes food apart with enzymes, and each enzyme cuts one kind of join. Many animals, including us, share this work with microbes.
- *what is this world:* The same sugar units join into starch in a potato, and they join in a different way into fibre in a leaf. Whether a meal counts as food depends on who is eating it.

A leaf is made of chains of sugar, which the plant builds from sunlight, water and air. A cow cannot cut some of those chains, but her microbes can. The cow gives the microbes a warm, wet home and a steady supply of grass. The microbes give the cow acids that she lives on. Neither partner could live on grass alone. In your own large intestine, the same kind of arrangement works on a smaller scale.

- **Bhagavad Gita 15.14:** “अहं वैश्वानरो भूत्वा प्राणिनां देहमाश्रितः। / प्राणापानसमायुक्तः पचाम्यन्नं चतुर्विधम्।।” The text says that the divine becomes the fire of digestion, called vaishvanara, in the body of every living being. With the breath it digests the four kinds of food. (Many traditions saw digestion as a kind of fire, which turns food into the stuff of life. Today we see that the work is done by enzymes, at body warmth and in the right acidity, and not by a flame. Both pictures say that digesting is more than breaking food into lumps.)
- **Charaka Samhita, Chikitsasthana:** This old text on medicine treats the body's digestive fire, called agni, as the root of health. It says that health depends on digesting well. (The text teaches that each person digests in their own way. Your own tests showed that two people can differ in amylase and lactase. The text gives a teaching about health. It does not describe enzymes, which were found much later.)
- Science (settled): The mitochondria in your cells came from bacteria that began to live inside a larger cell, more than a billion years ago. Lynn Margulis argued this in 1967, and it is now widely accepted.
- Science (open): How much your gut microbes shape your weight, your mood and your health is still being studied. Many studies show links, and few show which one causes which.
- Other view, Many biologists: A body and its microbes are separate living beings who cooperate. Animals raised with no gut microbes survive in clean laboratories with extra food. So the microbes are partners, not parts of the body. Whether the pair should count as one unit is still argued.

## Story: Paati's Stomach Page
*Nila, Paati, Chithi and Saroja Athai are made up. These facts are real. Kumbakonam in Tamil Nadu is known for its filter coffee, called degree coffee, which is made with undiluted milk. In most grown-ups in the world, the small intestine makes little lactase, the enzyme that cuts milk sugar. A study in 1981 found this in about two in three South Indians tested. When curd sets, bacteria use up part of the milk sugar, and they carry lactase of their own. People differ in how much milk they can take, and many manage a small tumbler with no trouble. A stomach ache that keeps coming back needs a doctor.*

Every time the family went to a wedding, Paati came home with a stomach ache.

"Hall food," she would say, pressing a hand under her ribs. "Too much oil. These cooks do not know when to stop." Then she would lie down with her knees drawn up, and nobody was allowed to talk about lunch.

Nila was ten, and she liked things to add up. This did not add up. At her cousin's wedding in Kumbakonam, Paati had eaten almost nothing from the leaf. She took a little rice, some rasam and one spoon of kootu. She waved the vadai away. Nila ate three vadai, and Nila felt fine.

On the train home, Nila opened her rough notebook and wrote at the top of a page: PAATI'S STOMACH. Under it she ruled three columns. What she ate. Where. Ache or no ache.

She kept the page for two weeks. Paati thought this was very funny. "Write that I ate your head," she said. But she answered every question.

The page grew. Idli and chutney, at home: no ache. Dosa at the bus stand hotel, shining with oil: no ache. That one surprised them both. Curd rice, at home, every single night: no ache. The wedding reception in Thanjavur: ache. Tea at Saroja Athai's house: ache. Bajji from the cart near the temple, fried in oil as black as the pan: no ache.

Nila read down the columns the way she checked a long sum. Oil did not fit. The dosa and the bajji had been swimming in oil. "Outside food" did not fit either. Saroja Athai's kitchen was cleaner than their own.

So what had Paati taken at the two weddings and at Saroja Athai's, and nowhere else?

Nila found her at the stove, making her evening coffee. Paati poured the dark decoction into her tumbler. Then she added milk, one spoon. "To change the colour," she always said. She had drunk it that way for forty years. Milk coffee, in Paati's opinion, was for children and guests.

"Paati, what coffee do they give at a wedding?"

"Degree coffee. What else, in Kumbakonam?" Paati looked pleased at the memory. "Thick milk, a little decoction, froth to the top. The boy comes down the row with the kettle and he does not ask how you take it. And when the coffee is that good, one does not say no to the second tumbler."

"And payasam?"

"Paal payasam. It was my own niece's wedding. Was I to refuse?"

"And Saroja Athai's tea?"

"Boiled in milk. Not one drop of water. She is very proud of it."

Nila went back to her page and wrote MILK at the bottom, and underlined it twice. Then she looked at the third line and crossed it out. Curd rice, every single night, no ache. Curd was milk. Paati ate a small hill of it.

She took the notebook to Chithi, her mother's younger sister, who tested milk at the dairy cooperative. Chithi read the whole page before she said anything. That was why Nila had chosen her.

"Your columns are right," said Chithi. "Milk gives her the ache, and curd does not. So what is the difference between milk and curd?"

"Curd is thick. And sour."

"Why is it sour?"

Nila set the curd every night, a spoon of old curd into warm milk. She knew this one. "The bacteria. They eat something in the milk and make it sour."

"They eat the sugar in the milk. By morning a good part of it is gone. Now listen. Milk sugar is too big to pass through the wall of the intestine. Something has to cut each piece in two first. A baby's intestine makes a cutter for it, an enzyme, because a baby lives on milk. In most people the intestine makes less and less of it as they grow up. Paati's makes hardly any now."

"So in her the milk sugar does not get cut."

"It goes straight through the small intestine to the large intestine. And who lives there?"

"More bacteria." Nila sat back. "They eat it there instead. And they make sour things. And gas?"

"A lot of gas, in a closed place. That is the ache."

Nila looked at her page. It was the same milk sugar, and nearly the same kind of eaters. In the curd pot, the bacteria did their eating on the kitchen shelf, the night before. In Paati, they did it afterwards, inside.

"Is Paati ill?"

"No. Most grown-ups in the world are like her. I am like her. The people who can drink a litre of milk at fifty are the unusual ones."

Nila told Paati all of it that evening. Paati listened with her chin up.

"A page in a rough notebook," she said. "Against forty years of knowing my own stomach." She thought for a while. "Then make me a wedding coffee. Here, in my own kitchen, in my own tumbler. No hall, no cook, no oil. If I ache, your page wins."

So on Sunday morning Nila made it, with more milk than decoction and froth to the top. Paati drank it slowly and said it was not bad for a first attempt. Then she asked for a second tumbler, to make the test fair.

Before noon she was lying down with her knees drawn up. "Write it down," she said crossly. "Go on. Write it down."

At the next wedding, in the month of Thai, the boy came down the row with his kettle. Paati put her hand flat over her tumbler.

"Decoction only," she said. "And one spoon of milk, to change the colour." She let the payasam pass. She took a second helping of curd rice.

On the train home she slept sitting up, with both hands in her lap. Nila took out the notebook. Under the last line of the page she wrote: Not the oil. Not the cook. Paati is not ill. She is short of one enzyme. Then she ruled a line under it, the way she did when a sum came out.

**For the parent:** For the parent: Paati blames the oil in wedding food for her stomach aches. Nila's page shows that oily food never gives her an ache, and milk does. But curd, which is made from milk, is safe. Lactose, the sugar in milk, must be cut in two by the enzyme lactase before it can be absorbed. Most grown-ups make little lactase, so the lactose reaches the bacteria of the large intestine, who ferment it and make gas. Curd bacteria have used up part of the lactose before the meal. One missing enzyme explains every line of the page.

- Ask: Why did oil not fit the facts on Nila's page?
- Ask: Why does milk give some grown-ups a stomach ache when curd does not?
- Ask: Paati asked for a second tumbler 'to make the test fair'. What did she mean?
- Ask: Chithi said the people who can drink a litre of milk at fifty are the unusual ones. Why might that surprise many people?
- Ask: What question about food and your own body could you answer with a page of three columns?

- **predict:** Guess first. You will add saliva to thick starch paste and to thick fibre gel. Will the fibre gel turn runny, as the starch paste does? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** There are four cups. Starch paste gets water in one cup and saliva in another. Fibre gel gets water in one cup and saliva in another. Every cup holds 1 tablespoon of paste or gel and half a teaspoon of liquid. Every cup is stirred 20 times and waits 10 minutes. Each food has its own water cup as a control, so you compare starch with starch and gel with gel.
- **safety:** Each person uses only their own saliva. Nobody eats the pastes or gels. Wash hands, cups and the plate with soap afterwards.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Then write one sentence that starts 'The enzyme in saliva can cut' and one that starts 'It cannot cut'.
- **results table:** Cup | Thick food | Liquid added | How far the blob ran in 1 minute (centimetres)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Recall the Run Test, where saliva turned the starch paste runny. 2. Show the isabgol husk and say that it is plant fibre. 3. Ask for a guess and a reason, and write both down.

  - Ask: Why do you think saliva will thin the gel, or will not? (good: Saliva digests food, and this is food, so it should.; The enzyme may fit only starch, so it may not.; Both are thick, so saliva should thin both.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Stir the husk into the water now, because the gel needs 15 minutes to thicken.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Your child rinses their mouth and collects about half a teaspoon of saliva. 2. Stir in half a teaspoon of water. 3.   - Note: The usual result: saliva changes the starch and leaves the gel as it was. The enzyme fits starch and nothing else. If the two gel blobs differ by a centimetre or two, that is within what two blobs of the same gel would do.

### e3 · Listen to a Gut: is the food tube busier before lunch or after? (25 min)
- **question:** The stomach and intestines squeeze and mix their contents, and this makes sounds. Does a belly make more sounds just before lunch, or 20 minutes after lunch?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will count belly sounds for three minutes before lunch and for three minutes after lunch. When will you count more? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Listen to the same person both times, lying on their back on the same mat. Put your ear on the same spot, just below and to the right of the navel. Count for exactly 3 minutes each time, in a quiet room with the fan off if you can. One count is not much to go on. So swap places and count the other person's belly too.
- **safety:** Ask before you listen, and listen over a thin shirt. Rest your ear or the tube gently. Do not press on a full stomach.
- **record:** Fill in the results table for both people. Write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you heard, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Whose belly | Sounds in 3 minutes before lunch | Sounds in 3 minutes, 20 minutes after lunch | Which was more?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Explain the test. 2. Ask for a guess and a reason. 3. Write both in the notebook.  
  *Parent says:* We will count the sounds a belly makes, before lunch and after lunch. When do you think it will make more sounds? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You will count belly sounds before and after lunch. When will there be more? Why? Write your guess.
  - Ask: Why do you think there will be more sounds before lunch, or after? (good: A hungry stomach growls, so before.; After lunch there is food to mix, so after.; The gut works all the time, so about the same.)
  - Note: All three guesses are reasonable, and real bellies differ. Do not say which is right.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Just before lunch, one person lies on their back. 2. The other rests an ear or a tube just below and to the right of the navel. 3. Count every gurgle, rumble or squeak for 3 minutes. 4. Swap places and count again.  
  *Parent says:* Lie still and breathe quietly. I will start the timer. Make a tally mark for every sound you hear, loud or soft.  
  *Child mode:* Put your ear on the belly, below the navel. Count every sound for three minutes. Then swap.
  - Note: Long quiet gaps are normal before a meal. A count of anything from 0 to 20 in three minutes is possible. A long rolling growl counts as one sound.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Eat lunch and note the time you finish. 2. After 20 minutes, listen again on the same spot for 3 minutes. 3. Swap places and count again.  
  *Parent says:* Twenty minutes have passed since lunch. Listen on the same spot as before. Count for three minutes.  
  *Child mode:* Wait twenty minutes after lunch. Listen on the same spot. Count for three minutes. Then swap.
  - Note: Most people hear more sounds after the meal, often a soft, steady gurgling. Some people hear the loudest sounds before lunch. Write down what you really hear.
  - Check: In Listen to a Gut, what makes the sounds you hear in a belly? → **Gut muscles moving food, juice and gas**
  - Wait: Do this 20 minutes after lunch on the same day
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the four counts side by side. 2. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they heard, and why it happened. 3. Then explain the cleaning wave of an empty gut.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you thought would happen, what you heard, and why you think it happened. Was the gut ever completely silent?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen. Say what you heard. Say why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Does the gut stop working when it is empty? (good: No, it still made some sounds before lunch.; It moves even when there is no meal to digest.)
  - Note: After a meal, the stomach and small intestine mix the food steadily, so most people hear more sounds. An empty gut is not idle. About every 90 to 120 minutes, a strong wave of squeezing sweeps from the stomach along the small intestine and clears out leftovers. That wave is one cause of a growling stomach. If your two bellies gave opposite results, say so in your record. Two people are too few to settle the question.

## Applications
- Lactose-free milk has had lactase added at the dairy, so the milk sugar is already cut. Many shops also sell lactase tablets for people who make little lactase.
- Raw papaya and pineapple hold enzymes that cut protein, called papain and bromelain. Cooks in many parts of India have used raw papaya to make tough meat tender.
- Sprouted ragi flour holds amylase. Mixed into a thick ragi porridge, it makes the porridge thinner, the way saliva thinned the starch paste. Porridges for small children are often made this way.
- Some people make too little pancreatic juice, so their food is not fully digested. Doctors give them capsules of the enzymes that the pancreas should make, and take care that the capsules are not dissolved by stomach acid.
- Farmers use a chaff cutter to chop fodder into short pieces. A cow's rumen microbes then have more surface to work on, and the cow wastes less of what she eats.
- Dentists tell people to rinse after sweet food, and to use toothpaste with fluoride. Fluoride makes enamel harder to dissolve in the acid made by mouth bacteria.
- Antibiotics kill many gut bacteria as well as harmful ones. Doctors give them only when they are needed, and the gut microbes usually recover over some weeks.

## Open questions
- **What does the appendix do in people?** One idea is that it keeps a store of helpful bacteria. An appendix has appeared separately more than 30 times in mammals. But nobody has shown clearly what it does in a person.
- **How much do gut microbes change a person's mood, weight and health?** In mice raised with no gut microbes, the brain and behaviour grow differently. In people, studies show links, but they rarely show which came first. Scientists are still testing.
- **Which mixture of gut microbes is a healthy one?** Healthy people carry very different mixtures, which also change with age and food. Scientists have not found one ideal mixture.
- **When did our ancestors start cooking, and how much did it change our guts?** Richard Wrangham thinks cooking began almost 2 million years ago. The oldest fireplaces that everyone accepts are much younger. The question is still open.
- **How many kinds of microbes live in the human gut?** Many kinds in one person are counted, and many have never been grown in a laboratory. Scientists know most of them only from their genes.

How does its floor compare with that surface? **The floor is also 30 square metres. They are about the same.**. The floor is 6 × 5 = 30 square metres. So the folded lining of the small intestine has about as much surface as the floor of one room. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L5`)

## Warm-up
- What is an enzyme? → A substance made by living cells that speeds up one kind of chemical change, such as cutting one kind of food molecule.
- Which enzyme starts to cut starch in the mouth? → Amylase, which is in saliva.
- Why does amylase stop working in the stomach? → The strong acid changes its shape, so it can no longer hold starch.
- What does pepsin need to work? → Acid.
- What does bile do to fat? → It breaks the fat into tiny droplets, so that lipase can reach more of it.
- Why can people not digest fibre? → No human enzyme fits the joins in fibre.
- Who cuts the fibre in the large intestine? → Gut bacteria, who ferment it into small acids.
- Name the four kinds of teeth. → Incisors, canines, premolars and molars.
