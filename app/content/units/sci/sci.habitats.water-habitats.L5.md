# Life in water: lakes, rivers and the deep sea, and where their light and food come from
`sci.habitats.water-habitats.L5`

**Summary.** Sunlight reaches only the top 200 metres of the sea, yet fish live more than 8,000 metres down. How do they find food in the dark? In this lesson you will lower a white disc into cloudy water to see how fast light fades, and follow the Kaveri from the hills to the sea.

**Intro.** *The Ganga river dolphin's eyes have no lens. She can tell light from dark, and that is all. Yet she catches fast fish every day in water as brown as tea. How does she find them?* She uses sound. She makes quick clicks and listens for the echoes that come back from fish, sandbanks and boats. Her river is full of fine mud, and light cannot pass far through muddy water. Eyes are of little use there, so her kind came to depend on ears. Light fades in clear water too, only more slowly. Even in the clearest sea, the water below about 200 metres is too dim for any plant to grow. Most of the sea is much deeper than that. In this lesson you measure how light fades in water. Then you find out what that means for everyone who lives in a lake, a river or the deep sea.

## Terms
- **Freshwater habitat**: A place where living beings make their home in water that is not salty. Lakes, ponds, tanks, streams, rivers and marshes are freshwater habitats. *e.g.* The Kaveri river and the tank beside a village temple are both freshwater habitats.
- **Wetland**: Land that is covered with shallow water, or soaked with water, for all or part of the year. *e.g.* Pallikaranai marsh in Chennai is a wetland. So are the paddy fields and backwaters around Vembanad lake in Kerala.
- **Sunlit zone**: The top layer of a lake or sea, where there is enough light for plants and algae to make food. *e.g.* In the open sea the sunlit zone is about 200 metres deep. In a muddy pond the sunlit zone may be less than one metre deep.
- **Turbid**: Cloudy with tiny bits floating in the water, such as mud or algae. Turbid water lets less light through. *e.g.* The Godavari in flood is turbid and red-brown. A hill stream in winter is clear.
- **Secchi depth**: The depth at which a white disc lowered into water can no longer be seen. Scientists use it to say how clear the water is. *e.g.* In a clear mountain lake the Secchi depth can be many metres. In a green village pond the Secchi depth may be 30 centimetres.
- **Phytoplankton**: Tiny algae and bacteria who drift in the sunlit water and make food from light, as plants do. *e.g.* Phytoplankton turn a pond green in summer. They feed tiny animals, who feed fish.
- **Thermocline**: The thin layer in a lake or sea where warm top water meets cold deep water, and the temperature drops quickly. *e.g.* A swimmer who dives in a deep lake in summer feels the water turn cold suddenly. They have reached the thermocline.
- **Turnover**: The mixing of a lake from top to bottom. It happens when the top water cools until the whole lake is at about the same temperature. *e.g.* After turnover, oxygen from the top reaches the bottom of the lake again.
- **Source and mouth**: The source is where a river begins. The mouth is where the river ends, in the sea or a lake or another river. *e.g.* The Kaveri's source is at Talakaveri in the hills of Kodagu. The river's mouth is on the Bay of Bengal in Tamil Nadu.
- **Tributary**: A smaller river or stream that flows into a bigger river. *e.g.* The Kabini and the Bhavani are tributaries of the Kaveri.
- **Sediment**: Sand, silt and clay carried by water and later dropped. Silt is finer than sand, and clay is finer still. *e.g.* After a flood goes down, a thin layer of soft silt is left on the fields. That silt is sediment.
- **Floodplain**: The flat land beside a river that goes under water when the river floods. *e.g.* The wide flat fields beside the Brahmaputra in Assam are a floodplain. They are covered almost every monsoon.
- **Delta**: Flat land built at the mouth of a river from the sediment the river drops as the water slows down. *e.g.* The Kaveri delta around Thanjavur and the Godavari delta in Andhra Pradesh are both built from river silt.
- **Mangrove**: A mangrove is a tree that grows in salty mud at the mouth of a river, with roots that stand in the water and take in air. *e.g.* The Sundarbans in West Bengal is a mangrove forest, and so is Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu.
- **Barrage**: A low wall with gates built across a river, to raise the water and send some of it into canals. A dam is a higher wall that holds back a deep lake. *e.g.* The barrage at Dowleswaram on the Godavari sends water to the canals of the delta.
- **Echolocation**: Finding things by making a sound and listening for the echo that comes back. *e.g.* A Ganga river dolphin finds a fish in muddy water by echolocation. Many bats find moths in the dark in the same way.
- **Coral reef**: A coral reef is a long ridge in warm shallow sea, built over thousands of years by tiny animals called corals. Fish and many other sea animals live in it. *e.g.* The Gulf of Mannar in Tamil Nadu and the islands of Lakshadweep have coral reefs.
- **Pressure**: How hard something presses on each small patch of a surface. Water presses on everything in it, from every side. *e.g.* At the bottom of a swimming pool your ears feel the pressure of the water above you.
- **Marine snow**: Small soft flakes that sink through the sea. They are made of dead plankton, droppings, slime and dust stuck together. *e.g.* A sea cucumber on the deep sea floor eats marine snow that left the surface weeks before.
- **Bioluminescence**: Light made by a living being through a chemical reaction in their body. *e.g.* A firefly's flash is bioluminescence. So is the blue glow of a lanternfish in the deep sea.
- **Chemosynthesis**: Making food by using the energy in chemicals, without light. Some bacteria do this. *e.g.* Bacteria at hot springs on the sea floor use a gas called hydrogen sulphide to make food by chemosynthesis.

## Explanations
### Fresh water: a small space with a great many lives · *defined*
**Definition:** A freshwater habitat is a home in water with very little salt. Still water habitats are lakes, ponds, tanks and marshes. Flowing water habitats are springs, streams and rivers.

Lakes, rivers and wetlands cover less than 1 part in 100 of the Earth's surface. Yet more than 1 in 10 of all known species live in them, and about half of all the kinds of fish. Why so many in so little space? Fresh waters are cut off from each other. A fish cannot walk from the Kaveri to the Godavari. So each river and lake has had millions of years to grow kinds of its own. Still water and flowing water ask different things of a body. In a lake, a tiny drifting alga can stay in the light for weeks. In a fast stream the same alga would be swept away in minutes. So stream life holds on to stones, and lake life drifts. People have made freshwater habitats too. The tanks of Tamil Nadu and Karnataka are hundreds of years old, and fish, frogs, birds and lotus plants live in them.
- e.g. Still water: Vembanad lake in Kerala, Kolleru lake in Andhra Pradesh, Loktak lake in Manipur and a village tank near Madurai.
- e.g. Flowing water: a hill stream in Kodagu, the Kaveri at Srirangapatna and the Brahmaputra in Assam.
- e.g. Wetlands: Pallikaranai marsh in Chennai, where more than 100 kinds of birds have been counted inside a big city.
### Light fades as it goes down through water · *defined*
**Definition:** Water takes light away a little at a time. Each layer of water lets through only a share of the light that entered it. So the light gets weaker and weaker with depth.

Two things take light away. Water soaks up some light and turns it into warmth. This is called absorbing. Tiny bits in the water, such as mud, algae or droplets of milk fat, bounce light off in all directions. This is called scattering. Suppose one hand's depth of cloudy water lets through half the light. The next hand's depth lets through half of that half, which is a quarter. The third leaves one eighth. That is why light does not fade evenly. It falls fast at first and then more slowly, and never quite reaches zero. Clear water does the same over a longer distance. Even in the clearest sea, the water below about 200 metres is too dim for photosynthesis. Photosynthesis is the way plants and algae make food from light, water and a gas in the air called carbon dioxide. Water also takes colours away unequally. Red light is soaked up first, in the upper water. Blue light travels deepest. That is why deep water looks blue, and why a red animal in the deep sea looks black.
- e.g. In the Vanishing Disc Test, each spoon of milk makes the white disc vanish at a smaller depth.
- e.g. The Godavari in flood is so full of silt that a hand held under the surface cannot be seen.
- e.g. Scientists lower a white disc to measure how clear a lake is. The depth at which the disc vanishes is called the Secchi depth.
### The sunlit layer feeds everything below it · *defined*
**Definition:** Plants and algae can make food only where there is enough light. In water, that is the top layer, called the sunlit zone. Almost every animal in the water depends on food made there.

A lake has three parts. Near the shore the water is shallow, so light reaches the bottom. Rooted plants such as lotus, water lily and reeds grow there, and young fish hide among them. In the open water the bottom is too deep for roots. The food makers there are phytoplankton, tiny drifting algae and bacteria. They must stay in the sunlit zone to live. Below the sunlit zone no food is made. The animals there eat what sinks down: dead plankton, droppings and dead animals. The sea is built the same way, only far deeper. Phytoplankton in the top 200 metres of the sea make about half of all the oxygen made on Earth each year. So the amount of life in a whole lake or sea depends on a thin bright layer at the top. If mud or waste makes the water turbid, that layer becomes thinner, and less food is made. Pond plants grow in three ways. Lotus and water lily are rooted in the mud. Their long stalks lift their leaves and flowers up to the light. Water hyacinth and duckweed float free. They have no roots in the mud, and their roots hang in the water. Water hyacinth came to India from South America. She spreads so fast that she can cover a whole pond, and then no light reaches the plants below.
- e.g. Lotus and water lily grow only near the edge of a deep tank, where their stalks can reach from the mud to the light.
- e.g. A catfish at the bottom of a lake eats worms, and the worms eat dead plankton that sank from the top.
- e.g. When soil washes into a lake from bare land, the water turns cloudy and the underwater plants at the edge die back.
### A deep lake in summer has two layers · *defined*
**Definition:** Warm water is lighter than cold water, so it floats on top. In a deep lake the Sun warms the top water, and a warm layer forms over a cold one. The thin band between them is the thermocline.

Why does this matter to a fish? Oxygen enters a lake at the top, from the air and from the algae and plants in the light. Wind stirs the warm layer, so that layer stays rich in oxygen. But the wind cannot easily push light warm water down into heavy cold water. So the two layers hardly mix. In the cold layer, bacteria and small animals keep using oxygen as they eat the dead plankton that sinks down. No new oxygen arrives. By the end of a long hot season, the deep water may have almost none, and fish must stay higher up. When the weather cools, the top water cools too. It becomes as heavy as the water below, and sinks. Now wind can mix the whole lake. This is turnover, and it carries oxygen to the bottom again. A shallow pond does not form lasting layers, because wind stirs it to the bottom. A sudden turnover can also do harm. If a storm mixes a lake very fast, water with no oxygen reaches the top, and fish can die.
- e.g. In the Warm on Top experiment, brown warm water stays above clear cold water until the top is cooled.
- e.g. In a deep reservoir, the water let out from the bottom of the dam is much colder than the water at the surface.
- e.g. Fish farmers run paddle wheels in their ponds on hot still nights, to stir oxygen into the water.
### A river changes from source to sea, and so do those who live in the river · *defined*
**Definition:** A river starts small, steep and fast, and ends wide, flat and slow. The water, the bed and the food change along the way, so different living beings fit each stretch.

Near the source, the stream is narrow and often shaded by forest. The water is cold, clear and full of oxygen, because it tumbles over rocks. Little light reaches it, so few algae grow. The main food is dead leaves that fall in from the trees. Insect young shred the leaves, and small fish eat the insects. Animals here have flat bodies or suckers, to hold on in the current. In the middle stretch the river is wider, and sunlight reaches the bed. Algae grow on the stones, and snails and insects graze on them. Big strong swimmers live here, such as the mahseer. In the last stretch the river is deep, slow and turbid. Few plants grow on the dark bed. The food is fine bits washed down from upstream, and plankton drifting in the slow water. Catfish, carp, turtles, river dolphins and crocodiles live here. Scientists call this pattern the river continuum. It fits many rivers well. It fits less well where a river crosses a dry land or is cut by many dams. A fish uses her fins to move and to steer. The tail fin sweeps from side to side and pushes her forward. The fins on her sides steer and stop her. The fin on her back keeps her upright. A fast swimmer like the mahseer has a strong tail. A fish who holds on in a fast stream, like the hill-stream loach, has wide flat fins that press on the rock.
- e.g. The Kaveri begins at Talakaveri in the forested hills of Kodagu and reaches the sea after about 800 kilometres.
- e.g. The humpback mahseer, a very large fish, lives only in the Kaveri and its tributaries. She needs clean, flowing, rocky stretches.
- e.g. The Ganga river dolphin lives in the deep, slow, muddy stretches of the Ganga and the Brahmaputra.
### Floods, floodplains and deltas: how a river feeds the land and the sea · *defined*
**Definition:** A river carries more than water. It carries sediment, dead leaves and dissolved plant food from the whole land it drains. In flood, the river spreads these over the floodplain. At its mouth the river drops them to build a delta, and the rest feeds the sea.

In the monsoon a river can carry many times more water than in the dry months. The water spills over the banks and slows down. Slow water cannot hold its silt, so the silt settles on the land. That is why floodplain and delta soil is so good for crops. The flood does more than that. The warm shallow water on the floodplain fills with plankton and insects. Many river fish swim out onto the flooded land to lay their eggs there, because the young find food and hiding places. When the water goes down, the young fish return to the river. Scientists call this yearly rise and fall the flood pulse. At the river's mouth, fresh water meets the sea. The silt builds new land, where mangrove trees take root. The dissolved plant food feeds the plankton of the coast, and the plankton feed fish. So rain that fell on hills a thousand kilometres away ends up as fish in the sea.
- e.g. The Godavari begins at Trimbakeshwar in Maharashtra, flows 1,465 kilometres, and builds a delta in Andhra Pradesh. The Coringa mangrove forest grows there.
- e.g. In Assam, the beels beside the Brahmaputra fill in every flood. A beel is a floodplain lake, and many fish breed there.
- e.g. The paddy fields of Thanjavur stand on silt that the Kaveri brought down from the hills of Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.
### Mangroves: forests in salty mud · *defined*
**Definition:** Mangroves are trees that grow in the salty mud where a river meets the sea. Their roots hold the soil, slow the waves and shelter young fish.

Mud at a river's mouth is soft, salty and short of air. Mangrove trees have found ways to live in it. Some have arched stilt roots that spread wide and hold the tree up. Others send up small roots that stand out of the mud like pencils, and these take in air. Together, the roots hold the mud, so the tide does not wash the land away. They also slow the waves, so a storm does less harm to the land behind. Among the roots, young fish, crabs and prawns hide and feed. The Sundarbans is the largest mangrove forest in the world. It stands where the Ganga, Brahmaputra and Meghna meet the sea, in India and Bangladesh. Fishers, crab catchers and honey collectors live and work on its edge. It is also home to the Bengal tiger, who swims from island to island.
- e.g. After cyclones, villages behind thick mangroves have often had less damage than villages with none.
- e.g. The Pichavaram mangroves in Tamil Nadu are a maze of small waterways where egrets and fish live.
- **People of the Sundarbans:** The forest feeds us with fish, crabs and honey, and it stands between us and the storm. But it is also home to tigers, so we must enter with care and respect.
### Fish who travel between the sea and the river · *defined*
**Definition:** Some fish live in one kind of water and breed in another. To them a river and the sea are two parts of one home. They need the whole way to be open.

The hilsa lives in the Bay of Bengal. In the monsoon, when rivers pour muddy fresh water into the sea, hilsa swim up the rivers to lay their eggs. The young drift and swim back down to the sea. On the Godavari, the hilsa caught in the flooded river is called pulasa. A wall across a river breaks this journey. The Farakka Barrage was built across the Ganga in West Bengal and began working in 1975. Before that, hilsa swam far upstream, as far as Prayagraj in Uttar Pradesh. After it, the hilsa catch above the barrage fell to almost nothing. A fish pass can help. A fish pass is a side channel or a row of small pools that lets fish swim round or over a wall. It must suit the fish of that river. Many river fish, hilsa among them, are not strong jumpers, so a ladder with high steps does not help them. A dam also changes the water itself. It holds back silt, slows the flow and evens out the floods that tell fish when to breed.
- e.g. Hilsa swim from the Bay of Bengal up the Godavari, the Hooghly and the Padma in the monsoon.
- e.g. Eels do the opposite. They grow up in rivers and ponds and swim to the sea to breed.
- e.g. The Ganga river dolphin now lives in stretches cut apart by dams and barrages, and groups cannot easily meet.
### Seeing with sound: the Ganga river dolphin · *defined*
**Definition:** The Ganga river dolphin finds her way and her food by echolocation. She makes clicks and listens for the echoes.

Sound travels well through muddy water, and light does not. The Ganga river dolphin's eyes are tiny and have no lens. They can tell light from dark, and probably little more. Her clicks leave her head many times each second. An echo that comes back quickly means something is near. From the echoes she builds a picture of the river around her: the bed, the bank, a net, a fish. In shallow water she often swims on her side, with one flipper trailing near the bed. She is a mammal, so she breathes air and must come up to the surface often. She was named India's national aquatic animal in 2009. India's first full count of river dolphins, reported in 2025, found about 6,300 of them, almost all in the Ganga and Brahmaputra rivers. The main dangers are fishing nets, in which a dolphin can drown, and dams, low water, pollution and the noise of boat engines. Noise matters because it covers the echoes she depends on.
- e.g. A Ganga river dolphin can catch fish at night and in water where a person cannot see their own hand.
- e.g. The Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary in Bihar protects a stretch of the Ganga for river dolphins.
- e.g. Ravindra Kumar Sinha of Patna studied these dolphins for many years, and is known as the Dolphin Man of India.
### The zones of the ocean · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists divide the ocean into layers by depth. The sunlit zone is the top 200 metres. The twilight zone runs from 200 to 1,000 metres. The midnight zone runs from 1,000 to 4,000 metres. The abyss runs from 4,000 to 6,000 metres. Below 6,000 metres are the trenches.

The average depth of the ocean is about 3,700 metres. So most of the ocean lies below the sunlit zone. In the twilight zone there is a faint blue glow by day, too weak for photosynthesis. Many animals of the twilight zone swim up to the surface at night to feed, and go down again before dawn. Below 1,000 metres no sunlight arrives at all. The water there is cold all year, a few degrees above freezing, in every ocean. The deepest known place is the Challenger Deep in the Pacific Ocean, about 10,935 metres down. All these zones are one body of water. Cold water sinks near the poles and creeps along the bottom of every ocean, carrying oxygen down with it. That is why deep-sea animals can breathe. The deep sea is the largest living space on Earth. People have seen only a very small part of it.
- e.g. Sunlit zone: tuna, sardines, corals, sea turtles and nearly all the seaweeds.
- e.g. Twilight zone: lanternfish, many squids and red prawns.
- e.g. Midnight zone and below: anglerfish, sea cucumbers, brittle stars and snailfish.
### Coral reefs: a city built by tiny animals · *defined*
**Definition:** A coral reef is a ridge in warm, shallow, sunlit sea, built by tiny living animals called corals, and it shelters many kinds of fish and other sea animals.

Corals look like stones or plants, but they are animals. Each coral animal is called a polyp and is only a few millimetres wide. She makes a hard cup of limestone around her body. Over thousands of years, many polyps living side by side build a reef. Tiny algae live inside the polyp's body. The algae use sunlight to make food, and they share it with her. This is why reefs grow only in warm, clear, shallow water. A reef covers less than 1 part in 100 of the sea floor, yet about a quarter of all kinds of sea animals live in or near reefs. The branches and holes give young fish places to hide and feed. A reef also breaks the waves before they reach the shore. When the water gets too warm, the polyp pushes out her algae and turns white. This is called bleaching. Corals can recover if the water cools soon.
- e.g. The Gulf of Mannar, off Tamil Nadu, has reefs where fishing families have fished for generations.
- e.g. The Lakshadweep islands are made of coral reefs, which rise only a few metres above the sea.
- e.g. Warm sea water has bleached reefs near the Andaman Islands and in many other seas.
- **Fishing families of the Gulf of Mannar:** The reef is our field and our kitchen. We know where the fish gather, and we want it to stay healthy. We also need to fish to live, so we must be careful together.
### Why the sea matters to the whole Earth · *defined*
**Definition:** The sea gives oxygen, takes in heat and carbon dioxide, and feeds people. Its life matters to all of Earth, not only to the coast.

The plankton of the sunlit zone make about half of our oxygen. So one in every two breaths you take comes from the sea. The sea also takes in much of the extra heat and carbon dioxide that people put into the air. Carbon dioxide is a gas that traps heat. And fish from the sea are the main food of millions of coastal families. If reefs, mangroves and plankton are harmed, these gifts shrink. That is why people look after the sea, even people who live far from it.
- e.g. A family in Delhi, far from the sea, still breathes oxygen made by sea plankton.
- e.g. A fishing family in Kerala eats and sells sardines every week.
### Pressure in the deep sea, and why it does not crush those who live there · *defined*
**Definition:** Water has weight, so the water above presses on everything below. For every 10 metres you go down, the pressure rises by about the same amount as the whole of the air presses at the surface.

At the surface, the air presses on each square centimetre of you about as hard as a 1 kilogram weight. At 10 metres down the press is doubled. At 4,000 metres it is about 400 times the press at the surface. Why are deep-sea animals not crushed? Pressure crushes things that have gas inside, because gas can be squeezed into a smaller space. Water can hardly be squeezed at all. A deep-sea animal's body is mostly water, and the same pressure pushes from inside and outside. Most deep-sea fish have no air-filled swim bladder. Many have soft, jelly-like flesh and thin bones. Pressure does change how the tiny working parts inside cells fold and move. Deep-sea animals have their own forms of these parts, which work under pressure. Because of this, many deep-sea animals cannot live at the surface, just as a surface fish cannot live in the deep. People go down inside a thick hollow ball of metal that keeps the pressure out.
- e.g. In 2022 a snailfish was filmed 8,336 metres down in a trench near Japan. This is the deepest fish ever filmed.
- e.g. A foam cup sent down on a deep-sea robot comes back much smaller, because the air in the foam was squeezed.
- e.g. India's National Institute of Ocean Technology in Chennai is building Matsya 6000, a vehicle meant to carry three people 6,000 metres down. It has not made that dive yet: a first test dive to 500 metres is planned for early 2027.
### Food in the dark: marine snow · *defined*
**Definition:** Almost all the food of the deep sea is made in the sunlit zone and sinks. The sinking flakes are called marine snow.

Marine snow is dead plankton, droppings, slime and dust, stuck together in soft flakes. A flake sinks roughly 100 metres in a day. So the fall to a sea floor 4,000 metres down takes weeks. On the way, bacteria and animals eat most of it. Only about 1 to 3 parts in 100 of the food made at the surface reach the deep sea floor. That is why deep-sea animals are few and spread far apart. It is also why many of them move slowly and grow slowly, and can wait a long time between meals. Sometimes a very large meal arrives. When a whale dies, the body sinks, and hundreds of animals feed on that one body for years. The explorer William Beebe gave marine snow its name in the 1930s, after watching it through the window of a steel diving ball. In the 1980s the scientist Alice Alldredge showed that the flakes are full of living bacteria and algae, so each flake is a small habitat too.
- e.g. A sea cucumber on the abyss floor swallows mud and digests the marine snow mixed into it.
- e.g. The vampire squid of the twilight zone catches marine snow on a long sticky thread and eats it.
- e.g. A dead whale on the sea floor feeds sleeper sharks, hagfish, crabs and bone-eating worms.
### Food without sunlight: hot springs on the sea floor · *defined*
**Definition:** At some places on the deep sea floor, hot water full of chemicals comes out of the rock. These are hydrothermal vents. Bacteria there make food by chemosynthesis, using the energy in the chemicals.

In February 1977, scientists in the small submarine Alvin dived about 2,500 metres down near the Galápagos Islands. They expected bare rock. They found warm water shimmering out of cracks, and around it giant white clams and red-tipped tube worms taller than a child. What were these animals eating, so far from the light? The tube worm has no mouth and no gut. In 1981 Colleen Cavanaugh, then a student, showed that the worm's body is packed with bacteria. The bacteria take in hydrogen sulphide, the gas that smells of rotten eggs, and use its energy to build food. The worm gives the bacteria a home and carries the gases to them, and the bacteria feed the worm. So here is a food web that does not start with sunlight. It is not fully cut off from the Sun, though. The bacteria and the animals also use oxygen dissolved in the sea water. That oxygen was made by algae and plants in the light.
- e.g. Giant tube worms at vents in the Pacific Ocean can grow more than 2 metres long.
- e.g. Vent water can be hotter than 350 degrees, yet a few metres away the sea is close to freezing.
- e.g. Similar bacteria feed mussels and worms at cold places on the sea floor where gas seeps out of the mud.
### Bodies and lights made for the dark · *defined*
**Definition:** Deep-sea animals have bodies that fit a home with no sunlight, little food and no place to hide. Most of them can make their own light, which is called bioluminescence.

In open water there is nothing to hide behind. So animals of the twilight zone hide by their colour. Many are see-through. Many are red. No red light reaches that depth, so a red body sends no light back and looks black. Deeper still, most animals are black or red. Food is rare, so a meal must not be missed. Some fish have very large mouths and long teeth, and stomachs that stretch to hold prey as big as themselves. Many have large eyes that catch the faintest glow. Others, in the deepest water, have small eyes and depend on touch, smell and the feel of moving water. In one large study off California, about three in every four animals seen between the surface and 4,000 metres could make light. They use it to hide their shadow from below, to find a mate, to draw prey close and to startle a hunter. Most of this light is blue, because blue travels farthest through sea water.
- e.g. A lanternfish has rows of small lights along the belly. Seen from below, the lights match the faint glow from above and hide the fish's shadow.
- e.g. A female anglerfish has a glowing lure above her mouth. The light comes from bacteria who live inside the lure.
- e.g. Deep-sea prawns are bright red in a net at the surface and nearly invisible in their own dark water.
### Should a river be dammed? · *debated*
India has thousands of large dams. A dam stores monsoon water for the dry months, and many dams make electricity. A dam also changes the river above and below it. People who know rivers well disagree about how to weigh the gains against the losses. The answer may differ from river to river.
- e.g. The Mettur dam on the Kaveri stores water for the paddy fields of the delta.
- e.g. Above the Farakka Barrage on the Ganga, the hilsa catch fell to almost nothing after 1975.
- e.g. The Kallanai, or Grand Anicut, on the Kaveri is a low stone wall first built many centuries ago. It turns water into canals without holding back a deep lake.
- **Many water engineers and farmers:** Most of India's rain falls in about four months. Without stored water there would be one crop a year in many places, and less drinking water for cities. A dam also holds back part of a dangerous flood.
- **Many river ecologists:** A dam stops fish who travel, traps the silt that builds the delta, and evens out the floods that fish breed by. Below a dam, a river can run almost dry for months. These losses are real, and are often left out when a dam is planned.
- **Many fishing families and people of the delta:** The water stored upstream grows someone else's crop. The fish and the silt that are lost were our living. If a dam is built, some water must be let through for the river all year, and those who lose must have a say.
### How deep can a fish live? · *idea*
Nobody has seen a fish deeper than 8,336 metres, though the sea goes down to almost 11,000 metres. Other animals, such as small shrimp-like amphipods and sea cucumbers, live at the very bottom. Why not fish? One idea is about chemistry. Under great pressure, the working parts inside cells are pushed out of shape. Deep-sea fish carry a substance in their bodies that helps to protect these parts, and deeper fish carry more of it. Some scientists think that below about 8,000 metres a fish would need more of this substance than a body can hold. Alan Jamieson, who led the dive that filmed the deepest fish, thinks that fish is close to the limit. This is a good idea with some evidence. It is not yet settled, because very few dives have looked.
- e.g. The deepest fish filmed so far was a young snailfish at 8,336 metres, in 2022.
- e.g. Amphipods, who look like small pale shrimps, have been caught at the bottom of the deepest trenches.

## Mostly agreed
- Fresh waters cover less than 1 part in 100 of the Earth's surface, yet hold more than 1 in 10 of all known species, and about half of all kinds of fish. *How we know:* Scientists have listed and counted the known species of fish, crabs, dragonflies and other freshwater animals. A study of more than 23,000 of these species was published in the journal Nature in 2025. It also found that about one in four is in danger of dying out.
- Light fades with depth in all water, and faster in turbid water. Below about 200 metres, even the clearest sea is too dim for photosynthesis. *How we know:* Light meters lowered into lakes and seas show the light falling by the same share for each metre. The white disc that Angelo Secchi lowered from a ship in 1865 is still used to compare waters.
- In warm weather a deep lake forms a warm top layer and a cold bottom layer, and the bottom layer can lose its oxygen. *How we know:* Thermometers and oxygen meters lowered on a line show a sudden drop in temperature at the thermocline. Below it the oxygen readings fall through the hot season.
- A river carries sediment and plant food to its floodplain, its delta and the sea, and many fish breed by the yearly flood. *How we know:* Deltas are made of river silt, as wells and boreholes dug in them show. Fish counts on floodplains rise after good floods and fall after poor ones.
- Walls across rivers stop fish who travel to breed, unless the fish are given a way round that suits them. *How we know:* Before the Farakka Barrage began working in 1975, hilsa were caught as far up the Ganga as Prayagraj. Afterwards the catch above the barrage fell to almost nothing.
- Animals live at every depth of the sea, down to the bottom of the deepest trenches. *How we know:* Nets, cameras and submarines have brought back animals or pictures from every depth. In 1869 a dredge brought up animals from 4,289 metres. In 2022 a fish was filmed at 8,336 metres.
- Most deep-sea food is made at the surface and sinks. A smaller share is made by bacteria from chemicals at vents and seeps. *How we know:* Traps set at different depths catch less and less sinking food the deeper they hang. At vents, the tube worms were found to be full of bacteria who make food from hydrogen sulphide.

## How we found out
**Edward Forbes, Michael Sars and Charles Wyville Thomson in the 1800s. Jack Corliss and Tjeerd van Andel, who dived in Alvin in 1977. Colleen Cavanaugh, who explained the tube worms in 1981., Forbes wrote in 1843. Thomson's deep dredge was in 1869. The vents were seen in February 1977, and the tube worm paper was published in 1981..** In 1841 the naturalist Edward Forbes dragged a net along the floor of the Aegean Sea. The deeper he went, the fewer animals came up. In 1843 he wrote that life in the sea probably stopped at about 550 metres. For 25 years most scientists agreed. The deep was dark, cold and under huge pressure, so the idea seemed sensible. Then Michael Sars, a professor in Norway, listed hundreds of species from deeper water off his own coast. In 1869 Charles Wyville Thomson brought up animals from 4,289 metres. Forbes had not been careless. He had sampled one sea that is poor in life, and stretched his result to every sea. A second surprise came in 1977. Scientists in the submarine Alvin found hot springs on the sea floor, crowded with giant tube worms. Nobody could say what the worms ate. In 1981 a student, Colleen Cavanaugh, showed that bacteria inside the worms make food from chemicals in the vent water.  
*What people believed before:* That no life exists in the sea below about 550 metres. Dredges in the 1860s brought up animals from far deeper, down to 4,289 metres in 1869. Forbes had sampled one sea that is poor in life.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Nothing can live in the sea below about 550 metres.** (Edward Forbes, a British naturalist, in 1843, and many scientists for about 25 years after him.; *rejected*)  
  For: Forbes dredged the Aegean Sea and found fewer and fewer animals as he went deeper. The deep is dark and cold, and the pressure is enormous. It seemed reasonable that life would stop.  
  Against: In the 1860s Michael Sars listed hundreds of species from deeper water off Norway. In 1869 Charles Wyville Thomson dredged animals from 4,289 metres. Forbes had sampled a part of the sea that happens to be poor in life.
- **River water that reaches the sea is wasted.** (Some planners and engineers, who want every river's water stored and used on land.; *debated*)  
  For: Much of India is short of water for part of the year. Water that flows out to sea in the monsoon could grow crops if it were stored.  
  Against: The water that reaches the sea carries silt that keeps the delta from sinking and washing away. It pushes salt water back from the wells and fields of the coast. It feeds the plankton of the coast, and it tells fish such as hilsa when to enter the river.
- **All life on Earth depends on energy from the Sun.** (Almost every biology book before 1977.; *debated*)  
  For: Plants, algae and some bacteria catch sunlight, and nearly every food web begins with them. Even the deep sea floor is fed mostly by marine snow from the sunlit zone.  
  Against: At hydrothermal vents, bacteria make food from the energy in chemicals that come out of the rock. Whole groups of animals live on them. But the same animals and bacteria use oxygen, and that oxygen was made in sunlight. So vent life is not fully free of the Sun.
- **A river can clean any waste put into it, as long as there is enough water.** (Many town engineers in the 1800s and 1900s. Their saying was 'the solution to pollution is dilution'.; *rejected*)  
  For: A river does break down some waste. Bacteria in the water eat sewage, and the flow brings in new oxygen. A little waste in a big river does disappear.  
  Against: The bacteria use up oxygen as they work. With too much waste, the oxygen runs out and fish die. Some wastes, such as metals and many farm chemicals, are not broken down at all. They build up in the mud and in the bodies of animals at the top of the food web, such as river dolphins.
- **A river dolphin with eyes that cannot form a picture must know very little about the river around her.** (A common belief about animals whose senses differ from ours.; *rejected*)  
  For: People depend on sight more than on any other sense. We find it hard to move through a room in the dark.  
  Against: A Ganga river dolphin catches fast fish in muddy water at night. She does it by echolocation, which tells her the distance, size and movement of things around her. Her knowledge of the river is detailed. It is built from sound.

## Roots
In this lesson you watched light fade in cloudy water, and you followed food from the top of the sea to the bottom. Here we ask what that says about where any home ends.

**Deep truth.** In the Vanishing Disc Test, a few spoons of milk hid the disc one hand's depth down. Light fades fast in water, so food can be made only near the top. Yet fish live at the bottom of the deepest lake and sea. They eat food that was made far above and sank to them. So most of a water home is fed from somewhere else.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of a habitat as a place with edges that looks after itself: this pond, this river, the deep sea. But the deep sea eats what the surface made, and a hill stream eats leaves from the forest. A habitat's food and oxygen often come from outside its edges.  
*Evidence:* Only 1 to 3 parts in 100 of the food made at the sea surface reach the deep floor, and nearly all deep-sea animals live on it. Shaded hill streams are fed mostly by fallen leaves. A delta is built from the silt of hills far upstream. Even vent animals breathe oxygen made in the light.

- *who am i:* The water in your body was in a river, a cloud and the sea before it was in you. The fish and rice on your plate grew with silt and rain from places you have never seen.
- *what is life:* Living beings do not stay inside the edges we draw. A hilsa needs the sea and the river. A deep-sea worm needs the algae four kilometres above her.
- *what is this world:* The sunlit top of the water, the dark bottom, the river, the floodplain and the sea are parts of one working whole. Food, oxygen and mud keep moving between them.

All the water on Earth is one water, moving. It rises from the sea, falls on the hills, and runs back as rivers. On the way it becomes many homes: a stream, a tank, a flooded field, a delta, a dark sea floor. Each home looks separate, and each has its own living beings. But the Vanishing Disc Test and the Falling Meal show that they feed each other. The Chandogya Upanishad says something close to this about rivers. Once they reach the sea, they are simply the sea.

- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.10:** “इमाः सोम्य नद्यः पुरस्तात्प्राच्यः स्यन्दन्ते पश्चात्प्रतीच्यः / ताः समुद्रात्समुद्रमेवापियन्ति / स समुद्र एव भवति / ता यथा तत्र न विदुरियमहमस्मीयमहमस्मीति” The teacher Uddalaka tells his son that the rivers of the east flow east and the rivers of the west flow west. They rise from the sea and go back to the sea, and become the sea. There, they do not know 'I am this river' or 'I am that river'. (This is the water cycle, seen more than 2,500 years ago. Uddalaka uses it to say that separate things share one source. In this lesson the science is plainer. The mud, the food and the fish of a river really do become part of the sea.)
- **Tao Te Ching 66:** “江海所以能為百谷王者，以其善下之，故能為百谷王。” The rivers and the seas can be kings of all the valley streams because they are good at staying low. That is why the streams all flow to them. (Water runs downhill, so the lowest place gathers what every higher place gives up. The deep sea floor is the lowest place of all. Everything that falls, from silt to marine snow, ends there, and feeds those who live there.)
- **Heraclitus, fragment B12 (Diels-Kranz numbering):** “ποταμοῖσι τοῖσιν αὐτοῖσιν ἐμβαίνουσιν ἕτερα καὶ ἕτερα ὕδατα ἐπιρρεῖ” On those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow. (Heraclitus, a Greek thinker of about 500 BCE, noticed that a river stays the same river while its water is always new. A river is water passing through, with mud and food and fish. The habitat is the flow.)
- Science (settled): Deep-sea animals depend on the sunlit surface. Sediment traps show that only 1 to 3 parts in 100 of surface food reach the deep floor, and that deep-sea life rises and falls with the supply.
- Science (debated): Some scientists think life on Earth began at vents on the sea floor, where rock, water and chemicals meet. Others think it began in shallow pools on land that dried and filled again. The evidence does not yet decide between them.
- Other view, Stephen Forbes, an American biologist, in his talk 'The Lake as a Microcosm' (1887), and scientists who study lakes in this way: Forbes described a lake as a little world within itself. The fish of a lake cannot leave, and everything they need must be found inside its shores. On this view a lake does have real edges, and that is exactly why it can be studied as a whole. Seeing every habitat as open to every other can blur a difference that matters to a fish.

## Story: The Red Water
*Sravani, her grandmother, her aunt, her cousin and their village are made up. These facts are real. The hilsa lives in the Bay of Bengal and swims up the Godavari in the monsoon floods to lay eggs. In Andhra Pradesh the river-run fish is called pulasa, and many people there call the same fish vilasa while she is in the sea. The flood water of the Godavari is red-brown with silt from far upstream. The river begins at Trimbakeshwar in Maharashtra, 1,465 kilometres from the sea. Teak forests grow along the middle Godavari. Nobody knows exactly how the fish in the sea sense that the flood has come.*

In Sravani's village the river was so wide that the far bank was a thin green line. The sea was close. On a still night she could hear it, a long way off, like someone breathing.

It was the last week of July, and nobody in the village was fishing. They were waiting.

Every year the pulasa came up the river from the sea. They were silver fish as long as Sravani's arm, and one of them could sell for more money than her aunt earned in a month. But they came only with the flood. This year the sky was white and hot, and it had not rained for sixteen days.

Sravani sat on the bank with her grandmother, who was mending a net. Her cousin Chinna, who was six, was throwing bits of coconut shell into the water.

'The sea is just there,' Chinna said. He pointed. 'The fish are in the sea. Why don't they just come?'

'They are waiting to be called,' said Ammamma, without looking up.

'Who calls them?'

'The river.'

Chinna looked at the river. The river was low and green and did not seem to be calling anybody. Sravani thought it was the kind of answer old people gave when they did not know.

That evening the tide came in, and she went down to wash her feet. The water tasted of salt. In this dry weather the sea pushed a long way up the river, and the river was too weak to push back. She spat it out.

Four days later Chinna woke her before it was light. 'Come and see. The river is a different colour.'

It was. The green was gone. The whole wide river was the colour of strong tea with a little milk in it, a red-brown, and it was moving fast. Branches went by. A whole banana plant went by, turning slowly. The water had climbed two steps of the bathing ghat in the night.

Sravani looked up. The sky was as white and dry as ever. There was not one cloud.

'It didn't rain,' she said. 'So where did this come from?'

Her aunt Padma was already dragging the boat down to the water. 'Does it matter? Bring the small net.'

But it did matter to Sravani, and she could not have said why. She waded in to her knees. The water was cool, cooler than yesterday. She cupped some in her hand and tasted it. There was no salt in it at all. It tasted of mud.

Something bumped her leg. She picked it up. It was a leaf, brown and tough, and bigger than her two hands side by side. She knew every tree in the village: coconut, mango, tamarind, neem, palmyra. None of them had a leaf like this.

She took it to Ammamma.

Her grandmother turned it over. 'Teak,' she said. 'I saw teak trees once, when I was a girl and we went to the temple at Bhadrachalam. That is four days up the river by boat. There are no teak trees here.'

'Then this leaf came four days down the river.'

'More, I think. The forests go on a long way beyond that.'

Sravani stood holding the leaf. So the rain had fallen, only it had not fallen here. It had fallen on hills she had never seen, days and days ago. It had run off those hills with their red mud and their leaves, into streams, and the streams had run into the river. All that time, while the village looked at its own empty sky, the flood had been on its way.

She looked downstream, towards the sea. The red water was pouring that way, very fast.

'Ammamma. How far out does it go? The red water?'

'Far. When your grandfather went out past the sandbar in flood time, he said the sea was brown as far as he could see. It was sweet enough to drink on top.'

Then Sravani understood, all at once, and she laughed out loud.

'Chinna! Come here. I know why they don't just come.'

He came, dripping.

'The fish are out there in salt water. They can't know that the river is ready, because the river doesn't reach them. In the dry days the river stops right here. It can't even push the sea out of our ghat. But now look.' She pointed at the racing water. 'Now the river goes all the way out to where they are. The mud goes out, and the taste goes out. That's the call. The hills are calling them. It just has to come through us first.'

Chinna thought about this. 'So the fish know it rained in a place they've never been.'

'So do we, now,' said Sravani, and she held up the leaf.

Nine days later her aunt came in at dawn with the first pulasa of the year in the bottom of the boat. The fish's back was dark and her sides were silver. Half the village came down to look.

Ammamma said that a fish caught in the sea was called a vilasa, and was nothing special. She said the fish became a pulasa only after swimming up through the red water.

Sravani kept the teak leaf on the shelf above her mat until it crumbled.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn comes when Sravani sees that the flood in her dry village is rain from distant hills. The river joins the hills to the sea, and the fish can come only when the river reaches them. The story does not say this as a lesson. Ask the questions below and let your child find it.

- Ask: No rain fell on the village, yet the river rose. Where did the water come from?
- Ask: What two things did Sravani notice about the red water that were different from the day before?
- Ask: Why did the fish not come into the river during the sixteen dry days?
- Ask: If a high wall were built across the river far upstream, what might change in Sravani's village?
- Ask: Nobody knows exactly how hilsa sense the flood. How could a scientist try to find out?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Vanishing Disc Test: how fast does light fade in cloudy water? (35 min)
- **question:** How deep can you see a white disc in water with 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 teaspoons of milk in it? If you double the milk, does the depth halve?
- **predict:** Before you add any milk, guess. With 1 teaspoon of milk in the bucket, how deep will the disc be when it vanishes? When the milk is doubled, will that depth be half, more than half or less than half? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same bucket of water, the same disc and the same milk all the way through. Keep the bucket in the same place, in the same light. The same person looks each time, from straight above. Stir the same way after each spoon of milk, and wait for the water to stop moving. Measure three times for each amount, because one reading can be a fluke. Only the amount of milk changes.
- **record:** Make a table with seven rows: no milk, then 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 teaspoons. For each row write the three depths in centimetres and circle the middle one. If the disc can still be seen on the bottom of the bucket, write 'deeper than the bucket'. Then draw a line graph, with teaspoons of milk along the bottom and depth up the side.
- **results table:** Teaspoons of milk in the bucket (total) | Depth when the disc vanished: try 1, try 2 and try 3 (centimetres) | The middle one of the three depths
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the bucket, the milk and the white cap. Say that you will make the water cloudy one teaspoon at a time. Ask for both guesses and the reason, and write them down.  
  *Parent says:* We will lower this white disc into the water until it disappears. With 1 teaspoon of milk in the bucket, how deep will that be? Then we double the milk. Will the depth be half, more than half or less than half? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess how deep the white disc goes in cloudy water before it vanishes. If the milk doubles, does the depth halve?
  - Ask: Why do you think the depth will change in that way? (good: More milk blocks more light.; Twice the milk should hide it twice as fast.; I think a little milk does a lot, and more adds less.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tape one end of the thread to the middle of the cap's top. Tape two coins under the cap, so that it hangs flat. Fill the bucket at least 25 centimetres deep. Stand it in daylight near a window, out of direct sunshine. Lower the disc to the bottom and look straight down.  
  *Parent says:* First we test plain water. Lower the disc right to the bottom. Can you still see it clearly? Then write 'deeper than the bucket' in the first row.  
  *Child mode:* Make the disc. Fill the bucket. Lower the disc to the bottom. Can you still see it?
  - Note: If the cap tips, move the thread nearer the middle. Direct sunshine makes a glare on the water, so choose bright shade. If there is no daylight, use one room light and do not change it.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add 1 level teaspoon of milk. Stir 10 times with the ladle and wait about 30 seconds for the water to stop moving. Your child lowers the disc slowly, looking straight down. They stop when the disc disappears and pinch the thread at the water's surface. Lift the disc out and measure from the pinch to the disc. Do this three times.  
  *Parent says:* Lower it slowly and keep looking. Stop the moment you cannot see the disc. Pinch the thread where it meets the water. Now we measure from your fingers to the disc. We do it three times, because one try can be a fluke.  
  *Child mode:* Add 1 teaspoon of milk and stir. Lower the disc slowly. Stop when it vanishes. Pinch the thread and measure. Do it three times.
  - Note: If the disc can still be seen on the bottom, write 'deeper than the bucket' and go on. If it vanishes within 3 centimetres, the bucket is small or the milk is rich. Start again with fresh water and use half-teaspoons.
  - Check: Why do we stir and then wait before each reading? → **So the milk is spread evenly and the water is still**
- **s4 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add 1 more teaspoon, so that the bucket holds 2. Stir, wait and measure three times. Do the same for 3 and 4 teaspoons. Then add 2 teaspoons at once to make 6, and 2 more to make 8. Write every depth in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Now there are 2 teaspoons in the bucket. Stir, wait and measure again. Before each new amount, tell me what depth you expect.  
  *Child mode:* Measure with 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 teaspoons of milk. Guess each depth before you measure. Write every depth down.
  - Ask: Is the depth dropping by the same amount with each teaspoon? (good: No. The first spoons made a big drop.; The later spoons change it only a little.)
  - Note: The same person should look each time. Eyes get used to the task, so readings become steadier after the first few.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Circle the middle depth in each row. Draw the line graph together. Then compare three pairs of rows: 1 and 2 teaspoons, 2 and 4, and 4 and 8.  
  *Parent says:* Look at 2 teaspoons and 4 teaspoons. The milk doubled. What happened to the depth? Check the other two pairs. Does your graph go down in a straight line, or does it curve?  
  *Child mode:* Circle the middle depths. Draw the graph, or ask your grown-up to help. When the milk doubled, what happened to the depth?
  - Ask: What shape is your graph? (good: It drops steeply and then flattens.; It is a curve, not a straight line.)
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they found, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below. Pour the milky water onto plants or down the drain.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you guessed, what you measured, and why you think it came out that way. A pond after rain is cloudy with mud. What does your graph tell you about the plants at the bottom of that pond?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed, what you found, and why you think it happened.
  - Ask: Why did the disc vanish sooner with more milk? (good: More bits in the water bounce more light away.; Less light reaches the disc, and less comes back up to my eye.)
  - Ask: What does muddy water mean for a plant on the bottom of a pond? (good: The plant gets less light.; Below a certain depth the plant cannot make food.)
  - Note: In most homes, doubling the milk roughly halves the depth. Your numbers will not be exact. Write down what you really found. The reason: milk is full of tiny droplets of fat and bits of protein. Each one bounces light off in all directions. Light must travel down to the disc and back up to your eye. With twice as many droplets in the way, the light is lost in about half the distance. Mud, silt and algae do the same in a pond or river. The test leaves one thing out. Clear water also soaks up light, but so slowly that a bucket cannot show it. That slow loss is why even the clearest sea is too dim for plants below about 200 metres.
  - Think: Scientists lower a white disc like yours into lakes every month. What could a falling depth, year after year, tell them about the lake?
### e2 · Warm on Top: what makes the layers of a lake mix? (31 min)
- **question:** Warm brown water rests on cool clear water in three glasses. Glass A is left alone. Glass B has wind blown across the top. Glass C has ice put on top. In which glass does brown water reach the bottom?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. In which glass will brown water reach the bottom first: the still glass, the windy glass or the iced glass? Will it reach the bottom in more than one glass? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use three glasses of the same size. Put the same amount of cool tap water in each, from the same jug. Pour the same amount of warm tea onto each, from the same pot, in the same gentle way. Start all three within a few minutes of each other and stand them side by side. Do not move or bump them. Only what you do to the top changes.
- **safety:** A grown-up makes the tea and cools it until it is only as warm as bath water. Never pour hot tea in this experiment.
- **record:** Take a photo of the three glasses from the side, once after 1 minute and once after 10 minutes. Or draw them and shade in where the brown water is. Under each picture write 'top only', 'part of the way down' or 'at the bottom'.
- **results table:** Glass | What was done to the top | Where the brown water is after 1 minute | Where the brown water is after 10 minutes
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the three glasses, the straw and the ice. Explain what will be done to each glass. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* Warm brown tea will float on cool clear water in all three glasses. We leave glass A alone. We blow across glass B, like wind on a lake. We put ice on glass C, like a cold night. In which glass will brown water reach the bottom? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three glasses have warm brown water on top. One is left alone. One gets wind. One gets ice. Guess which one will get brown water at the bottom.
  - Ask: Why do you think that glass will mix? (good: Wind stirs things up.; Ice makes the top cold, and cold water might sink.; I think none will mix, because the layers look steady.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children and adults expect the wind to mix the glass.
- **s2 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up boils 2 teaspoons of tea leaves in 2 cups of water and strains it. Let the tea cool until a finger can rest in it comfortably. Meanwhile half fill the three glasses from the jug. Stand them in a row in front of the white paper.  
  *Parent says:* The tea is only for colour, so that we can see the warm water. It must be warm, not hot. Check that all three glasses have the same amount of water.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to make strong tea and cool it. Half fill three glasses with tap water.
  - Note: The tea should feel like warm bath water, about 40 to 45 degrees. If it is hot it can scald, and if it is cool it will not float.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hold the tablespoon so that its bowl just touches the water in glass A. Pour warm tea very slowly onto the spoon, until the glass is nearly full. Do the same for glasses B and C. Look from the side.  
  *Parent says:* Pour slowly onto the spoon, so that the tea spreads out gently. Now bend down and look from the side. What do you see? Where is the brown water, and where is the clear water?  
  *Child mode:* Pour warm tea slowly onto a spoon at the surface. Look from the side. Where is the brown water?
  - Ask: What do you notice about the two kinds of water? (good: The brown water stays on top.; There is a line between them.; They do not mix by themselves.)
  - Note: If the layers mix while you pour, the tea was too cool or was poured too fast. Empty that glass and try again more slowly.
  - Check: Why do all three glasses get the same amounts of water and tea? → **So that only the top treatment differs**
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Leave glass A alone. For glass B, your child holds the straw level, just above the water. They blow gently across the surface for 1 minute. The straw must not dip into the water. Look from the side while they blow.  
  *Parent says:* Blow across the top, like a breeze over a lake. Do not blow down into the water. What is the surface doing? What is the line between brown and clear doing?  
  *Child mode:* Blow gently across the top of glass B for one minute. Watch the line between brown and clear.
  - Ask: What did the wind change? (good: It made ripples on the top.; The brown layer moved about, but the line stayed.; The clear water below hardly moved.)
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lower two ice cubes gently onto the top of glass C. Do not stir. Watch from the side for 3 minutes, with the white paper behind the glass.  
  *Parent says:* Now a cold night for glass C. Watch the water just under the ice. Tell me everything you see. Where is the brown water going?  
  *Child mode:* Put two ice cubes gently on glass C. Watch from the side for 3 minutes.
  - Ask: What did you see under the ice? (good: Thin brown streams fell down.; Brown water went through the clear water.; Brown water began to collect at the bottom.)
  - Note: Thin streams of brown water should start to fall within a minute. They are easier to see against white paper.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ten minutes after the ice went in, compare the three glasses from the side. Your child points to the brown water in each glass. They say if it is at the top only, part of the way down, or at the bottom.  
  *Parent says:* Look at all three. In which glass is there brown water at the bottom? Tell me what you see, not what you expected.  
  *Child mode:* After ten minutes, look at all three glasses. Point to where the brown water is in each.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say what they guessed, what they saw, and why they think it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Say what you guessed, what you saw, and why you think it happened. In a deep lake, fresh oxygen comes in at the top. In a hot month, can it reach the fish at the bottom? When can it?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you guessed, what you saw, and why. When can oxygen reach the bottom of a deep lake?
  - Ask: Why did the brown water sink only under the ice? (good: The ice made it cold.; Cold water is heavier than warm water, so it sank.; It became colder than the water below it.)
  - Ask: Why could the wind not mix glass B? (good: The warm water is light and stays on top.; Wind only stirred the top layer.)
  - Note: In most homes glass A keeps its two layers, with the line a little blurred. Glass B has ripples and a stirred top layer, but the clear water below stays clear. In glass C, brown water reaches the bottom. Write down what you really saw. The reason: warm water is lighter than cool water, so it floats. Wind pushes the top about, but cannot easily push light water down under heavy water. Ice cools the brown water until it is colder, and so heavier, than the water below. Then it sinks. A deep lake works the same way. In hot months the warm top layer hardly mixes with the cold deep water, so little new oxygen reaches the bottom. When the weather cools, the top water cools and sinks, and the lake mixes. The glass leaves two things out. A very strong wind can mix a shallow lake. And water is heaviest at 4 degrees, so near freezing it behaves differently.
  - Think: Glass C has brown water at the top and the bottom. What would have to happen for the whole glass to become one even colour?

- **record:** Two scores out of 10: right answers in a quiet room, and right answers in a noisy room.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Tell your child that a river dolphin finds things by sound. Ask whether they think a person could notice a plate by sound alone, and why.  
  *Parent says:* A river dolphin clicks and listens for the echo. You will make a 'shhh' sound with your eyes shut. I will hold a plate in front of your face, or take it away. Do you think you will be able to tell? Why?  
  *Child mode:* A plate is held in front of your face. Can sound alone tell you? Why?
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child shuts their eyes and says a long steady 'shhh'. First let them practise: move the plate slowly towards their face and away, about 10 centimetres at the nearest. Then play 10 turns. On each turn, silently hold the plate there or keep it away. Your child says 'plate' or 'no plate'. Keep score.  
  *Parent says:* Keep the 'shhh' going. Listen to how it changes as the plate comes close. Now the test. Ten turns. Tell me each time: plate or no plate?  
  *Child mode:* Shut your eyes. Say a long 'shhh'. Listen to it change. Then say 'plate' or 'no plate' ten times.
  - Ask: How did the sound change when the plate was near? (good: It got louder.; It sounded closer and sharper.; It sounded hollow.)
  - Note: Move the plate without making a breeze or a sound. Most children score 8 or more out of 10 after a little practice.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Switch on a noisy fan, a tap or some music, and play 10 more turns. Compare the two scores. After your child has said what they noticed, read them the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Now the room is noisy. Ten more turns. Was it harder? A river dolphin depends on echoes to find food and to keep clear of nets. What might the noise of boat engines do to her?  
  *Child mode:* Play ten more turns with noise in the room. Count how many you get right.
  - Ask: Why was it harder in a noisy room? (good: The noise covered the echo.; I could not hear the small change in the sound.)
  - Note: The 'shhh' bounces off the plate and returns to the ears, so it sounds louder and nearer. That is a small form of echolocation. A dolphin's clicks are much sharper and her hearing far finer than ours. Engine noise covers her echoes, as the fan covered yours.
  - Check: Why does a Ganga river dolphin use sound to find fish? → **Light cannot pass far through muddy water**

## Challenges
### c1 · Bring Up the Deep: collect bottom water without mixing in the top (30 min)
- **problem:** Scientists need water from 4,000 metres down, to measure its oxygen and its salt. On the way up, the sample must not mix with any of the water above. Your deep sea is a tall jar. The bottom 3 centimetres are brown salty water, and the rest is clear. Bring up a sample of the brown water that is still brown.
- **your turn:** Now design your own sampler. Draw it first, or describe it to your grown-up, and mark what keeps the top water out on the way down and on the way up. Build it, test it and drop the sample on the white plate. Then change one thing to make the sample darker.
- **constraints:** Your fingers must not go below the surface of the water.; The sample must be at least 10 drops.; Dropped on a white plate, the sample must be clearly darker than a drop taken from the top of the jar.; You may not stir, tilt or pour the jar.; Use only things from home.
- **general:** Hold a finger over the top of a straw and lower it to the bottom. The air inside keeps the clear water out. Lift the finger, so that brown water enters. Press the finger down again and lift the straw out.; Tie a thread to a small weighted bottle with a loose plug of dough. Lower it to the bottom. Pull a second thread to take the plug out, wait for the bottle to fill, and lift it slowly.; Push a straw onto the end of a syringe or dropper. Lower the straw to the bottom and draw the water up.
- **creative:** Join two straws to reach deeper, and seal the join with tape so that no air leaks in.; Give the small bottle two threads, one to pull the plug out and one to push a second plug in, so that it comes up closed.; Lower a bit of sponge on a thread inside a straw, let it soak at the bottom, and pull it up through the straw.
- **s1 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up cuts the bottle, if you use one. Pour the salty tea into the jar, about 3 centimetres deep. Hold a tablespoon just above the tea. Pour tap water very slowly onto the spoon, until the jar is nearly full. Read the problem together.  
  *Parent says:* The brown water is your deep sea. It is salty, so it is heavy and stays at the bottom. You must bring some up without mixing it with the clear water. How could you do that? Tell me three ideas before you build.  
  *Child mode:* The brown water at the bottom is your deep sea. How can you bring some up, still brown? List three ideas.
  - Ask: What is the hard part of this problem? (good: The tool passes through the clear water on the way down.; The sample could leak or mix on the way up.)
  - Note: If the layers mix while you pour, the water went in too fast. Start again and pour more slowly.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child builds a sampler from the materials. Help to hold things, but let your child decide the design. Put a drop from the top of the jar on the white plate, for comparing later.  
  *Parent says:* Build your first idea. Before you test it, tell me two things. What keeps the clear water out on the way down? What keeps the brown water in on the way up?  
  *Child mode:* Build your sampler. What keeps clear water out on the way down? What keeps brown water in?
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child lowers the sampler slowly, takes a sample and lifts it out. They drop the sample on the white plate, beside the drop from the top. Compare the two colours.  
  *Parent says:* Go down slowly, so that you do not stir the jar. Take your sample and bring it up. Put it on the plate. Is it darker than the drop from the top? Is it as dark as the bottom layer looks?  
  *Child mode:* Lower your sampler slowly. Take a sample. Drop it on the plate. Is it darker than the top drop?
  - Ask: If the sample is pale, where did the clear water get in? (good: On the way down, before the tool reached the bottom.; The tool was not right at the bottom when it opened.; It leaked on the way up.)
  - Check: A straw is lowered with a finger pressed on top. Why does no water enter? → **The air trapped inside keeps the water out**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child changes one thing and tests again. Then compare the design with real deep-sea samplers, using the note below.  
  *Parent says:* What went wrong, or what could be better? Change one thing and test again. Real samplers go down open and are shut at the right depth. How do you think someone on a ship shuts a bottle 4,000 metres below?  
  *Child mode:* Change one thing to get a darker sample. Test again.
  - Ask: How could a bottle be shut from far above? (good: Send a weight sliding down the cable to hit a catch.; Send an electric signal down a wire.; Use a timer.)
  - Note: Praise a clear account of what went wrong as much as a dark sample. Ocean scientists lower a ring of open tubes on a long cable. At each chosen depth one tube is snapped shut, by a signal from the ship or by a weight sent down the cable. Each tube then holds water from one depth only.
### c2 · A Way Round the Wall: build a fish pass (30 min)
- **problem:** A barrage has been built across a river. The water above the wall is 6 centimetres higher than the water below. Hilsa are coming up from the sea to lay their eggs, and they are not strong jumpers. Build a way round the wall, made of small steps and resting pools, that a fish could climb.
- **your turn:** Now design your own fish pass. Draw it from the side first, or describe it to your grown-up, and write the height of each step. Build it and pour the water. Measure every step and every pool. Then change one thing to make the flow gentler.
- **constraints:** The pass must go from the low end of the tray to the high end, 6 centimetres up.; No step between two pools may be higher than 1 centimetre.; Every pool must hold water at least 1 centimetre deep after the pouring stops.; When one tumbler of water is poured in slowly at the top, it must pass through every pool.; Use only clay or dough, foil, pebbles and the tray.
- **general:** Make a foil gutter along the tray. Build low walls of dough across it, like steps, so that a pool forms behind each wall.; Cut a small notch in the top of each wall, so that the water flows through one place and not over the whole wall.; Make more steps than you think you need. Six centimetres needs at least six steps of 1 centimetre.
- **creative:** Bend the gutter into a zigzag, so that it is longer and each step can be lower.; Put the notches on opposite sides of each wall in turn, so that the water winds and slows down.; Lay pebbles in the pools, so that a fish has still water to rest behind.
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Raise one end of the tray on books until it is 6 centimetres higher than the other end. Read the problem together. Ask for ideas before any building.  
  *Parent says:* The top of the tray is the river above the wall. The bottom is the river below. A fish must get up, and she cannot jump high. What could you build? How many steps will you need if each is 1 centimetre?  
  *Child mode:* A fish must climb 6 centimetres. She cannot jump more than 1 centimetre. What will you build?
  - Ask: How many steps of 1 centimetre are needed to climb 6 centimetres? (good: At least six.; More than six, if some steps are lower.)
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child lines a channel with foil and builds dough walls across it. Help to press the dough down firmly, so that water does not leak underneath. Let your child decide where the walls go.  
  *Parent says:* Build your pools. Press each wall down hard at the bottom and the sides. Where will the water leave each pool? Check each step with the ruler as you go.  
  *Child mode:* Build your pools with dough walls. Press them down well. Measure each step with the ruler.
  - Note: Dough walls need to be about a finger thick. Water finds every gap, so the first test nearly always leaks. That is part of the challenge.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child pours one tumbler of water slowly into the top pool. Watch where the water goes. When it stops flowing, measure the depth of each pool and the height of each step.  
  *Parent says:* Pour slowly and watch. Does the water go through every pool, or does it find a short cut? Now measure. Is any step higher than 1 centimetre? Is any pool too shallow to rest in?  
  *Child mode:* Pour water in at the top. Does it pass through every pool? Measure each step and each pool.
  - Ask: Where would a fish have trouble in your pass? (good: At the step that is too high.; Where the water rushes fast through a narrow gap.; In the pool that is too shallow.)
  - Check: Why does a fish pass have pools between the steps? → **So the fish can rest in slower water**
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child changes one thing to fix the worst step or pool, and tests again. Then compare the design with real fish passes, using the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Which step or pool was the worst? Change one thing and test again. Your pass suits a fish who can climb 1 centimetre. Would the same pass suit every kind of fish in the river?  
  *Child mode:* Fix the worst step or pool. Test again.
  - Ask: Why might one fish pass not suit every fish? (good: Some fish jump and some do not.; Big fish need deep pools.; Some fish swim near the bottom and some near the top.)
  - Note: Praise a clear account of what went wrong as much as a working pass. Real fish passes are of several kinds: rows of pools, long winding side channels like small streams, and lifts that carry fish up in a tank of water. Passes built for strong jumpers have often failed for other fish. A pass has to be designed for the fish of that river, and tested with them.

## Worksheets
### Life in water: the main ideas (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which of these is a freshwater habitat?  
   Options: A village tank / A coral reef / The deep sea floor / A salt pan  
   **Answer:** A village tank. *Why:* A tank holds rain or river water with very little salt. A coral reef, the deep sea floor and a salt pan all have salty water.
2. (mcq) Why do plants and algae grow only in the top layer of a lake or sea?  
   Options: Only that layer has enough light / Only that layer has water / The deep water is too clean / Fish eat all the deep plants  
   **Answer:** Only that layer has enough light. *Why:* Plants and algae make food from light. Water takes light away with depth, so below the sunlit zone no food can be made.
3. (mcq) What does a river drop when its water slows down?  
   Options: Sediment / Oxygen / Salt / Heat  
   **Answer:** Sediment. *Why:* Slow water cannot carry sand and silt, so they settle. This builds floodplains and deltas.
4. (mcq) What is marine snow?  
   Options: Dead plankton and waste sinking through the sea / Ice that falls on the sea in winter / Salt that settles on the sea floor / White sand washed in by rivers  
   **Answer:** Dead plankton and waste sinking through the sea. *Why:* Marine snow is dead plankton, droppings, slime and dust stuck together. It is the main food of the deep sea.
5. (fill) The flat land beside a river that goes under water in a flood is the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['floodplain']. *Why:* A floodplain is covered when the river spills over its banks. The flood leaves silt there.
6. (fill) Finding things by making a sound and listening for the echo is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['echolocation']. *Why:* River dolphins and many bats use echolocation where eyes are of little use.
7. (truefalse) Light fades faster in muddy water than in clear water.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Bits of mud bounce light away in all directions, so less light gets through each layer.
8. (truefalse) Nothing lives in the sea below 1,000 metres.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Animals live at every depth. A fish has been filmed at 8,336 metres.
9. (match) Match each ocean zone to its depth.  
   **Answer:** Sunlit zone → 0 to 200 metres; Twilight zone → 200 to 1,000 metres; Midnight zone → 1,000 to 4,000 metres; Abyss → 4,000 to 6,000 metres; Trenches → Below 6,000 metres. *Why:* The zones are set by light and depth. Enough light for photosynthesis ends at about 200 metres, and all sunlight ends at about 1,000 metres.
10. (sort) Sort these. Is each one still water or flowing water?  
   **Answer:** a lake → Still water; a village tank → Still water; a marsh → Still water; a hill stream → Flowing water; a river → Flowing water; a spring → Flowing water. *Why:* In still water, tiny drifting beings can stay in one place. In flowing water, living beings must hold on or swim against the current.
11. (sort) Sort these pond plants. Are they rooted in the mud, or floating free on the water?  
   **Answer:** Lotus → Rooted in the mud; Water lily → Rooted in the mud; Water hyacinth → Floating free; Duckweed → Floating free. *Why:* Lotus and water lily have roots in the mud and long stalks up to the light. Water hyacinth and duckweed float, with no roots in the mud.
12. (order) Put these in order, as a river flows from source to sea.  
   **Answer:** ['A spring in forested hills', 'A fast, cold, shaded stream', 'A wide river with rocky pools', 'A slow, deep, muddy river', 'A delta with many branches']. *Why:* A river starts small, steep and fast. It grows wider, slower and muddier, and drops its sediment at the mouth.
13. (write) A hill stream is shaded and has few algae. What do the small animals there eat?  
   **Answer:** They eat dead leaves that fall into the stream from the trees. Insect young shred the leaves, and small fish eat the insects.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the food comes from outside the stream, from the forest.
14. (write) Why is a deep-sea fish not crushed by the water above her?  
   **Answer:** Her body is mostly water, and water can hardly be squeezed. She has no air-filled spaces. The pressure is the same inside her and outside.. *Why:* A strong answer says that pressure crushes gas-filled things, and that the fish has none.
15. (draw) Draw a lake from the side. Show where plants grow, where plankton drift and where it is dark.  
   **Answer:** A lake in side view. Rooted plants stand at the shallow edge. Dots of plankton are in the bright top water. The deep middle is shaded dark, with no plants.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts every food maker in the light: rooted plants where the bottom is shallow, and plankton near the surface.
16. (perspective) A new dam is planned on a river where fish swim upstream to breed.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that hilsa above the Farakka Barrage almost vanished after 1975. Another is that most rain falls in four months, so stored water matters. A strong answer also says what the other view gets right.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses what a dam gives and what it stops: water for crops, and fish, silt and floods.
### Life in water: light, layers and rivers (stretch, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) A disc vanishes at 12 centimetres in cloudy water. The cloudiness is doubled. About where does the disc vanish now?  
   Options: About 6 centimetres / About 12 centimetres / About 24 centimetres / About 10 centimetres  
   **Answer:** About 6 centimetres. *Why:* Twice as many bits take the light away in about half the distance. If you chose 24, you doubled the depth, but more bits mean a smaller depth.
2. (mcq) In a deep lake in a hot month, where is there least oxygen?  
   Options: In the cold bottom layer / At the surface / Among the plants at the edge / Just under the surface  
   **Answer:** In the cold bottom layer. *Why:* Oxygen comes in at the top. The warm layer hardly mixes with the cold layer below, where bacteria keep using oxygen up.
3. (mcq) Which living being fits a slow, deep, muddy river best?  
   Options: A dolphin who finds fish by echoes / A fish who hunts by sight / A lotus rooted in the river bed / An insect who clings to clean stones  
   **Answer:** A dolphin who finds fish by echoes. *Why:* In muddy water sight is of little use, and the deep dark bed grows few plants. Sound works well there.
4. (truefalse) A strong wind can easily push warm top water down under cold water.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Warm water is lighter and floats. Wind stirs the top layer, but the layers mix only when the top cools.
5. (truefalse) River water that reaches the sea still does useful work.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. It carries silt that builds the delta, pushes back salt water, and feeds the plankton of the coast.
6. (match) Match each living being to the clue that fits their home.  
   **Answer:** Hill-stream loach → A sucker belly for holding on to rock; Ganga river dolphin → Clicks and echoes in muddy water; Lotus → Long stalks from mud up to the light; Mangrove → Breathing roots in salty mud; Anglerfish → A glowing lure in the dark. *Why:* Each body answers the main problem of its home: fast water, muddy water, deep still water, airless mud or darkness.
7. (write) A hill-stream loach has wide flat fins pressed to the rock. A mahseer has a strong tail fin. Why does each have the fins she has?  
   **Answer:** The loach lives in fast water, and her wide flat fins hold her to the rock so the current does not sweep her away. The mahseer swims for long distances in a river, and her strong tail pushes her forward.. *Why:* A strong answer links each fin to the work its owner has to do: holding on in a fast stream, or driving a strong swimmer forward.
8. (sort) Sort these. Is the food made inside this habitat, or brought in from outside?  
   **Answer:** algae on sunny river stones → Made inside; plankton in a lake's top water → Made inside; bacteria's food at a hot vent → Made inside; fallen leaves in a shaded stream → Brought in from outside; marine snow on the abyss floor → Brought in from outside; silt and plant food in a delta → Brought in from outside. *Why:* Food is made inside only where there is light, or chemical energy at a vent. Dark and shaded waters are fed from elsewhere.
9. (odd) Three of these are harmed by a wall across a river. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: Hilsa swimming up to breed / Silt on its way to the delta / River dolphins meeting other groups / Paddy fields fed by a canal  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A dam or barrage sends water into canals, so the fields gain. The fish, the silt and the dolphins are stopped by the wall.
10. (count) Marine snow sinks 100 metres a day. How many days does it take to fall 3,000 metres?  
   **Answer:** 30. *Why:* 30 days. 3,000 divided by 100 is 30.
11. (order) Put these in order, from the cool season to the end of the hot season in a deep lake.  
   **Answer:** ['The whole lake is mixed and rich in oxygen', 'The Sun warms the top water', 'A warm layer floats on a cold layer', 'Bacteria use up oxygen in the cold layer', 'Fish leave the deep water']. *Why:* Layers form because warm water floats. Once the layers stop mixing, the deep water gets no new oxygen.
12. (write) A tank turns muddy after building work nearby. What happens to the plants on the bottom, and why?  
   **Answer:** The mud makes the water turbid, so light fades in a shorter distance. Plants on the deeper parts of the bottom no longer get enough light to make food, and die back. Only plants in the shallowest water remain.. *Why:* A strong answer links turbid water to less light, and less light to less photosynthesis.
13. (write) No rain falls on a delta village, yet the river rises and turns red-brown. Explain how.  
   **Answer:** Rain fell far upstream, days earlier. It washed soil into streams, and the streams joined the river. The flood and its silt travelled down the river to the delta.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a river gathers water and sediment from all the land it drains, and carries them a long way.
14. (write) Why do fish farmers stir their ponds on hot, still nights?  
   **Answer:** Warm water holds less oxygen, and at night no algae are making any. With no wind, little oxygen comes in from the air. Stirring brings water to the surface, where it takes up oxygen.. *Why:* A strong answer names the surface as the place where oxygen enters, and says that stirring brings deep water there.
15. (write) A cyclone is coming to a village on the Sundarbans coast. How could the mangroves help the village, and how could the village help the mangroves?  
   **Answer:** The thick roots slow the waves and hold the mud, so the village behind them gets less damage. The village can help by not cutting the mangrove trees for fuel or building, and by leaving the roots alone.. *Why:* A strong answer says that roots slow the waves and hold the soil, and then names one way people can protect the trees.
16. (perspective) A city marsh is full of birds. The city wants to fill part of it to build houses.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that a wetland takes in flood water and feeds many living beings. Another is that people do need homes. A strong answer suggests where else the houses could go, or how much of the marsh must stay.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer shows what the marsh does, and takes the families' need seriously.
17. (label) Draw a river from hills to sea. Label the source, a tributary, the floodplain, the delta and the mouth.  
   **Answer:** ['Source: where the river begins, high in the hills', 'Tributary: a smaller river that joins the main river', 'Floodplain: flat land beside the river that floods', 'Delta: flat land built of sediment where the river splits', 'Mouth: where the river meets the sea']. *Why:* A strong answer puts the parts in the right order, and shows the river growing wider towards the sea.
### Life in water: think it through (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Each 10 centimetres of a pond lets half the light through. How much is left at 30 centimetres?  
   Options: One eighth / One third / One sixth / None  
   **Answer:** One eighth. *Why:* Half is left at 10 centimetres, a quarter at 20 and an eighth at 30. If you chose 'none', remember that halving never quite reaches zero.
2. (mcq) Vent bacteria make food without light. What do vent animals still need that was made in sunlight?  
   Options: Oxygen dissolved in the sea water / Heat from the Sun / Marine snow from the surface / Salt from rivers  
   **Answer:** Oxygen dissolved in the sea water. *Why:* The bacteria and animals at vents use oxygen. That oxygen was made by algae and plants in the light, and carried down by sinking cold water.
3. (mcq) A dam lets out water from the bottom of a deep reservoir. What is that water like in a hot month?  
   Options: Cold and low in oxygen / Warm and rich in oxygen / Warm and low in oxygen / The same as the surface water  
   **Answer:** Cold and low in oxygen. *Why:* In hot months the deep layer is cold and has lost oxygen. Fish below the dam then meet water that is colder and poorer in oxygen than the river was.
4. (mcq) Why are many animals of the twilight zone see-through or red?  
   Options: Both colours are hard to see there / Both colours keep them warm / Red and clear bodies need less food / The pressure removes other colours  
   **Answer:** Both colours are hard to see there. *Why:* In open water there is nothing to hide behind. A clear body lets the faint light pass. A red body sends back no light, because no red light reaches that depth.
5. (truefalse) A foam cup shrinks in the deep sea because foam is full of tiny pockets of air.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Air can be squeezed into a smaller space. Water can hardly be squeezed, so a water-filled body keeps its shape.
6. (truefalse) A fair test of cloudy water can change the milk and the lighting together.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. If two things change, you cannot tell which one caused the result. Only the milk should change.
7. (count) Pressure rises by 1 kilogram on each square centimetre for every 10 metres. How much at 2,500 metres?  
   **Answer:** 250. *Why:* 250 kilograms. 2,500 divided by 10 is 250. Add about 1 kilogram for the air above the sea.
8. (count) Of every 100 parts of surface food, 2 reach the sea floor. How many of 1,000 parts?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* 20 parts. 1,000 is 10 times 100, and 10 times 2 is 20.
9. (fill) Bacteria at hot vents make food from the energy in chemicals. This is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['chemosynthesis']. *Why:* Chemosynthesis means making food with chemical energy. Photosynthesis means making food with light.
10. (order) Put these in order, from first to last in the story of a meal on the abyss floor.  
   **Answer:** ['Phytoplankton make food in the sunlit zone', 'The plankton die and clump into flakes', 'The flakes sink for several weeks', 'Most flakes are eaten on the way down', 'A sea cucumber eats what reaches the mud']. *Why:* Deep-sea food begins in the light and travels down. Only about 1 to 3 parts in 100 arrive.
11. (write) A scientist dredged one sea and said no life exists below 550 metres anywhere. What was wrong with his reasoning?  
   **Answer:** He stretched a result from one sea to all seas. The sea he sampled happens to be poor in life. He also had no samples from very deep water, so he was guessing beyond his evidence.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a result holds only for what was tested, and that more places and greater depths had to be sampled.
12. (write) A lake's Secchi depth was 3 metres ten years ago and is 1 metre now. Give two possible causes.  
   **Answer:** More mud may be washing in, from building or bare land around the lake. Or more algae may be growing, fed by sewage or farm fertiliser. Both make the water turbid.. *Why:* A strong answer names things that put more bits into the water. It may add how to tell them apart: mud makes water brown, and algae make it green.
13. (write) Is the deep sea floor a habitat that looks after itself? Give evidence for your answer.  
   **Answer:** No. Almost no food is made there. Its food sinks from the sunlit zone as marine snow, and its oxygen is carried down from the surface. Only at vents is food made in the dark, and even there the oxygen comes from above.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the Falling Meal, and says where the food and the oxygen come from.
14. (perspective) Metal-rich lumps lie on the deep sea floor. Some countries want to collect them for making batteries.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that deep-sea animals get very little food, so they grow and recover slowly. Another is that the metals are needed, and mining on land also does harm. A strong answer says what should be found out first.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses what the unit says about the slow life of the deep sea, and about how little has been seen.
15. (write) Many people never see the sea. Give two reasons why the loss of coral reefs and sea life matters to them too.  
   **Answer:** Sea plankton make about half of the oxygen that everyone breathes. The sea also takes in heat and a gas called carbon dioxide from the air. Reefs shelter young fish that feed millions of people, and they break waves that would hit the coast.. *Why:* A strong answer gives two real reasons, such as oxygen, heat, food or coastal protection, and says who is helped by each.
16. (draw) Draw three glasses with warm brown water on top: still, windy and iced. Show where the brown water goes.  
   **Answer:** The still glass has a brown layer on top and clear water below. The windy glass has ripples and the same two layers. The iced glass has thin brown streams falling and brown water at the bottom.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that only cooling sent the top water down. Wind stirred the top layer only.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which pair are both freshwater habitats?  
   Options: A lake and a river / A lagoon and a coral reef / A salt pan and a tank / The abyss and a pond  
   **Answer:** A lake and a river. *Why:* Lakes and rivers hold water with very little salt. Reefs, salt pans and the abyss are salty.
2. (mcq) About what share of all the kinds of fish live in fresh water?  
   Options: About half / About 1 in 100 / About 1 in 10 / Nearly all  
   **Answer:** About half. *Why:* About half of all kinds of fish live in fresh water. Rivers and lakes are cut off from each other, so each grows kinds of its own.
3. (mcq) What happens to light as it goes deeper into water?  
   Options: It gets weaker with every layer / It stays the same to the bottom / It gets stronger / It turns red  
   **Answer:** It gets weaker with every layer. *Why:* Each layer of water soaks up and scatters a share of the light. If you chose 'turns red', remember that red is the first colour to be lost.
4. (mcq) Where in a deep lake do rooted plants such as lotus grow?  
   Options: At the shallow edge / In the deepest middle / Floating far from shore with no roots / Everywhere on the bottom  
   **Answer:** At the shallow edge. *Why:* A rooted plant needs a bottom shallow enough for light to reach, or for the stalk to reach the surface.
5. (mcq) Which pond plant floats free on the water, with roots that hang down and do not touch the mud?  
   Options: Water hyacinth / Lotus / Water lily / Reed  
   **Answer:** Water hyacinth. *Why:* Water hyacinth floats on swollen stalks that hold air. Lotus, water lily and reeds are rooted in the mud. Water hyacinth came from South America and can spread over a whole lake, which cuts off light for the plants below.
6. (mcq) Why does warm water stay on top of cold water in a lake?  
   Options: Warm water is lighter / Warm water is saltier / Wind holds the warm water up / Fish push the cold water down  
   **Answer:** Warm water is lighter. *Why:* Warm water is a little lighter than cold water, so it floats. That is why the two layers hardly mix in hot months.
7. (mcq) What makes a layered lake mix from top to bottom?  
   Options: The top water cools and sinks / The Sun heats the top water more / Fish swim up and down / Rain falls on the surface  
   **Answer:** The top water cools and sinks. *Why:* When the top water cools, it becomes as heavy as the water below and sinks. Then wind can mix the whole lake.
8. (mcq) Which stretch of a river is cold, clear, fast and rich in oxygen?  
   Options: The upper stretch, near the source / The lower stretch, near the sea / The delta / The floodplain lakes  
   **Answer:** The upper stretch, near the source. *Why:* Near the source the stream is steep and tumbles over rocks, which stirs in oxygen. Shade keeps the water cold.
9. (mcq) Which fin of a fish does most of the pushing forward?  
   Options: The tail fin / The fin on the back / The small fin on the belly / The fin of a loach  
   **Answer:** The tail fin. *Why:* The tail sweeps from side to side and pushes the fish through the water. The back fin keeps her upright, and the side fins steer and brake.
10. (mcq) Why do many river fish lay their eggs on flooded land?  
   Options: The young find food and hiding places there / The water there is colder / There is no oxygen there / The eggs need dry soil  
   **Answer:** The young find food and hiding places there. *Why:* Warm shallow flood water fills with plankton and insects, and plants give cover. The young return to the river as the flood goes down.
11. (mcq) How do the roots of mangrove trees help the coast?  
   Options: They hold the mud and slow the waves / They stop the river from flowing / They make the water fresh / They stop silt from settling  
   **Answer:** They hold the mud and slow the waves. *Why:* Thick roots hold the soil in place and slow waves, so a storm does less harm to the land behind. They do not stop the river or take away the salt.
12. (mcq) How does a wall across a river harm a fish like the hilsa?  
   Options: She cannot reach her breeding places upstream / The wall makes the water salty / The wall takes away her gills / She forgets how to swim  
   **Answer:** She cannot reach her breeding places upstream. *Why:* Hilsa live in the sea and breed in rivers. A wall with no suitable way round cuts the journey.
13. (mcq) Why do coral reefs hold so many kinds of fish and other sea animals?  
   Options: The reef gives food and hiding places in warm, sunlit water / Reefs are the deepest part of the sea / Fish cannot live anywhere else / Corals make the sea water fresh  
   **Answer:** The reef gives food and hiding places in warm, sunlit water. *Why:* Reefs grow only in warm, bright, shallow water. Their many nooks give young fish and small animals a place to hide and feed. They are not deep, and the sea stays salty.
14. (mcq) By how much does the pressure rise for every 10 metres of depth in the sea?  
   Options: By about 1 kilogram on each square centimetre / By nothing / By half each time / By 10 kilograms on each square centimetre  
   **Answer:** By about 1 kilogram on each square centimetre. *Why:* Every 10 metres of water adds about as much press as the whole of the air above us: about 1 kilogram on each square centimetre.
15. (mcq) Where does most of the food eaten on the deep sea floor come from?  
   Options: It sinks from the sunlit zone / It grows in the mud / Rivers pour it straight onto the floor / Deep-sea fish make it from light  
   **Answer:** It sinks from the sunlit zone. *Why:* Food is made where there is light, and sinks as marine snow. Only about 1 to 3 parts in 100 reach the floor.
16. (mcq) In a fair test of how milk hides a disc, what is the one thing that changes?  
   Options: The amount of milk / The person who looks / The light in the room / The size of the disc  
   **Answer:** The amount of milk. *Why:* Only the thing you are testing should change. If the light or the observer changed too, you could not tell what caused the result.

## Applications
- Lake scientists and volunteers lower a white disc every month. A falling Secchi depth warns them that mud or algae are building up.
- Water treatment plants measure how turbid the river water is before they clean it, so that they know how much cleaning is needed.
- Fish and prawn farmers run paddle wheels on hot still nights, to stir oxygen into the pond before the fish run short.
- Engineers build fish passes at dams and barrages, and plan how much water to let through for the river all year.
- Flood warning offices measure river levels far upstream. They work out when the flood will reach the towns and villages lower down.
- Builders of deep-sea vehicles use thick metal balls and small windows, because the water presses hundreds of times harder than air.
- Fishing boats and research ships find fish and map the sea floor with echo sounders, which work by sending sound and timing the echo.
- Scientists who count river dolphins can listen for their clicks with underwater microphones, as well as watching for them to surface.

## Spotted around us
- A tank or lake after heavy rain. The water turns brown, and you cannot see the steps under the surface. Mud has made the water turbid.
- A green pond in summer. The colour is phytoplankton. Your hand vanishes just below the surface, so the sunlit zone there is very thin.
- Paddle wheels on a prawn or fish pond. They splash water into the air to stir oxygen in.
- Lotus and water lily at the edge of a tank, and none in the middle. The middle is too deep for a stalk to reach the light.
- A river in the monsoon and the same river in April. In flood it is wide, fast and brown. In summer it is a clear thin stream among sand.
- Dried fish, prawns and fresh sardines in a coastal market. All of them fed in the sunlit zone, close to where plankton grow.
- A barrage or check dam on a river near your town. Look at the water above and below the wall. Is there any way round for a fish?

## Open questions
- **How do hilsa in the sea sense that a river is in flood?** They might follow the fresher water, the mud, a smell or the change in flow. Tracking small fish in a muddy sea is hard, and nobody has shown which sign matters most.
- **How deep can a fish live?** The deepest fish filmed was at 8,336 metres, and the sea goes down to almost 11,000. Very few dives have looked, so the true limit is not known.
- **How many kinds of animals live in the deep sea?** Almost every deep dive finds species that have no name yet. People have seen only a very small part of the deep sea floor.
- **Did life on Earth begin at vents on the sea floor?** Vents offer energy and chemicals with no sunlight. Shallow pools on land offer other things. Nobody has yet made life from chemicals in either setting.
- **How much water must be left flowing in a river to keep the river alive?** Fish, silt, the delta and the sea all need some flow, and the amounts differ for every river. Scientists and governments still argue about how to work it out.

## Connected maths
- In a made-up Vanishing Disc Test, the disc vanished at 24 centimetres with 1 teaspoon of milk. It vanished at 12 centimetres with 2 teaspoons, and at 6 centimetres with 4. What is the pattern? Predict the depth for 8 teaspoons and for 16. **Each time the milk doubles, the depth halves. With 8 teaspoons the depth would be about 3 centimetres. With 16 it would be about 1.5 centimetres.**. 24 divided by 2 is 12, and 12 divided by 2 is 6. So doubling the milk halves the depth. Half of 6 is 3, and half of 3 is 1.5. Another way to see it: teaspoons times depth is always 24. 1 times 24, 2 times 12 and 4 times 6 are all 24. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In a muddy pond, every 10 centimetres of water lets through half of the light that enters it. 100 units of light arrive at the surface. How much light is left at 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 centimetres? A water plant needs at least 10 units. How deep can she grow? **50, 25, 12.5, 6.25 and about 3 units. The plant can grow down to about 30 centimetres, where 12.5 units are left. At 40 centimetres only 6.25 units are left, which is too little.**. Halve the light for each 10 centimetres. Half of 100 is 50, half of 50 is 25, half of 25 is 12.5, half of 12.5 is 6.25, and half of 6.25 is about 3. The light never reaches zero, but it soon falls below what a plant needs. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In the sea, the press on each square centimetre rises by about 1 kilogram for every 10 metres of depth. A vehicle dives to 6,000 metres. By how much has the press on each square centimetre risen? Its window is 10 centimetres by 10 centimetres. What is the total extra press on the window? **The press has risen by 600 kilograms on each square centimetre. The window has 100 square centimetres, so the total extra press is 60,000 kilograms. That is 60 tonnes, about the weight of 12 elephants.**. 6,000 divided by 10 is 600. The window's area is 10 times 10, which is 100 square centimetres. 600 times 100 is 60,000 kilograms. There are 1,000 kilograms in a tonne, so that is 60 tonnes. A large elephant weighs about 5 tonnes, and 60 divided by 5 is 12. (queues `math.area-perimeter.area.L5`)
- Marine snow sinks about 100 metres a day. Plankton bloom at the surface on 1 June. The sea floor is 3,700 metres down. On what date does the first of that food reach the floor? **On 8 July. The fall takes 37 days.**. 3,700 divided by 100 is 37 days. June has 30 days, so 29 days after 1 June is 30 June. That leaves 8 more days, which brings us to 8 July. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- About 2 parts in 100 of the food made at the surface reach the deep sea floor. Above one patch of floor, the surface makes 5,000 kilograms of food in a year. How much reaches the floor? If the surface made only 3,000 kilograms, how much would reach it? **100 kilograms reach the floor. In the poorer year, 60 kilograms reach it.**. One part in 100 of 5,000 is 50, so 2 parts are 100 kilograms. One part in 100 of 3,000 is 30, so 2 parts are 60 kilograms. The floor loses 40 kilograms of a supply that was already small. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- Heavy rain falls on hills 1,200 kilometres up a river from a delta village. Suppose the flood moves down the river at about 5 kilometres an hour, day and night. How many days later does the river rise at the village? The village had no rain that week. What would the people there see? **The river rises 10 days later. The people would see the river rise and turn muddy under a dry sky.**. 1,200 divided by 5 is 240 hours. 240 divided by 24 is 10 days. The speed is made up for this problem. Real floods move at different speeds in different stretches of a river. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L5`)
