# Models and diagrams: how scientists show ideas, and what every model leaves out
`sci.how-we-know.models-and-diagrams.L3`

**Summary.** A globe is a model of the Earth, yet no rain falls on a globe and nobody lives there. So how can something that leaves out nearly everything help us understand the real thing? In this lesson you find out what a model is, how a diagram differs from a picture, and why scientists use several kinds of model. You build a model of something you cannot see, inside a sealed box, and test it. Then you ask a harder question: is the world you see with your own eyes also a kind of model?

**Intro.** *Draw the way from your bed to your front door, so that a visitor could follow it. Now look at your drawing. Did you draw the fan, the smell of the kitchen, the colour of the floor? Why not?* You left those things out without being told to. You kept only what a visitor needs: the doors, the turns, the order of the rooms. That drawing is a model of your home. Scientists do the same thing when they study the Earth, a heart or an atom. They keep a few important features and leave the rest out. Today you will learn how to make a model, how to read one, and how to ask what it is hiding.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can say what a model is: something that shows an object or an idea in a clear way.
1. I can name different types of models in science, including physical models and diagrams.
2. I can say how a diagram is different from a picture.
3. I can point out what a model shows and what it leaves out.
4. I can use a model to explain something and to predict what will happen.
5. I can build a model of something I cannot see, test it, and change it when the test goes against it.
6. I can explain why one thing can have several models, each good for a different job.

## Terms
- **Model**: Something that stands for a real thing or idea and shows it in a clear way. It keeps the important features and leaves the rest out. *e.g.* A globe stands for the Earth. You can turn it in your hands, which you cannot do with the real Earth.
- **Feature**: One part or property of a thing that you can point at, such as its shape, colour, size or place. *e.g.* A mango has features: a yellow-orange skin, a smooth shape, a big flat seed inside and a sweet smell.
- **Physical model**: A model you can hold and turn round. It is an object, with length, width and height. *e.g.* A plastic skeleton in a school lab is a physical model of the bones inside you.
- **Scale**: How the size of a model compares with the size of the real thing. *e.g.* On a globe, 1 centimetre stands for about 425 kilometres of the real Earth.
- **Diagram**: A simple drawing made to explain something. It has plain lines, labels and often arrows. *e.g.* A drawing of a mango cut in half, with the word 'seed' written beside the seed.
- **Label**: A word or short note written next to a part of a drawing to name it. *e.g.* In a drawing of a flower, the word 'petal' next to a petal is a label.
- **Arrow**: A line with a point at one end, used in a diagram to show which way something moves or which part a label is about. *e.g.* In a diagram of rain, an arrow rising from the sea shows water going up into the air.
- **Picture**: An image that shows one real thing as it looked at one moment, with whatever the eye or the camera happened to catch. *e.g.* A drawing of your street that includes the dog asleep by the gate, as he was that morning.
- **Photograph**: A picture made by a camera. It records the light that reached the camera at one moment. *e.g.* A phone photo of a hibiscus shows its shadow and the ant on one petal, as they were then.
- **Map**: A model of a place, seen from above, drawn smaller than the real place. *e.g.* A map of your school shows the gate, the classrooms and the playground as shapes seen from above.
- **Key**: The box on a map or diagram that tells you what its signs and colours mean. It is also called a legend. *e.g.* On a map, a blue wavy line in the key means a river.
- **Globe**: A ball-shaped model of the Earth, with the seas and lands drawn on its surface. *e.g.* A globe shows India as a curved shape. A flat map must stretch it a little.
- **Computer model**: A model made of numbers and rules that a computer works through. It has no shape you can hold. *e.g.* A computer model of the air over India can work out tomorrow's rain.
- **Forecast**: A statement about what will probably happen, worked out from a model. A weather forecast tells you tomorrow's weather. *e.g.* A forecast of rain on Tuesday tells a family to carry an umbrella.
- **Analogy**: A comparison that says 'this is like that', so that what you know about one thing helps you understand another. *e.g.* 'The heart is like a pump' lets you use what you know about a water pump to think about the heart.
- **Comparison**: Looking at two things side by side to find how they are alike and how they are different. *e.g.* Comparing a photo of a flower and a diagram of a flower shows that only the diagram has labels.
- **Explain**: To make clear how or why something happens, so that another person can understand it. *e.g.* You explain day and night by showing a ball turning in torchlight.
- **Predict**: To say what you think will happen next, before you look or try. *e.g.* Before you tilt a box, you predict that the marble will roll to the left end.
- **Prediction**: What you say will happen, said before you find out. A prediction can turn out right or wrong. *e.g.* 'The marble will stop at the wall' is a prediction, and tilting the box tells you if it was right.
- **Evidence**: What you actually see, measure or find, which you use to decide if an idea is right. *e.g.* That the dot vanished at arm's length is evidence about your eye.
- **Scale model**: A model that is the real thing made smaller or larger, with every part in the right proportion. *e.g.* A model bridge that is 50 times smaller than the real bridge, with all its parts kept in proportion.
- **Not to scale**: Words written on a diagram to say that its sizes or distances are not true to the real thing. *e.g.* A diagram of the solar system says 'not to scale' because the planets are drawn far too close together.
- **Solar system**: The Sun and everything that goes round it: the eight planets, their moons and many smaller things. *e.g.* The Earth, where you live, is part of the solar system.
- **Planet**: A large round world that goes round a star. The Earth is a planet that goes round the Sun. *e.g.* Jupiter is a planet. Eleven Earths would fit across it.
- **Ellipse**: A shape like a flattened circle, like an egg seen from the side. Planets move round the Sun on ellipses. *e.g.* If you tilt a circular steel plate and look at its rim, you see an ellipse.
- **Orbit**: The curved path of one thing round another, such as the Earth round the Sun. *e.g.* The Moon is held in an orbit round the Earth, and goes once round in about a month.
- **Eclipse**: When one thing in the sky moves in front of another and hides some of its light. *e.g.* In a solar eclipse the Moon moves between us and the Sun, and the day grows dark for a few minutes.
- **Gravity**: The pull that makes things fall and keeps the planets going round the Sun. Scientists can say how it acts. Nobody yet has a full theory of what it is. *e.g.* When you drop a ball, gravity pulls it to the floor.
- **Magnetism**: The push and pull between magnets, and between a magnet and some metals, such as iron. *e.g.* A fridge magnet sticks to the steel door because of magnetism.
- **Atom**: A particle so small that you cannot see it, which all things are built from. A full stop on this page is about as wide as a million atoms in a row. *e.g.* A single grain of salt holds far more atoms than anyone could count in a whole life.
- **Particle**: A very small piece of something. An atom is one kind of particle. *e.g.* A grain of salt is a particle you can see. An atom is a particle you cannot.
- **Taste bud**: A tiny group of cells on the tongue that senses the taste of what you eat or drink. *e.g.* When you lick a lemon, the taste buds on your tongue sense the sour.
- **Engineer**: A person who plans and builds things such as bridges, roads, machines and dams, using science. *e.g.* An engineer tests a small model of a dam before building a real one.
- **Wind tunnel**: A tube or room with a big fan, used to blow air past a model so that the push of the air can be measured. *e.g.* A model aeroplane held in a wind tunnel shows how air flows over its wings.
- **Test model**: A model made only to be tested, so that the real thing is built after the mistakes have been found. *e.g.* A small paper bridge that is loaded with coins until it bends is a test model.
- **Pattern**: Something that repeats or follows a rule, so that you can tell what comes next. *e.g.* The days of the week repeat in a pattern.
- **Waggle dance**: The dance of a honeybee who has found flowers. Her run, her angle and her time tell the others where the flowers are. *e.g.* She runs straight, waggling her body, and her sisters read her run to find flowers they have never seen.
- **Blind spot**: The small place at the back of each eye where the nerve leaves and there are no light sensors, so nothing is seen there. *e.g.* When the dot on the paper falls on your blind spot, it vanishes.
- **Nerve**: A thin cable of living cells that carries signals between parts of the body and the brain. *e.g.* The nerve from your eye carries signals about light to your brain.
- **Guess**: An idea you have before you have tested it. A guess is a start, and a test can show it right or wrong. *e.g.* You guess there is a wall in the box, and then you tilt the box to test the guess.
- **Useful**: Helping you do a job, such as finding your way or making a good prediction. A model can be useful without being exactly true. *e.g.* The straight-line metro map is useful for changing trains, though it is wrong about distances.
- **True**: Matching how things really are. Scientists test an idea again and again before they trust that it is true. *e.g.* 'The Earth turns once in a day' matches how things really are, and many tests agree.
- **Standpoint**: The place or way of looking from which a person sees something. Two people in different places see different parts. *e.g.* A child looking at a hill from the top and a child at the bottom see different things, and each has a different standpoint.
- **Territory**: The real place that a map or model is about, as against the map itself. *e.g.* The street outside your gate is the territory, and the drawing of it on paper is the map.
- **Pulse**: The beat you can feel in your wrist or neck each time your heart pushes blood along. Counting the beats in a minute tells you how fast the heart is going. *e.g.* After you run, your pulse is faster than when you sit still.
- **Cyclone**: A huge spinning storm with strong wind and heavy rain, which forms over warm sea and can flood the coast. *e.g.* A cyclone that crosses the coast of Odisha can blow down houses and flood villages.

## Explanations
### A model shows an object or an idea in a clear way · *defined*
**Definition:** A model is something made to stand for a real object, a process or an idea. It helps us see the real thing and think about it clearly.

Some things are hard to study directly. The Earth is too big to see all at once. A germ is too small. A mountain growing is too slow. Lightning is too fast and too dangerous. An idea, such as how sound travels, cannot be seen at all. So people make something that stands for the real thing and is easy to look at. That is a model. A globe stands for the Earth. A plastic skeleton stands for the bones inside you. A drawing of arrows stands for moving air. A model is not the real thing. It is a tool for thinking about the real thing.
- e.g. A globe on a teacher's table is a model of the Earth.
- e.g. A toy auto-rickshaw is a model of a real one.
- e.g. A clay heart with tubes is a model of the heart in your chest.
- e.g. A row of children passing a ball along is a model of how a message travels along a nerve.
### Every model leaves things out on purpose · *defined*
**Definition:** A model shows some important features of the real thing and leaves out the rest. What it keeps depends on what the model is for.

A globe shows the shapes of the land and the sea. It leaves out rain, smells, traffic and nearly everything else. That is not a mistake. Leaving things out is what makes a model clear. A classroom globe is about 30 centimetres across. The real Earth is about 12,742 kilometres across. So one centimetre on the globe stands for about 425 kilometres. At that size Mount Everest would be a bump only one-fifth of a millimetre high, too small to feel. The maker has to choose. A model that kept everything would be as big and as confusing as the thing itself.
- e.g. A toy car shows the shape and the wheels. It leaves out the engine, the petrol and the noise.
- e.g. A plastic skeleton shows the bones. It leaves out the muscles, the blood and the living person.
- e.g. In a story by Lewis Carroll, printed in 1893, a country makes a map as large as the country itself. The farmers complain that it would cover the fields and shut out the sunlight, so the map is never unrolled.
### Physical models are things you can hold and turn · *defined*
**Definition:** A physical model is a solid object built to stand for something real. It may be smaller than the real thing, larger, or the same size.

A physical model has length, width and height. You can pick it up, turn it round and look from behind. Some physical models are smaller than the real thing, such as a globe or a model bridge. Some are larger, such as a plastic flower as big as a football, made so that a whole class can see the parts. Some move. A model of the Sun, the Earth and the Moon on wire arms can show how an eclipse happens. The size of a model compared with the real thing is called its scale.
- e.g. A model eye that comes apart shows the lens and the layers behind it.
- e.g. A doll's house is a physical model of a house.
- e.g. A lump of atta dough pressed with a thumb is a model of how soft rock can be squeezed into folds.
- e.g. Shop models of teeth let a dentist show a child how to brush.
### A diagram is a simple drawing that explains · *defined*
**Definition:** A diagram is a drawing made of simple lines, labels and arrows that shows the parts of something and how they join or work.

A diagram is a model on paper. It uses plain lines and few colours. It has labels, which are words that name the parts. It often has arrows to show what moves and which way. A diagram can show things you could never see at one glance. It can slice a mango in half to show the seed. It can show the inside of your body with the skin left off. It can show a whole year of rain and sunshine in one loop of arrows. A diagram is made to be understood, not to look pretty.
- e.g. A diagram of a plant with labels for root, stem, leaf, flower and fruit.
- e.g. The diagram on a medicine leaflet showing how to use an inhaler.
- e.g. The steps printed on a packet of instant noodles, with arrows from one step to the next.
- e.g. A raised diagram made of dots and lines that a child who cannot see can read with the fingers.
### How a diagram is different from a picture · *defined*
**Definition:** A picture shows how a thing looks. A diagram shows how a thing is put together or how it works.

Take a photograph of a hibiscus flower. It shows this one flower, on this one day, with its colour, its shadow and the ant walking on a petal. Now look at a diagram of a flower. It has no shadow and no ant. It has clean lines and the names of the parts. It stands for every flower of that kind. A picture keeps whatever the eye or the camera happened to catch. A diagram keeps only what its maker chose, and adds labels and arrows that are not there in real life. So a picture is good for knowing what something looks like. A diagram is good for understanding the thing.
- e.g. A photo of a bicycle shows the mud, the bell and the rust. A diagram of a bicycle shows how the pedals, the chain and the back wheel are joined.
- e.g. A painting of a river shows the light on the water. A diagram shows where the river starts, which streams join, and where the river meets the sea.
- e.g. A picture of the Moon shows one evening's Moon. A diagram can show eight shapes of the Moon over a month in one row.
- **Science teachers and textbook writers:** A diagram is clearer than a picture for learning, because everything that does not matter has been taken away.
- **Bird watchers and other field naturalists:** A clean drawing shows the perfect bird. The real bird in the bush is half hidden, wet or young. To know a living being you need pictures, notes and many hours of watching as well as the diagram.
- **Artists of the Warli people of Maharashtra:** Warli paintings show people, animals and trees with circles, triangles and lines. They do not copy how things look. They show how things are joined: the dance, the harvest, the village. A drawing can sit between a picture and a diagram.
### A map is a diagram of a place, and a flat map must stretch something · *defined*
**Definition:** A map is a diagram of land or sea, drawn smaller than real size, using a scale and agreed signs.

A map is a model of a place seen from above. It has a scale, which tells you how much real ground each centimetre stands for. It has a key, which explains its signs, such as a blue line for a river. Here is a problem. The Earth is round like a ball, and paper is flat. Try to flatten an orange peel and it tears or stretches. So every flat map of the world stretches some part. On the map used in most classrooms, Greenland looks about as large as Africa. In truth Africa is about 14 times larger than Greenland. India is larger than Greenland too.
- e.g. The classroom map was designed by Gerardus Mercator in 1569 to help sailors steer. It keeps directions right and makes lands far from the middle look too big.
- e.g. A globe shows sizes truly, but you cannot fold it into a school bag.
- e.g. A map of your town on a phone leaves out every tree and every dog, and shows every road.
- **Sailors and pilots:** On Mercator's map a straight line is a steady compass direction. For steering a ship this matters more than true sizes.
- **Arno Peters in 1973, following James Gall in 1855:** They drew a world map that keeps the sizes of lands true. It makes the shapes look pulled long. Peters argued that the usual map made Europe look more important than Africa, India and South America.
- **Map-makers today:** No flat map can keep sizes, shapes, distances and directions all true at once. This was proved by the mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1827. So you pick the map for the job.
- **Navigators of the Marshall Islands, in the Pacific Ocean:** Their stick charts are maps of the sea, not of the land. Bent palm ribs show how ocean swells curve round the islands, and a shell marks each island. The navigators learned the charts on land and did not carry them on voyages. At sea they felt the real swells through the canoe, and used the chart in their memory.
- **Pacific navigators, such as Mau Piailug of Satawal island in 1976:** In 1976 he sailed the canoe Hokule'a from Hawaii to Tahiti, more than 4,000 kilometres, with no map or compass. His model was a star compass, a ring of the places where stars rise and set. He carried it in his memory. Voyagers who steered by such models, including the Polynesian ancestors of the Maori, settled the islands of the Pacific.
### Some models are made of numbers and run inside computers · *defined*
**Definition:** A computer model is a set of rules and numbers that stands for a real process, such as the weather. A computer works it out.

A model does not have to be a thing or a drawing. It can be a rule. 'Distance equals speed multiplied by time' is a model of a journey. A weather forecast comes from a very large model of this kind. The air over India is divided into boxes. For each box the computer holds numbers for heat, wind and wetness. Rules from science say how each box changes the boxes beside it. The computer steps the numbers forward, hour by hour, and out comes tomorrow's weather. The model leaves out a great deal, so the forecast is a likelihood and not a promise.
- e.g. In October 2013, forecasters in India used computer models to say where Cyclone Phailin would reach the coast. They gave the warning days ahead. About ten lakh people in Odisha and Andhra Pradesh moved to safety, and fewer than fifty died. In 1999 a cyclone hit Odisha when warnings were weaker, and about 10,000 people died.
- e.g. A recipe that says 'one tumbler of rice needs two tumblers of water' is a number model of cooking rice.
- e.g. Doctors use number models to work out how fast an illness might spread through a city.
### A model in words: 'this is like that' · *defined*
**Definition:** An analogy is a comparison that explains something unfamiliar by saying it is like something familiar in one way.

You can build a model out of words. 'The heart is like a pump.' 'Electricity in a wire is like water in a pipe.' A comparison like this is called an analogy. It lets you carry what you know about one thing over to another. But an analogy is right in some ways and wrong in others, like every model. Cut a pipe and water spills on the floor. Cut a wire and no electricity pours out. So with every 'this is like that', ask two things. In what way is it like? And where does the likeness stop?
- e.g. 'A seed is like a packed tiffin box.' True: the seed holds a food store for the young plant. Not true: no cook packs a seed. The parent plant makes the food store, and the young plant inside is alive and grows.
- e.g. 'The Earth is like a spinning top.' True: both spin about a line through the middle. Not true: nobody set the Earth spinning with a string.
- e.g. 'A nerve is like a telephone wire.' True: both carry signals. Not true: a nerve is made of living cells, which pass the signal along by their own chemistry.
- **Nyaya Sutra 1.1.6, by Akshapada Gautama, composed perhaps about 2,000 years ago:** The Nyaya school of Indian philosophy counts comparison, called upamana, as one of four proper ways of knowing. The usual example: a man is told that a gavaya, a wild ox, looks like a cow. Later he meets one in the forest and knows what he is seeing.
- **The Vaisheshika and Buddhist schools of the same period:** They replied that comparison is not a separate way of knowing. It is only seeing joined with reasoning. The schools argued about this for centuries.
- **The physicist James Clerk Maxwell, in 1855:** He worked out the rules of electricity and magnetism by comparing them with flowing water. He warned that a comparison like this lends ideas. It does not prove that the two things are truly alike.
### We use a model to explain what we see · *defined*
**Definition:** To explain with a model is to show, step by step on the model, how the real thing comes about.

Why is it day in Kochi while it is night in Mexico? Words alone make this hard. A model makes it easy. Hold a ball in a dark room and shine a torch on it. Half the ball is lit and half is dark. Put a dot of pen on the ball for Kochi and turn the ball slowly. The dot goes into the light and then into the dark. Now you can say why day and night happen: the Earth turns, and each place is carried into the Sun's light and out again. The model did not prove this. It let you see how the explanation works.
- e.g. A sponge squeezed in a bowl of water explains how soil holds rain and lets it go.
- e.g. Marbles packed in a box, then shaken, explain how the tiny particles of a solid differ from those of a liquid.
- e.g. A doctor uses a model of the knee to explain to a patient what is torn.
### A good model also predicts, and a prediction can show the model is wrong · *defined*
**Definition:** A model predicts when it lets you say what will happen in a case you have not yet seen. If the prediction fails, the model must be changed.

A model earns trust by telling you something before you look. Take the torch and ball again. Ask it a new question: at the very top of the ball, how long does a day last? Turn the ball. If the ball stands straight, the top is always at the edge of the light. If you tilt the ball, the top stays in the light for turn after turn. So the tilted model predicts that the far north has a season when the Sun does not set. That is true. In June the Sun stays up all night in the far north of Norway. When a model's prediction turns out false, scientists do not hide it. They mend the model or build a new one.
- e.g. A model of a paper bridge predicts where it will fold. Load it with coins and see.
- e.g. A weather model predicts rain on Thursday. Thursday comes and tests the model.
- e.g. In 1846 astronomers used Newton's model of gravity to predict where an unseen planet must be. They pointed a telescope there and found Neptune.
### One thing can have many models, each good for one job · *defined*
**Definition:** Different models of the same thing keep different features. The best one depends on the question being asked.

Think of a city's metro. One map shows the lines as straight coloured strokes with the stations evenly spaced. It is wrong about distances and bends. It is perfect for answering 'where do I change trains?' Another map shows the true path of the tracks under the streets. It is hard to read on a platform. It is what an engineer needs to dig a tunnel. Neither map is the real metro. Each is right for its job. In science too, one thing often has several models, and a scientist picks one the way you pick a tool.
- e.g. The metro map idea came from Harry Beck in London in 1933. His job was drawing electrical wiring plans, and he drew the railway the same way.
- e.g. The Earth has a globe, a flat map, a model cut open to show its layers, and a computer model of its weather.
- e.g. Light is modelled as straight rays to explain shadows and as waves to explain the colours on a soap bubble.
- e.g. Your body has a skeleton model, a model of the blood vessels and a model of the nerves. No single model shows all three clearly.
- **Jain philosophers, in the teaching called anekantavada:** A real thing has countless sides, and any one statement about it is true only from one standpoint. So several different accounts can each be right in part.
- **The Udana, a Buddhist text, section 6.4:** A king has men who were born blind each touch one part of an elephant. One says the elephant is like a pot, another like a plough, another like a pillar. They quarrel, because each takes a part for the whole.
- **Physicists who look for one final theory, such as Steven Weinberg:** They hold that the many models are steps on the way. Underneath there should be one set of laws from which all the others follow.
### Scale: when a model cannot be drawn to true size · *defined*
**Definition:** A scale model keeps every size and distance in the same proportion to the real thing. Some real things are spread out so widely that a true scale model will not fit on a page.

Textbook diagrams of the solar system show the Sun and the planets in a neat row, close together. Let us make that model true to scale. Let the Sun be a ball 22 centimetres across, about the size of a football. Then the Earth is a mustard seed 2 millimetres across. The seed must sit about 24 metres from the ball, which is two bus lengths. Neptune is a peppercorn about 700 metres away. No page can hold that. So the diagram cheats on distance to keep everything in view. Honest diagrams say so, with the words 'not to scale'.
- e.g. A diagram of an atom draws the centre large. Picture a grain of sand at the middle of a cricket ground. That is the centre of a light atom, and the whole atom reaches almost to the boundary rope.
- e.g. A map of India in an atlas draws the Ganga as a line you can see. At true scale the river would be far thinner than a hair on that page.
### A wrong model can still predict well: the Earth-centred sky · *defined*
**Definition:** A model can give correct predictions and still be wrong about how the real thing is arranged.

About 150 CE, in Egypt, Ptolemy built a model of the sky with the Earth standing still at the centre. The Sun, the Moon and the planets went round the Earth on circles carried by other circles. This model predicted where each planet would appear, well enough to be used for about 1,400 years. Yet the Earth is not at the centre. The model worked because circles on circles can copy almost any path. A model that gives the right answers has not yet shown that it is built the way the world is built.
- e.g. A timetable predicts when the bus comes. It tells you nothing true about how the engine works.
- e.g. You can predict the Sun's place in the sky tomorrow perfectly well while believing that the Sun goes round the Earth.
- **Aryabhata, in the Aryabhatiya, Gola section, verse 9, written in 499 CE:** He wrote that the stars only seem to move westward, because the Earth turns, as the bank seems to move backward to someone in a boat. Most astronomers of his time, in India and outside, did not accept this.
- **Nilakantha Somayaji of Kerala, in the Tantrasangraha, written in 1500 CE:** He changed the model so that Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn go round the Sun, while the Sun goes round the Earth. It fitted the planets better than earlier models.
- **Nicolaus Copernicus in Poland in 1543, and Johannes Kepler in 1609:** Copernicus put the Sun at the centre with the Earth as one of the planets. Kepler found that the paths are stretched circles called ellipses. That model predicted better still, and is the one used today.
### Models change when new evidence arrives: the atom · *defined*
**Definition:** A scientific model is kept only while it fits the evidence. When a test goes against it, a better model replaces it.

All things are made of tiny particles called atoms. Nobody could see one, so scientists made models. In 1803 John Dalton pictured an atom as a tiny solid ball. In 1904 J. J. Thomson, who had found still smaller particles inside the atom, pictured a soft ball with bits dotted through it. In 1909 Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden fired fast particles at thin gold. A few bounced straight back, which a soft ball could never do. So in 1911 Ernest Rutherford drew a new model: a tiny hard centre with mostly empty space round it. The model has been changed twice more since. Each change came from a test the old model failed.
- e.g. Your model of what is inside a wrapped present changes when you shake it and hear a rattle.
- e.g. Doctors once pictured the stomach as too sour for anything to live there. In 1982 Barry Marshall and Robin Warren found bacteria living in the stomach, and the model had to change.
- **Kanada, in the Vaisheshika Sutra, composed in India more than 2,000 years ago:** He reasoned that if you keep dividing a thing you must reach a smallest part that cannot be divided, which he called paramanu. He reached this by argument and not by experiment.
- **Ernst Mach, a physicist in Vienna, until his death in 1916:** He held that atoms were only a handy model for doing sums, since nobody had seen one. He asked, 'Have you seen one?'
- **Erwin Müller and his student Kanwar Bahadur, in the United States in 1955:** With a new kind of microscope they made the first pictures in which single atoms showed as separate dots. After that, few doubted that atoms are real things.
### A diagram can be wrong and still be copied for years · *defined*
**Definition:** A diagram looks certain because its lines are clean. It is only as true as the evidence behind it.

Many school books once had a diagram of the tongue divided into zones. Sweet was at the tip, bitter at the back, and sour and salty at the sides. It is wrong. Every part of the tongue that has taste buds can sense every taste. The mistake began in 1901, when a German scientist, David Hänig, found only very small differences between parts of the tongue. In 1942 a book redrew those small differences as a map with firm borders. The neat map was copied from book to book. In 1974 Virginia Collings tested it again and showed that the differences are tiny. You can test it too. Put a few grains of sugar on the back of your tongue and see what you taste.
- e.g. A picture of an ape slowly straightening into a walking human suggests that living beings change in one straight line towards us. They do not. The family of living beings branches like a banyan.
- e.g. Diagrams often draw blood in veins as blue. Blood is always red, darker or brighter.
### Engineers test models before they build the real thing · *defined*
**Definition:** A test model is a small, cheap copy of something that is tried out first, so that mistakes are found before the real thing is built.

A real bridge, dam or rocket costs a great deal, and a mistake can kill. So engineers build a small model and test that. A model aeroplane is held in a tunnel while a fan blows air past, and instruments measure the push of the air. A model river, with real water and sand, shows where a flood would break the bank. Today many of these tests are first run as number models inside a computer. A physical model is then built to check the computer.
- e.g. In 1901 Wilbur and Orville Wright built a wind tunnel from a wooden box and a fan. They tested some two hundred small wing shapes before building the aeroplane that flew in 1903.
- e.g. The Central Water and Power Research Station in Pune, founded in 1916, builds models of rivers, dams and harbours that are as large as playgrounds.
- e.g. Small models of India's rockets and aircraft are tested in wind tunnels in Bengaluru before the real ones are built.
- e.g. A tailor cuts a paper pattern, a flat model of the blouse, before cutting the silk.
### Other living beings use models too · *defined*
**Definition:** Many animals pass on or carry a simplified version of the world, which is what a model is.

A honeybee who finds flowers flies home and dances on the comb in the dark hive. She runs in a straight line, waggling her body, then circles back and repeats. The direction of her run, measured from straight up, shows the direction of the flowers measured from the Sun. The time she waggles shows how far to fly, roughly one second for each kilometre. Her sisters read the dance and fly to flowers they have never seen. The dance is a small moving diagram of a journey. It leaves out everything except direction and distance.
- e.g. Karl von Frisch in Austria worked out the meaning of the dance in the 1940s and shared a Nobel Prize for it in 1973.
- e.g. In 1948 Edward Tolman showed that rats who had explored a maze could take a new short cut when the usual path was blocked. He said they carry a map of the maze in their heads.
- **Karl von Frisch and most bee scientists today:** The dance carries information that other bees use, and radar tracking of bees in 2005 confirmed that they fly where the dance points.
- **Adrian Wenner, an American bee scientist, from the 1960s:** He argued for decades that bees find the flowers by smell alone and that the dance is not read. Later tests went against him, but his challenge forced better experiments.
- **Scientists who study animal minds:** Bees think, remember and decide. Some scientists prefer to call the dance an automatic signal and avoid the word 'model'. What it is like for a bee to dance or to follow a dance is not known to anyone.
### Is a good model true, or only useful? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists and philosophers disagree about whether a successful model describes the world as it really is or is only a tool that works.

The atom model works. The Sun-centred model works. Does that mean the world really is the way these models say? Or only that the models are handy for getting right answers? This is one of the oldest arguments in science, and it is not settled. Remember Ptolemy, whose model worked for 1,400 years and was wrong. Remember also the atom, which began as a model and was later photographed. One model turned out to be a mere tool. The other turned out to be a real thing. Nobody has a sure way to tell in advance which kind a new model is.
- e.g. A weather forecast of '70 per cent chance of rain' is useful. It makes no claim that the sky contains a 70.
- e.g. A map is useful for finding the bus stand, and the bus stand is really there.
- **Realists, such as the philosopher Hilary Putnam in 1975:** If our best models were not at least roughly true, their success in predicting new things would be a miracle. So good models describe what is really there.
- **George Box, a statistician, in 1976:** He wrote that all models are wrong, and later added that some are useful. A model always leaves things out, so the right question is whether it helps.
- **Alfred Korzybski, a Polish-American thinker, in 1931:** He put it in a phrase that is still quoted: a map is not the territory. The words and pictures we use for a thing are never the thing.
- **Nagarjuna, a Buddhist philosopher of South India, about the 2nd century CE, in the Mulamadhyamakakarika, chapter 24:** He taught two levels of truth. Everyday truth, in words and ideas, is needed for living and for teaching. Final truth cannot be held in any idea. The first is the way to the second, and should not be mistaken for it.
### The world you see may be a model that your body builds · *idea*
**Definition:** Many scientists think that seeing is not like a camera taking a picture. The brain builds a best guess about what is out there and checks the guess against signals from the eyes.

Each of your eyes has a small spot at the back where the nerve leaves. That spot has no light sensors, so it sees nothing. Yet you never notice a hole in the world. Why not? The usual explanation is that the brain fills the gap with a guess made from what lies around it. If that is right, part of what you see at this moment is not coming in through the eye. It is being supplied. Some scientists go further. They say all seeing works like this: the brain keeps a running model of the world and uses the eyes to correct it. Others disagree. What seeing finally is, and why there is any inner experience at all, science cannot yet say.
- e.g. The blind spot was found by Edme Mariotte in France in 1660. You can find your own with a dot and a cross drawn 10 centimetres apart on paper, as in the blind-spot test.
- e.g. In dim light a coil of rope can look like a snake. When the light comes on, the rope is only a rope. The rope did not change. What changed was how much your brain had to guess.
- e.g. A bee sees ultraviolet patterns on a flower that you cannot see. Her world and yours are built from different signals.
- **Hermann von Helmholtz in 1867, and the brain scientist Anil Seth today:** Seeing is a kind of guessing that we are not aware of doing. The brain predicts what is there and the senses correct the prediction.
- **James Gibson, an American scientist of seeing, in 1979:** He argued that there is no inner model. The light reaching a moving animal already holds all the information she needs. She picks it up directly.
- **Gaudapada, in the Mandukya Karika, chapter 2, verses 17 and 18, about the 7th century CE:** He writes that a rope, not seen clearly in the dark, is imagined to be a snake. When the rope is known, the snake is gone. He uses this to say that we lay our own ideas over what is really there.
### Three questions to ask of any model · *defined*
**Definition:** To judge a model, ask what it shows, what it leaves out, and where it stops being like the real thing.

You now have a way to read any model, diagram, map or comparison. First: what does this model show clearly? Second: what has it left out, and does that matter for my question? Third: where does it break, which means where would it give me a wrong answer? Try it on a globe. It shows the shapes and places of the lands. It leaves out height, weather and life. It breaks if you use it to guess how rough the Earth feels, because the globe is smooth and the Himalayas are not. A person who asks these three questions is hard to fool with a neat diagram.
- e.g. A food pyramid on a packet shows which foods to eat more of. It leaves out how much food your own body needs, which depends on your age and your work.
- e.g. A toy stethoscope shows the shape of a doctor's tool. It breaks if you try to hear a heartbeat with it.
- e.g. A diagram of the water cycle shows one drop going round one loop. It leaves out that a drop may wait in the deep sea for hundreds of years.

## Mostly agreed
- People drew careful maps of places thousands of years ago. *How we know:* A clay tablet from Nippur in ancient Iraq, made about 1500 BCE, shows the city's walls, canals and temple. Archaeologists who dug at Nippur found that the layout on the tablet matched the ruins closely.
- Building a physical model can lead to a discovery that measurements alone did not give. *How we know:* In 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick built a metal model of DNA. They fitted its pieces together until it agreed with the X-ray measurements of Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling. Their first model of 1951 had three strands and was wrong. Franklin, who saw it, pointed out its flaws. Brenda Maddox tells this in her 2002 book about Franklin.
- People understand and remember an explanation better when they draw their own diagram of it. *How we know:* In 2011 Shaaron Ainsworth, Vaughan Prain and Russell Tytler wrote a review of classroom studies in the journal Science. They found that children often reasoned better when they drew their own ideas of what was happening. This included the parts they could not see. Children who only read or copied gained less.
- Honeybees who watch a dance fly in the direction and to the distance that the dance shows. *How we know:* In 2005 Joe Riley and his colleagues in England fixed tiny radar aerials to bees who had watched a dance, and tracked their flights. The bees flew straight to the area the dance pointed to, even when they were released from a different spot.

## How we found out
**William Herschel, John Couch Adams, Urbain Le Verrier, Johann Galle, Heinrich d'Arrest, Albert Einstein, 1781 to 1915.** In 1781 William Herschel found a new planet, Uranus. By the 1820s Uranus was not where Newton's model of gravity said it should be, and its path kept drifting from the sums. People had two ideas. Either Newton's law fails far from the Sun, or an unseen planet is pulling Uranus. Most trusted Newton, because his law had worked everywhere else. In 1845 and 1846 John Couch Adams in England and Urbain Le Verrier in France each worked out where such a planet must be. On 23 September 1846 Johann Galle and his student Heinrich d'Arrest searched Berlin's sky where Le Verrier had said. They found Neptune within about one degree of that place. A model had pointed to a world nobody had seen. Then, in 1859, Le Verrier tried the same move on a small oddity in Mercury's path. He predicted a planet, Vulcan, and nobody ever found it. In 1915 Albert Einstein showed that his new model of gravity explained Mercury's path with no new planet at all.  
*What people believed before:* People believed that when a model's answer does not match what we see, the same repair always works: add something unseen. For Uranus this was right, and a new planet was found. For Mercury the same repair was wrong, and a whole new model of gravity was needed. Both guesses were reasonable until they were tested.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A model that only predicts, without explaining why, is good enough for science.** (Some builders of artificial intelligence weather models, since about 2022; *debated*)  
  For: Computer programs trained on forty years of past weather now forecast several days ahead about as well as the models built from the rules of physics. They are far faster. They contain no rules about air or heat.  
  Against: Many scientists reply that a model you cannot look inside may fail without warning when the world changes, as the climate is doing. Ptolemy's circles also predicted well without being right.
- **There is no world 'as it really is' apart from our models. Each good model makes its own reality.** (Stephen Hawking and Leonard Mlodinow, in 'The Grand Design', 2010; *debated*)  
  For: A goldfish in a curved bowl sees a bent world and could still work out correct laws for it. We have no way to step outside all our models and compare them with the bare world.  
  Against: Realists answer that the world pushes back. Models fail, and something makes them fail. That something is the world, whatever our models say.
- **Seeing is direct. The brain builds no inner model of the world.** (James Gibson in 1979, and scientists of the 'ecological' school after him; *minority*)  
  For: Animals with very small nervous systems steer through the world skilfully. The light reaching a moving eye changes in ways that directly signal distance and speed, with no need for a built picture.  
  Against: Filling-in at the blind spot, and the many pictures that fool the eye, are hard to explain unless the brain is supplying something. Most brain scientists today favour some kind of inner model.
- **Children should learn from real things first, because models and diagrams plant wrong ideas.** (Some science educators, following the work of Rosalind Driver in the 1980s on children's ideas; *debated*)  
  For: Studies find that many children take a model literally. They believe that atoms are coloured balls, or that the planets are close together, because the diagram showed them so.  
  Against: Other educators answer that nobody can study an atom or a solar system directly. The cure is to teach, with every model, what it leaves out.

## Roots
You have learnt that every model keeps a little and leaves out a lot. Now we ask what that tells us about our own seeing, about life and about this world.

**Deep truth.** Each of your eyes has a spot that sees nothing. You found it with a dot and a cross. Yet you have never seen a hole in the world, because the brain fills the gap with a likely guess. So part of what you see is supplied and not received. Plain seeing already has things left out and things put in, as every model does.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'I saw it with my own eyes' as if seeing were the thing itself, with nothing chosen and nothing added. That habit makes us treat our own view as the world and other views as mistakes.  
*Evidence:* In the blind-spot test, the dot vanished at about 30 to 40 centimetres from the eye, and the paper looked whole where the dot had been. Nothing in the eye reported that white paper. In the sealed box test, the child also built a confident picture of something never seen.

- *who am i:* You carry a model of yourself: your name, what you are good at, what you are like. It is useful, and it leaves most of you out, as a globe leaves out the rain. Knowing that it is a model lets you ask what is there before any model is made.
- *what is life:* A bee, a bat and a child each build a world from different signals, and each world is enough to live by. In that sense every living being makes models. Understanding this, you stop asking which being sees correctly and start asking what each one's seeing is for.
- *what is this world:* The Earth has a globe, a map and a weather model, and none of them is the Earth. The same is true of our best science. The world is what all the models are about, and it is more than any of them holds.

This lesson showed that leaving things out is how understanding works. A globe leaves out the rain, a diagram leaves out the shadows, and your eye leaves out a patch and covers the gap. That is not a fault to fix. It does mean that what we call 'the world' is always the world as shaped by a viewer. Some traditions go one step further. They say that awareness is the ground in which every view arises, the bee's and the child's alike. The views are many and partial, and the ground they arise in is one. Many scientists stop before this last step, and their view is given below.

- **Tao Te Ching, chapter 1:** Laozi's book opens with a warning. The way that can be spoken of is not the lasting way. The name that can be named is not the lasting name. (A name is a small model. The verse says that whatever can be put into a name or a description has already left the whole behind. The lesson found the same about maps and diagrams, by measuring what a globe leaves out.)
- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.3.6:** The sage describes the ground of everything by saying 'neti, neti', which means 'not this, not this'. Whatever you can point to or picture, that is not the whole of it. (This is a method of knowing by setting models aside one after another. Science keeps its models and tests them. The Upanishad uses them as steps and then lets them go. The two methods aim at different things.)
- **The Shurangama Sutra, a Buddhist text known in China from about the 8th century CE:** The Buddha says that when someone points a finger at the Moon, a foolish person looks at the finger and misses the Moon. (A diagram is a pointing finger. The lesson's tongue map shows what happens when the neat drawing is studied in place of the tongue.)
- Science (settled): Each eye has a blind spot where the nerve leaves, and we do not notice a gap in what we see. This has been measured since 1660.
- Science (debated): Some scientists think the brain works mainly by predicting what the senses will report, and then correcting the prediction. This idea is called predictive processing. Many experiments support it, but it is not yet agreed.
- Other view, Scientific realists, and the Nyaya school of Indian philosophy: The world exists just as it is whether or not anyone models it, and careful looking and reasoning give us true knowledge of it. The blind spot is a small fault in a reliable instrument. To say that all seeing is a model goes too far, and risks suggesting that nothing can be known.
- Practice, Looking before naming (3 min): Choose one thing in the room, such as a tumbler. Look at it for one minute without saying its name in your head. Look at the edge, the light on it, the dark side. Each time the word 'tumbler' comes, let it go and keep looking. After a minute, close your eyes for three breaths. Then tell me one thing you saw that the word 'tumbler' leaves out.
- Ask: In the blind-spot test, what did you see in the place where the dot had been? Where did that come from?
- Ask: If a bee and you look at the same flower and see different things, is one of you wrong?
- Ask: You have a model of who you are. What is one true thing about you that it leaves out?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* If you kept adding detail to a map until nothing was left out, what would you end up with? (hint: Imagine adding every lane, then every stone, then every ant. Ask how big the map would be and what you could use it for.)
- *deep:* Ptolemy's sky model gave right answers for 1,400 years and was wrong. How could we find a hidden mistake in today's models? (hint: Think about what finally went against Ptolemy. Was it a better argument, or a new kind of looking?)
- *deep:* A diagram of a flower stands for every flower of that kind. Has anyone ever seen the flower that the diagram shows? (hint: Pick three real flowers from one plant and compare each with the diagram. Then ask where the diagram's flower exists.)
- *deep:* Your eye has a spot that sees nothing, yet you see no hole. How much of what you see is filled in? (hint: Do the blind-spot test first. Then ask how you could ever check the rest of your view.)
- *big:* A bee's dance leaves out the flowers' colour and smell. Who decides what a model leaves out? What does that show? (hint: Compare a map drawn by a bus driver, a postman and a crow, if a crow could draw.)
- *big:* You have a picture in your mind of your best friend. Is that picture a model? What might it leave out? (hint: Think of a time a friend surprised you. Ask what in your picture had to change.)
- *big:* Most world maps put north at the top and Europe in the middle. Would we think differently with the map upside down? (hint: Turn an atlas page upside down and look at India for a full minute. Notice what looks important now.)
- *big:* We say 'the heart is like a pump'. Could people understand the heart before pumps were invented? (hint: Ask what people compared the heart with before there were pumps, and what a comparison can and cannot give you.)
- *big:* Is your idea of yourself also a model? If so, who made it, and what does it leave out? (hint: Try to list everything that is true of you. Notice what you put first and what you never thought to add.)

## Story: Three Maps of One Hill
*The children, the aunt, the dog and the bakery are made up. Munsiyari is a real town in Uttarakhand. Nain Singh Rawat was a real surveyor, born in 1830 in the village of Milam in the Johar valley. In 1865 and 1866 he walked to Lhasa for the Survey of India, measuring the way by counting equal steps on a string of 100 beads and hiding his notes in a prayer wheel. He found his height above the sea by boiling water and reading a thermometer. The Royal Geographical Society gave him a gold medal in 1877. What the herders he met carried in their heads is imagined, though herders everywhere hold such knowledge.*

Karan, who was ten, had come from Chennai to stay with his cousin Pema in Munsiyari, a town high in the mountains of Uttarakhand. Pema was nine and had lived there all her life. On the second morning Karan wanted to walk to the bakery alone. 'Draw me a map,' he said.

Pema drew fast. Her house. Stone steps going down. A big walnut tree. A dog with very large teeth, and the word RUN beside him. A tap where the path split in two. Then the bakery, with a bun drawn on the roof.

Karan held his phone next to her paper. 'Your map is wrong. The phone shows a road going right round the hill. Yours goes straight down. And the phone has no dog.'

'The phone has no steps,' said Pema, 'because cars cannot use steps. And the phone does not care if you get bitten.'

Their aunt Lhamu taught geography at the school. She had been listening from the doorway. 'Neither map is wrong,' she said. 'Each one leaves out what its maker did not need. Shall I show you a third? It was made by a man from the next valley, and he was not allowed to carry even a measuring tape.'

She took a book from the shelf. 'Nain Singh Rawat was born in 1830 in Milam, a few days' walk from here. In 1865 the Survey of India wanted a map of Tibet. The rulers of Tibet did not allow map-makers in. So Nain Singh went dressed as a trader. He had trained himself to make every step the same length. He carried a string of prayer beads, but his string had one hundred beads and not the usual one hundred and eight. Every hundred steps, he slipped one bead along.'

'He counted every single step?' said Karan.

'All the way to Lhasa and far beyond. He wrote the numbers on paper rolled up inside his prayer wheel. And to find how high he had climbed, he boiled water and read a thermometer, because water boils at a lower temperature the higher you go.'

She opened the book at his map. It was a thin line with a river beside it, a few dots with names, and rows of numbers. There was no dog, no bakery and no walnut tree.

'It is so empty,' said Karan.

'It holds exactly what the Survey asked for. How far, in which direction, how high. With only those three things, people who had never left their desks could mark Lhasa correctly on a globe.'

Pema frowned. 'But the people in Tibet did not want to be on that map.'

'No, they did not,' said Lhamu aunty. 'A map is always made for someone. The yak herders who walked beside Nain Singh had a map as well, in their heads. Theirs held where the grass comes first in spring, which pass is deadly in a storm, which stream is safe to drink from. None of that is on his sheet. Their map could keep you alive up there. His could tell a trader or an army how many days the march would take.'

Karan looked from the phone to the paper to the book. 'So which one is the real Munsiyari?'

'Go and see,' said Lhamu aunty.

He took Pema's map. On the steps he met things that were on no map at all. The smell of wood smoke. A woman spreading red chillies to dry on a roof. Air so cold that it stung his teeth. The dog was exactly where the map said. Karan ran.

He came back with four buns. He picked up Pema's pencil and wrote 'chillies' beside the steps. Then he stopped and rubbed it out. 'They will be gone next week,' he said. 'A map cannot keep things that change. It would have to choose one day.'

'What if you made a map with everything on it?' said Pema. 'Every stone and every smell, on every day?'

Karan thought. 'It would be as big as the town. I would have to live inside it.'

'You already do,' said Pema, and took the biggest bun.

That night Karan lay awake. In his head he walked from his flat in Chennai to the beach. He knew every turn. But it was a map as well, he saw now, and he had made it himself. What had he left out of it, without ever deciding to?

**For the parent:** Each map answered its maker's question and left out everything else. No map was the hill. Even the picture of home that Karan carried in his own head was a map he had drawn without noticing.

- Ask: Pema's map had a dog and Nain Singh's had numbers. What does each map tell you about the person who made it?
- Ask: The people of Tibet did not want their land mapped. Can making a model of something ever be unfair to it?
- Ask: Karan rubbed out the chillies. What would be on a map of your street that is true only today?
- Ask: What is in the map of your home that you carry in your head, and what have you left out?

### g1 · The right model for the job: a card game for 2 to 4 players (25 min)
- **rules:** There are 10 model cards. Each model has a plain name, what it shows and, on the back, what it leaves out and where it breaks.; Shuffle the 16 job cards into a face-down pile. Shows how one quantity depends on another, such as distance equals speed multiplied by time. (back: It leaves out the look and the feel of the thing. It breaks if the rule holds only when the speed is steady.)
- **Printable score sheet: Score sheet to copy by hand: one row for each job, with columns for each player's points:** Job 1 to job 16. Write 2 points for the best model, 1 point for a good argument, 0 for a weak one.
- **g1s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cut out the 16 job cards and the 10 model cards. Read all the model cards aloud together. Say what each one shows and what it leaves out. Shuffle the job cards and lay the model cards face up in the middle.  
  *Parent says:* Here are ten kinds of model. Each one is good for some jobs and bad for others. Which would you use to find a train change? Which for a tunnel?  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Read the ten models. Lay them face up.
  - Note: Cutting is for the grown-up if the child is under 8. Keep the cards in an envelope. A game for one child can be played alone, using all ten models.
- **g1s2 (14 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the game. Each player turns up a job card, chooses a model card from their hand, says why, takes one challenge and scores. Read the back of each job card aloud, and ask the other player if they agree.  
  *Parent says:* Turn up a job. Pick the model you think is best. Tell us why. Then we will see if anyone can find a problem with it.  
  *Child mode:* Take turns. Turn up a job. Pick a model. Say why. Then score.
  - Ask: Which job had two models that could both be defended? What would you say for each? (good: The bird-watcher job, because a photograph and a labelled diagram can both be defended.; The bridge job, because a physical model and a computer model both work.; Any job where the reasons for two models were both good.)
  - Note: Let the children argue. The game is meant to show that every model leaves something out and that the best choice depends on the job. Score honestly, but do not let a point matter more than the talk.
- **g1s3 (5 min)**

  *Parent says:* Add up your points. Which model did you use most? Which job was hardest to match, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Add up your points. Say which job was hardest.
  - Ask: Is there a model that is the best for every job? Why or why not? (good: No. Each one leaves something out, and the job decides what can be left out.; A model that kept everything would be as big and as hard to use as the real thing.; Any answer that links the job to the choice.)
  - Note: Put the cards back in the envelope for next time. The game can be extended by writing new job cards.
  - Think: Was there a job for which none of the ten models was good enough? What would you make?
### g2 · The waggle dance treasure hunt: read a dance like a bee (20 min)
- **rules:** One player is the bee. The other is her sister. A window or a lamp stands for the Sun. Put the cards face down in a pile.; The sister waits out of the room, or with her eyes shut. The bee draws a dance card and reads the front without showing it. She hides a small treat on the floor, as the back says.; The sister comes back and watches. The bee stands on a sheet of paper and dances. She walks in a straight line in the direction on the card, waggling her hips for the time on the card. Then she comes back to the paper.; The sister reads the dance. She decides which way to go from the direction and how many steps from the time. One second of waggle is 3 steps. She walks that way from the paper and picks up the treat if she finds it.; The bee shows the back of the card. If the sister found the treat, both score 1 point. If she was off by one step or less, both score 1 point too. Then swap roles.; Play 6 rounds, three each. You are a team and add up your points together. With 5 or more points you win. With fewer, play the six rounds again.; Real bees use the angle to the Sun and about one second for every kilometre, so the game is a scale model of their dance.

(back: Hide the treat 6 steps directly away from the window.); Direction: directly away from the window. Waggle for 3 seconds. (back: Hide the treat 9 steps directly away from the window.); Direction: half of a quarter-turn to the right of the window. Waggle for 3 seconds. (back: Hide the treat 9 steps half of a quarter-turn to the right of the window.); Direction: half of a quarter-turn to the left of the window. Waggle for 3 seconds. (back: Hide the treat 9 steps half of a quarter-turn to the left of the window.); Direction: three-quarters of the way round to the right of the window. Waggle for 2 seconds. - **g2s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Choose a room or terrace with a window or a lamp at one end, and at least 15 steps of clear floor. Cut out the dance cards. Put a sheet of paper on the floor to be the dance floor. Explain that a bee tells others where flowers are with a dance.  
  *Parent says:* A bee who has found flowers comes home and dances. Her dance says which way and how far. Today we play the dance with a window as the Sun.  
  *Child mode:* Pick a window as the Sun. Put a sheet of paper on the floor.
  - Note: A bee dances on the comb in the dark hive and 'straight up' stands for the direction of the Sun. The game puts the window where the Sun would be. Do not play on a slippery floor and clear away anything to trip on.
- **g2s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play 6 rounds, three each. In each round the bee reads a card, hides the treat and dances. The sister finds the treat and the pair check the card.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a card. Hide the treat. Dance it. Now your sister reads the dance. Where is the treat?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a card. Hide the treat. Dance the route. Your sister reads it.
  - Ask: What two things did the dance tell the sister, and how did she use them? (good: Which way to go, and how far, from the time of the waggle.; Direction and distance, which is all that is needed to find a place.; Any answer that names both.)
  - Note: Use a treat such as a raisin or a pebble, and eat or put it away afterwards. A child under 8 can dance and read with a grown-up helping. Make the direction clear with an arm held out. The sister can count the seconds of waggle aloud, one by one, or the bee can wave an arm for each second.
- **g2s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what the dance leaves out and what it shows. Ask what a bee's dance cannot tell the other bees.  
  *Parent says:* The dance does not tell the colour of the flower or what it smells like. What does it show clearly?  
  *Child mode:* Say what the dance shows and what it leaves out.
  - Ask: What does the dance leave out, and who decided that? (good: The colour and smell of the flowers, and the shape of the way. The bee's way of dancing decides what is left out.; Anything but the direction and the distance.; Any answer that names something left out.)
  - Note: Real bees also carry the smell of the flowers on their bodies. They share nectar too, so the dance is only a part of what the others know.
  - Think: A bee's dance is a model made by a bee. Who else makes models?

## Experiments
### e1 · The sealed box test: build a model of what you cannot see (35 min)
- **question:** Can you work out what is inside a sealed box without opening it, by building a model and testing it?
- **predict:** Before you open the box, guess what is inside and how it is arranged. Say why you think so. Later, before each new tilt, guess what the marble will do and say why.
- **fair test:** The same box and the same inside stay unchanged for the whole test. The box is never shaken wildly or opened until the end. Each new test tilts the box one way only, slowly, so you can feel and hear what happens. You write down every test, including the ones that go against your drawing, and you try at least six tilts, because one tilt can fool you.
- **record:** Draw your first model of the inside of the box as seen from above. Fill in the results table for at least six tilts. Draw your changed model after the tests. Then write one sentence each for what you think is inside, what you felt and heard, and why you think so.
- **results table:** Test number | Which way I tilted the box | What I predicted the marble would do | What the marble did | Did my drawing predict it? (yes or no)
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Do this part alone, out of the child's sight. Fold 1 centimetre at the bottom of the card strip to make a foot. Tape the foot to the floor of the box. The strip must stand upright across the width of the box, about halfway along. Leave a gap of about 4 centimetres between one end of the strip and the side of the box. The marble can roll through it. Drop the marble into one half of the box, put on the lid and tape it shut all round.  
  *Parent says:* Now we make a puzzle. Something is inside this box and you may not look. Your job is to find out what it is, as scientists do with things they cannot see.  
  *Child mode:* Wait while a grown-up hides something in the box and seals it. Do not look.
  - Note: Tape the foot of the strip well, because a wall that slides about spoils the test. The wall should be about 3 centimetres high in a box 7 or more centimetres deep. Test it once yourself, tilting the box, to be sure the marble rolls through the gap and also stops at the wall. Keep the shape secret. Pick the other half of the box for the marble each time you make a new puzzle.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Hand over the sealed box. Let your child tilt it slowly to each side, to each end and to each corner, and listen. Ask what the marble does each time. Say that no one may open the box or shake it hard.  
  *Parent says:* Tilt the box slowly. What do you hear? What do you feel? Where does the thing inside seem to go, and where does it stop?  
  *Child mode:* Tilt the box slowly each way. Listen and feel. Say what the thing inside does.
  - Ask: What do you think is inside? How can you tell? (good: Something round that rolls, because I feel and hear it rolling.; Something that stops in the middle, because the rolling stops and clicks.; Any honest guess that is tied to what the child heard or felt.)
  - Note: Do not say what is inside or whether a guess is right. Ask 'What makes you think so?' after each guess. A child of 6 or 7 may only say 'something rolls'. That is a good start. Ask where it stops.
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Give your child paper and a pencil. Ask for a drawing of the inside of the box, seen from above. Ask for the things the child thinks are there and for the parts that were never seen, such as a wall. Ask the child to write one label.  
  *Parent says:* Draw the inside of the box as if the lid were off and you were looking down. Put in everything you think is there. Mark a part you are not sure about with a question mark.  
  *Child mode:* Draw the inside of the box from above. Put in what you think is there.
  - Ask: Which parts of your drawing did you see, and which parts are guesses? (good: I saw nothing. All of it is built from what I felt and heard.; The marble is a good guess, but the wall is a guess from where the rolling stopped.)
  - Note: Whatever the child draws is the first model. Do not correct it. A wall with a gap, or a simple divider, is the likely idea. A drawing with only a marble is fine too, and the next tests will push it forward.
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for one tilt that the child has not yet tried. Ask what the child's drawing says the marble will do, and why. Write both in the table before anyone tilts the box.  
  *Parent says:* Pick a new way to tilt the box. Use your drawing. What will the marble do? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Choose a new tilt. Say what the marble will do and why. Write it down.
  - Ask: Which part of your drawing does this prediction come from? (good: The wall, because I think the marble will stop when it reaches it.; The gap, because I think the marble will get past it at the end.; A tilt to a corner I have not tried yet, using the drawing.)
  - Note: A good test is one where the drawing makes a clear prediction that could turn out wrong. Help the child pick a tilt that the drawing speaks about. For example: tilt towards the far corner from the gap, then slowly towards the other half.
- **s5 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let your child tilt the box in the planned way and watch, listen and feel. Write what the marble did in the table. Then do at least four more tilts, each time predicting first. Mark yes or no in the last column.  
  *Parent says:* Do your test. Then do the same for four more new tilts. Each time, say what your drawing predicts before you tilt.  
  *Child mode:* Tilt the box as you planned. Write what happened. Do four more.
  - Ask: Which tilt gave the biggest surprise, and what did it tell you? (good: The marble did not go where I said, so my drawing was wrong in some way.; The marble stopped in the middle, so there must be something there.; It rolled past where I thought the wall was, so there is a gap.)
  - Note: Expect the marble to roll freely along one side, stop with a click at a wall and pass through a gap at one end only. If the marble ever sticks in a corner, tilt the box the other way. If your child's drawing is already right, ask for a tilt that could show it wrong, for example along the very edge near the gap.
  - Check: A tilt goes against your drawing. What is the best thing to do? → **Change the drawing to fit the test**
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to draw the changed model next to the first one, using a different colour for what changed. Ask for one more test of the new drawing with a new prediction. Write the result.  
  *Parent says:* Draw your model again, now that you have tested it. Show with a new colour what you changed. Then test the new drawing with one more tilt.  
  *Child mode:* Draw your model again. Use a new colour for what you changed. Test it once more.
  - Ask: What did you change in your model, and what test made you change it? (good: I added a gap, because the marble got through at one end.; I moved the wall, because the marble stopped sooner than I thought.; Any change tied to a particular test.)
  - Note: Praise the change, never the first drawing. Scientists change their models many times. A child who changes nothing can be asked to find a tilt that would test the weakest part of the drawing.
- **s7 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Open the box and let your child compare the inside with both drawings. Ask what each drawing got right, what it left out, and what no tilt could have told you.  
  *Parent says:* Now open the lid. Look at your first drawing, your second drawing and the real inside. What did each one get right? What did every drawing leave out?  
  *Child mode:* Open the box. Compare it with both drawings. What did you get right? What did you leave out?
  - Ask: What could no tilt have told you about the inside of the box? (good: How high the wall is, because the marble stops the same for a low wall and a high wall.; What colour or what thing the wall is made of.; Anything the marble never touched, such as a corner.)
  - Note: Be honest if the child's model was different from the real box. A model that predicted every tilt correctly can still look different inside. The tilts could not tell the wall's height, colour or material. This is how it is for an atom, which nobody can open.
- **s8 (1 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write the three parts: what I think, what I saw, why I think so.  
  *Parent says:* Finish with three short sentences. 'I think there is...' 'I felt and heard...' 'I think so because...'  
  *Child mode:* Say or write what you think, what you felt and heard, and why.
  - Note: Answers will vary. A strong answer ties the model to particular tilts, not just a feeling.
  - Think: Scientists cannot open an atom the way you opened the box. How do they decide that their model of an atom is good?
### e2 · The blind-spot test: find the place in your eye that sees nothing (20 min)
- **question:** Can a dot in front of you vanish while your eye is open and the dot is still there?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. You will stare only at the cross while the paper moves closer. Will a dot 10 centimetres to the side stay in view? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The same eye looks at the cross every time, and the other eye stays covered. The paper moves slowly towards the face and then away, always at the same pace. The test is done three times with each of two sheets, one blank and one ruled, because one try can be spoiled by a wandering eye. The head stays still and the eye stays on the cross.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw an eye from above with the nerve at the back and mark the blind spot. Write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Sheet (blank or ruled) | Try number | Distance from my eye when the dot vanished (centimetres) | What I saw where the dot had been
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a cross on the left side of the blank sheet. Draw a black dot about 1 centimetre across, 10 centimetres to the right of the cross. Draw the same cross and dot, at the same distance apart, on the ruled sheet. Put the dot on a ruled line, so that a line runs through it.  
  *Parent says:* We will look for a place in your eye that cannot see. Do you think there is one? What would it feel like?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a cross and a dot, 10 centimetres apart, on each sheet.
  - Note: Make the dot a solid black circle, not a ring. Make the cross clear. The two marks must be 10 centimetres apart, measured from the middle of each.
- **s2 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the prediction and the reason. Write it at the top of the page. Do not explain anything yet.  
  *Parent says:* Will the dot stay in view as I move the paper closer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will the dot stay in view? Why do you think so?
  - Note: Most children say the dot will stay. That is the useful guess.
- **s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child how to do it. Cover the left eye with the left hand. Hold the blank sheet at arm's length with the cross straight in front of the right eye. Look only at the cross. Move the paper slowly towards the face, and then slowly away. Do this three times. When the dot vanishes, stop and measure the distance from the paper to the eye with the ruler. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the cross only. Keep looking at it, even though you want to look at the dot. Move the paper slowly closer. Tell me when the dot goes.  
  *Child mode:* Cover your left eye. Look at the cross. Move the paper slowly closer. Say stop when the dot goes.
  - Ask: Where was the paper when the dot vanished? What did the place look like? (good: About arm's length less a hand, around 30 to 40 centimetres from my eye, and it looked like white paper.; The dot just went and the paper looked whole.; Any honest distance, and that the paper looked normal where the dot had been.)
  - Note: The dot usually vanishes at about 30 to 40 centimetres from the eye. If it does not vanish, keep the eye on the cross and move the paper more slowly. Check that the dot is on the same side as the open eye. If the left eye is covered, the dot is to the right of the cross. A very small child may need help to keep still. Expect most children to succeed in three or four tries. The distance is about 3.5 times the gap between the cross and the dot, so a longer gap makes the dot vanish farther away. Move the paper slowly and keep it from touching the face.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same with the ruled sheet, three times. Ask your child what is seen where the dot was.  
  *Parent says:* Now do the same with the ruled page. When the dot goes, look at where it was. What do you see there?  
  *Child mode:* Try the ruled sheet three times. Look at where the dot was. What do you see?
  - Ask: What did you see in the place where the dot had been on the ruled sheet? (good: The ruled line, running straight on, with no dot and no hole.; I saw the lines continue, as if nothing was missing.; Any honest answer that notes that no hole or black patch was seen.)
  - Note: The usual result is that the ruled line seems to run straight through the place where the dot was. The brain fills the gap with a guess made from the lines around it. Some children see a faint grey for a moment. That is fine.
  - Check: On the ruled sheet, what did the dot's place look like? → **The ruled lines running straight on**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Switch to the other eye. Cover the right eye, look at the cross with the left eye and move the paper again. Ask your child where the dot has to be for this eye.  
  *Parent says:* Now try the other eye. Where do you think the dot should be this time, to the left or to the right of the cross?  
  *Child mode:* Try the other eye. Put the dot on the other side of the cross.
  - Note: For the left eye, the dot must be to the LEFT of the cross. Draw a new dot and cross for this, or turn the sheet over and draw them there. If your child enjoys this, let the child try it as an extra.
### e3 · Torch and orange: use a model to explain day and night, then to predict the midnight Sun (25 min)
- **question:** Can a torch and an orange explain why day and night happen? Can they also tell us what happens in the far north, a place we have never seen?
- **predict:** Before you tilt the orange, guess. If the orange leans towards the torch, will a dot right at the top ever go dark as the orange turns? Say why you think so.
- **fair test:** The torch stays in one place, at the same height as the middle of the orange, about 50 centimetres away. The room is as dark as you can make it. The orange turns at the same slow pace each time, three full turns for each lean. Only one thing changes between tests: the lean of the pencil, which is straight up, leaning towards the torch, or leaning away from it.
- **record:** Fill in the table for the three leans. Draw the orange, the torch and the pencil for each lean, and shade the lit half. Write one sentence each for what you think, what you saw and why.
- **results table:** Lean of the pencil | What I predicted for the dot at the top | What happened to the dot at the top in three turns | What happened to the dot near the middle
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Push the pencil through the middle of the orange from top to bottom. Draw a small ring about 1 centimetre across round the point where the pencil comes out at the top. Find the halfway point between the two ends of the pencil. Draw a dot on the side of the orange, about one finger-width above it. Put the torch on a pile of books. It must shine at the middle of the orange when the orange is held 50 centimetres away.  
  *Parent says:* This orange will stand for the Earth. The pencil is the line it spins on. The ring at the top is the far north. The dot is where we live. What will the torch stand for?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pushes the pencil through the orange. Draw a ring at the top and a dot near the middle.
  - Note: Use a sharp pencil and push gently, because a slippery orange can slide. The ring stands for the far north, and the dot for a place such as Kochi or Chennai, which are not far above the middle. Kochi and Chennai are only a little above the equator, so the dot should be near the middle.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Turn off the room lights and close the curtains. Hold the orange by the pencil with the pencil straight up, 50 centimetres from the torch, and turn it slowly. Count three turns, and ask what happens to the dot near the middle. Write it in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Watch the dot as I turn the orange. When is it in the light? When is it in the dark? What does that make me think of?  
  *Child mode:* Turn the orange slowly. Watch the dot. Say when it is in the light.
  - Ask: Why does the dot go from light to dark and back again? (good: The orange turns, so the dot is carried into the light and then out of it.; The torch lights only one half, and the dot turns from one half to the other.; It is the same as day and night on the real Earth.)
  - Note: Half of the orange is lit and half is dark, with a soft edge between them. This edge is the dawn or the dusk. The dot goes from lit to dark once in each turn. Make sure the torch is at the same height as the middle of the orange, and keep the room dark. If your room cannot get dark, wait until evening and close the curtains.
  - Check: Which turning of the orange makes your dot pass from day into night? → **The orange turns while the torch stays still**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask for the prediction and the reason. Ask: if the pencil leans towards the torch, will the ring at the top ever go dark? Write what your child says before you try it.  
  *Parent says:* I will lean the pencil towards the torch. The ring at the top is the far north. As I turn, will it ever go dark? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Guess. Will the ring at the top go dark when the pencil leans? Say why.
  - Note: Many children say yes, since everything went into the dark before. Do not say what will happen. The reason matters more than the guess.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lean the pencil towards the torch, about 30 degrees from straight up. That is about a third of the way towards the torch. Turn the orange slowly, three turns. Watch the ring at the top and the dot near the middle. Write what you see.  
  *Parent says:* Now the pencil leans towards the torch. Turn the orange. Watch the ring at the top. Does it go dark?  
  *Child mode:* Lean the pencil towards the torch. Turn the orange. Watch the ring.
  - Ask: Did the ring at the top ever go dark? What does that mean for a place in the far north? (good: No. It stayed in the light for all three turns, so a place in the far north would have daylight all day and all night.; The ring stayed lit while the dot near the middle still went into the dark.; Any honest answer tied to what was seen.)
  - Note: The ring stays in the light for every turn, while the dot near the middle still goes dark. This is the model's prediction: in the far north, in summer, the Sun does not set. This happens in northern Norway in June. The real Earth leans about 23.5 degrees. The orange leans more, about 30 degrees, to make the effect easy to see, and the ring is small to keep within the lit part. If the ring goes dark for part of a turn, lean the pencil a little more towards the torch or move the torch a little closer.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lean the pencil away from the torch by the same amount. Turn the orange three times. Watch the ring and the dot. Write what happens. Then set the pencil straight and look at the ring.  
  *Parent says:* Now lean the pencil away from the torch. What will the ring do? Try it and see.  
  *Child mode:* Lean the pencil away from the torch. Turn the orange. Watch the ring.
  - Ask: What happens to the ring when the pencil leans away from the torch? (good: It stays in the dark for all three turns.; The far north has night all day, which is a season of no Sun.; Any answer tied to what was seen.)
  - Note: With the lean away, the ring stays dark in every turn. In the real far north, this is the winter, when the Sun does not rise for weeks. With the pencil straight up, the ring sits on the line between the lit and the dark side, in a kind of twilight.
- **s6 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child what the model got right, and what it left out or did wrong. Ask for one thing it cannot tell us.  
  *Parent says:* A model is never the whole truth. What did the orange and torch get right? What did they leave out?  
  *Child mode:* Say what the model got right and what it left out.
  - Ask: Name one thing the orange and torch leave out or get wrong. (good: The torch is much closer than the Sun, so its light spreads out, while the Sun's light is nearly parallel.; It has no seasons from going round the Sun yet, and no air or weather.; The orange leans more than the real Earth, which leans about 23.5 degrees.)
  - Note: It predicted something true, but a model that predicts well is not proved to be right in every way. The real Sun is far away and its rays are nearly parallel. The real Earth also travels round the Sun. As an extra, walk the orange in a circle round the torch with the pencil always pointing at the same corner of the room. Watch the ring light up and go dark as you go round.
  - Think: The model predicted the midnight Sun before anyone told you. Does that prove the model is how the Earth really works, or only that it is useful?

### a2 · A photo and a diagram of a cut tomato (20 min)

  *Child mode:* Compare the photo, the diagram and the tomato. Which is good for what?
  - Ask: Which is better for knowing this tomato, and which for understanding all tomatoes? (good: The photo shows this one tomato as it looked today.; The diagram shows what every tomato of this kind has, with names for the parts.; Each is better for a different job.)
  - Note: Make sure to eat the tomato afterwards, or put it in the compost.
  - Think: A diagram has labels and arrows that are not in the tomato. Are those parts of the model or parts of the tomato?
### a3 · Walk the solar system: a model that is true to scale (30 min)
- **record:** Write down the number of big steps for each planet. Beside it, write one thing that your usual diagram of the solar system left out.
- **a3s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Take your things to a safe open path. Put the football on the ground as the Sun. Say that 1 step in the model stands for a big adult step of about 75 centimetres. Check the steps work by pacing out 10 steps and measuring them against 7 or 8 metres if you have a tape.  
  *Parent says:* Here is the Sun, the size of a football. We shall put the planets at their true distances. What size do you think the Earth should be? How far away?  
  *Child mode:* Put the football down as the Sun. Guess how far away the Earth should be.
  - Ask: Guess first. How many big steps from the football should the Earth be? (good: Any guess, then compare with 32 steps.; A guess that gives a reason, such as 'far, because the Sun is hot'.)
  - Note: The scale is simple. If the Sun is 22 centimetres across, the Earth is 2 millimetres across and 24 metres away. One big adult step is about 75 centimetres, so 24 metres is about 32 steps. The distances are these. Mercury is about 9 metres (12 steps), Venus 17 metres (23 steps), Earth 24 metres (32 steps) and Mars 36 metres (48 steps). Jupiter is 123 metres (164 steps). Saturn is 226 metres, Uranus 454 metres and Neptune 711 metres.
- **a3s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pace out the distances for Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars and Jupiter. Mark each place with chalk or a piece of paper. Put a poppy seed at Mercury and at Mars. Put a mustard seed at Venus and at the Earth. Put a marble at Jupiter.  
  *Parent says:* Walk 12 steps. Put down the smallest seed. That is Mercury. Now 23 steps from the Sun for Venus. Keep going.  
  *Child mode:* Pace out the planets. Put a small thing at each place.
  - Ask: What do you notice about how far apart the planets are, compared with how big they are? (good: They are far apart, and almost all of the space is empty.; The planets are tiny beside the Sun and the gaps are huge.; Any answer tied to the pacing.)
  - Note: The Earth is a seed 32 steps from a football. Jupiter, a marble that is a little too small for its true size of about 2 centimetres, is 164 steps away. If your path is too short for Jupiter, stop at Mars and say how much farther Jupiter would be. Do not run, and keep to the safe path.
- **a3s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand at the Sun and look at the Earth's seed. Then ask your child to look at a diagram of the solar system in a book or on the phone. Ask what the diagram does to the distances and sizes, and how it could say so honestly.  
  *Parent says:* Look back at the Sun from the Earth. Then look at a picture of the solar system. What does the picture change so that it fits on a page?  
  *Child mode:* Look back at the Sun from the Earth. Compare it with a diagram. What did the diagram change?
  - Ask: What does a diagram of the solar system have to change to fit on a page, and what should it say? (good: It squashes the distances and makes the planets bigger, and it should say 'not to scale'.; It makes the planets much closer and bigger than they are.; Any answer that names the change and the words 'not to scale'.)
  - Note: Pick the seeds up afterwards, so that no small objects are left behind. If the Sun is a steel plate, wash it after use.
  - Think: No page can show the solar system true to scale. Is a diagram that cheats still a good model?
### a4 · The heart is like a pump: where does the likeness stop? (20 min)
- **record:** Draw two columns headed 'Like a pump' and 'Not like a pump'. Write at least two things in each column.
- **a4s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the dispenser with plain water. Pump it 10 times into the steel bowl. Ask your child to watch the water and to say what a pump does. Ask where the water comes from, what makes it move and what makes it stop.  
  *Parent says:* Pump the bottle ten times. What does the pump do to the water? What do you have to do to make it work?  
  *Child mode:* Pump the bottle ten times. Say what the pump does to the water.
  - Ask: What does a pump do, and what does it need to keep working? (good: It pushes liquid along, and it needs a push each time, from a hand or from a motor.; It has a part that squeezes and lets go, again and again.; Any answer that names pushing liquid and needing something to push it.)
  - Note: Wash the bottle afterwards and do not drink the water. A tap with a soap pump gives the same picture.
- **a4s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to find the pulse on the inside of the wrist, below the thumb. Count the beats for 15 seconds, sitting still. Multiply by 4. Then do 20 star jumps, count again, and multiply by 4. Write both numbers.  
  *Parent says:* Find your pulse. Count the beats for 15 seconds. Then do 20 star jumps and count again. What changed?  
  *Child mode:* Find your pulse. Count for 15 seconds. Do 20 star jumps. Count again.
  - Ask: What happened to your pulse after the jumps, and what does the heart do about it? (good: It went faster, because the heart beats faster when the body needs more.; The heart pumps harder or faster when the muscles need more blood.; Any honest answer tied to the numbers.)
  - Note: A child's resting pulse is usually 70 to 100 beats a minute and goes up to 120 to 160 after exercise. A pump that speeds itself up and slows itself down when the body needs it is not like a hand pump. Press with two fingers and not the thumb, because the thumb has its own pulse. Stop the star jumps if your child feels dizzy or short of breath. A child with a heart or breathing problem should skip them and only count the resting pulse.
- **a4s3 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw two columns. In the first, ask your child to list what is like a pump about the heart. In the second, what is not. Use the pump and the pulse.  
  *Parent says:* Where is the heart like a pump? Where is it not? Look at what you found.  
  *Child mode:* Make two lists. Where is the heart like a pump? Where is it not?
  - Ask: Where does the likeness stop? (good: A pump needs a hand or a motor, but the heart beats by itself.; A soap pump never speeds up by itself. The heart does.; The heart is a living part of a body, and grows and mends itself. A pump is made and does not.)
  - Note: Real likeness: the heart has valves that let blood flow one way, as a pump does. It squeezes and lets go. It pushes liquid through tubes. Real differences: it is alive, it beats without a hand, and it speeds up and slows down on its own.
  - Think: The pump helped us understand the heart, but the heart was there long before pumps. What does that tell us about the pump as a model?
### a5 · Peel an orange: why a flat map must stretch (15 min)
- **record:** Draw what the shape looks like on the flat peel. Write one sentence about what happened to it.
- **a5s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Use a marker to draw a circle and a square, each about 3 centimetres across. Draw a long line from the top to the bottom of the orange. Ask your child what will happen to them when the skin is flattened.  
  *Parent says:* Draw a circle, a square and a line down the orange. What will happen to them if we press the peel flat?  
  *Child mode:* Draw a circle, a square and a long line on the orange. Guess what will happen.
  - Note: Draw with a light hand, because the skin can be oily. Use a dark marker. Ask for the guess and the reason before the peeling.
- **a5s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Peel the orange by hand, using your thumb. Try to keep the skin in as large a piece as you can. Lay the pieces on the plate. Press them flat with the palm and look at the drawings.  
  *Parent says:* Peel the orange. Press the peel flat. What happens to the circle, the square and the line?  
  *Child mode:* Peel the orange. Press the skin flat. Look at your drawings.
  - Ask: What happened to your shapes when you flattened the skin? (good: The skin split or stretched, and the shapes were pulled out of shape.; The line broke into pieces, and the circle stretched at the edges.; Any answer that says what happened to the shapes.)
  - Note: Citrus peel always tears when pressed flat because it is a curved surface. A round ball cannot be flattened without stretching or tearing. This is true of the Earth. If your skin tears at once, that is a good result: you can say that is where a map must cut or stretch.
- **a5s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to look at a flat map of the world or a page in a school atlas. Ask where it stretches, and how the map-maker had to choose.  
  *Parent says:* A flat map of the world has the same problem as your peel. Where do you think it stretches? Look at Greenland and Africa.  
  *Child mode:* Look at a flat world map. Where do you think it stretches?
  - Ask: Why can no flat map of the Earth be right in every way? (good: A round surface cannot be flattened without stretching or tearing something.; Every map-maker has to choose which thing to keep true, such as shapes or areas.; Any answer tied to the orange peel.)
  - Note: Greenland looks about as large as Africa on the map of most classrooms, but Africa is about 14 times larger. A globe has no such problem, but you cannot fold it into your pocket. Eat the orange afterwards.
  - Think: Every flat map must be wrong in some way. How would you choose which way it is wrong?

### p1 · The map that a stranger can follow: draw, test, fix (70 min, 4 days)
- **record:** Keep every version of the map. Write down every place where the helper hesitated or went wrong. Write one sentence about what you changed and why.
- **schedule:** day 1: Walk the route with a grown-up, noticing what you pass. Then draw your first map from memory. (20 min); day 2: Give the map to the first helper, a person who does not know the way, and watch where they go wrong or ask questions. Do not help. (20 min); day 3: Fix the map using what you saw. Give the new map to a second helper. (15 min); day 4: Compare your final map with a phone map of the same route. List what each shows and leaves out. (15 min)
- **p1s1 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Walk a short, safe route with your child to a place within sight, such as the building gate. Ask your child to notice what could help a stranger find the way. Back home, ask for a map of the route drawn from memory. It needs a start, an end and any signs along the way.  
  *Parent says:* Walk to the gate and notice what helps you know where you are. Then draw a map of the way, so that someone who has never been here could follow it.  
  *Child mode:* Walk the route with a grown-up. Then draw a map of it from memory.
  - Ask: What did you decide to put in your map, and what did you leave out? (good: The turns, a tree, a tap and a staircase, because a stranger could use these.; Not the colour of the walls or the people, because they change.; Any answer that gives a reason for each thing.)
  - Note: Choose a route that is safe and short, and stay beside your child. Do not tell your child what to put on the map, even if it is missing things. Day 2's test will show what is missing.
  - Wait: Leave it until the next day
- **p1s2 (20 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Give the map to a helper who does not know the route. Ask them to follow it, saying aloud what they see and where they are not sure. Walk behind them. Do not help or say anything. Write down every place they stop, go wrong or ask a question.  
  *Parent says:* Give this map to a friend who does not know the way. Watch. Do not help. Write down every place where they get stuck.  
  *Child mode:* Give your map to a helper. Watch them follow it. Do not help.
  - Ask: Where did the helper go wrong, and what in the map made it happen? (good: At a turn, because the map did not show which way to turn.; At a place where the map had no sign, so there was nothing to check against.; Any answer that links a mistake to something in the map.)
  - Note: Stay quiet, even if the helper is clearly lost. Their mistakes are the useful part. A route that is too long makes this hard, so end the test after five minutes. Stay with them the whole time.
  - Wait: Leave the fixing until the next day
- **p1s3 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to fix the map, using every note from the helper's test. Ask for a new copy with the changes in a different colour. Give the new map to a second helper and note again where they stop.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your notes. What will you add? What will you change? Draw the new map. Then try it on someone else.  
  *Child mode:* Fix your map using your notes. Try it on a second helper.
  - Ask: Did your fixed map work better? How can you tell? (good: The helper stopped fewer times or went straight to the place.; The helper used the new sign I added.; Any answer that points to something counted or seen.)
  - Note: Praise the fixing more than the first map. If the second helper still goes wrong, count that as useful data and ask what the map would need.
- **p1s4 (15 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Open a phone map of the same route. Lay your final map beside it. Ask your child to list what each one shows well and what each leaves out. Then ask which is better for a stranger on foot, for a driver and for someone in a wheelchair.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your map and the phone's map of the same route. What does each show? What does each leave out? Which is better for which person?  
  *Child mode:* Compare your map with the phone's map. Say what each leaves out.
  - Ask: Is your map or the phone map the better one? (good: Neither is better for everything. Mine has the tap and the tree. The phone's has the exact distances.; It depends on who is using it and for what job.; Any answer that gives a job and a reason.)
  - Note: The aim is that the child sees what a model for a particular person needs. The phone map may leave out steps, tap and trees, and show roads. Yours may leave out distances.
  - Think: You made a model and then tested it on other people. Why do scientists also want other people to test their models?

### ch1 · A touch map for someone who cannot see it (35 min)
- **problem:** Make a map of your home, from the front door to the kitchen, that a person who cannot see can read with their fingers. It must show the walls, the doors and the way. There is more than one good answer.
- **your turn:** Make your own touch map in a way that nobody here has thought of. What did you choose to leave out, and who decided it should be left out?

- **general:** Glue string for the walls. Use a rough patch of sandpaper for the floor of the corridor, so that the walls and the route feel different.; Use a raised line for the way and a different patch for each room, with a key made of the same patches in a corner.; Mark the doors by leaving a gap in the string wall and putting a bead in the gap.
- **creative:** Use a different smell for each room. Put a dab of cardamom on cotton for the kitchen and a dab of coconut oil on cloth for the bedroom. Then the reader's nose helps their fingers. Check first that nobody at home is allergic to the smells.; Make the map out of a cloth that folds, so that it can be carried. A corridor can then be pulled out and felt in its full length.; Add small bells or a rattle of dal in a sealed packet at the door, so that the reader can feel and hear where they are.

  *Parent does:* Say that some people read with their fingers. Ask your child what a map for them would need that a drawn map does not. Ask the child to sketch a plan of the route from the front door to the kitchen. Then ask which material will stand for a wall, a door, the route and the kitchen.  
  *Parent says:* Imagine a person who cannot see this map. What would they use instead of their eyes? What things must feel different from each other?  
  *Child mode:* Plan the map. Pick a feel for walls, doors, the route and the kitchen.
  - Ask: Which things on a map must feel different from each other, and why? (good: Walls, doors and the route, because a person has to tell them apart without seeing.; Rough for a rug or a step, smooth for a clear floor.; Anything that has a reason.)
  - Note: A touch map is a real tool. Many schools and railway stations in India have raised maps. The point is that a different reader needs a different model of the same place.
- **ch1s2 (14 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Child mode:* Build the touch map. Make a key from the same materials.
  - Ask: What did you use for the key, and how can a person know it is the key? (good: A small patch in one corner for each material, in the same order each time.; Each material is shown next to a bit of string.; Any key that can be found by touch alone.)
  - Note: Glue and scissors are for the grown-up. Let the glue dry for 10 minutes before the test. Rice is the cheapest way to make a patch. If you have no rice, use dal or sand.
- **ch1s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Cover a friend's or family member's eyes with a scarf. Ask them to follow the map with two fingers from the front door to the kitchen. Watch where they pause. Write down each pause.  
  *Parent says:* Put on the scarf and follow the map with your fingers. Tell us what you feel. Where does it get hard?  
  *Child mode:* Cover your eyes. Follow the map by touch. Say where it gets hard.
  - Ask: Where did your tester get stuck, and what would make the map better? (good: At a place where two materials felt too alike, so I would make them more different.; At a doorway that felt like a wall, so I would raise the walls.; Any answer that links a stuck place to a change.)
  - Note: Make sure that the tester sits safely and cannot fall. The test is meant to find mistakes. Ask the tester to say aloud what they feel.
- **ch1s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to improve the map once, using what the tester found. Then ask what the map leaves out, and whether that matters for a person who cannot see.  
  *Parent says:* Fix one thing that did not work. What does your map still leave out? Does that matter for the person using it?  
  *Child mode:* Fix one thing. Say what the map still leaves out.
  - Ask: What does your map leave out that a blind person might need? (good: Furniture, steps or things in the way, which could be added as small bumps.; Sounds and smells, which many blind people use. A map could use a different smell for the kitchen.; Any answer that thinks about the reader.)
  - Note: Blind people read a place by touch, sound, smell, memory and a stick or a dog. A sighted person's map shows only one of the ways.
  - Think: A map for fingers has a different job from a map for eyes. What else could a map use besides sight and touch?
### ch2 · Test a model bridge before you build: the strongest paper bridge (30 min)
- **problem:** Build the strongest bridge from one sheet of ordinary paper across a gap of 15 centimetres. The strongest holds the most coins in a small cup. There is more than one good answer.
- **your turn:** Design a bridge of your own from one sheet of paper that nobody here has tried. Predict how many coins it will hold, and test it.
- **constraints:** Use one sheet of A4 paper, or a page of the same size from a notebook, for each bridge. Tape may join the paper to itself, but must not stick the bridge to the books.; The bridge must rest on two piles of books with a gap of 15 centimetres between them, with 2 centimetres of paper on each side.; Load the bridge by adding coins one at a time to a light cup in the middle, and count how many it holds. Use one kind of coin, such as one-rupee coins, so that the counts can be compared.; Test each shape at least twice and write down both numbers.; Make at least three different shapes and predict how many coins each will hold before you test it.
- **general:** Fold the paper into an accordion of 8 to 10 pleats, so that each fold is a small beam that resists bending.; Roll the paper into a tube and tape its edge, which is stiff because a curved shape is hard to bend.; Fold two edges up to make a channel, like a gutter, so that the paper has a deep edge on each side.
- **creative:** Fold a zigzag and glue a flat strip, cut from the same sheet, over the top. This makes a hollow box of folds, like the hollow beams of real bridges.; Glue several thin paper tubes side by side, as bamboo tubes are tied together in a real footbridge.; Curve the paper into an arch with its ends against the books, so that the load pushes the ends outward. Put a heavy book against each end so that the ends cannot slide.
- **ch2s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Build the test stand. Put two piles of books, the same height, 15 centimetres apart. Lay one flat sheet across the gap with about 2 centimetres on each side. Put the cup in the middle and add coins one at a time. Count how many it holds before the paper sags or falls.  
  *Parent says:* First try a flat sheet. How many coins do you think it will hold? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Lay a flat sheet across the gap. Guess how many coins it will hold.
  - Ask: Guess first. How many coins will the flat sheet hold? (good: Any guess with a reason, for example 'just one or two, because it is thin'.; A guess that compares with a folded one.)
  - Note: A flat A4 sheet will usually sag with the empty cup, or hold only a coin or two. This is the first model and the one to beat. Measure the books to make sure the gap is the same each time.
- **ch2s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to make two more shapes from a sheet each. One may be an accordion fold, like a paper fan, with 8 to 10 pleats each about 1 centimetre deep. Another may be a roll into a tube. For each, ask for a prediction, then test it with coins and write the result.  
  *Parent says:* Make two new shapes. Before you test each one, say how many coins it will hold. Then test it.  
  *Child mode:* Make two new bridges. Predict each one. Test with coins.
  - Ask: Which shape was strongest, and was it the one you predicted? (good: The folded one held the most, because folds stiffen paper.; The tube held a lot, because a curved shape is hard to bend.; Any honest answer, whether or not it matched the guess.)
  - Note: A pleated bridge usually holds many times what a flat sheet does. How many coins depends on the paper and the coin, so compare your own shapes with each other and do not expect our numbers. A tube may be stronger or weaker, depending on how it is held in place, so ask your child to hold or tape the ends. Stop adding coins when the bridge sags a lot or falls. Do each test twice and use both numbers.
  - Check: A bridge held 20 coins in one test and 12 in a second. What is the best thing to do? → **Do it a third time and use the middle value**
- **ch2s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to improve the best bridge once. Ideas are a deeper fold, a tube inside a fold, or two layers of paper. Test it twice. Write what changed.  
  *Parent says:* Take your strongest bridge and make it stronger. What will you change? Predict, then test.  
  *Child mode:* Improve your best bridge. Predict. Test it twice.
  - Ask: What change made it stronger, and why do you think so? (good: Deeper folds, because a deep fold is harder to bend.; Two layers, because there is more paper to carry the load.; Any change with a reason.)
  - Note: A fold makes the paper deeper, and a deeper beam is much harder to bend, even when it is made of the same paper.
- **ch2s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what the paper model cannot tell us about a real bridge made of steel. Ask what an engineer would still need to test.  
  *Parent says:* Your paper bridge held coins. Will a steel bridge behave the same way? What can your test not tell us?  
  *Child mode:* Say what a paper test cannot tell you about a real bridge.
  - Ask: What can a small paper model not tell us about a real bridge? (good: How steel bends, because paper and steel are different.; How wind and a moving crowd would shake it.; How big things change, because a bigger bridge carries more weight in itself.)
  - Note: Engineers use small models and computers before building. The model leaves out the material, the size, the wind and the crowd. That is why they use several tests.
  - Think: The real bridge costs a very great deal. What other things could we test in a model before building the real one?

### What a model is and what it leaves out (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A plastic model of a heart is in a school lab. What is it for?  
   Options: To work as a real heart / To show the parts of a heart clearly / To be a smaller copy with nothing left out  
   **Answer:** To show the parts of a heart clearly. *Why:* A model is made to show something clearly, not to do the real thing's job. Choosing the last option treats a model as a perfect copy, but every model leaves something out.
2. (truefalse) A good model keeps every detail of the real thing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A model that kept every detail would be as big and as hard to use as the real thing. Leaving things out is what makes a model clear.
3. (sort) Sort each one into physical model or diagram.  
   **Answer:** a plastic skeleton in a school lab → Physical model; a drawing of the digestive system with arrows → Diagram; a globe → Physical model; a labelled drawing of a leaf → Diagram; a doll's house → Physical model. *Why:* A physical model is an object you can hold and turn round. A diagram is a simple drawing with labels, and often arrows, made to explain.
4. (match) Match each one to what it does.  
   **Answer:** A photograph of one hibiscus → shows that one flower on one day, with its shadow; A diagram of a hibiscus → shows what every flower of that kind has, with names; An arrow in a diagram → shows which way something moves; A label → names a part. *Why:* A photograph keeps whatever the camera caught. A diagram keeps only what its maker chose, and adds labels and arrows that are not in the real flower.
5. (fill) A globe shows the shapes of the lands but leaves out ____.  
   **Answer:** ['rain', 'weather', 'traffic', 'smells', 'people', 'height']. *Why:* A globe is made to show the shapes and places of the lands. Rain, traffic and smells are left out on purpose, so that the shapes can be seen clearly.
6. (order) Put these steps in order for finding out what is inside a sealed box.  
   **Answer:** ['Tilt the box and listen', 'Draw your first model of the inside', 'Predict what a new tilt will do', 'Test the prediction', 'Change the drawing if the test disagrees']. *Why:* You need clues before you can draw. A prediction comes from the drawing, a test comes from the prediction, and the test decides if the drawing must change.
7. (write) Think of your classroom. Name two things you would leave out of a map of it, and say why.  
   **Answer:** Any two things, such as the colour of the walls or what is on the notice board, with a reason about what the reader needs. A strong answer says the map is for finding things, so only the things that help are kept.. *Why:* A good answer names two things and gives a reason tied to the job of the map.
8. (mcq) Which of these can you pick up and turn round to look at from behind?  
   Options: A diagram of a flower / A map of India on paper / A model of the Sun, Earth and Moon on wire arms  
   **Answer:** A model of the Sun, Earth and Moon on wire arms. *Why:* A physical model is a solid object. A diagram and a map are drawings on paper, so you can only look at them from the front.
9. (truefalse) A diagram of a flower stands for every flower of that kind. It leaves out one flower's shadow.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A diagram keeps what all flowers of that kind share. A photograph keeps the shadow, the ant and everything else that was there at that moment.
10. (perspective) A new child needs a school map. One shows every tree and bench. Another shows only the gate, classrooms and library.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer says the second map is quicker to read for finding the library. The first could be useful for a different job, such as finding a tree to sit under. Neither is wrong, and each has a job.. *Why:* The first map keeps more but is harder to read. The second keeps less but is clearer for one job. This is the trade-off in every model.
11. (mcq) An orange turns in torchlight and a dot on it goes from light to dark. What does the orange stand for?  
   Options: The Sun, which moves / The Moon, which glows / The Earth, which turns  
   **Answer:** The Earth, which turns. *Why:* In this model the orange is the Earth and the torch is the Sun. Day and night come from the Earth turning, which carries each place into the light and out of it.
12. (mcq) A map has a box that says a blue line means a river. What is that box for?  
   Options: To explain what the signs and colours mean / To make the map prettier / To show how big the real place is  
   **Answer:** To explain what the signs and colours mean. *Why:* The box is called a key. It tells the reader what each sign and colour stands for. The size of the real place is told by the scale, which is a different part of a map.
13. (count) A globe has a scale of 1 centimetre for every 425 kilometres. How many kilometres are 4 centimetres on the globe?  
   **Answer:** 1700. *Why:* Each centimetre stands for 425 kilometres, so 4 centimetres stand for 4 × 425 = 1,700 kilometres.
14. (draw) A marble stops with a click in the middle of a box. It still rolls along the far edge. Draw the inside.  
   **Answer:** A rectangle with a wall drawn across the middle, and a gap between the end of the wall and the far side. The marble is shown on one side. A question mark or note says what is not certain, such as the wall's height.. *Why:* The marble stopping in the middle says there is a wall. Rolling on only along the far edge says there is a gap there. A good model uses each clue, and a good drawing shows what is still a guess.
15. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? A globe, a plastic skeleton, a sparrow, a diagram of a leaf.  
   **Answer:** The sparrow, because a sparrow is a real bird and not a model of one. The other three are models made to stand for something real.. *Why:* A model stands for a real thing. A living bird is a real being, and not a model of anything.
16. (write) Why can one thing, such as a town, have more than one good model?  
   **Answer:** Because different jobs need different things kept. A map for drivers keeps roads, and a map for walkers keeps paths and steps. Each leaves out what the other needs.. *Why:* A strong answer names a job for each model and what each leaves out.
### Scale, maps and changing a model (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A diagram of the solar system has the words 'not to scale'. What do they say?  
   Options: The whole diagram is wrong / The sizes or distances are not true to the real thing / The planets are drawn at their true sizes  
   **Answer:** The sizes or distances are not true to the real thing. *Why:* The words warn the reader that something was changed to fit the page. The planets and distances are not in true proportion, but the order of the planets can still be right.
2. (fill) A 30 centimetre globe stands for the Earth, which is 12,742 kilometres across. So 1 centimetre stands for about ____ kilometres.  
   **Answer:** ['425', '420', '430']. *Why:* 12,742 ÷ 30 is close to 425. Each centimetre on the globe stands for 425 kilometres of the real Earth, roughly.
3. (truefalse) On a flat world map Greenland looks about as big as Africa, so the two lands are about the same size.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Africa is about 14 times larger than Greenland. A flat map must stretch some part of the round Earth, and this map stretches the lands near the top.
4. (mcq) A model predicts that a spot stays lit. A test shows it going dark. What is best?  
   Options: Say the torch must be broken / Check the test, then change the model if it still disagrees / Ignore the test and keep the model  
   **Answer:** Check the test, then change the model if it still disagrees. *Why:* A test that disagrees is information. First check that the test was fair, then change the model if it still disagrees. Blaming the torch or ignoring the test keeps the model safe, but teaches nothing.
5. (match) Match each comparison to where it stops being like the real thing.  
   **Answer:** The heart is like a pump → A pump does not start beating by itself; Electricity in a wire is like water in a pipe → A cut wire does not spill electricity on the floor; A flat map is like the land → A flat map stretches some lands, because the Earth is round. *Why:* Every analogy is right in some ways and wrong in others. The useful question is where the likeness stops.
6. (order) Put these models of the atom in the order they were made, oldest first.  
   **Answer:** ['Dalton: a tiny solid ball (1803)', 'Thomson: a soft ball with small bits dotted through it (1904)', 'Rutherford: a tiny hard centre with most of the space empty (1911)']. *Why:* Each model changed when new evidence came. Particles that bounced straight back from gold in 1909 could not happen with a soft ball, so Rutherford drew a new model.
7. (write) Your model says a marble cannot pass the middle. You see it pass. Give two things you could do.  
   **Answer:** Check the tilt was done as planned and try again. Change the drawing, for example by adding a gap. Predict a new tilt from the changed drawing and test that.. *Why:* A strong answer treats the surprise as a clue and changes the model, then tests the new model with a new prediction.
8. (sort) A plan of a school is made to help a new child find the library. Sort each thing.  
   **Answer:** the gate and the library → Kept; the colour of the walls → Left out; the way from the gate, with arrows → Kept; the names of the teachers → Left out; the main corridors → Kept. *Why:* The plan is made for finding the way. It keeps the places and the route, and leaves out what does not help with that job.
9. (perspective) Is a model that gives right answers also true?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer uses both examples and notes that nobody has a sure way to tell in advance which kind a model is. Scientists still argue about it.. *Why:* The atom began as a model and was later seen as a real thing. Ptolemy's model gave right answers and was not built like the sky. Both histories are true.
10. (mcq) Why does a diagram have labels and arrows that the real thing does not have?  
   Options: The real thing is always missing them / They are added to explain, not to copy / They make the diagram prettier  
   **Answer:** They are added to explain, not to copy. *Why:* A diagram is made to be understood. Labels name the parts and arrows show what moves. A copy of the real thing would have neither.
11. (truefalse) If a model gives right answers, it must be built in the same way as the real thing.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Ptolemy's model of circles on circles gave right answers for about 1,400 years, though the Earth is not at the centre. Right answers do not show that the inside is right.
12. (mcq) Which clue best shows that a wall across the middle of a sealed box is there?  
   Options: A marble rolls to a corner and stays / A marble stops at the middle every time / The box feels heavy  
   **Answer:** A marble stops at the middle every time. *Why:* A marble that always stops at the same line in the middle says there is something there. Rolling to a corner and staying is what you would get with no wall.
13. (draw) Draw water going from a tap into a glass, with labels and arrows. Then write one thing you left out.  
   **Answer:** A tap, a glass and an arrow showing water moving down, with labels such as 'tap', 'water' and 'glass'. A good answer names a left-out thing, such as the pipe in the wall, the splashes or the colour of the glass.. *Why:* A diagram has plain lines, labels and arrows. Everything not needed to explain the flow is left out.
14. (count) A model is 50 times smaller than a bridge 300 metres long. How long is the model, in metres?  
   **Answer:** 6. *Why:* 300 ÷ 50 = 6. The model is 6 metres long. Every length on it is 50 times smaller than the real one.
15. (mcq) Which one is a model made of words?  
   Options: A globe / 'The heart is like a pump' / A plastic skeleton  
   **Answer:** 'The heart is like a pump'. *Why:* A comparison in words is called an analogy. A globe and a plastic skeleton are physical models, which are objects you can hold.
16. (write) What must a diagram of the solar system change to fit on a page? How can the maker be honest about it?  
   **Answer:** The distances are squeezed and the planets drawn too big. The maker can write 'not to scale' on the diagram.. *Why:* A strong answer names the change and the honest note.
### Right answers, wrong insides and open questions (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Ptolemy's model of the sky predicted the planets well for about 1,400 years. What does that show?  
   Options: It was built just as the sky is built / A model can predict well and still be wrong inside / Predictions never matter  
   **Answer:** A model can predict well and still be wrong inside. *Why:* The model used circles on circles, which can copy almost any path. It gave right answers and was still wrong about the Earth being at the centre.
2. (truefalse) A map that kept every detail of a city would be the best map.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. It would be as large as the city and as hard to use. A map is useful because the maker chose what to leave out.
3. (mcq) Why does Greenland look so big on a flat map of the world?  
   Options: The map must stretch some part of a round Earth / Greenland is as big as Africa / The mapmaker made a careless mistake  
   **Answer:** The map must stretch some part of a round Earth. *Why:* A round surface cannot be flattened without stretching or tearing, like an orange peel. The usual map stretches the lands near the top.
4. (write) A friend says, 'My model must be right, because it has never been wrong.' What test could still show it wrong?  
   **Answer:** A new prediction in a new situation that the model has never been tried on, one that could fail. Only a test that could go wrong can show that a model is right so far.. *Why:* A model that has only been used where it is known to work has not been tested hard.
5. (order) Put these in the order they happened in the story of the tongue map.  
   **Answer:** ['A German study of 1901 finds tiny differences in taste across the tongue', 'A 1942 book draws the tiny differences as firm zones', 'The zone map is copied from book to book', 'A 1974 test shows that every part of the tongue can sense every taste']. *Why:* A small, careful result was redrawn as a neat map with firm borders. The neat map was copied without being tested, until someone tested it again.
6. (match) Match each model to what it cannot tell you.  
   **Answer:** A flat world map → the true sizes of lands near the top; A weather forecast model → what the weather will be, for certain, on your street; A plastic skeleton → how strong a real bone is; A diagram of a flower → the colour of your own hibiscus. *Why:* Every model leaves something out. Asking what it leaves out tells you what not to use it for.
7. (fill) On a globe, 1 centimetre is 425 kilometres. Everest is 9 kilometres high. On the globe it is ____ millimetres.  
   **Answer:** ['0.2', 'one-fifth', '0.21', '1/5']. *Why:* 9 ÷ 425 is about 0.02 centimetres, which is 0.2 of a millimetre. That is too small to feel on a globe.
8. (truefalse) A model that is very useful for one job is useful for every job.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A metro map is perfect for choosing where to change, and no use for digging a tunnel. Each model suits the job it was made for.
9. (perspective) Is the picture in your mind of a friend a model?  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer notices that the picture in the mind does leave out most of the friend, and asks whether being made on purpose matters. This question has no single answer.. *Why:* Both views can be defended. The first sees the likeness to a model. The second sees that knowing a person is more than a drawing.
10. (mcq) Nobody can open an atom. How can scientists still test a model of it?  
   Options: They cannot, so every model of the atom is only a guess / They predict what will happen when something hits it, then look / They open it with a very small tool  
   **Answer:** They predict what will happen when something hits it, then look. *Why:* A model of the atom predicts what will happen, for example when fast particles hit gold. Then people look. The bounces that a soft ball could not give led to a new model.
11. (write) The eye has a blind spot, yet we see no hole. Give one reason for calling seeing a model, and one against.  
   **Answer:** For: the brain fills the gap with a guess made from what lies around it, so part of what we see is supplied. Against: the eye still receives real light from the world, and what we see changes when the world changes.. *Why:* A strong answer gives a reason on each side and does not claim the question is settled.
12. (mcq) Which one is NOT a physical model?  
   Options: A plastic heart in a lab / A doll's house / The rule: distance equals speed times time  
   **Answer:** The rule: distance equals speed times time. *Why:* A physical model is an object you can hold. The rule is a model made of numbers and words, and it has no shape to hold.
13. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? A labelled diagram, a metro map, a photograph, a globe.  
   **Answer:** The photograph, because it keeps whatever the camera happened to catch. The other three are made by choosing what to keep.. *Why:* A photograph records a moment. The others are made on purpose, with parts kept and parts left out.
14. (draw) Draw two models of a mango: one for choosing a ripe mango, one for planting its seed. Say what each leaves out.  
   **Answer:** One shows the outside, with the colour, the skin and the smell. The other shows the inside, with the flesh and the flat seed. Each leaves out what the other needs.. *Why:* The first model is for a buyer, so the outside matters. The second is for a grower, so the seed matters. The job decides what to keep.
15. (count) A diagram of a sparrow is 30 centimetres long. The real bird is 15 centimetres. How many times bigger is the diagram?  
   **Answer:** 2. *Why:* 30 ÷ 15 = 2. The diagram is twice the size of the real bird. Scale tells you how a model compares with the real thing.
16. (write) Scientists still argue about whether a model that works is true. Write one test that might help, and say why.  
   **Answer:** Any test with a reason. One test is to use the model to predict something new and then check it. Another is to look with a new instrument at what the model says is there.. *Why:* A strong answer picks a test that could show the model wrong.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) A friend says a model must be exactly like the real thing. What is the best reply?  
   Options: A model keeps what matters for a job and leaves out the rest / Yes, otherwise it is only a drawing / Only scientists may call something a model  
   **Answer:** A model keeps what matters for a job and leaves out the rest. *Why:* A model is a tool for understanding. Making an exact copy would give you the whole world again, and no clearer picture of it.
2. (mcq) A lab has a plastic eye that comes apart and a labelled chart of the eye. Which is the physical model?  
   Options: The labelled chart / Both of them / The plastic eye  
   **Answer:** The plastic eye. *Why:* A physical model is an object you can hold and turn round. A labelled chart is a diagram, which is a model on paper.

   Options: It is a photograph of the same flower / It shows what all flowers of that kind have / It shows the exact shadow  
   **Answer:** It shows what all flowers of that kind have. *Why:* A diagram stands for every flower of that kind, with parts named. A photograph shows only one flower as it looked once.
4. (mcq) A diagram of a heart is not red. Is that a fault?  
   Options: Yes, the heart is red / No, colour is not needed for its job / Yes, every diagram must have colour  
   **Answer:** No, colour is not needed for its job. *Why:* A diagram keeps what its job needs, here the parts and the way blood flows. A model is not faulty because it leaves something out.
5. (mcq) A mapmaker flattens a round globe onto paper. What cannot be avoided?  
   Options: Some sizes or shapes are stretched / Everything stays true in size and shape / Nothing changes near the top  
   **Answer:** Some sizes or shapes are stretched. *Why:* A round surface cannot be flattened without stretching or tearing, as an orange peel shows. Each flat map chooses which wrongness to accept.
6. (mcq) A leaning ball in torchlight predicts a season with no sunset near the poles. Why is a prediction like that valuable?  
   Options: It proves the model right in every way / We can check it against the real world / It is a guess that nobody can check  
   **Answer:** We can check it against the real world. *Why:* A prediction that can be checked tests the model. If it comes true, the model is trusted a little more. It does not prove the model is built just as the world is.
7. (mcq) A model gives right answers for 100 years. Is it certain to be built like the real thing?  
   Options: Yes, that proves it / No, a wrong model can still predict well / Yes, if scientists agree  
   **Answer:** No, a wrong model can still predict well. *Why:* Ptolemy's model of the sky worked for about 1,400 years and the Earth is not at the centre. Right answers show a model is useful, not that it is built like the world.
8. (mcq) You cannot open a sealed box. What is the best way to improve your drawing of what is inside?  
   Options: Predict a new tilt, test it and change the drawing if wrong / Guess harder / Shake it as hard as you can  
   **Answer:** Predict a new tilt, test it and change the drawing if wrong. *Why:* A prediction that could fail tests the drawing. Shaking hard gives noise, and guessing harder gives no new clues.
9. (mcq) Gold foil bounced a few fast particles straight back. What does this show about models?  
   Options: Models are always wrong / Models change when new evidence disagrees / A first model should never be changed  
   **Answer:** Models change when new evidence disagrees. *Why:* The soft-ball model of the atom could not give bounces like this. So Rutherford made a new model. A model is improved by evidence that disagrees with it.
10. (mcq) One metro map has straight strokes and another shows the true track. Which one is wrong?  
   Options: The straight one, because it bends the tracks / Neither, because each suits a different job / The true one, because it is hard to read  
   **Answer:** Neither, because each suits a different job. *Why:* The straight one is right for finding where to change. The true one is right for an engineer who digs. Neither is the real metro.
11. (mcq) Why do engineers test a small model of a dam before building the real one?  
   Options: A model is always stronger than the real dam / A mistake in a model costs far less / A model needs no tests  
   **Answer:** A mistake in a model costs far less. *Why:* A model can be wrecked and rebuilt cheaply. A mistake in a real dam could cost lives. The model leaves out some things, so engineers also test in other ways.
12. (mcq) One friend will swim in a river. Another will build a dam on it. Why might they need different models?  
   Options: Each job needs different things kept in the model / Models of rivers are all the same / Only one of them needs a model  
   **Answer:** Each job needs different things kept in the model. *Why:* The swimmer needs where the water is deep and where it flows fast. The builder needs the ground, the width and how much water comes. Each model keeps what its job needs.
13. (mcq) Which of these has labels and arrows that are not in the real thing?  
   Options: A photograph / A diagram / A mirror  
   **Answer:** A diagram. *Why:* A diagram adds labels and arrows to explain. A photograph and a mirror show what is there, without choosing or adding.
14. (mcq) Which question do scientists still argue about?  
   Options: Does a globe have a scale? / Does a model that works always show how the world is built? / Can a diagram have arrows?  
   **Answer:** Does a model that works always show how the world is built?. *Why:* The first and third are settled by looking. Whether a good model is true or only useful is an old argument. Ptolemy's model and the atom model each show a side of it.

## Applications
- Engineers test small models of bridges and tall buildings in wind tunnels before the real ones are built. A model is placed in a tunnel with a fan, and sensors measure the push of the air. Mistakes are found cheaply.
- Forecasters in India use computer models of the air to forecast the monsoon and cyclones. The model is stepped forward in time, and they compare its answer with what they see. In 2013 a forecast of where Cyclone Phailin would cross the coast of Odisha helped move about ten lakh people to safety.
- Doctors use plastic models of bones and organs to explain an operation to a patient, and surgeons practise on models before they operate on a person.
- Town planners make a map of where a new road should go, then check the map against the land. They use maps of different scales for different jobs, such as one for the whole district and one for a single street.
- A tailor makes a paper pattern before cutting the cloth. The pattern is a model of the shirt, and a mistake on paper costs much less than a mistake in cloth.
- Teachers and children use diagrams in every science book. Every diagram in a textbook is a model, and a reader can ask what it keeps and what it leaves out.

## Spotted around us
- At a metro station in Chennai, Kochi, Delhi or Kolkata, look at the line map on the wall or the door. Notice that the stations are evenly spaced and the lines are straight. The real tracks bend, and the real stations are not evenly apart.
- Open a map on a phone and zoom in and out. Notice that some things appear when you zoom in, such as small lanes and shop names, and others vanish when you zoom out. Each zoom has left out what it does not need.
- Find a floor plan in a flat advertisement or in the builder's brochure. Notice what it shows, such as walls, doors and room sizes. Notice what it leaves out, such as the view and the noise from the road.
- Look at the heart or the digestive system in your science textbook. Notice the labels and arrows. Then look at a photograph of a real heart, if you can find one, and notice how different they look.
- When the weather forecast on TV or a phone says 'rain tomorrow, 70 per cent', notice that this is a prediction from a model. It is not a certain fact. Check the next day and see if it was right.
- On a cricket broadcast, look at the diagram of where each run was scored, or the line showing a ball's path. It is a model of the match, with the grass and the crowd taken away.
- Look at a bus route board or a train route chart in your town. Notice that it shows the stops in order and nothing else, like a model made for one job.

## Open questions
- **Does the brain build an inner model of the world, or does it pick up what is there directly?** Experiments on filling-in and on pictures that fool the eye favour an inner model. Studies of how animals steer by the changing light favour direct pick-up. Both sides explain some findings and neither explains all.
- **Why is there any inner experience of seeing at all?** Science can follow the signals from the eye into the brain. It cannot yet say why those signals come with an experience, such as the redness of red. The philosopher David Chalmers named this the hard problem in 1995.
- **Is there one final model from which all other scientific models follow?** Physics has two very successful models, one for gravity and one for the tiny particles, and they do not fit together. Many physicists are looking for one model that joins them. Nobody knows if one exists.
- **Can a computer model that predicts well, but cannot be looked inside, count as understanding?** Some new weather models learn only from past weather, with no rules of physics. They forecast about as well as the physics models. Scientists disagree on two things. Is a forecast with no explanation real knowledge? And when can such a model be trusted?
- **What is it like to be a bee following a dance?** We can track where bees fly. We have no way to learn what a bee experiences, or if she pictures the route in anything like the way a person does.

## Questions to ask kids
- Think of a map you have used. What did it show, and what did it leave out? Was that a good choice?
- If you had to explain your home to someone in a different country, what would you put in a model, and what would you leave out?
- Which is better for remembering a friend's face, a photograph or a drawing? Which is better for explaining what a face is made of?
- Is the world you see with your eyes a picture, or a model your brain makes? What makes you think so?

## Connected maths
- A classroom globe is 30 centimetres across. The real Earth is 12,742 kilometres across. How many kilometres does 1 centimetre of the globe stand for? **About 425 kilometres.**. 12,742 ÷ 30 comes to 424.7. Round it to 425. So 1 centimetre of the globe is 425 kilometres of the Earth. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- In a model of the solar system the Sun is 22 centimetres across. The real Sun is about 1.4 million kilometres across, and the Earth is 150 million kilometres from it. About how far from the Sun does the Earth go in the model? **About 23 to 24 metres.**. The Sun is 22 centimetres in the model, so 1 centimetre is about 64,000 kilometres. 150,000,000 ÷ 64,000 is about 2,340. So the Earth is about 2,340 centimetres away, which is about 23 metres. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A model of a 120-metre bridge is 60 centimetres long. How many centimetres of the real bridge does 1 centimetre of the model stand for? A tower on the real bridge is 40 metres tall. How tall is the tower on the model? **200 centimetres, which is 2 metres. The model tower is 20 centimetres tall.**. 120 metres is 12,000 centimetres. 12,000 ÷ 60 is 200. So 1 centimetre stands for 200 centimetres. The tower is 4,000 centimetres, and 4,000 ÷ 200 is 20 centimetres. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Greenland covers about 2.2 million square kilometres, and Africa about 30.4 million. On a common classroom map they look about the same size. About how many times is Greenland stretched, compared with Africa? **About 14 times.**. 30.4 ÷ 2.2 is about 13.8, so about 14. Africa is 14 times larger, yet it is drawn the same size as Greenland. So Greenland has been stretched by about 14 times in area, compared with Africa. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- A honeybee waggles for 3 seconds, and the dance means about one second for each kilometre. A bee flies about 7 metres each second. About how long will her sisters take to fly to the flowers? **About 7 minutes.**. 3 seconds of waggle means about 3 kilometres, which is 3,000 metres. 3,000 ÷ 7 is about 430 seconds. 430 ÷ 60 is about 7 minutes. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L4`)
- A dot vanishes into the blind spot when it is 10 centimetres from the cross and your eye is 35 centimetres away. How far apart must the cross and dot be for the dot to vanish when your eye is 70 centimetres away? **20 centimetres.**. The blind spot sits at a fixed angle from where you look. If you move twice as far away, the gap on the paper must be twice as wide. 10 × 2 = 20. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a map of your school for? → To help a person find places, such as the gate and the classrooms.
- What is the difference between a photograph and a drawing? → A photograph records the light that reached a camera. A drawing is made by a person, who chooses what to put in.
- Why is a map drawn smaller than the real place? → So that it fits on paper and can be carried. The real place is too big to hold.
- What do the words on a drawing of a flower, such as 'petal', do? → They name the parts, so that a reader knows what each part is.
- Name one thing you can see in your room that is a model of something real. → A toy car, a globe, a doll or a drawing are all models. Any honest answer is fine.
- A friend says her picture of a mango looks 'wrong' because it has no stripes. What could you ask? → What the picture is for. A picture for showing a mango's shape does not need stripes.
