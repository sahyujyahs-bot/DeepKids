# Viruses, vaccines, prions and viroids: how a cell is taken over, and how a body gets ready
`sci.microorganisms.viruses.L5`

**Summary.** A virus has no hands, no engine and no food of their own. So how does one virus particle turn a healthy cell into a place that makes thousands of new viruses? In this lesson you will run a model cell with marked grains to see whose jobs get done, test whether changed milk can change fresh milk, and play a card game in which a body with a vaccine meets a virus. You will also meet prions and viroids, which are copied with even less than a virus has.

**Intro.** *A polio virus particle is a small ball of protein with one strand of instructions inside. They cannot move by themselves, and they cannot build anything. Yet four to six hours after one particle enters a cell, that cell can release up to 10,000 new ones. Who built them?* The cell built them. The virus brought only a message, and the cell's own builders read the message and did the work. In this lesson you will find out why a cell does this, why you feel fine for a day or two after a virus gets in, and how a vaccine uses the same facts to get your body ready. Then you will meet two things that are copied with even less: a prion, which is one wrongly folded protein, and a viroid, which is one bare loop of RNA.

## Learning outcomes

1. By the end I can explain why a person feels well for some time after a virus gets in, and name three ways a virus carries on: burst, bud or hide.
2. By the end I can describe how a cell warns the cells nearby with interferon, and how bacteria remember viruses.
3. By the end I can explain what a vaccine shows the body, and name four kinds of vaccine with an example of each.
4. By the end I can explain why some vaccines protect for many years, why the flu vaccine changes, and why some viruses have no vaccine yet.
5. By the end I can say what a prion and a viroid are, and how each gets copied.
6. By the end I can run a model with a fair test, and say what the model shows and what it leaves out.

## Terms
- **Host cell**: The living cell that a virus enters and is copied in. *e.g.* A cell in the lining of your nose is the host cell of a cold virus.
- **Receptor**: A protein on the outside of a cell that the cell uses to take things in or to receive signals. A virus can enter only if part of their coat fits a receptor. *e.g.* A polio virus fits a receptor found on the cells of humans and some monkeys, so polio does not infect dogs or cows.
- **Genome**: The full set of instructions that a living being carries, written in DNA or RNA. *e.g.* The genome of a polio virus is one strand of RNA about 7,500 letters long. Yours is about 3,000 million letters of DNA.
- **Messenger RNA (mRNA)**: A short working copy of one instruction. A cell makes it from a gene, and a ribosome reads it to build one protein. *e.g.* When a skin cell needs more keratin, the cell copies the keratin gene into mRNA and sends the mRNA to the ribosomes.
- **Ribosome**: A tiny builder inside every cell. It reads a message of mRNA and joins amino acids into the protein that the message describes. *e.g.* One human cell has millions of ribosomes. A virus particle has none.
- **Protein**: A long chain of amino acids, folded into an exact shape. The shape decides what the protein can do. *e.g.* The keratin in your hair, the haemoglobin in your blood and the coat of a virus are all proteins.
- **Takeover**: What happens when a cell's ribosomes and materials are used to make virus parts in place of the cell's own parts. *e.g.* A few hours after a polio virus enters a cell, that cell is building almost nothing but virus proteins.
- **Burst size**: The number of new virus particles that one infected cell releases. *e.g.* A bacterium infected by lambda phage releases about 100 new phages. A cell infected by polio virus can release up to 10,000.
- **Incubation period**: The time between a germ getting in and the first sign of illness. *e.g.* A cold shows after about one to three days. During that time the virus is being copied, but there are too few infected cells for you to feel anything.
- **Latent virus**: A virus whose genome stays quietly inside a cell, sometimes for many years, without new particles being made. *e.g.* After chickenpox, the virus stays latent in nerve cells beside the spine. Decades later they can wake and cause shingles.
- **Bacteriophage (phage)**: A virus who infects bacteria. *e.g.* Lambda phage infects the gut bacterium E. coli. Esther Lederberg found lambda in 1950.
- **Interferon**: A signal protein that an infected cell releases. It warns the cells nearby, and they slow down their own protein building so that a virus gains less. *e.g.* The aches and tiredness of flu come partly from your own interferon and similar signals, and not only from the virus.
- **Antigen**: Any part of a germ that the body's defenders can recognise by its shape. *e.g.* The spike on the outside of the Covid virus is an antigen. Covid vaccines show the body this spike.
- **Antibody**: A Y-shaped protein made by white blood cells called B cells. Its tips fit one antigen and stick to it. *e.g.* Antibodies that fit a measles virus's coat cover the coat, so the virus no longer fits the cell's receptor.
- **Memory cell**: A white blood cell that stays in the body for years after an infection or a vaccine, and can quickly make the right antibodies again. *e.g.* A person who had measles as a child still carries memory cells for measles at seventy.
- **Vaccine**: A safe preparation that shows the body an antigen of a germ, so that memory cells are ready before the real germ arrives. *e.g.* The measles vaccine is given to babies in India at nine months and again in the second year.
- **Live weakened vaccine**: A vaccine made of a living virus that has been changed so that the virus multiplies a little and does not cause the disease. The other word for weakened is attenuated. *e.g.* The measles vaccine, the oral polio drops and the Indian rotavirus vaccine Rotavac are live weakened vaccines.
- **Inactivated vaccine**: A vaccine made of whole virus particles that have been treated with a chemical or heat, so that they can no longer be copied. *e.g.* Covaxin, made in Hyderabad, is an inactivated Covid vaccine. The injected polio vaccine is another.
- **mRNA vaccine**: A vaccine that carries the message for one virus protein. Your own ribosomes read the message and build that one protein, and your defenders learn its shape. *e.g.* Two of the Covid vaccines used across the world from 2020 were mRNA vaccines.
- **Antigenic drift**: The slow change in a virus's antigens as copying mistakes build up, until old antibodies fit less well. *e.g.* Flu viruses drift so fast that the World Health Organization chooses new strains for the flu vaccine twice a year.
- **Eradication**: Ending a disease everywhere in the world, so that nobody catches it any more. *e.g.* Smallpox was declared eradicated in 1980. India's last case of wild polio was on 13 January 2011.
- **Prion**: A protein that has folded into a wrong shape and makes normal proteins of the same kind take the wrong shape too. A prion has no DNA or RNA. *e.g.* Mad cow disease and kuru are prion diseases of the brain.
- **Viroid**: A bare loop of RNA, a few hundred letters long, with no coat and no gene for any protein. Viroids are known only in plants. *e.g.* The coconut cadang-cadang viroid is 246 letters long. They have killed more than 30 million coconut palms in the Philippines.
- **CRISPR**: A stretch of DNA in which a bacterium stores short pieces of the viruses who attacked their ancestors. The bacterium uses the pieces to recognise and cut the same virus later. *e.g.* The bacteria that make yoghurt resist a phage better after they have stored a piece of that phage's DNA.

## Explanations
### What taking over means: the cell builds the virus · *defined*
**Definition:** A virus takes over a cell when the cell's ribosomes build virus proteins from the virus's messages, and the cell's enzymes and materials are used to copy the virus genome.

A cell is always building. The cell's genes are copied into short messages called mRNA, and millions of ribosomes read those messages and build proteins. A ribosome builds from any message that has the right starting signal. It does not turn a message away because of where the message came from. This is the fact a virus depends on. When a virus genome gets into a cell, messages made from that genome join the cell's own messages. Ribosomes meet messages by bumping into them. So the more virus messages there are, the more often a ribosome picks one up. At the same time the virus genome is copied again and again, so the virus messages multiply while the cell's messages do not. After a few hours most of the building in the cell is for the virus. Nothing was forced. The virus brought instructions, and the cell supplied the ribosomes, the amino acids, the energy and the work. This is a simplified picture. A real cell has millions of ribosomes, and each virus has extra ways to get ahead. But the main point holds for every virus.
- e.g. A polio virus particle carries one strand of RNA about 7,500 letters long. The cell's ribosomes read that strand directly, as if it were one of the cell's own messages.
- e.g. In the Factory Test, you drew six grains each round and built whatever each one said. The marked grains doubled each round, so more and more of your six jobs were for the virus.
- e.g. An mRNA vaccine works because of the same fact. Your ribosomes build the spike protein from a message that your own genes never wrote.
- e.g. The tumbler in the Factory Test is a model. It shows how ribosomes pick up messages, but it leaves out the cell's defences and the tricks that real viruses play. Every model leaves things out.
### The quiet gap: why nothing seems to happen at first · *defined*
**Definition:** For some time after a virus enters a cell, no finished virus particle can be found in that cell. In a body, the same gap shows as the incubation period, when the person is infected and feels well.

In 1939, Emory Ellis and Max Delbrück added phages to bacteria and counted the phages every few minutes. They expected the number to climb steadily. It did not. For a stretch of time the count stayed flat, and then it jumped all at once. The phages had been copied in one step, inside the bacteria, and released together when the bacteria burst. Later work showed something stranger. If you break open an infected cell early, you find no whole virus inside at all. The particle that went in has come apart, and the new ones are not yet put together. Scientists call this hidden stretch the eclipse. In lambda phage the first new particle appears about 20 minutes after infection, and the bacterium bursts at about 50 minutes. The same thing happens in your body on a larger scale. One infected nose cell is far too few to notice. The virus must go through several rounds of cells before enough cells are infected, and before your own alarm signals make you feel ill. So a person can pass a cold on a day before they sneeze.
- e.g. In the Factory Test, rounds 1, 2 and 3 usually gave no virus jobs or just one. The virus was in the cell and doubling the whole time.
- e.g. A cell infected with polio virus looks normal for the first hours. It breaks open four to six hours after the virus got in.
- e.g. A cold shows about one to three days after the virus gets in. Chickenpox takes about two weeks.
### How some viruses make sure their own messages are read first · *defined*
**Definition:** Many viruses do more than add messages. They block, cut or steal from the cell's own messages, so that the ribosomes are left with mostly virus messages to read.

If a virus only added messages, the virus would have to share the ribosomes with the cell. Many viruses do better than that. Most of a cell's messages carry a small chemical cap at the front end. The cap is how a ribosome finds the start. Polio virus messages have no cap. They have a folded stretch of RNA that lets a ribosome start in the middle. Soon after infection, the cell's capped messages stop being read, while the virus's messages still are. Within hours the cell is building almost nothing but polio proteins. Flu viruses do it another way. A flu enzyme cuts the caps off the cell's own messages and sticks them onto flu messages. Scientists call this cap snatching. The cell's message is ruined, and the flu message now looks like the cell's own. Some viruses simply make so many messages that the cell's are crowded out. Each trick is different, but each does the same job. It makes sure that the cell's ribosomes spend their time on the virus.
- e.g. Polio virus: the cell's own protein building is shut down, and the virus's messages are read by a different way of starting.
- e.g. Flu virus: the caps are cut from the cell's messages and reused on the virus's messages.
- e.g. In the Factory Test you can add this rule: take out two plain grains every round. The virus then wins the ribosomes sooner.
### Three ways to carry on: burst, bud or hide · *defined*
**Definition:** New virus particles leave a cell by bursting the cell or by budding out through the cell's membrane. Some viruses also stay hidden in the cell as a quiet genome and are copied only when the cell divides or when they wake.

Once new particles are assembled, they have to get out. Polio viruses and many phages burst the cell, and the cell dies. Flu viruses bud. Each new particle pushes out through the cell's membrane and leaves wrapped in a piece of it. The cell can go on releasing flu particles for hours before dying. The third way is to wait. In 1950, Esther Lederberg at the University of Wisconsin was growing a strain of the gut bacterium E. coli that had been treated with ultraviolet light. When she mixed this strain with the original, untreated strain, clear holes appeared in the layer of bacteria. Holes like that are made by phages. But she had added no phage. She worked out that the original strain had been carrying a phage all along, joined into the bacteria's own DNA and copied quietly at every division. The treated strain had lost theirs, so they had no protection. She named the phage lambda. Lambda senses the state of the cell. In a well-fed cell they usually burst out. In a starving cell they usually join the cell's DNA and wait. If the cell's DNA is damaged, they wake and leave. Viruses of humans hide too. The chickenpox virus stays in nerve cells for life. HIV writes a DNA copy of their genome into the DNA of white blood cells, which is why HIV is so hard to clear.
- e.g. Burst: polio virus, and lambda phage in a well-fed bacterium, with about 100 new phages from each cell.
- e.g. Bud: flu virus and HIV, each particle wrapped in a piece of the cell's own membrane.
- e.g. Hide: lambda in a starving bacterium, the chickenpox virus in nerve cells, and HIV in white blood cells.
### The cell fights back: interferon, the first alarm · *defined*
**Definition:** An infected cell releases signal proteins called interferons. Cells nearby respond by slowing their own protein building and by cutting up RNA, so that a virus who enters them gains much less.

A cell is not helpless. Cells carry sensors for things that should not be there, such as long double strands of RNA, which many viruses make while being copied. When a sensor finds one, the cell does two things. The cell sends out interferon and begins to shut down. Interferon drifts to the cells nearby and fits receptors on them. Those cells have not met the virus yet, but they get ready. They make one enzyme that slows all protein building, and another that cuts up RNA. Both hurt the cell a little. But a cell that builds slowly is a poor factory for a virus. Interferon also pushes badly infected cells to destroy themselves, which ends the virus's copying in that cell. Alick Isaacs and Jean Lindenmann described interferon in London in 1957. They named it for the way it interferes with virus copying. This alarm works within hours, long before any antibody is made. It is also the reason for much of what you feel. The fever, the aches and the tiredness of flu come partly from interferon and similar signals. So feeling ill is partly your own defence at work.
- e.g. A cell next to an infected cell slows their ribosomes after receiving interferon, even though no virus has reached them yet.
- e.g. An infected cell who self-destructs early releases few or no new virus particles.
- e.g. In the Factory Test, the 'alarm' rule is to take out two marked grains each round before copying. With one starting grain the virus is cleared at once.
### Bacteria remember viruses too: CRISPR · *defined*
**Definition:** Many bacteria keep short pieces of the DNA of phages who attacked them, stored in a part of their own genome called CRISPR. They use the pieces to recognise the same phage later and cut that phage's DNA.

Phages have attacked bacteria for billions of years, and bacteria have many defences. One of them is a kind of memory. When a phage injects DNA into a bacterium, the bacterium sometimes cuts out a short piece of that DNA and stores it in a row with other pieces. The row is called CRISPR. The bacterium copies each stored piece into a short guide RNA. A cutting protein called Cas carries the guide around the cell. If DNA matching the guide ever turns up, Cas cuts it. Because the row is part of the bacterium's own DNA, it is copied at every division. So the daughters inherit the memory. In 2007, Rodolphe Barrangou and his team showed this in the bacteria that make yoghurt. Bacteria that survived a phage attack had added a new piece matching that phage, and they now resisted the phage. About half of all bacteria whose genomes have been read have CRISPR. In 2012, Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna showed how to give Cas a guide of their own choosing, so that it cuts any DNA they pick. They received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020. The gene-editing tool used in laboratories today began as a bacterium's memory of a virus.
- e.g. Streptococcus thermophilus, a yoghurt bacterium, stores pieces of the phages that spoil yoghurt cultures.
- e.g. A bacterium's stored pieces are in the order the attacks came, so the row is a record of that family's past infections.
- e.g. Your memory cells are not passed to your children. A bacterium's CRISPR row is.
### What a vaccine shows the body · *defined*
**Definition:** A vaccine shows the body an antigen of a germ without the disease. The body makes antibodies and memory cells that fit the antigen, so the response to the real germ starts days earlier.

Your body's defenders recognise a virus by shape. They do not need the whole virus, and they do not need the virus to be dangerous. They need to meet the shape of some part of the virus, called an antigen. The first time B cells meet a new antigen, it takes about one to two weeks before large amounts of well-fitting antibody are made. During those weeks the virus is doubling. After the first meeting, some of the cells that fit stay on as memory cells. If the same antigen appears again, they respond within a few days, with more antibody and a better fit. A vaccine is a first meeting arranged on purpose. This matters because of the quiet gap. A virus needs several rounds of copying before the person is ill. If antibodies arrive during the gap, they cover the virus particles as they leave each cell, and the particles no longer fit their receptor. The infection ends while it is still small. So a vaccinated person may still be infected for a short time. The vaccine changes the timing. It does not put up a wall.
- e.g. In Seven Steps or Stopped, the body with a vaccine starts with antibody cards in hand. The body without one starts with no cards and draws them slowly.
- e.g. A child who has had two doses of measles vaccine and meets measles usually never knows it.
- e.g. The vaccine against rabies can be given after a dog bite, because the rabies virus takes weeks to reach the brain.
### Four kinds of vaccine · *defined*
**Definition:** A vaccine can show the body a live weakened virus, a whole inactivated virus, one part of a virus, or the instructions for making one part.

Every vaccine has to show the body an antigen. There are four main ways to do it. The first is a live weakened virus. The virus multiplies a little in the body, so one or two doses give strong, long protection. The measles vaccine, the oral polio drops and Rotavac are of this kind. The second is a whole virus that has been inactivated with a chemical, so that the genome can no longer be copied. The shape is still there. Covaxin is of this kind, and so is the injected polio vaccine. These usually need more doses, because nothing multiplies. The third shows only a part. The hepatitis B vaccine holds one protein from the virus's surface. Since 1986 that protein has been made by yeast cells that were given the virus's gene for it. It was the first human vaccine made by moving a gene into another cell. The fourth kind gives instructions and lets your own cells build the part. Covishield does this with a harmless chimpanzee cold virus that carries the gene for the Covid spike. An mRNA vaccine does it with the message alone. Each kind has costs. Live vaccines are strong but are not given to people whose defences are very weak. Part-only vaccines are very safe but need boosters.
- e.g. Live weakened: measles, oral polio drops, and Rotavac, the Indian rotavirus vaccine licensed in 2014.
- e.g. Whole inactivated: Covaxin, made by Bharat Biotech with the National Institute of Virology, and the injected polio vaccine.
- e.g. One part: hepatitis B. Instructions: Covishield, made in Pune, and the mRNA Covid vaccines.
### A message as a vaccine: the work of Katalin Karikó · *defined*
**Definition:** An mRNA vaccine is a message for one virus protein, wrapped in a tiny bubble of fat. Cells take it in, their ribosomes build the protein, and the body's defenders learn its shape. The message breaks down within days.

If ribosomes build from any message, then a message could be a medicine. Katalin Karikó, a biochemist born in Hungary in 1955, worked on this idea for decades. There was a serious problem. Cells have sensors for RNA that comes from outside, because that is what many viruses are. When mRNA made in a laboratory was put into the body, the sensors raised the alarm, and the mRNA was destroyed before it could be read. Her grant requests were turned down again and again, and in 1995 the University of Pennsylvania demoted her. She kept working. In 2005 she and the immunologist Drew Weissman found the answer. RNA has four kinds of letter. They swapped one of them, uridine, for a slightly different form called pseudouridine, which cells also use in some of their own RNA. The sensors now stayed quiet, and the ribosomes still read the message. Two leading journals rejected the paper before a third one published it. When Covid arrived in 2020, vaccines built on this finding were ready within a year. Karikó and Weissman received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2023. An mRNA vaccine does not enter the nucleus and does not change DNA. It is read for a short time and then broken down, like any other message.
- e.g. The mRNA in a Covid vaccine is the message for the spike protein only. No other part of the virus is made.
- e.g. mRNA is fragile, so the message travels inside a tiny bubble of fat, and the vaccine is stored very cold.
- e.g. The same method is being tested for vaccines against flu and for treatments made for one patient's cancer.
### Why some vaccines last and some must change · *defined*
**Definition:** A vaccine protects for as long as the virus's antigens stay the same and the body's memory lasts. Viruses whose antigens change quickly, or who come in very many types, are hard or impossible to vaccinate against so far.

Two doses of measles vaccine are about 97 percent effective, and the protection lasts for many years. The flu vaccine is remade every year and is about 40 to 60 percent effective in an average year. Why are they so different? Both viruses make copying mistakes at a high rate. The difference lies in what the mistakes do. The part of the measles virus that fits the cell's receptor seems to tolerate almost no change. A measles virus with a changed coat usually can no longer enter cells. So the measles virus of today has the same antigens as the measles virus of 1963, when the first vaccine was licensed. A flu virus's spikes can change shape and still work. Each year the flu viruses going round have drifted a little, and old antibodies fit less well. The World Health Organization studies flu samples from across the world and chooses the strains for the next vaccine twice a year. The common cold has a different problem. At least 165 types of rhinovirus are known, and antibodies against one type give little or no protection against the others. HIV is harder still. HIV changes faster than flu, and hides inside the very white blood cells that organise the defence. After forty years of work there is still no HIV vaccine.
- e.g. Measles: one kind of antigen that hardly changes. Two doses protect for many years.
- e.g. Flu: antigens drift each year. A new vaccine is chosen twice a year, once for each half of the world.
- e.g. Common cold: at least 165 types of rhinovirus. No vaccine exists.
### Ending a virus for good · *defined*
**Definition:** A virus who can be copied only in human cells, and against whom a good vaccine exists, can be ended completely, because the virus needs an unbroken chain of human hosts.

A virus particle outside a cell does not last long. Every virus who infects only humans must keep reaching new humans, without a break, or that kind of virus ends. Smallpox infected only humans, and the vaccine was very good. After a worldwide effort, the last natural case was in Somalia in 1977, and the world was declared free of smallpox in 1980. Only one other disease has been ended in this way: rinderpest, a virus disease of cattle, whose end was announced in 2010. Polio may be the third. In the 1980s India had thousands of polio cases every year. The Pulse Polio programme began in 1995, with the aim of giving every child under five two drops of vaccine on the same days. India's last case of wild polio was recorded on 13 January 2011, in West Bengal. The World Health Organization declared India polio-free on 27 March 2014. Two of the three types of wild polio virus are now gone from the whole world. The third type still passes between people in Afghanistan and Pakistan. Until that chain breaks too, children everywhere still need the vaccine. A virus who also lives in other animals, such as rabies or bird flu, cannot be ended this way. Protection also reaches people who cannot be vaccinated. When most people nearby are vaccinated, the virus has few people left to reach, so a baby too young for the vaccine is safer. Covering a cough and staying home when ill protect others in the same way.
- e.g. Smallpox: only humans, a strong vaccine, eradicated in 1980.
- e.g. Polio in India: Pulse Polio from 1995, the last wild case in January 2011, declared polio-free in March 2014.
- e.g. Rabies: the virus also lives in dogs, bats and other animals, so vaccinating people alone cannot end it.
### Hidden viruses that stayed: virus DNA in our own genome · *defined*
**Definition:** Some viruses write their genome into the DNA of the host cell. When this happened long ago in an egg or sperm cell, the virus DNA was passed to the children. About 5 to 8 percent of human DNA came from such viruses.

Hiding in a cell's DNA can become permanent. A group of viruses called retroviruses make a DNA copy of their RNA genome and join it into the DNA of the host cell. HIV does this today. Usually the cell is a body cell, and the virus DNA ends when that person does. But over millions of years, retroviruses sometimes joined the DNA of an egg cell or a sperm cell. Then every cell of the child carried the virus DNA, and so did the child's children. When the human genome was read, scientists found that somewhere between 3 and 8 percent of it is made of these old virus genomes. Almost all of them are broken by mutations and can no longer make a virus. A few of their genes are still in use. One is called syncytin. In a retrovirus, the gene made the coat protein that fuses the virus with a cell. In us, the same protein fuses cells of the placenta into one continuous layer. That layer passes food from the mother to the baby and keeps the mother's defenders away from the baby. So a gene that once helped a virus enter cells now helps to build the organ that feeds a human baby before birth.
- e.g. HIV joins the DNA of white blood cells. That virus DNA is not inherited, because those cells are not egg or sperm cells.
- e.g. The syncytin gene came from a retrovirus and is used in the human placenta.
- e.g. Most of the old virus DNA in our genome is broken and makes nothing.
### Prions: a shape that gets copied · *defined*
**Definition:** A prion is a wrongly folded form of a normal body protein. When a prion touches a normal protein of the same kind, the normal one refolds into the prion shape. Prions have no DNA or RNA.

Your nerve cells carry a protein called PrP on their outer surface. Nobody is yet sure what it is for. In its normal shape the chain is folded mostly into coils. The same chain can also fold another way, into flat sheets that stack on top of each other. That second shape is the prion. The stacked form is very hard for the cell to break down. When a prion touches a normal PrP, the normal one changes to the stacked shape and joins the pile. The pile grows, breaks, and each piece grows again. No genome is copied. What passes on is a shape, and all the material comes from the cell's own normal protein. Mice that have no PrP gene cannot get prion disease at all, because there is nothing to convert. In the brain, the piles slowly damage nerve cells. Prion diseases take years to show and cannot yet be cured. They are also rare. Prions are much harder to destroy than viruses. Cooking does not destroy them, and hospitals need stronger methods than usual to clean instruments. In ordinary proteins a changed shape does not pass on. Cooked egg white does not cook the raw egg white beside it. A prion is unusual because its changed shape does.
- e.g. Kuru among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, traced to funeral customs that ended around 1960.
- e.g. Mad cow disease in Britain: more than 184,000 cattle were diagnosed between 1986 and 2015, after cattle were fed meal made from other cattle and sheep.
- e.g. In Lemon, Chhena or Nothing, a piece of curdled milk did not curdle the fresh milk around it.
### Are prions always harmful? · *debated*
All the prions known in humans and other mammals cause disease. In the early 1990s, Reed Wickner found that yeast cells have prions too. A yeast prion passes from a mother cell to her daughters in the cytoplasm, without any change in DNA. Yeast cells with a prion often behave differently from yeast cells without one. This raised a question that is still argued over. Is a prion always a protein gone wrong? Or can a cell use a shape that copies itself as a way to pass on a useful change?
- e.g. A yeast prion can change which sugars the cell can use, and the daughters inherit the change.
- e.g. In mammals, no prion with a clear benefit has been shown.
- **Susan Lindquist and her group:** They argued that some yeast prions are useful. A prion can switch on hidden abilities, and in hard times a few cells with the prion may survive where the others do not.
- **Other yeast biologists:** They point out that prions are rare in wild yeast. If prions were useful they should be common, so most yeast prions are better seen as diseases of yeast.
- **Some brain scientists:** They have proposed that prion-like proteins at the junctions between nerve cells help to keep long-term memories. This is an idea, tested mostly in sea slugs, fruit flies and mice, and not settled.
### Viroids: a bare loop of RNA · *defined*
**Definition:** A viroid is a closed loop of RNA, 246 to 467 letters long, with no protein coat. The loop holds no gene for any protein. Viroids are known only in plants, and a plant enzyme copies them.

In 1971, Theodor Diener, a plant scientist in Maryland in the United States, was studying a disease that makes potatoes long, thin and cracked. Everyone assumed that a virus caused it. But the thing that carried the disease was far too small to be any virus, and had no protein. It was a bare loop of RNA. He named it a viroid. A viroid's RNA is not a message for a ribosome, and no protein is ever made from it. So how are viroids copied? Inside the plant's nucleus there is an enzyme that copies DNA into mRNA. The viroid loop folds into a rod that this enzyme accepts, and the enzyme copies the loop round and round. The copies are cut and closed into new loops. Why the plant falls ill is only partly understood. Pieces of viroid RNA seem to match parts of the plant's own messages, and the plant's defences then cut up those messages too. Viroids move from plant to plant on cutting tools, by leaves touching, and through insects such as aphids. The best-known viroid is in coconut palms. The cadang-cadang viroid of the Philippines is only 246 letters long. A palm takes eight years or more to die. More than 30 million palms have died since the disease was first reported in the late 1920s, and it is spread mainly on the blades used for harvesting.
- e.g. Potato spindle tuber viroid, found by Theodor Diener in 1971, the first viroid known.
- e.g. Coconut cadang-cadang viroid: 246 letters of RNA, and more than 30 million palms dead in the Philippines.
- e.g. In the Knife Trail, turmeric from one piece of banana showed on several pieces that were cut afterwards with the same knife.
### Where did viroids come from? · *debated*
Viroids are the smallest things known that are copied inside cells and cause disease. Nobody knows where they came from. The question matters because of an older question: what was there before cells? Many scientists think that before DNA and proteins, there was a time when RNA did both jobs, storing instructions and speeding up reactions. This is called the RNA world, and it is itself an idea, not a settled fact.
- e.g. Some viroids can cut themselves without any protein. RNA that speeds up a reaction is what the RNA world idea needs.
- e.g. Viroids have so far been found only in plants, which appeared late in the history of life.
- **Theodor Diener, in 1989:** He proposed that viroids are living relics of the RNA world: small loops of RNA that were being copied before there were any cells, and have carried on ever since.
- **Scientists who doubt this:** They point out that viroids are found in plants and not in bacteria. Something left over from before cells should be in the oldest kinds of cell. They think viroids more likely escaped from the RNA of plant cells much later.
- **Scientists looking at new finds:** Recently, other small loops of RNA have been found in bacteria. Some researchers think this supports an old origin. The matter is open.

## Mostly agreed
- A virus particle builds nothing. The host cell's ribosomes build every virus protein, from messages that come from the virus genome. *How we know:* Virus particles have no ribosomes, which can be seen under the electron microscope and in their list of genes. Virus proteins appear only in cells that still have working ribosomes.
- After a virus enters a cell there is a stretch of time when no finished virus particle can be found in that cell. *How we know:* Ellis and Delbrück counted phages in 1939 and found a flat stretch followed by a sudden jump. Later workers broke infected cells open early and found no whole particles inside.
- Some viruses stay in a cell as a quiet genome for a long time, and can wake later. *How we know:* Esther Lederberg showed in 1950 and 1951 that a strain of E. coli carried lambda phage through many generations without bursting. Shingles appears along one nerve decades after chickenpox, and the chickenpox virus is found in the blisters.
- A vaccine protects by giving the body memory of an antigen before the real germ arrives. *How we know:* Vaccinated people make antibodies within days of meeting the germ, where unvaccinated people take one to two weeks. Smallpox ended in 1980 and wild polio ended in India in 2011 after mass vaccination.
- Prion diseases are passed on by a wrongly folded protein, with no DNA or RNA. *How we know:* The scrapie agent survives doses of ultraviolet light and radiation that destroy DNA and RNA, as Tikvah Alper showed in 1967. Mice with no gene for the PrP protein cannot be infected.
- Viroids are bare loops of RNA that hold no gene for any protein, and plant enzymes copy them. *How we know:* Theodor Diener showed in 1971 that the cause of potato spindle tuber disease was RNA with no protein coat. The full sequences of viroids have been read, and no part of them is used to build a protein.

## How we found out
**Tikvah Alper, a radiobiologist in London. John Stanley Griffith, who worked out how a protein could be copied by shape. Stanley Prusiner, an American doctor and biochemist., Alper's paper was published in 1967. Prusiner named the prion in 1982 and received the Nobel Prize in 1997..** Scrapie is a brain disease that passes from sheep to sheep. In the 1960s every biologist was sure that a slow virus caused it, because anything that is copied and passed on must carry DNA or RNA. Tikvah Alper, a scientist born in Cape Town, led a radiation laboratory at Hammersmith Hospital in London. She knew that ultraviolet light and radiation wreck DNA and RNA. She gave scrapie material doses that would have destroyed any virus. It could still infect. In 1967 she published the result in the journal Nature under a title that was a question: does the agent of scrapie replicate without nucleic acid? In the same years John Griffith showed on paper how a protein alone could do it. The idea was met with astonishment, and few accepted it. In 1982 the American doctor Stanley Prusiner purified the scrapie agent and found protein and nothing else. He named it a prion. He was attacked for years. In 1997 he received the Nobel Prize.  
*What people believed before:* That scrapie was caused by a slow virus, and that nothing can be copied and passed on without DNA or RNA. Alper showed that the scrapie agent survived treatment that destroys DNA and RNA.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Nothing can be copied and passed on unless it carries DNA or RNA.** (Almost all biologists from the 1950s to the 1980s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Every cell and every virus ever studied carried DNA or RNA, and the way those are copied was well understood.  
  Against: Prions carry neither. The scrapie agent survives treatment that destroys DNA and RNA, and pure wrongly folded protein can pass the disease on. The rule holds for genes, but a shape can also be passed on.
- **A virus is a pure enemy of the living beings they infect.** (The everyday view, and most of medicine until recently.; *debated*)  
  For: Viruses cause polio, measles, rabies, Covid and many diseases of crops and farm animals.  
  Against: Most viruses infect bacteria and never harm a human. Phages kill a large share of the bacteria in the sea each day, which returns nutrients to the water. A gene that came from a virus builds part of the human placenta.
- **A vaccinated person cannot be infected at all.** (A common belief about vaccines.; *rejected*)  
  For: For measles and smallpox, vaccinated people almost never fall ill.  
  Against: A vaccine gives the body a head start. The virus may still enter some cells and be copied for a short time before antibodies stop the spread. For flu and Covid, vaccinated people are often infected and are ill for less time and less severely.
- **An infected cell is not a damaged cell. It is the virus in their living form.** (The French biologist Patrick Forterre, who calls the infected cell a 'virocell'.; *minority*)  
  For: Inside the cell, the virus genome directs feeding, building and copying. The particle outside is like a seed or a spore, a travelling stage.  
  Against: All the machinery and energy still belong to the cell, and the cell has a genome of their own too. Most biologists prefer to say that there is one living cell, which is being used.
- **Kuru was passed from parent to child in the genes.** (Some early researchers in the 1950s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Kuru ran in families and was found almost only among the Fore people.  
  Against: It struck mostly women and children, which inherited diseases do not usually do. The anthropologists Shirley Lindenbaum and Robert Glasse traced it to funeral customs in which women and children took part most. In the 1960s Carleton Gajdusek's team passed kuru to a chimpanzee, which showed that it could be caught.
- **Viroids are living relics from the time before cells.** (Theodor Diener, who found the first viroid, in 1989.; *debated*)  
  For: Viroids are tiny loops of RNA, and some can cut themselves without proteins. That is what the earliest things to be copied are thought to have been like.  
  Against: Viroids have been found only in plants, which appeared late. They may have escaped from the RNA of plant cells.

## Roots
We have run a model cell with grains and learned how a virus, a vaccine, a prion and a viroid each get copied or built. Now we ask what this tells us about being open to the world.

**Deep truth.** In the Factory Test, you built whatever each grain said, and never asked who had sent it. A cell's ribosomes build in this way too. So a virus takes over a cell without any force, using only a message. An mRNA vaccine gets in by the same opening. What lets a cell be robbed is also what lets a cell be taught.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think of an infection as an attack by force, and of safety as a wall that keeps things out. But a virus brings no force at all. Building from messages is how a cell does everything, and every protein in your body was built that way. A cell that built from no new message could not be infected, and could not be vaccinated either. So being open and being in danger are not two separate things that we can pull apart.  
*Evidence:* In the Factory Test, one marked grain among twenty plain ones took half of the jobs or more within five or six rounds, and nobody was forced. One polio virus particle becomes up to 10,000 in four to six hours, all built by the cell. An mRNA vaccine and a virus both work because ribosomes build from whatever message they meet. The body stays safe by checking what was built afterwards, with interferon, antibodies and memory cells. If your own Factory Test came out differently, write down what you got and run it again.

- *who am i:* You are a being who can be changed by what you take in. That is how a virus makes you ill. It is also how a vaccine protects you, and how you learn anything at all.
- *what is life:* A living cell builds from messages. Some of those messages are the cell's own, some arrive from outside, and some that arrived long ago are now part of the cell's genome.
- *what is this world:* A very small thing with the right shape or the right message can redirect the work of a very large thing. One loop of RNA 246 letters long can kill a coconut palm.

A virus particle on their own does nothing that we can see. A cell alone builds only more cell. When the two meet, one message is read thousands of times and becomes thousands of particles. So the many new viruses come from one message and one cell's work, together. Neither of them made the viruses alone.

- **Thirukkural 423:** “எப்பொருள் யார்யார்வாய்க் கேட்பினும் அப்பொருள் / மெய்ப்பொருள் காண்ப தறிவு.” Whatever is said, and whoever says it, wisdom is to find out what is true in it. The body's defenders do something like this when they check each new protein by its shape.)
- **Anguttara Nikaya 3.65, the Kesamutti Sutta (the Buddha's talk to the Kalamas):** The Buddha tells the people of a town not to accept a teaching only because it is repeated, handed down, written in a text, or spoken by a respected teacher. They should accept it when they have seen for themselves that it leads to good and not to harm. (A cell that has met a virus keeps a record of the result, in memory cells or in a CRISPR row. Next time, the same message is judged by what it led to before. The Buddha asks people to judge teachings in the same way, by what they lead to.)
- Science (settled): Ribosomes build proteins from virus messages and from the cell's own messages by the same steps. This is why viruses can be copied in cells and why mRNA vaccines work.
- Science (settled): Between 3 and 8 percent of the human genome came from viruses who joined the DNA of our ancestors, and at least one of their genes is used in the placenta.
- Science (debated): Viruses sense the state of their host and act on it. Lambda phage bursts out of a well-fed cell and waits in a starving one. Some scientists call these automatic chemical switches and would not call them decisions.
- Other view, Most molecular biologists: They would say that words like 'open', 'robbed' and 'taught' are human stories laid over chemistry. A ribosome and a message meet by chance, and they fit or they do not. Nobody is trusting anybody. On this view the lesson of the virus is practical: learn the steps, and block one. Many would add that a virus is not alive, and so is not a someone at all.

## Story: The Stripe
*Yazhini, her family, Dr Shahana and the summer of 1962 are made up. These facts are real. Shingles is caused by the chickenpox virus, who stays in nerve cells beside the spine for life after chickenpox. When the virus wakes, they travel along one nerve, so the rash forms a band on one side of the body and stops at the middle. About one person in three gets shingles at some time. The medicine works best if started within three days of the rash. A person cannot catch shingles from someone else, but someone who has never had chickenpox or the vaccine can catch chickenpox from the fluid in the blisters. In Tamil, shingles is commonly called akki.*

For two days Paatti said that the left side of her back was burning, though nothing showed on the skin.

On the third morning Yazhini's mother lifted the edge of Paatti's blouse and drew in her breath. A band of small blisters ran from beside the spine, under the arm, and round to the front.

'Akki,' said the neighbour, who had come to return a steel dabba and stayed to look. 'My uncle had it. It goes away. But keep the baby from her.'

Yazhini was eleven, and she did not like answers that came without reasons. She looked at the band for a long time.

It was about as wide as her hand. It began at the middle of Paatti's back and ended at the middle of her chest. It did not cross either line by so much as one blister. The right side of Paatti was as smooth as ever.

'Who gave it to you?' Yazhini asked.

'Nobody,' said Paatti. 'I have not gone past the gate in two weeks, with this knee.'

'Somebody who came here, then.'

'The milk boy. The woman who brings greens. You. Which of you has akki?'

None of them had. Yazhini took her notebook to the back step and wrote down what she knew, the way her science teacher had shown her. One: it burned before it showed. Two: nobody brought it. Three: it kept to one side. Four: it stopped at the middle as if someone had ruled a line.

She thought about the things that give a rash from outside. A caterpillar. A new soap. The sap of the mango tree. But a caterpillar walks where they like. Soap goes everywhere the water goes. None of them would stop at the middle of a person.

So what is there in a body that stops at the middle?

Her brother's Class 9 biology book was on the shelf above the rice bin. She turned the pages until she found a picture of a person with no skin, only the nerves drawn in yellow.

The nerves came out from the spine in pairs, one to the left and one to the right, at every level from the neck down. Each one curved round its own side of the body like half a hoop on a barrel. Each one ended at the front, at the middle.

Yazhini put her finger on one yellow half-hoop on the left side, at about the height of the bottom of the ribs. She ran her finger along it, from the back to the front. Then she closed the book and went to look at Paatti again.

It was the same path.

At the government hospital that afternoon, Dr Shahana looked at the band for about four seconds and then asked a question that seemed to have nothing to do with it.

'Amma, did you have chickenpox when you were small?'

Paatti laughed. 'In Kumbakonam, the summer I was seven. Nineteen sixty-two. They kept me in the back room for two weeks with neem leaves on the bed. My sister Kamala was not allowed in, so she pushed slices of raw mango with salt under the door.'

'Then that is where this came from,' said the doctor. 'When the chickenpox ended, a few of the viruses did not leave. They went up the nerves and settled beside your spine, in the roots of the nerves. They have been quiet there ever since. Your body kept them quiet. Now you are seventy, and you have been tired and in pain with that knee. In one nerve root, they woke. They have come back down that one nerve to the skin.'

Yazhini stood very still. 'So it came from inside,' she said. 'Along one nerve. That is why it is a stripe.'

The doctor turned to look at her properly. 'Who told you that?'

'The stripe did. And my brother's book.'

'Then you have done half my work,' said Dr Shahana, and wrote out the tablets. 'These slow the virus down. It is good that you came on the third day, because they do the most when they are started early. The blisters will dry in a week or ten days. Until they have dried, keep them covered, and keep away anyone who has not had chickenpox or the vaccine. They cannot catch this from you. But they can catch chickenpox.'

Yazhini's mother found the old vaccination card in her handbag and showed it. Two doses, when Yazhini was small. The baby next door had had none yet. He would have to wave from the gate for a while.

On the bus home Paatti was quiet, and Yazhini did the subtraction on the back of the hospital slip. Two thousand and twenty-five take away nineteen sixty-two. Sixty-three years.

'Paatti,' she said, 'nobody gave it to you. You got it from a girl of seven in Kumbakonam.'

Paatti looked out at the paddy fields going past, flat and green to the edge of the sky.

'Kamala died eleven years ago,' she said after a while. 'I thought I had nothing left of that summer but the taste of the mango.' She put her hand lightly against her left side, over the cloth. 'And all this time it was travelling with me.'

That night Yazhini drew the person from the biology book in her own notebook, with every yellow half-hoop. She coloured one of them red, on the left, under the ribs. Under the drawing she wrote the four things she had known that morning, and then a fifth. Five: a virus can wait.

**For the parent:** The rash kept to one side and stopped at the middle of the body. Nothing that touches the skin from outside does that, but a nerve does. So the virus had come from inside, along one nerve. They had been in Paatti's body since she had chickenpox at seven. Entering a cell and bursting out at once is only one of the things a virus can do. A virus can also stay hidden for most of a lifetime.

- Ask: Yazhini decided the rash had not come from outside. Which of her four clues told her most? Why?
- Ask: The virus was in Paatti's nerve cells for 63 years without making her ill. What do you think kept them quiet?
- Ask: Why can the baby next door catch chickenpox from Paatti's blisters, but not shingles?
- Ask: Paatti said the virus had been travelling with her all this time. Is a virus who stays for life a part of the person? What do you think?

HIV does this in white blood cells, and lambda phage does it in bacteria.)
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Cut out the 20 cards. 2. Write the four names on slips: virus, prion, viroid, bacterium. 3. Ask your child to say one fact about each before you start.  
  *Parent says:* Four very small things. Tell me one fact about each that you remember.  
  *Child mode:* Cut out the cards. Write the four names on slips. Say one fact you remember about each.
- **s2 (9 min)**

  - Note: The clues about copying are the hardest. A ribosome reads a virus's messages, but nothing reads a viroid. An enzyme only copies the loop.

## Experiments
### e1 · The Factory Test: whose jobs get done inside an infected cell? (50 min)
- **question:** One virus message gets into a cell that holds twenty messages of their own. The virus message is copied each round. After how many rounds is half of the cell's work being done for the virus?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will draw 6 grains in each of 7 rounds. In which round will 3 or more of the 6 be marked grains for the first time? Will the number of marked grains you draw rise evenly, or slowly at first and then fast? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** Every round uses the same tumbler, the same shake and six draws with eyes shut. Only the number of marked grains changes, and it doubles each round. In the second run only one thing is changed: the cell starts with 40 plain grains in place of 20.
- **record:** Fill in the table for all 7 rounds of each run. Draw a line graph of the virus jobs out of 6 against the round number, one line for each run. Then write one sentence each for what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Round | Marked grains in the tumbler | Plain grains in the tumbler | Virus jobs out of 6 | Cell jobs out of 6
- **s1 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Count 20 plain grains into the tumbler. 2. Mark one more grain with the pen and add it. 3. Put 70 plain grains in the bowl. 4. Draw the table with five columns and seven rows. 5. Read the question aloud and ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* The tumbler is a cell. Each plain grain is one of the cell's own messages. The marked grain is a message from a virus who has just got in. Each round you are the cell's builder. You pick six messages without looking and build whatever they say. In which round will half of your six be for the virus? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Put 20 plain grains and 1 marked grain in the tumbler. Put 70 plain grains in the bowl. Draw the table. Guess the round when 3 of your 6 are marked. Write why.
  - Note: Many children guess round 2 or 3, and expect an even rise. Write the guess down and do not correct it. The bowl is the cell's store of building materials.
- **s2 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Shake the tumbler. 2. Your child shuts their eyes and draws 6 grains, one at a time. 3. Count the marked and the plain ones and write them in the table. 4. Put all 6 back. 5. Now the virus message is copied. For every marked grain in the tumbler, mark one grain from the bowl and add it. 6. Do rounds 1, 2, 3 and 4 in this way.  
  *Parent says:* Take the first grain your fingers touch. Do not feel for a mark. After each round, every virus message gets one copy. The copies are made from the cell's own store.  
  *Child mode:* Shake. Shut your eyes. Draw 6 grains, one at a time. Count the marked ones. Put them back. Then mark one new grain for every marked grain in the tumbler. Do 4 rounds.
  - Ask: After round 3, how many marked grains are in the tumbler? How do you know without counting them? (good: Four. It went 1, then 2, then 4.; It doubles each round.)
  - Note: The marked grains in the tumbler go 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. In rounds 1 to 3 most children draw no marked grain or just one. Let your child notice this without comment.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Do rounds 5, 6 and 7 in the same way. 2. After round 7, do not copy again. 3. Look at the bowl and count what is left in it.  
  *Parent says:* Keep going for three more rounds. Watch what happens to the store in the bowl.  
  *Child mode:* Do rounds 5, 6 and 7. Stop after round 7. Count the grains left in the bowl.
  - Ask: What happened to the cell's store of materials? (good: It is nearly used up.; Only 7 grains are left out of 70.; The virus copies were made from the cell's own store.)
  - Note: Most runs first reach 3 or more marked grains in round 5. Round 4 or round 6 is also common, because chance decides each draw. The bowl should hold 7 grains at the end.
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at the filled table together. 2. Ask what your child notices. 3. Ask the questions below. 4. Do not explain yet.  
  *Parent says:* Look down the column of virus jobs. What do you notice? Did anything force you to build for the virus?  
  *Child mode:* Look down the column of virus jobs. What do you notice? Say it out loud. Did anyone force you to pick marked grains?
  - Ask: In rounds 1 to 3 you drew almost no marked grains. Was the virus in the cell during those rounds? (good: Yes, from the start.; They were there and doubling, but there were too few to be picked often.)
  - Ask: Why did you build more and more for the virus? (good: There were more and more marked grains, so I picked them more often.; I built whatever I picked. I did not check whose it was.)
  - Ask: What happened to the cell's own jobs? (good: Fewer and fewer got done.; By round 7 only one or two of the six were for the cell.)
  - Note: Good answers mention the quiet early rounds, the fast rise at the end, and that nothing was forced. If your child's run was unusual, say so and keep the numbers. Chance is part of the model.
  - Check: During which rounds was the virus message inside the model cell? → **Every round, from round 1**
- **s5 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Empty the tumbler. Keep the 64 marked grains in one pile. 2. Put 40 plain grains and 1 marked grain in the tumbler. 3. Ask for a new guess and a reason. 4. Run 7 rounds as before. To copy, add marked grains from the pile. 5. Fill in a second table.  
  *Parent says:* This cell is twice as busy. It has 40 messages of its own. Will the virus take much longer to get half of your 6 jobs? How many rounds longer? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Start again with 40 plain grains and 1 marked grain. Guess how much longer the virus will take. Write why. Run 7 rounds. Add marked grains from your pile to copy.
  - Note: Many children guess that it will take twice as long. It usually takes just one round longer, and sometimes no longer at all. About one run in twenty does not get there in 7 rounds. Doubling catches up very fast.
- **s6 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the two tables side by side. 2. Ask what the model shows, and what it leaves out. 3. Your child writes three sentences: what I thought, what I saw, and why.  
  *Parent says:* A real cell has millions of builders called ribosomes, and many thousands of messages. Our tumbler had six draws and twenty messages. What did the model get right? What did it leave out?  
  *Child mode:* Compare the two tables. What did the model get right? What did it leave out? Write what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: What does the model leave out? (good: A real cell has far more messages and builders.; The cell fights back, and our model cell did not.; Some viruses also destroy the cell's messages.)
  - Note: Now you can explain. A ribosome builds from whichever message it meets. More virus messages means more virus jobs. The model leaves out the cell's defences and the tricks of real viruses.
### e2 · Lemon, Chhena or Nothing: can changed milk change fresh milk? (30 min)
- **question:** Lemon juice changes the protein in milk into solid lumps. Can a lump of that changed protein make fresh milk change too?
- **predict:** Guess first. You will drop a rinsed lump of curdled milk into a tumbler of hot fresh milk. After 10 minutes, will the fresh milk have curdled, partly curdled, or stayed the same? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** All three tumblers get the same milk, the same amount, the same heat and the same wait. Only what is added changes: lemon juice in A, a rinsed lump of curdled milk in B, and nothing in C.
- **safety:** A grown-up heats the milk and pours it. Hot milk scalds.
- **record:** Fill in the table for tumblers A, B and C. Draw each tumbler as it looked after 10 minutes. Then write one sentence each for what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.
- **results table:** Tumbler | What went in | After 2 minutes | After 10 minutes | Did the milk change?
- **s1 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Label the tumblers A, B and C. 2. A grown-up heats the milk until it just begins to rise, then turns off the stove. 3. The grown-up pours one third of the milk into A. 4. Add 2 teaspoons of lemon juice to A and stir once. 5. Watch A for 2 minutes. 6. Put a lid on the pan to keep the rest hot.  
  *Parent says:* Watch tumbler A closely. Tell me what you see happening to the milk.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to heat the milk and pour some into A. Add 2 teaspoons of lemon juice. Stir once. Watch for 2 minutes. What happens?
  - Ask: What has the milk in A turned into? (good: White lumps and a thin, pale liquid.; It has separated into solid bits and watery stuff.)
  - Note: Within a minute or two the milk separates into soft white lumps and a thin, pale greenish liquid. If it does not, the milk was not hot enough. Add one more teaspoon of lemon juice.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour A through the tea strainer over the sink. 2. Hold the strainer under a slow tap for 30 seconds, and turn the lumps with the spoon. 3. Press out the water. 4. Read the question aloud and ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* These lumps are chhena, the solid that paneer is made from. The milk protein in them has changed. We have washed the lemon off. If we drop one lump into fresh hot milk, what will happen to that milk? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Strain the lumps. Rinse them under the tap for 30 seconds. Press out the water. Now guess. Will a lump change fresh milk? Write why.
  - Note: Rinsing matters. Any lemon juice left on the lump would spoil the test. Many children guess that the lump will curdle the new milk, as a spoon of curd sets milk. Write the guess down and do not correct it.
- **s3 (4 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up warms the rest of the milk again until it steams. 2. The grown-up pours half into B and half into C. 3. Drop one lump of rinsed chhena, about the size of a marble, into B. 4. Add nothing to C. 5. Note the time.  
  *Parent says:* B gets the lump. C gets nothing. Why do we need tumbler C at all?  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up pours hot milk into B and C. Drop one lump into B. Add nothing to C. Note the time. Why do we need C?
  - Ask: Why do we need tumbler C? (good: To see what hot milk does by itself.; So we know any change in B came from the lump.)
  - Note: C is the control. A skin may form on top of both B and C as they cool. That is normal for hot milk and is not curdling.
- **s4 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Look at B and C after 2 minutes and write what you see. 2. After 5 minutes, stir B gently once. 3. After 10 minutes, look again. Lift a spoonful from each and compare. 4. Fill in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Look for lumps, for thin watery liquid, or for any change at all. Compare B with C each time.  
  *Child mode:* Look at B and C after 2 minutes. Stir B once after 5 minutes. Look again after 10 minutes. Lift a spoonful from each. Fill in the table.
  - Ask: What happened to the milk in B? (good: Nothing. It is still smooth milk, with the lump sitting in it.; It looks the same as C.)
  - Ask: What did change the milk in tumbler A? (good: The lemon juice.; The acid in the lemon.)
  - Note: B should stay smooth milk with the lump in it, the same as C. If B has curdled a little, some lemon was left on the lump. Rinse another lump for longer and try again. That result still tells you something: the lemon did it, and the lump did not.
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child what they think now, and what they saw. 2. Then read the note below together. 3. Your child writes three sentences: what I thought, what I saw, and why. 4. The milk in B and C is good to drink or to use for tea.  
  *Parent says:* What do you think now? What did you see that tells you so?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you think now. Say what you saw. Then write three sentences: what I thought, what I saw, and why.
  - Note: Now you can explain. Acid from the lemon made the milk protein, called casein, tangle into lumps. The lumps do not make other casein tangle. In almost every protein, a changed form does not pass its change on. A prion is the rare protein whose changed form does. No kitchen test can show a prion at work, so this test shows what a prion is not. If your family eats eggs, try a piece of boiled egg white in raw egg white. The raw egg white stays raw.
  - Check: A spoon of curd sets a pot of milk overnight. A lump of chhena does not. Why? → **Curd holds living bacteria who multiply**

  - Note: Antibodies fit by shape, so a slightly changed coat is still partly recognised. After enough changes a new vaccine is needed. A person's memory is not an antibody. The model shows only that recognition by shape fades step by step.

### p1 · Four Rules, Four Days: my own takeover trial (60 min, 4 days)

## Worksheets
### Takeover, the quiet gap, vaccines, prions and viroids (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Who builds the new virus proteins in an infected cell?  
   Options: Ribosomes inside the virus particle / The cell's own ribosomes / Nobody, they grow / White blood cells  
   **Answer:** The cell's own ribosomes. *Why:* A virus particle has no ribosomes. The cell's ribosomes build every virus protein, from the virus's messages. White blood cells fight viruses and do not build them.
2. (truefalse) A virus breaks into a cell by force and then builds copies of themself.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A virus builds nothing. Their coat fits a receptor, the cell takes them in, and the cell's ribosomes do all the building.
3. (order) Put the seven steps of a virus in a cell in order, from first to last.  
   **Answer:** ['Attach', 'Enter', 'Uncoat', 'Read', 'Copy', 'Assemble', 'Leave']. *Why:* The virus attaches to a receptor, enters and loses their coat. The cell's ribosomes read the virus's messages and the genome is copied. New particles assemble and leave.
4. (fill) The time between a germ getting in and the first sign of illness is the ____ period.  
   **Answer:** ['incubation']. *Why:* During the incubation period the virus is being copied in a growing number of cells, but too few are infected for the person to feel ill.
5. (mcq) In the Factory Test, why did you draw more marked grains in the later rounds?  
   Options: The rules told you to pick them / There were more marked grains to pick / Plain grains were taken out / Marked grains are easier to feel  
   **Answer:** There were more marked grains to pick. *Why:* The marked grains doubled each round, so a blind pick landed on them more often. Nothing forced the pick. A ribosome meets virus messages more often for the same reason.
6. (match) Match each way of carrying on to a virus who does it.  
   **Answer:** Burst the cell → Polio virus; Bud out through the membrane → Flu virus; Hide in nerve cells for years → Chickenpox virus; Join the DNA of a bacterium → Lambda phage. *Why:* Polio viruses burst the cell. Flu viruses bud out wrapped in the cell's membrane. The chickenpox virus hides in nerve cells. Lambda phage can join the DNA of E. coli and wait.
7. (truefalse) A person can pass on a cold before they feel ill.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The virus is copied for a day or more before enough cells are infected for the person to notice. New particles are already leaving the nose in that time.
8. (mcq) What does a vaccine show the body?  
   Options: A medicine that kills the germ / A wall the germ cannot cross / An antigen of the germ / The illness in a mild form  
   **Answer:** An antigen of the germ. *Why:* A vaccine shows the body an antigen, which is a part of the germ that defenders recognise by shape. It is not a medicine or a wall. Most vaccines cause no illness at all.
9. (sort) Sort these vaccines by what they show the body.  
   **Answer:** Measles vaccine → A live weakened virus; Covaxin → A whole inactivated virus; Hepatitis B vaccine → One part, or instructions for one part; Oral polio drops → A live weakened virus; mRNA Covid vaccine → One part, or instructions for one part; Injected polio vaccine → A whole inactivated virus. *Why:* Measles vaccine and oral polio drops hold living, weakened viruses. Covaxin and the injected polio vaccine hold whole viruses that cannot be copied. The hepatitis B vaccine holds one protein, and an mRNA vaccine holds the message for one protein.
10. (fill) A wrongly folded protein that makes normal proteins fold wrongly too is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['prion']. *Why:* A prion has the same chain of amino acids as the normal protein, in another shape. It has no DNA or RNA.
11. (mcq) What is a viroid made of?  
   Options: A bare loop of RNA / One very small cell / A protein with no genes / DNA in a protein coat  
   **Answer:** A bare loop of RNA. *Why:* A viroid is a loop of RNA a few hundred letters long, with no coat. A protein with no genes describes a prion. DNA in a coat describes some viruses.
12. (write) Explain why you feel well for a day or two after a cold virus gets into your nose.  
   **Answer:** At first only a few cells are infected. The virus must be copied through several rounds of cells before many are infected. Only then do the body's alarm signals make me feel ill.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the virus is present and being copied the whole time, and that there are too few infected cells at first to notice.
13. (draw) Draw a ribosome with a cell's message and a virus's message. Show what the ribosome builds from each.  
   **Answer:** The drawing shows the same ribosome building a cell protein from the cell's message and a virus protein from the virus's message.. *Why:* A ribosome builds from any message with the right starting signal. It builds a virus protein in the same way as one of the cell's own.
14. (label) Draw a virus particle with a spike, a coat and a genome. Label each part and write what it does.  
   **Answer:** ['Spike: fits a receptor on the host cell', 'Coat: protects the genome outside a cell', 'Genome: the instructions that the cell copies and builds from']. *Why:* The spike decides which cells the virus can enter. The coat protects the genome. The genome is the only part that carries instructions.
15. (write) A girl has chickenpox at seven. At seventy she gets shingles. Where was the virus for 63 years?  
   **Answer:** The virus stayed hidden in nerve cells beside her spine. Her body's defenders kept them quiet. In old age the virus woke and travelled down one nerve to the skin.. *Why:* A strong answer says that the virus never left the body, names nerve cells, and says the virus woke when the defences were weaker.
### Tricks, alarms and changing coats (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A polio virus blocks the cell's own messages. Why are polio messages still read?  
   Options: They are much longer / Ribosomes prefer virus messages / They hide in the nucleus / They start in a different way  
   **Answer:** They start in a different way. *Why:* The cell's messages need a cap at the front for a ribosome to start. Polio messages have a folded stretch that lets a ribosome start without a cap. Ribosomes read both kinds of message in the same way.
2. (truefalse) Flu viruses cut the caps off the cell's messages and use them on their own.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. This is called cap snatching. The cell's message is ruined, and the flu message now carries a cap that ribosomes accept.
3. (mcq) What does interferon do to the cells near an infected cell?  
   Options: It gives them antibodies / It kills them at once / It makes them divide faster / It makes them slow their protein building  
   **Answer:** It makes them slow their protein building. *Why:* Warned cells slow their ribosomes and cut up RNA, so a virus who enters them gains little. Antibodies come later, from B cells.
4. (fill) Many bacteria store pieces of phage DNA in a row called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['CRISPR']. *Why:* The stored pieces let the bacterium recognise the same phage later and cut that phage's DNA. The row is copied at each division, so the daughters inherit it.
5. (match) Match each defence to the step of the virus that it stops.  
   **Answer:** Antibody → Attach; Interferon → Read; RNA-cutting enzyme → Copy; Killer T cell → Leave. *Why:* Antibodies cover the spikes so the virus cannot attach. Interferon slows the ribosomes that read. The RNA cutter destroys genomes being copied. A killer T cell destroys the cell before particles leave.
6. (mcq) Why is the flu vaccine changed every year?  
   Options: Old vaccine goes bad in storage / The flu virus's antigens drift / The body forgets in a year / Flu is a new virus each year  
   **Answer:** The flu virus's antigens drift. *Why:* Copying mistakes change the flu virus's spikes a little each year, and old antibodies fit less well. It is the same family of viruses, slowly changing.
7. (truefalse) A vaccinated person can never be infected by that virus.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A vaccine gives a head start. The virus may enter some cells before antibodies stop the spread, so the person is ill mildly or not at all.
8. (order) Put in order what happens after an mRNA vaccine is given.  
   **Answer:** ['The fat bubble merges with a cell', 'Ribosomes build the spike protein', 'B cells that fit the spike multiply', 'Memory cells stay for years']. *Why:* The message gets into a cell inside a fat bubble. Ribosomes build the spike from it. Defenders that fit the spike multiply, and some remain as memory cells.
9. (odd) Three of these diseases have a vaccine. Which one does not?  
   Options: Polio / The common cold / Measles / Hepatitis B  
   **Answer:** 1. *Why:* Colds are caused mostly by rhinoviruses, and at least 165 types are known. Antibodies against one type give little protection against the rest.
10. (sort) Sort each action: does it protect only you, or does it also protect other people?  
   **Answer:** A measles vaccine → Protects others too; Covering your cough with your elbow → Protects others too; Drinking a glass of milk every day → Protects only me. *Why:* A vaccine and a covered cough cut the chain of a virus, so the people around you are safer too. Milk helps your own body, but it does not stop a virus passing to someone else.
11. (write) Two doses of measles vaccine protect for many years. A flu vaccine protects for about one. Explain the difference.  
   **Answer:** The measles virus's antigens hardly change, so the same memory cells keep fitting. The flu virus's spikes drift a little every year, so old antibodies fit less well and a new vaccine is needed.. *Why:* A strong answer is about the virus changing or not changing. The body's memory lasts in both cases.
12. (mcq) A rinsed lump of curdled milk did not curdle fresh milk. What does this show?  
   Options: Prions are in milk / A changed protein rarely passes its change on / Lemon juice does nothing to milk / Milk protein cannot change  
   **Answer:** A changed protein rarely passes its change on. *Why:* In Lemon, Chhena or Nothing, the lemon changed the milk and the lump did not. In ordinary proteins a changed form is not catching. A prion is unusual because its changed form is.
13. (write) A virus and a prion both get 'copied'. Say what is copied in each case.  
   **Answer:** In a virus, the genome is copied, and new coat proteins are built from it. In a prion, nothing is built. Normal proteins already in the cell are refolded into the prion's shape.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a virus passes on instructions and a prion passes on a shape.
14. (perspective) Here are three views about what an infected cell is.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view and uses the test. For example: the cell biologist's view fits, because my tumbler held both kinds of message and I built from both.. *Why:* Real scientists hold each of these views. The facts are agreed, and the argument is about which words describe them best.
15. (draw) Draw two line graphs of virus numbers over six days: one for a person with a vaccine, one without.  
   **Answer:** Without a vaccine the line stays low for a day or two, rises high and falls after antibodies arrive. With a vaccine the line begins to rise and is turned down early, staying low.. *Why:* Memory cells make antibodies within days, during the quiet gap. So the virus is stopped while there are still few infected cells.
16. (sort) Sort these by whether a ribosome ever reads them.  
   **Answer:** A flu virus's messages → A ribosome reads it; A viroid → No ribosome reads it; A prion → No ribosome reads it; A polio virus's genome → A ribosome reads it; The mRNA in a vaccine → A ribosome reads it. *Why:* Virus messages and vaccine mRNA are read by ribosomes, which build proteins from them. A viroid is copied by an enzyme but never read. A prion is a protein and holds no message.

   **Answer:** Virus → A genome in a coat; Viroid → A bare loop of RNA; Prion → A shape; Bacterium → A whole cell. *Why:* A bacterium divides into two whole cells. A virus passes on a genome packed in a coat. A viroid passes on RNA alone. A prion passes on only a way of folding.

The vaccine stops hepatitis B, so hepatitis D has nothing to borrow.. *Why:* A strong answer says that hepatitis D depends on hepatitis B. Stopping the one stops the other.

   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view and names its risk. For example: keep them, but the risk is an accident or a theft.. *Why:* This argument is real and has gone on for decades. Each view protects something that matters and risks something else.

The cutting protein carries the guide and cuts any DNA that matches it.

3. (mcq) Why is there a quiet time after a virus gets in, before a person feels ill?  
   Options: The virus has not entered any cell / Antibodies are hiding the virus / The virus is asleep / Too few cells are infected at first  
   **Answer:** Too few cells are infected at first. *Why:* The virus is being copied from the start, but it takes several rounds of cells before many are infected. If you chose 'asleep', that describes a latent virus, which is a different thing.

   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. The virus stays hidden in nerve cells for life. Decades later they can wake and cause shingles along one nerve.
5. (mcq) Which defence starts within hours, long before antibodies are made?  
   Options: An antibiotic / Memory cells / A vaccine / Interferon  
   **Answer:** Interferon. *Why:* An infected cell releases interferon at once, and the cells nearby slow their protein building. New antibodies take one to two weeks the first time. Antibiotics do not act on viruses.
6. (mcq) How does a bacterium recognise a phage who attacked their ancestors?  
   Options: From a stored piece of the phage's DNA / Bacteria have no way / From antibodies in their blood / By the phage's smell  
   **Answer:** From a stored piece of the phage's DNA. *Why:* The bacterium keeps a short piece of the phage's DNA in a CRISPR row and uses it as a guide for cutting matching DNA. Bacteria have no blood and no antibodies.
7. (mcq) Which kind of vaccine holds whole virus particles that can no longer be copied?  
   Options: A one-part vaccine / A live weakened vaccine / An mRNA vaccine / An inactivated vaccine  
   **Answer:** An inactivated vaccine. *Why:* In an inactivated vaccine the whole virus is treated so that the genome cannot be copied, and the shape remains. A live weakened virus can still multiply a little.
8. (mcq) What do your cells build after an mRNA vaccine?  
   Options: New DNA / The whole virus / Antibiotics / One protein of the virus  
   **Answer:** One protein of the virus. *Why:* The mRNA is the message for one protein, such as the spike. Ribosomes build only that. The message does not enter the nucleus and does not change DNA.
9. (mcq) Why is there no vaccine against the common cold?  
   Options: At least 165 types of cold virus exist / Colds are too mild to matter / Cold viruses have no antigens / Colds are caused by prions  
   **Answer:** At least 165 types of cold virus exist. *Why:* Antibodies against one type of rhinovirus give little protection against the others. A vaccine would have to cover a very large number of types.
10. (truefalse) A virus who infects only humans could be ended for good by vaccination.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Such a virus needs an unbroken chain of human hosts. Smallpox was ended this way in 1980, and wild polio is close to its end.
11. (mcq) How does a prion spread in a brain?  
   Options: It makes normal proteins refold / It divides in two / Ribosomes read it / Its genes are copied  
   **Answer:** It makes normal proteins refold. *Why:* A prion has no genes and does not divide. It touches a normal protein of the same kind, and that protein takes the prion shape. If you chose genes, you were thinking of a virus.
12. (mcq) Which of these is a bare loop of RNA with no coat?  
   Options: A bacterium / A prion / A phage / A viroid  
   **Answer:** A viroid. *Why:* A viroid is RNA alone. A prion is protein alone. A phage is a virus, with a genome in a coat, and a bacterium is a whole cell.
13. (mcq) Which of these did the Factory Test leave out?  
   Options: The copying of virus messages / The cell's own messages / The cell's defences / Chance  
   **Answer:** The cell's defences. *Why:* The model cell never fought back. A real cell senses virus RNA, sends out interferon and may self-destruct. The model did include the cell's messages, the copying and chance.
14. (mcq) A tumbler model shows how ribosomes pick up messages. What is true of every model like this?  
   Options: It shows every part of the real thing / It leaves things out of the real thing / It is exactly the same as the real thing  
   **Answer:** It leaves things out of the real thing. *Why:* Every model leaves things out. The tumbler has six draws and twenty messages, not millions, and it has no cell defences.
15. (mcq) Your neighbour's baby is too young for a measles vaccine. Why does your own vaccination still help that baby?  
   Options: Your antibodies float into the baby's lungs / The vaccine passes to the baby by touch / Fewer people with the virus are left nearby to pass it on  
   **Answer:** Fewer people with the virus are left nearby to pass it on. *Why:* When most people around the baby are protected, the virus has few people to reach. Your antibodies stay in your own blood, so they do not reach the baby through the air or by touch.

## Applications
- Vaccine makers choose which part of a virus to show the body. The hepatitis B vaccine shows one surface protein, made by yeast cells.
- mRNA vaccines can be redesigned in weeks when a virus's spike changes, because only the message has to be rewritten.
- Doctors give medicine for shingles within three days of the rash, because it works by stopping the virus's DNA from being copied.
- Health workers in India mark a child's little finger with ink after polio drops, so that no child is missed on an immunisation day.
- Farmers and plant nurseries clean their cutting tools between plants, because viroids and some plant viruses travel on blades.
- Hospitals use extra-strong cleaning for instruments that have touched brain tissue, because ordinary heat does not destroy prions.
- Many countries do not allow cattle to be fed meal made from other cattle and sheep, to keep prions out of the food chain.
- Scientists use the CRISPR system of bacteria as a tool to cut DNA at a chosen place. A treatment for sickle cell disease is made this way.

## Open questions
- **Can a vaccine be made against HIV?** HIV changes faster than flu and hides inside the white blood cells that organise the defence. Many vaccines have been tested since the 1980s and none has protected well enough.
- **What is the normal PrP protein for?** It sits on nerve cells in every mammal, so it is probably useful. Mice without it live, with some problems of movement and memory. Its exact job is not known.
- **Can prion disease be cured or stopped?** There is no treatment yet. Researchers are testing ways to make the body produce less normal PrP, so that prions have less to refold. Nobody knows yet whether this will work in people.
- **Where did viroids come from?** They may be left over from a time before cells, or they may have escaped from plant RNA much later. They leave no fossils, so the question is hard to test.
- **Do prion-like proteins help to store memories?** Some experiments in sea slugs, fruit flies and mice suggest that a self-copying protein shape helps keep a memory at a nerve junction. It has not been shown in humans.
- **Can one flu vaccine be made that works against every flu virus, every year?** It would have to show the body a part of the virus that cannot change. Several such vaccines are being tested, and none is in use yet.

## Connected maths
- In the Factory Test, the marked grains start at 1 and double every round. How many are in the tumbler in round 7? How many had to be taken from the store? **64 marked grains. 63 came from the store.**. The numbers go 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. Round 7 has 64. The first one was there at the start, so 64 take away 1 is 63 grains from the store. The store began with 70, which leaves 7. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In round 5 of the Factory Test the tumbler holds 16 marked grains and 20 plain grains. What fraction is marked? About how many of 6 draws do you expect to be marked? **16 out of 36, which is 4 ninths. About 2 or 3 of the 6 draws.**. 16 and 20 make 36 grains. 16 out of 36 is 4 out of 9, because both divide by 4. Four ninths of 6 is 24 divided by 9, which is about 2.7. So expect 2 or 3 marked grains, though chance can give 1 or 4. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- With 20 plain grains, the marked grains first outnumber the plain ones in round 6. With 40 plain grains, in which round does this happen? Why is it not round 12? **Round 7. Doubling needs only one more round to pass a number twice as big.**. The marked grains go 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64. 32 is more than 20, so that is round 6. 64 is more than 40, so that is round 7. Each round multiplies by 2. So making the cell twice as big costs the virus one extra round, and not twice the rounds. (queues `math.number-sense.powers-and-roots.L5`)
- One cell infected with polio virus can release up to 10,000 particles. Suppose every particle infected a new cell, and each of those released 10,000. How many particles is that? Why does this not happen in a body? **100,000,000 particles, which is one hundred million. In a body, most particles never reach a cell.**. 10,000 times 10,000 is 100,000,000. Count the zeros: four and four make eight. In a real body, most particles are trapped in mucus, break down, or are covered by antibodies. So the real number is far smaller, but the sum shows how fast the number could grow. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
- Rule: take out 2 marked grains, then double what is left. Start with 5 and write the next four numbers. Which starting number stays the same for ever? **5, then 6, 8, 12, 20. A start of 4 stays at 4.**. 5 take away 2 is 3, doubled is 6. Then 4 doubled is 8, 6 doubled is 12, and 10 doubled is 20. For a number to stay the same, taking away 2 must leave exactly half of it. Half of 4 is 2, and 4 take away 2 is 2. So 4 stays at 4. (queues `math.algebra.input-output.L5`)
- Two doses of measles vaccine are about 97 percent effective. If 200 vaccinated children all met the measles virus, about how many might still fall ill? With one dose, about 93 percent effective, how many? **About 6 children with two doses. About 14 children with one dose.**. 97 percent protected leaves 3 percent. 3 percent of 200 is 6. With one dose, 7 percent are left unprotected, and 7 percent of 200 is 14. So the second dose more than halves the number who fall ill. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- A Drift Card is a 3 by 3 grid, and each square is shaded or blank. How many different patterns are possible? How many of them differ from your card 0 in exactly one square? **512 patterns. 9 of them differ from card 0 in exactly one square.**. Each of the 9 squares has 2 choices. So there are 2 times 2, nine times over, which is 512. To differ in exactly one square, you change one of the 9 squares, so there are 9 such patterns. (queues `math.logic.counting-ways.L5`)
- In Seven Steps or Stopped, a defence card stops the virus on a roll of 3, 4, 5 or 6. What is the chance that it works? What is the chance that it fails twice in a row? **4 out of 6, which is 2 thirds. It fails twice in a row 1 time in 9.**. Four of the six faces stop the virus, so the chance is 4 out of 6, or 2 thirds. It fails on 1 or 2, which is 1 third. Failing twice is 1 third of 1 third, which is 1 ninth. (queues `math.probability.experiments.L5`)
- The cadang-cadang viroid is 246 letters long. A polio virus genome is about 7,500 letters. About how many times as long is the polio genome? **About 30 times as long.**. 246 is close to 250. 7,500 divided by 250 is 30. So the genome of one of the smallest viruses is about 30 times as long as this viroid. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)

## Warm-up
- Who builds the new viruses in an infected cell? → The cell does. Its ribosomes build virus proteins from the virus's messages.
- Why do you feel fine for a day or two after a cold virus gets in? → Too few cells are infected at first. The virus needs several rounds of copying before you notice.
- Name three things a virus can do after entering a cell. → Burst out, bud out, or stay hidden.
- What does a vaccine show the body? → An antigen, which is a part of the germ that the body's defenders recognise by shape.
- Why does the flu vaccine change every year? → The flu virus's spikes drift a little each year, so old antibodies fit less well.
- What is a prion? → A wrongly folded protein that makes normal proteins of the same kind fold wrongly too.
- What is a viroid? → A bare loop of RNA with no coat, found only in plants.
- What signal does an infected cell send to the cells nearby? → Interferon. The neighbours slow their protein building.
- Where does the chickenpox virus go after the spots heal? → Into nerve cells beside the spine, where they stay hidden for life.
