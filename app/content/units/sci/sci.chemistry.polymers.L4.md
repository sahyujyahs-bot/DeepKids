# Polymers: how lined-up chains, twists and ties decide whether a material bends, stretches or snaps
`sci.chemistry.polymers.L4`

**Summary.** Tear a sheet of newspaper from top to bottom, and the tear runs straight. Tear it from side to side, and the tear wanders. Pull a wisp of cotton wool and it falls apart, but twist the same wisp and it becomes a thread. Cornflour and water dry into a film that cracks when folded, until a little glycerine is added. All of these are polymers, long chains of small pieces, and what matters is how the chains are arranged and how they are held to each other. Your child tears, twists, spins and cooks to find out, and follows the idea to Kevlar, to the rice they chew, and to the proteins that every cell builds.

**Intro.** *Take a sheet of newspaper. Tear it from top to bottom. Now tear another sheet from side to side. One tear runs as straight as a ruler. The other wanders about. It is the same kind of paper, and you tore it the same way. So what is different?* Paper is a mat of tiny plant fibres. When it is made, most of the fibres end up lying in one direction. A tear finds it easy to go between lined-up fibres, so it runs straight. Across them, the tear has to break fibres and wanders. In this unit you find out why lined-up fibres matter, and what the fibres are made of. You twist cotton into thread, cook a plastic-like film from cornflour, and heat plastic strips in hot water. You meet the chemist who found a fibre stronger than steel in a bowl of cloudy liquid.

## Terms
- **Monomer**: One small piece that can join to others to make a chain. Mono means one. *e.g.* Glucose, a sugar, is the monomer of starch and cellulose.
- **Polymer**: A very long molecule made of many monomers joined in a chain. Poly means many. *e.g.* Cotton, hair, rubber and a plastic carry bag are all made of polymers.
- **Polymerisation**: The joining of monomers, one after another, to make a polymer. *e.g.* A cotton plant joins glucose pieces into cellulose, and a factory joins ethylene pieces into polythene.
- **Cellulose**: A polymer made of glucose pieces joined into long straight chains. Plants build their walls and fibres from it. *e.g.* Cotton thread and paper are mostly cellulose.
- **Starch**: A polymer made of the same glucose pieces as cellulose, but joined so the chains coil and branch. Plants store food as starch. *e.g.* Rice, potato and cornflour are mostly starch.
- **Protein**: A polymer made of pieces called amino acids joined in a chain. Every cell builds proteins. *e.g.* Hair, wool and silk are made of protein.
- **Fibre**: A very thin, thread-like piece of material, much longer than it is wide. *e.g.* A cotton fibre is about as long as your thumb joint. A silk fibre can be hundreds of metres long.
- **Yarn**: A long thread made by twisting fibres together. *e.g.* The thread in a sari or a cotton towel is yarn.
- **Spinning**: Twisting loose fibres into a yarn. *e.g.* A takli, a charkha and a coir wheel are all used for spinning.
- **Plasticiser**: A small molecule that sits between polymer chains and lets them slide past each other, so the material bends more easily. *e.g.* Glycerine makes a cornflour film bend instead of crack.
- **Thermoplastic**: A plastic that softens when heated and hardens again when cooled, so it can be melted and shaped again. *e.g.* A polythene bag and a PET water bottle are thermoplastics.
- **Thermoset**: A plastic whose chains are tied to each other in many places during making. Once set, it does not melt again, and it burns or cracks if heated too much. *e.g.* The black handle of a pressure cooker is often a thermoset.
- **Cross-link**: A tie that joins one polymer chain to another. *e.g.* Sulfur makes cross-links in tyre rubber.

## Explanations
### How a polymer is built: monomers joined in a chain · *defined*
**Definition:** A polymer is a very long molecule made of many small pieces, called monomers, joined one after another. Making the chain is called polymerisation.

A chain of paper clips is a good picture. One clip is a monomer. Link thousands together and you have a polymer. In a real polymer, each link is a strong chemical bond between atoms. A polythene bag is made of ethylene monomers joined thousands of times. A PET bottle is made of two kinds of monomer, joined in turn, one kind then the other. Living things build polymers too. A cotton plant joins glucose pieces into cellulose. Every cell in your body joins amino acids into proteins, in the order written in your genes. A chain in a plastic often has ten thousand pieces or more. If each piece were a paper clip, one chain would be about three hundred metres long.
- e.g. Ethylene monomers joined into polythene, the plastic of a carry bag.
- e.g. Amino acid monomers joined into silk, wool and hair.
- e.g. Glucose monomers joined into starch in rice and cellulose in cotton.
### Same pieces, different chains: starch and cellulose · *defined*
**Definition:** Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. The pieces are joined in a slightly different way, so starch chains coil and cellulose chains lie straight.

Rice and cotton are both made of glucose chains. The difference is in the join between one glucose and the next. In starch the join makes the chain curl into a coil, and a coil is easy to pull apart. In cellulose the join keeps the chain straight, so many chains can lie side by side and hold each other tightly. That is why cotton makes a strong fibre and rice makes soft food. Your saliva and your gut have enzymes, which are tools made by cells, that cut the starch joins. When you chew rice for a while, the chains are cut into small sugars, and the rice tastes sweeter. You have no enzyme for the cellulose joins, so you cannot digest cotton. A cow can eat grass because microbes in her stomach cut cellulose for her.
- e.g. Chewed rice tastes sweeter because saliva cuts starch chains into small sugars.
- e.g. Cotton cannot be digested by people, because we have no tool to cut cellulose.
- e.g. Termites and cows depend on microbes to digest cellulose.
### Lined-up chains make a material strong in one direction · *defined*
**Definition:** A material made of chains or fibres is strongest along the direction they line up in, and weakest across it.

Pulling along a chain pulls on strong bonds. Pulling across chains only pulls on the weak holds between neighbours. Newspaper has a grain because most of its fibres lie one way, so it tears straight along the grain. In a factory, plastics are often stretched on purpose, so that the chains line up. A stretched nylon thread is much stronger than the same nylon before it is stretched. Stephanie Kwolek found that her stiff polymer chains lined up by themselves in a liquid, and the fibre she spun was stronger than steel for the same weight. Bone and wood are the same: strong along the grain, easy to split along it.
- e.g. Newspaper tears straight along its grain.
- e.g. Wood splits easily along the grain and is hard to snap across.
- e.g. Nylon thread is stretched in the factory so the chains line up.
### Short fibres, long thread: why twist holds · *defined*
**Definition:** A yarn is made of short fibres twisted together. Twisting presses the fibres against each other, and the grip between them holds the yarn together.

A cotton fibre is only about two or three centimetres long. A cotton thread can be many kilometres long. No fibre runs from one end of the thread to the other. Each fibre overlaps the ones next to it. When you pull the yarn, the fibres try to slide past each other. The twist squeezes them together, so they grip, and the harder you pull, the harder they grip. Too little twist and the fibres slide out. Too much twist and the yarn kinks and hurts itself. Silk is different. A silkworm spins one fibre hundreds of metres long, so silk needs far less twist. A rope breaks where its twist has been worn loose, because the fibres slide out.
- e.g. Cotton yarn: short fibres, twisted.
- e.g. Coir rope: coconut fibres a hand long, twisted into a rope that holds a boat.
- e.g. Silk: one very long fibre from a cocoon.
### Water, heat and plasticisers loosen the holds between chains · *defined*
**Definition:** Chains in a material are held to each other by weak ties. Water, heat or a plasticiser such as glycerine loosen the ties, so the chains slide and the material bends.

In hair, wool and cotton, chains are held side by side by weak ties called hydrogen bonds. Water gets between the chains and loosens the ties. That is why a curl stretches when wet, and why damp cotton thread bends easily. In a dry cornflour film, the starch chains stick tightly to each other, so the film is stiff and cracks when you fold it. Glycerine is a small molecule that sits between the chains and keeps them a little apart. Now the chains can slide, and the film bends. Heat does the same thing. Warm chains wiggle more, and holds between them break and form again. That is why a thermoplastic softens in heat. Plastic makers add a lot of plasticiser to PVC to make soft hose, and leave it out to make stiff pipe.
- e.g. A wet curl stretches and springs back when dry.
- e.g. Glycerine turns a stiff starch film into a bendy one.
- e.g. A soft PVC garden hose has plasticiser added, and a stiff PVC pipe is the same polymer without it.
### Thermoplastics and thermosets: tied loosely or tied everywhere · *defined*
**Definition:** A thermoplastic has chains that are free to slide when heated, so it softens and can be shaped again. A thermoset has chains tied to each other in many places, so it does not melt.

A thermoplastic is like a bowl of cooked noodles. Warm it and the noodles slide. Cool it and they hold their new shape. Polythene, PET and polystyrene are thermoplastics, and so they can be melted and recycled. A thermoset is like a fishing net. The strands are tied at every crossing, so heat cannot make them slide. Leo Baekeland made the first fully man-made plastic, Bakelite, in 1907, and it was a thermoset. Pressure cooker handles are often made of a similar kind of plastic, because they must not soften on the stove. A thermoset cannot be melted and moulded again. Heated too much, it chars. Tyre rubber is a lightly tied thermoset. Ties placed here and there keep it stretchy, and many ties make it hard.
- e.g. A PET bottle is a thermoplastic and can be melted and reshaped.
- e.g. A black pressure cooker handle is a thermoset and does not soften on the stove.
- e.g. Tyre rubber is tied by cross-links made with sulfur.
### Is a pressure cooker handle one giant molecule? · *debated*
In a thermoset, the strong ties run through the whole piece. Every atom is joined to the others by strong bonds. If you trace a path along the bonds, you can reach almost any atom from any other. So some chemists say that a thermoset handle is one single giant molecule. Others say that a molecule is a particular group of atoms with a particular size, and a net of unknown size is not one. Nobody has to settle this to use the handle, but it shows how a word like molecule gets stretched when we look closely.
- e.g. A cooker handle made of a thermoset.
- e.g. A vulcanised rubber band, where chains are tied together in a loose net.
- **Some chemists:** Yes. All the atoms are joined by strong bonds into one connected piece, so it is one giant molecule.
- **Other chemists:** No. A molecule means a group of atoms with a set size and shape. A net that could be any size is a network, not a molecule.

## Mostly agreed
- Plastics, rubber, cotton, silk, wool, hair and starch are all polymers: long chains of small pieces joined end to end. *How we know:* Chemists can measure the length of chains and can join monomers into chains in the lab, as Wallace Carothers did when he made nylon in the 1930s.
- Starch and cellulose are made of the same sugar, glucose, joined in different ways. *How we know:* The shapes of both molecules have been worked out by X-rays and chemistry, and enzymes that cut starch do not cut cellulose.
- A material made of chains is strongest along the direction the chains are lined up in. *How we know:* Paper tears straight along its grain, wood splits along its grain, and nylon and polythene become stronger when they are stretched in the factory.
- Twisting short fibres together holds them in a yarn, because the twist makes the fibres grip each other. *How we know:* Yarn made of fibres of a few centimetres can be many kilometres long, and a yarn untwisted comes apart into loose fibres.
- A thermoplastic softens in heat and can be reshaped, and a thermoset does not. *How we know:* Bottles can be melted and recycled, while Bakelite and similar plastics burn or crack when heated too much.

## How we found out
**Stephanie Kwolek, a chemist at DuPont, with Paul Morgan, who worked with her on making the polymer, and Charles Smullen, the technician who spun it, 1965.** In 1965, Stephanie Kwolek was a chemist at a company called DuPont in the United States. She was looking for a very light, very strong fibre that could be used inside car tyres. She made a new polymer with very stiff chains and dissolved it. Chemists expected a thick, clear syrup. Hers was thin, cloudy and runny, and most chemists would have thrown it away. She asked a technician to spin it into a fibre. He worried the cloudy liquid would clog his machine, but he agreed. The fibre was stiff and very strong. She tested it again and again, because she could hardly believe the numbers. The cloud was a sign that the stiff chains had lined up side by side in the liquid. Spun into a fibre, the lined-up chains made a fibre stronger than steel for the same weight. This fibre is called Kevlar.  
*What people believed before:* Chemists thought that a good polymer solution should be clear and thick, and that a cloudy, runny one was a failure. Kwolek spun the cloudy liquid anyway and showed that the cloud was a sign of lined-up chains, which make a very strong fibre.

## Challenging perspectives
- **A long, strong thread must have long, strong fibres that run from one end to the other.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: A rope looks like it must be made of fibres as long as the rope.  
  Against: Cotton fibres are two or three centimetres long, and cotton yarn can be kilometres long. Twist makes the short fibres grip each other.
- **Pulling on a plastic only damages it.** (Many people.; *rejected*)  
  For: A plastic bag stretches and then tears.  
  Against: Makers stretch nylon and polythene on purpose, because stretching lines up the chains and makes the plastic stronger along the stretch.
- **Natural materials are always better than plastics.** (Many people, and many shoppers.; *debated*)  
  For: Cotton, wood and paper can rot back into soil, and plastic bags can stay in the environment for a very long time.  
  Against: Cotton needs a lot of water to grow. Some people are allergic to natural rubber. Plastic keeps hospitals clean and food fresh. Natural and made are not the same as good and bad.
- **A plastic sold as biodegradable breaks down quickly wherever it ends up.** (Some makers and shoppers.; *debated*)  
  For: Some plastics do break down in a compost heap that is kept hot.  
  Against: In one study, some bags sold as biodegradable could still carry shopping after three years in soil. How fast a plastic breaks down depends on where it is.
- **A stronger chemical bond always means a stronger material.** (Some students.; *rejected*)  
  For: Strong bonds hold the atoms of a chain together.  
  Against: A cotton thread, a tyre and a spider's silk are held together by the holds between chains and fibres. How chains are arranged and tied matters as much as the strength of a bond.

## Roots
We have torn, twisted, cooked and heated polymers. Now we ask what a thread made of short pieces tells us about wholes, and about ourselves.

**Deep truth.** A cotton thread is far longer than any cotton fibre in it. Twisted, short fibres grip each other and hold a load together. So the thread's strength is not in any one fibre, but in the way many pieces hold each other.  
*Habit it breaks:* We picture a long, strong thing as one long, strong piece, like a rod. A thread, a rope and a sheet of paper are not like that, even though they look like one piece.  
*Evidence:* In the Wisp and Thread activity, a loose wisp of cotton came apart in your fingers. The same cotton, twisted, held together as a thread. A single fibre from it was only a centimetre or two long.

- *who am i:* Your hair, nails and skin are made of protein chains that your body joined from the food you ate. You are made of short pieces that hold on to each other, just as a thread is.
- *what is life:* Every cell joins amino acids into protein chains, one piece at a time, in the order written in the genes. Making long chains out of short pieces is part of what living things do.
- *what is this world:* Rope, cloth, paper, wood and bone are all long, strong things made of short pieces holding on. At every size, from chains to fibres to yarn to rope, the holding is done by pieces that are short.

A cotton plant grows short fibres. A spinner twists many of them into a thread much longer than any one fibre, and a weaver crosses threads into cloth. At each step, nothing new is added. The same small pieces are held together in a new way. What they can do together is more than any one of them could do alone.

- **Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 3.6 and 3.8:** The thinker Gargi asks Yajnavalkya what the world is woven on, as a cloth is woven from warp and weft. He answers step by step, and each answer is woven on the next. (The Upanishad pictures the world as woven cloth, where each thread is held by the others. In a thread, each fibre is held by its neighbours, and nothing outside holds the whole thing up.)
- **Kabir, the poem Jhini Jhini Bini Chadariya (15th century, Varanasi):** Kabir was a weaver. In this poem he sings of the body as a fine cloth woven on a spinning wheel, and asks who wove it. (Kabir used the work of his own hands to ask about life. A cloth is short fibres twisted into thread and crossed into cloth, and a body is made of small parts held together too.)
- Science (settled): Materials are built in layers. Chains pack into fibrils, fibrils into fibres, fibres into yarn, and yarn into cloth. Wood and bone are built this way too, and strength comes from every layer.
- Science (open): Living things build strong materials from simple foods at room temperature. Scientists would like to design materials that build themselves in the same way, but this is still far from done.
- Other view, Nagasena, a Buddhist teacher, in the Milindapanha (The Questions of King Milinda): A chariot is only a name for its wheels, axle, frame and pole put together. There is no chariot apart from the parts. In the same way, a thread is only a name for fibres with a twist, and there is nothing more to find.
- Practice, Twist and hold (3 min): Take two thin threads and hold them side by side. Pull one gently, and feel it slide. Now twist the two together and pull again. Breathe in as you twist. Breathe out as you pull.
- Ask: If no fibre runs from one end of a thread to the other, where does the thread begin and end?
- Ask: You are made of short pieces that hold on to each other. Where do you begin and end?
- Ask: What other long, strong things are made of short, weak things holding on?

## Story: The Rope That Came Apart
*A made-up story. Coir spinning near Alappuzha in Kerala is real, and it is mostly done by women. The coir fibres, the twist of yarn and strand in opposite directions, and a rope wearing out where it rubs are all real. Anitha, her family and the storm are imagined.*

Anitha lived beside a canal near Alappuzha, in Kerala, where the backwaters wind between coconut palms. At the bottom of her steps, a small wooden boat called a vallam was tied to a post. Her grandmother, Ammumma, spun coir for a living, as many women in the village did.

In the monsoon, a storm came in the night. The wind pushed the palms flat, and the canal heaved. In the morning, the post stood empty. The rope was gone, and so was the vallam.

Her uncle Shaji found the boat two hours later. It was stuck in the reeds at the edge of a paddy field, dented but whole. He carried the rope home in his hand, and held up the broken end.

'Old rope,' he said. 'It was bound to go one day.'

Anitha took the broken end to the veranda. It did not end in a neat cut. It ended in a brown brush of rough hairs. She pulled one out and held it in front of her face. It was as long as her hand. She pulled it between her fingers, and it snapped.

'How can this hold a boat?' she said. 'One hair breaks if I just pull.'

Ammumma was sitting at her spinning wheel. 'Come here, and I will show you,' she said.

She took a handful of loose coconut husk fibres and laid them in a line on Anitha's palm. 'Pull,' she said. Anitha pulled one end. The fibres slid apart like hair coming out of a comb.

Then Ammumma hooked a few fibres to the wheel. She turned the handle with one hand, and walked backwards, feeding more fibres with the other. A thin brown thread grew out of her fingers. It grew longer, and longer, until it reached the far wall of the veranda.

'Now pull,' she said.

Anitha pulled the thread. It did not come apart. She pulled harder. It held.

'It is only twist,' said Ammumma. 'The twist squeezes the fibres together. Each one grips its neighbours. And the harder you pull, the harder they grip.'

Anitha looked at the long thread, and at the tiny hairs in her hand. 'But the thread is as long as the wall, and each fibre is only as long as my hand. Where does one fibre go?'

'It stops,' said Ammumma, 'and another starts next to it. No single fibre goes the whole way. They are all short, and they hold each other.' She smiled. 'A rope is not one long thing. It is a great many short things that hold on.'

Anitha ran her thumb along the broken rope. Near the break, the rope was smooth and shiny. Further along, it was rough and tight. She frowned, and called her uncle.

'Uncle Shaji, where did the rope touch the step?'

He thought. 'It rubbed on the stone, right there, all season.'

'Look.' She held up the shiny part. 'The twist is loose here. The fibres have been rubbed until they slide.' She pulled one fibre out of the shiny part, and it slid out easily. Then she tried the same on the rough part, and the fibre would not move. 'The fibres did not break. They slid out. The rope did not fail where it was weak. It failed where the holding was gone.'

Uncle Shaji looked at the rope for a long time. Then he laughed. 'My niece is teaching me about rope.'

'Ammumma taught me,' said Anitha.

That afternoon, the three of them fixed it. Uncle Shaji cut off the worn end and tied the new end to the post. Anitha wrapped an old sack around the place where the rope would touch the stone step. Ammumma checked every handspan of the rope with her fingers, pressing to feel any place where the twist was loose.

At school, Anitha's teacher had told her that every plant fibre is made of chains of tiny pieces, too small to see. Anitha thought about that on the veranda. Chains twisted into fibres. Fibres twisted into yarn. Yarn twisted into strands. Strands twisted into a rope. At every size, the holding was done by pieces that were short, and gripped the ones beside them.

The next storm came a week later. Anitha stood on the steps with a torch. The wind pulled at the vallam, and the vallam pulled at the rope. The rope went tight, and it held.

'It is only twist,' said Anitha, to nobody. And she went in to dry her hair.

**For the parent:** For the parent: a rope, a cotton thread and a yarn are made of short fibres that overlap. Twist makes them grip each other, so the thread is longer and stronger than any fibre in it. A rope fails where the twist is worn loose, because the fibres slide out, rather than because the fibres break.

- Ask: Why did Anitha's one fibre snap, but the whole thread hold?
- Ask: Where did the rope fail, and why did it fail there?
- Ask: No fibre runs the whole length of the rope. So how can the rope be one strong thing?
- Ask: What do you think would have happened if Ammumma had not twisted the fibres?

### e1 · Cornflour Film: what does glycerine do? (53 min, 3 days)
- **question:** Three films are cooked from the same cornflour mix, with none, one or two teaspoons of glycerine. Which film bends the most before it cracks?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which film will fold the most times before it cracks, and which will crack first? Why do you think so?

- **safety:** An adult does the cooking. The mix gets hot and sticky, like hot custard, so keep children back from the stove. Do not taste the films.
- **record:** For each film, write the glycerine amount, the number of folds before it cracked on each try, and how far it stretched. Draw a bar chart with one bar for each film. Write three lines: what I thought, what I saw, and why.
- **results table:** Film | Glycerine added (teaspoons) | Folds before it cracks: try 1 | Folds before it cracks: try 2 | Stretch before it tears (cm)

- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the cornflour, the vinegar and the glycerine. Say you will make three films with none, one and two teaspoons of glycerine. Write down their guess and reason.

  - Ask: Why do you think that film will bend the most? (good: It has the most glycerine.; The glycerine makes it soft.; The glycerine gets between the starch.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Write each guess and reason at the top of the results table. Glycerine is sold at chemist shops, often as glycerin.
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**

  *Parent says:* Stir the mix until it is smooth. An adult heats it on low and stirs all the time. After about four minutes it turns clear and thick, like custard. Then switch off the heat.  
  *Child mode:* Stir the mix in the pan. A grown-up heats it and stirs.
  - Note: Do not leave the pan. If it is still milky after five minutes, keep stirring a little longer. If lumps form, beat quickly with a fork. Let it cool for two minutes before the next step. The mix is hot and sticky, like hot custard.
  - Check: Why do we cook the mix before we make the films? → **Heat makes the starch swell with water, and the mix becomes a clear gel**

   **Answer:** One small piece that can join with others in a chain. *Why:* Mono means one. A monomer is one small piece. Many monomers joined in a chain make a polymer.

   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Hair is a protein chain that your body makes. Polythene is a chain of ethylene pieces joined in a factory. Both are very long chains of small pieces.

   **Answer:** ['polymer']. *Why:* Poly means many. A polymer is many pieces joined in a chain.

   **Answer:** Cotton thread → a cotton plant; Silk → a silkworm; Polythene bag → a factory; Hair → your own body. *Why:* Cotton plants and silkworms build their polymers in their own bodies. Factories join ethylene pieces to make polythene. Your body builds hair from food.

   **Answer:** wool → Made by a living being; nylon → Made in a factory; rice starch → Made by a living being; PET bottle → Made in a factory; paper → Made by a living being; thermocol foam → Made in a factory. *Why:* Wool, starch and the cellulose in paper are built by living things.    Options: Most of its fibres line up in that direction / It is thicker in that direction / It is wetter in that direction

   **Answer:** 20000. *Why:* 400 metres is 40,000 centimetres. 40,000 divided by 2 is 20,000. A real thread needs far more fibres than this, because the fibres overlap.

9. (odd) Which is the odd one out, and why? Cotton, silk, steel, wool.  
   **Answer:** Steel, because it is a metal. The others are polymers.. *Why:* Cotton is cellulose, silk and wool are proteins. Steel is metal atoms packed together, not chains.

   **Answer:** The new film bends more, because glycerine sits between the chains and lets them slide. *Why:* A plasticiser is a small molecule that sits between chains and keeps them a little apart. The chains can then slide, and the film bends.
11. (truefalse) A thermoset can be melted and moulded again, just like a thermoplastic.

13. (perspective) Lakshmi and Farhan are choosing a shirt for hot, rainy Chennai days.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a side, gives a reason, and says what to test or look up, such as how fast each dries or how much water each needs.. *Why:* Both have a point. A made material is not good or bad because of where it came from. It depends on what it is used for, and on what it costs to make.
14. (fill) Starch and cellulose are both made of the same sugar, called ____.

## Open questions
- **Can we make plastics that mend their own scratches and cracks?** Scientists have made polymers that mend themselves in the lab, but most do not yet last long enough or cost little enough to be used every day.
- **How do wood, bone and tendon line up their chains so well, and can people copy it?** Living things build strong materials at room temperature from water and simple foods. Factories still need heat and oil to make anything as strong.
- **How much plastic is in the sea, and where does it go over time?** Plastic has existed for only about a hundred years. Estimates of how much is in the sea differ a lot, and nobody has followed a piece for its whole life.
- **Is a thermoset such as a cooker handle one giant molecule?** The answer depends on what we mean by a molecule, and chemists do not all agree on that.

Would a real thread need more or fewer, and why? **250 fibres. A real thread needs more.**. 5 metres is 500 centimetres. 500 divided by 2 is 250. Real fibres overlap, so many more are needed to cover the same length. (queues `math.length.kilometres-and-conversions.L4`)
- These are made-up results from three cornflour films. The best film folded how many times as many as the worst film? Which film would you choose for a bag, and why? **7 times as many. The 1 teaspoon film.**. 14 divided by 2 is 7. The best film folded 7 times as many as the worst. The film with 1 teaspoon bends the most and is probably not too sticky, but you would test it. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- The recipe for three films uses 3 tablespoons of cornflour and 12 tablespoons of water. A class wants to make 12 films from the same recipe. How many tablespoons of cornflour and of water do they need? **12 tablespoons of cornflour and 48 tablespoons of water.**. 12 films is 4 times as many as 3 films. 3 times 4 is 12 tablespoons of cornflour. 12 times 4 is 48 tablespoons of water. (queues `math.multiplication.multiplication-facts.L4`)
- In the Hot Water test, one strip was 8 centimetres long and shrank to 6 centimetres. Another strip was 10 centimetres long and shrank to 5 centimetres. What fraction of its length did each strip lose? Which shrank by the bigger fraction? **The first lost one quarter. The second lost one half. The second shrank by the bigger fraction.**. The first lost 2 centimetres out of 8, which is 2 over 8, or one quarter. The second lost 5 out of 10, which is one half. One half is bigger than one quarter. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- In the Wisp and Thread activity, a child spun a pencil 30 times for a thread 10 centimetres long. How many turns is that for each centimetre? If the thread is 25 centimetres long, how many turns would give the same twist? **3 turns for each centimetre. 75 turns.**. 30 divided by 10 is 3 turns for each centimetre. 25 centimetres times 3 turns is 75 turns. Turns for each centimetre is a fair way to compare twist. (queues `math.division.L4`)

## Warm-up
- What is a polymer? → A very long molecule made of many small pieces joined in a chain.
- What is a monomer? → One small piece that can join others in a chain.
- Why does newspaper tear straight one way? → Most of its fibres line up in that direction.
- What holds a cotton thread together when no fibre runs its whole length? → Twist makes the overlapping fibres grip each other.
- What does glycerine do in a cornflour film? → It sits between the chains so they can slide, and the film bends.
- What is the difference between a thermoplastic and a thermoset? → A thermoplastic softens in heat. A thermoset has tied chains and does not.
- Why does chewed rice taste sweet? → Saliva cuts the starch chains into small sugars.
- Why do makers stretch nylon thread? → Stretching lines up the chains and makes the thread stronger.
