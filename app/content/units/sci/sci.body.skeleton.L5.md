# Muscles in pairs, joints, and skeletons of shell and water: how muscles that can only pull move a body
`sci.body.skeleton.L5`

**Summary.** A muscle can only pull. It can never push. So how do you push your tongue out of your mouth? In this lesson you will roll a dough worm to find how much longer a body gets when it is squeezed thinner. You will find the partner of each muscle, hunt for six kinds of joint, and measure how high each finger can lift alone. Then you will see how a crab moves inside armour, and how an earthworm moves with a skeleton made of water.

**Intro.** *Bend your arm. A muscle pulled it. Now stick out your tongue. No muscle can push. So what moved your tongue forward, out of your mouth?* Every muscle in every animal works in one way. It gets shorter, and so it pulls. It cannot push, and it cannot make itself long again. Your arm has bones for muscles to pull on. Your tongue has no bone at all, and neither has an earthworm. Yet a tongue can reach out and an earthworm can stretch forward. By the end of this lesson you will be able to say exactly how they do it.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can explain why a muscle can only pull, using the sliding threads inside a muscle fibre.
1. I can explain why muscles work in antagonistic pairs, and find the partner of a muscle on my own body.
2. I can name six kinds of moving joint, find each on my body, and say how each one moves.
3. I can say what cartilage, synovial fluid, ligaments and tendons each do.
4. I can explain how an animal with an exoskeleton moves and grows, and what moulting costs.
5. I can explain how a hydrostatic skeleton works, and why a body that gets thinner must get longer.
6. I can explain how a tongue, an elephant's trunk and an octopus's arm move with no bone.
7. I can run a fair test, and say what I thought, what I saw and why I think it happened.

## Terms
- **Contract**: To get shorter. When a muscle contracts, it pulls on whatever it is joined to. *e.g.* Your biceps contracts when you lift a tumbler to your mouth.
- **Antagonistic pair**: Two muscles that pull in opposite ways across the same joint. Each one stretches the other back to its long shape. *e.g.* The biceps bends your elbow. The triceps straightens it. They are an antagonistic pair.
- **Flexor**: A muscle that bends a joint. *e.g.* The biceps is the flexor of the elbow.
- **Extensor**: A muscle that straightens a joint. *e.g.* The triceps is the extensor of the elbow.
- **Voluntary muscle**: A muscle that moves when you decide it should. *e.g.* The muscles in your fingers are voluntary. You decide to pick up a spoon, and they move.
- **Involuntary muscle**: A muscle that works by itself, without you deciding. *e.g.* Your heart is an involuntary muscle. It keeps beating while you sleep.
- **Muscle fibre**: One muscle cell. It is long and very thin, and a muscle is a bundle of thousands of them. *e.g.* Pull a thread from a piece of cooked chicken, or look at the strings in the flesh of a cooked fish. Each thread is a bundle of muscle fibres.
- **Actin and myosin**: Two kinds of protein thread inside every muscle fibre. Myosin threads grab actin threads and pull them along. *e.g.* When you make a fist, countless myosin threads in your forearm are pulling on actin threads.
- **ATP**: A small molecule that cells use to carry energy from food to wherever work is done. Its full name is adenosine triphosphate. *e.g.* A myosin thread uses one ATP each time it lets go of actin and reaches for a new grip.
- **Tendon**: A tough cord that joins a muscle to a bone. *e.g.* Press the back of your hand and wiggle your fingers. The cords that move under the skin are tendons.
- **Ligament**: A tough band that joins one bone to another bone at a joint. *e.g.* Ligaments at the sides of your knee stop it from bending sideways.
- **Cartilage**: A smooth, firm, slightly springy material that covers the ends of bones in a joint. *e.g.* The shiny white cap on the end of a chicken leg bone is cartilage. The tip of your nose is cartilage too.
- **Synovial fluid**: A slippery liquid inside a moving joint. It lets the cartilage surfaces slide and it carries food to the cartilage. *e.g.* A healthy knee holds only a little synovial fluid, and that is enough to keep the joint sliding.
- **Pivot joint**: A joint where one bone turns round, like a key in a lock. *e.g.* The joint at the top of your neck lets your head turn to say no.
- **Saddle joint**: A joint where two bone ends are each shaped like a saddle and sit across each other. It bends two ways. *e.g.* The joint at the base of your thumb is a saddle joint. It lets your thumb touch each fingertip.
- **Exoskeleton**: A skeleton on the outside of the body. The muscles are inside it. *e.g.* A crab, a prawn, an ant and a cockroach each have an exoskeleton.
- **Chitin**: A tough, light material that insects, spiders, crabs and prawns make. Their exoskeleton is built mostly from chitin. *e.g.* The thin clear shell you peel off a prawn is mostly chitin.
- **Moulting**: Shedding an old exoskeleton and growing a larger one. An animal in armour can grow only by moulting. *e.g.* The empty brown skin of a cicada on a tree trunk was left behind after a moult.
- **Hydrostatic skeleton**: A skeleton made of liquid shut inside the body. Muscles squeeze the liquid, and the liquid pushes back. *e.g.* An earthworm has a hydrostatic skeleton. Each segment of their body is a small bag of liquid wrapped in muscle.
- **Muscular hydrostat**: A body part made almost entirely of muscle, with no bone and no space of liquid. The muscle itself is the skeleton. *e.g.* Your tongue, an elephant's trunk and an octopus's arm are muscular hydrostats.
- **Peristalsis**: A wave of squeezing that travels along a body or a tube. *e.g.* An earthworm moves forward by peristalsis. Your food pipe moves food down by peristalsis too.

## Explanations
### A muscle can only pull · *defined*
**Definition:** A muscle works by contracting, which means getting shorter. A muscle that gets shorter pulls. No muscle can push, and no muscle can make itself long again.

This is true of every muscle in every animal, from an ant's leg to an elephant's trunk. The reason is inside the muscle. A muscle is made of threads that slide past each other to make it shorter. The threads can pull themselves together. They have no way to push themselves apart. So after a muscle has contracted, something else must pull it long again. That something is usually another muscle. It can also be gravity, a springy band, or liquid under pressure. The rest of this lesson is about the different ways bodies solve this one problem.
- e.g. Your biceps pulls your forearm up. It cannot push the forearm down again.
- e.g. A rope works the same way. You can pull a bucket up from a well with a rope, but you cannot push the bucket down with the rope.
- e.g. Your heart is a muscle. It squeezes blood out by getting shorter all round. Blood coming back in stretches it again.
### Muscles you choose and muscles that work alone · *defined*
**Definition:** A voluntary muscle moves when you decide it should. An involuntary muscle works without you deciding.

Your body has about six hundred named muscles. You choose to move some of them. These are voluntary muscles, such as the muscles of your hands, feet and fingers. You decide to pick up a pencil, and your fingers do it. Other muscles work by themselves, all day and all night. These are involuntary muscles. Your heart is one. It keeps squeezing while you sleep. The muscles in the walls of your intestines are another. After you eat a roti, they squeeze it slowly along, and you never have to ask them. Both kinds can only pull, and both are made of the same sliding threads.
- e.g. Voluntary: the muscles that move your fingers when you write, and your feet when you walk to school.
- e.g. Involuntary: your heart beating, and your intestines squeezing food along.
### Inside a muscle: threads that slide · *defined*
**Definition:** A muscle fibre is packed with two kinds of protein thread, called actin and myosin. A muscle contracts when the myosin threads pull the actin threads past them. The threads do not shrink. They slide.

Each myosin thread has many tiny arms. An arm grabs the actin thread beside it and bends, which drags the actin along a tiny distance. Then the arm lets go, reaches forward and grabs again. Millions of arms do this many times a second, and the whole fibre gets shorter. The energy comes from ATP, a small molecule that every cell makes from food. Here is a surprise. The arm needs ATP to let go, not only to pull. If ATP runs out, the arms stay locked on. That is why the body of an animal goes stiff a few hours after death. The muscles have no ATP left, so they cannot let go. The stiffness passes later, when the threads themselves begin to break down. Muscle work also gives off heat. That is why you feel warm after running, and why you shiver when you are cold. Shivering is muscles contracting quickly to make heat.
- e.g. When a muscle contracts, the actin and myosin threads overlap more. Each thread stays the same length.
- e.g. Letting go costs energy. A myosin arm cannot release its grip until a fresh ATP arrives.
- e.g. Gerty Cori and Carl Cori worked out how muscles and the liver pass fuel between them. In 1947 Gerty Cori became the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
### Antagonistic pairs: every muscle needs a partner · *defined*
**Definition:** An antagonistic pair is two muscles that pull in opposite ways across the same joint. When one contracts, it moves the bone and also stretches the other back to its long shape.

The flexor bends the joint and the extensor straightens it. At the elbow, the flexor is the biceps and the extensor is the triceps. At the knee, the muscles on the front of the thigh straighten the leg and the muscles on the back of the thigh bend it. The partners do not simply take turns. When your brain tells the biceps to pull, nerve cells in your spinal cord tell the triceps to relax at the same moment. If both pull at once, the joint goes stiff. You do this on purpose when you hold your arm rigid. Sometimes the partner is not a muscle. When you lower a full tumbler slowly, gravity is straightening your elbow. Your biceps is still working, letting the arm down a little at a time. So a muscle can be working while it is getting longer.
- e.g. Elbow: the biceps bends it, and the triceps straightens it.
- e.g. Knee: the front thigh muscles straighten it, and the back thigh muscles bend it.
- e.g. Ankle: the calf muscle points the foot down, and the shin muscle lifts the foot up.
- e.g. Lowering a bucket into a well slowly: the same arm muscles that lift it are working to let it down.
### Counting your bones: the longest and the smallest · *defined*
**Definition:** An adult skeleton has 206 bones. A baby is born with more, and some join together as the child grows.

The longest bone in your body is the thigh bone. It runs from your hip down to your knee. The smallest bones are deep inside your ears. Each ear has three tiny ones, and the smallest of them is smaller than a grain of rice. When a sound shakes your eardrum, these bones pass the shaking inward. That is how you hear a bell or a friend's voice. Bones meet at joints, and joints are where your body bends.
- e.g. The thigh bone is the longest bone. Put your hand on the top of your leg and feel how long the bone under it must be.
- e.g. The smallest bone is in the ear, inside your head, where you cannot feel it at all.
### Six kinds of moving joint · *defined*
**Definition:** A joint is a place where two bones meet. The shape of the bone ends decides how the joint can move. There are six main kinds of moving joint.

A hinge joint bends one way and back, like a door. Your elbow, your knee and the joints in your fingers are hinges. A ball and socket joint has a ball on one bone in a cup on the other, so it can swing in a full circle. Your shoulder and your hip are of this kind. A pivot joint lets one bone turn round. One pivot at the top of your neck turns your head. Another near your elbow turns your palm up and down. A saddle joint bends two ways, and the base of your thumb has one. An ellipsoid joint is an oval knob in an oval cup. It bends two ways but cannot spin, and your wrist is one. A gliding joint has flat surfaces that slide a little, as between the small bones of your wrist and ankle. Some joints do not move at all. The plates of your skull are locked together by zigzag joints called sutures. There is a price for free movement. A joint that can swing every way, like the shoulder, has to be held in place mostly by muscles and ligaments, and not by the fit of the bones. So joints fall into two groups. Movable joints, such as the knee, elbow, wrist and fingers, bend or turn. Immovable joints, such as the ones between the plates of the skull, do not move at all.
- e.g. Hinge: elbow, knee, finger joints.
- e.g. Ball and socket: shoulder, hip.
- e.g. Pivot: turning the head, turning the palm.
- e.g. Saddle: base of the thumb.
- e.g. Ellipsoid: wrist, knuckles.
- e.g. Gliding: between the small bones of the wrist and the ankle.
### Inside a moving joint: cartilage, fluid and ligaments · *defined*
**Definition:** In a moving joint, the bone ends are capped with smooth cartilage. The joint is closed in a bag that holds slippery synovial fluid. Ligaments tie bone to bone.

Bone rubbing on bone would wear out quickly. Cartilage on cartilage, wetted with synovial fluid, slides with very little friction. Friction is the grip between two surfaces that rub. The fluid has a second job. Cartilage has no blood vessels of its own, so it takes its food from the fluid. This is one reason a damaged cartilage heals slowly and poorly. Ligaments are tough bands that hold the bones together and stop the joint from bending the wrong way. A sprain is a ligament that has been stretched too far or torn. Tendons, ligaments and cartilage all get their strength from a protein called collagen. Collagen is a rope of three strands twisted together. In 1954 G. N. Ramachandran and Gopinath Kartha, at the University of Madras, worked out this twisted rope of three strands. Their model, with later corrections, still stands.
- e.g. The white shiny cap on a chicken leg bone is cartilage.
- e.g. A twisted ankle hurts for weeks because a ligament has been overstretched.
- e.g. Collagen's rope of three strands was worked out in Madras, which is now Chennai, in 1954.
### Muscles can be far from the part they move · *defined*
**Definition:** A muscle does not have to sit beside the part it moves. A long tendon can carry its pull to a bone far away.

Your fingers hold almost no muscle. The muscles that bend and straighten them are in your forearm. Long tendons run from those muscles, through the wrist, to the finger bones. You can see the tendons move on the back of your hand. This keeps the fingers thin and light, so they can move quickly. It also means that some fingers are tied together. The tendons that straighten the middle, ring and little fingers are joined to each other by small bands on the back of the hand. And some of the muscles in the forearm send tendons to more than one finger. So your ring finger cannot move fully by itself. Scientists have found that these links in the hand are a main reason, and that the way the brain controls the muscles adds to it.
- e.g. Grip your forearm near the elbow and wiggle your fingers. You can feel the finger muscles working there.
- e.g. Lay your hand flat and try to lift only the ring finger. It lifts less than the others.
- e.g. The index finger and the little finger each have an extra muscle of their own for straightening. The ring finger has none.
### An exoskeleton: muscles inside a tube · *defined*
**Definition:** An animal with an exoskeleton has a hard skeleton on the outside, and the muscles are inside it. The muscles still work in antagonistic pairs.

Insects, spiders, crabs and prawns are arthropods. An arthropod is an animal with a hard outer covering and jointed legs. The covering is made mostly of chitin. In crabs and prawns it is hardened further with minerals from the sea. Each leg is a row of hard tubes. Between the tubes the covering is thin and soft, and that soft part is the joint. Inside each tube are a flexor muscle and an extensor muscle. They are fixed to the inside of the shell, often on flat plates that grow inwards from it. If your family eats crab, you may have pulled a flat white blade out of a claw. That blade is one of these plates, and the muscle that closes the claw was fixed to it. A hollow tube is a good shape for a small animal. It is light and stiff, and it is armour as well as skeleton.
- e.g. A crab's claw has a big muscle to close it and a small muscle to open it. Both are inside the claw.
- e.g. An ant's leg is a row of hard tubes with soft joints between them.
- e.g. The coconut crab is the largest arthropod living on land. A big one weighs about 4 kilograms. Coconut crabs have been recorded on India's Nicobar Islands.
### Growing inside armour: moulting · *defined*
**Definition:** A hard exoskeleton cannot grow. So an arthropod grows by moulting. The animal sheds the old exoskeleton and swells up before the new one hardens.

Before a moult, the animal grows a new soft exoskeleton under the old one. The old one splits, and the animal climbs out. Then they take in water or air and swell, so that the new covering hardens at a larger size. For hours or days the new covering is soft. In that time the animal cannot move well, because soft tubes bend when the muscles pull on them. The animal also has no armour. Many arthropods hide while this lasts. So an outside skeleton has a cost that an inside skeleton does not have. An animal with bones grows without ever losing the use of the skeleton.
- e.g. The empty cicada skins on tree trunks in the rainy season are old exoskeletons.
- e.g. A crab who has just moulted is soft all over and hides until the shell hardens.
- e.g. A caterpillar moults several times before becoming a pupa.
### A spider's leg: a muscle on one side, liquid pressure on the other · *defined*
**Definition:** At two of the main joints in a spider's leg there is a flexor muscle and no extensor muscle. The leg is straightened by the pressure of the spider's blood.

This was shown in 1959 by two scientists in Cambridge, D. A. Parry and R. H. J. Brown. They measured the pressure of the liquid inside spiders' legs and found that it rises when the legs straighten. So at these joints the partner of the flexor muscle is not another muscle. It is liquid, pushed into the leg by muscles in the front part of the spider's body. This explains something you may have seen. The legs of a dead spider are curled in. With no pressure in the liquid, nothing holds the legs out against the pull of the flexors. Later work has found that in some large spiders, springy parts of the joint help as well.
- e.g. Parry and Brown also argued that a small jumping spider leaps by a sudden rise of pressure in the back legs. Later studies of larger jumping spiders disagree about how much the leg muscles add.
- e.g. A dead spider has curled legs, because the pressure that held them straight is gone.
- e.g. Here the antagonistic pair is a muscle and a liquid.
### A hydrostatic skeleton: the earthworm · *defined*
**Definition:** A hydrostatic skeleton is liquid shut inside a body and wrapped in muscle. The liquid cannot be squeezed into less space, so when muscles squeeze it one way, it pushes out another way.

An earthworm's body is a row of ringed segments, and a large earthworm has more than a hundred of them. Thin walls separate the segments, and each one is a closed bag of liquid. Two sets of muscles wrap each segment. Circular muscles go round it like a ring, so they are also called ring muscles. Longitudinal muscles run along it from front to back, so they are also called lengthwise muscles. When the circular muscles contract, the segment gets thinner. The liquid has to go somewhere, so the segment gets longer. That lengthening stretches the longitudinal muscles. When the longitudinal muscles contract, the segment gets shorter and fatter, and that stretches the circular muscles. So the two sets are an antagonistic pair, and the liquid between them does the job that a bone does in your arm. To move, the worm sends a wave of thinning and fattening along their body. Tiny bristles on the fat segments grip the soil, so the thin segments can push forward. In the Dough Worm experiment you find how much longer a body gets when it is squeezed thinner.
- e.g. Circular muscles contract: the segment gets long and thin.
- e.g. Longitudinal muscles contract: the segment gets short and fat.
- e.g. Sea anemones, jellyfish and many worms also have hydrostatic skeletons.
- e.g. A caterpillar is an insect with a soft body. Their body is held firm by liquid under pressure.
### A tongue, a trunk and an octopus arm: muscle that is its own skeleton · *defined*
**Definition:** A muscular hydrostat is a body part made almost entirely of muscle. It has no bone and no bag of liquid. The muscle itself keeps the same volume, and so it acts as its own skeleton.

Muscle is mostly water, and water keeps its volume when it is squeezed. Volume is the amount of space a thing takes up. In a tongue, muscle fibres run in three directions: along it, across it, and from top to bottom. When the fibres that run across and from top to bottom contract, the tongue gets narrower and thinner. Its volume cannot change, so it must get longer. This is how you stick your tongue out. No muscle pushes it. Muscles pull it thin, and the thinning makes it long. A muscle from your chin to the tongue also pulls the back of the tongue forward. To pull the tongue in, the fibres that run along it contract. William Kier and Kathleen Smith described this in 1985. They showed that tongues, the arms and tentacles of octopuses and squid, and the trunks of elephants all work this way. A human tongue has eight muscles. An elephant's trunk has about 90,000 small bundles of muscle, counted in 2023 from scans of the trunk of a baby Asian elephant.
- e.g. Stick your tongue out: it becomes narrower and thinner as it gets longer.
- e.g. An elephant can pick up a single peanut or lift a log with the same trunk.
- e.g. An octopus can bend her arm at any point along it, because the arm has no bones to limit it.
### Thinner by half, longer by four · *defined*
**Definition:** When a body of fixed volume is squeezed to half its width, it does not get twice as long. It gets about four times as long.

You measure this yourself in the Dough Worm experiment. The reason is that a round body is narrow in two directions at once, from side to side and from top to bottom. Halving the width halves both. So the same amount of stuff has to spread along four times the length. This is why muscular hydrostats can be so quick. A small squeeze across gives a large movement along. A squid shoots out her two long tentacles to catch a prawn in a small fraction of a second, by squeezing them thinner. The rule is exact only for a perfect cylinder, which is a shape like a tumbler or a pipe. Real tongues and worms are close to it, but not exact.
- e.g. A dough roll 4 centimetres wide and 4 centimetres long becomes about 16 centimetres long when rolled to 2 centimetres wide.
- e.g. A tongue that gets only a little narrower and thinner reaches out a long way.
- e.g. It works backwards too. A worm segment that shortens a little gets much fatter, and grips the burrow wall.
### Water skeletons in plants · *defined*
**Definition:** Soft parts of a plant are held up by water pressing outward inside each cell. A plant with too little water wilts, because that pressure falls.

A plant cell is a small box with a firm wall, full of watery liquid. When the cell is full, the liquid presses on the wall, and the cell is stiff. Thousands of stiff cells make a leaf stand out flat. When the plant loses more water than their roots take in, the cells go slack and the leaf droops. Plants have no muscles, but some plants move by using this pressure. A touch-me-not plant folds their leaves when touched. At the base of each leaf is a small swelling. Cells on one side of the swelling let water out quickly, that side goes slack, and the leaf folds. So a plant can move by changing a water skeleton, with no muscle at all.
- e.g. Coriander left out on the kitchen counter droops. Stood in water, the stalks rise again.
- e.g. A touch-me-not plant folds their leaves in about a second.
- e.g. A crisp cucumber is crisp because the cells are full of water under pressure.
### Why are there no giant insects on land? · *debated*
The largest land arthropod alive, the coconut crab, weighs about 4 kilograms. The largest insects weigh far less. Nobody has a single agreed answer for why. Several ideas are being tested, and more than one may be true.
- e.g. About 300 million years ago there were insects like giant dragonflies, with wings about 70 centimetres from tip to tip.
- e.g. In the sea, where water holds a body up, crabs and lobsters grow much larger than any land insect.
- **The oxygen idea:** Insects breathe through fine air tubes that run to every part of the body. In 2007 Alexander Kaiser and others measured beetles of many sizes with X-rays. They found that bigger beetles give a larger share of the body to air tubes. They argued that in a giant insect the tubes would leave too little room in the legs.
- **A study that disagrees:** In 2026 a study of 44 kinds of living insects reported that the finest air tubes take up very little room at every body size. Its authors argue that oxygen supply is not what stops insects from being giants.
- **The moulting idea:** A freshly moulted arthropod is soft. On land, a very heavy soft body would sag under its own weight before the new skeleton hardened. In water the body is held up, so this problem is smaller.
- **The other-animals idea:** Large insects are easy food for birds and bats, who fly better and are warm-blooded. On this view insects stay small because the place of the large flying animal is already taken.

## Mostly agreed
- Muscles contract by threads sliding past each other. The threads themselves do not shorten. *How we know:* In 1954 two teams looked at working muscle under new kinds of microscope. Both saw that the dark bands, where the thick threads lie, stay the same width while the muscle shortens. Later pictures from electron microscopes showed the two sets of threads and the tiny arms between them.
- A muscle fibre can pull but cannot push, so every movement needs something to pull the muscle long again. *How we know:* Every muscle that has been studied works against an opposing muscle, a springy part, gravity or liquid under pressure. In the laboratory, a muscle fibre that has shortened has to be drawn out again by a weight or a spring.
- Letting go needs energy. A myosin arm cannot release actin without ATP. *How we know:* Muscle fibres kept in a dish with no ATP go stiff and stay stiff. Adding ATP makes them soften again. The stiffness of a body after death has the same cause.
- The shape of the bone ends decides how a joint can move. *How we know:* Skeletons, X-ray pictures and scans show a ball in a cup at every shoulder and hip, and a hinge at every elbow. A hinge joint in any person swings one way only.
- Tongues, trunks and octopus arms move without bones because muscle keeps its volume. *How we know:* William Kier and Kathleen Smith compared the muscles of tongues, tentacles and trunks in 1985. In each they found fibres running in several directions, and they showed that a change in width must give a change in length.
- Arthropods can grow only by moulting, and they are soft and slow just after a moult. *How we know:* Moulting has been watched and filmed in crabs, prawns, cicadas, cockroaches and spiders. Fishers have long known that a crab with a soft shell has just moulted.

## How we found out
**Jean Hanson and Hugh Huxley, and Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke, 1953 and 1954 for the two studies, 22 May 1954 for the two papers, and 1957 for the first clear pictures of the bridges.** Under a microscope, a muscle fibre has dark and light stripes. By 1950 scientists knew that muscle holds two proteins, actin and myosin. Many thought that a muscle shortens because its long protein threads fold up or coil, like a spring closing. In 1953 Jean Hanson, a British scientist who was expert with a new kind of microscope, joined Hugh Huxley at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in America. Looking at rabbit muscle, they saw that the dark stripe, where the myosin lies, stays the same width when the muscle shortens. Only the light stripes get narrower. In Cambridge in England, Andrew Huxley and Rolf Niedergerke saw the same thing in living frog muscle. If the threads were folding, the dark stripe would shrink. It did not. So both teams proposed that two sets of threads slide past each other. Their papers were printed one after the other in the journal Nature on 22 May 1954. Few scientists were convinced at first. In 1957 Hugh Huxley's pictures showed tiny bridges between the two sets of threads, and the idea slowly won.  
*What people believed before:* That a muscle shortens because its protein threads fold or coil up. The dark stripes of the fibre kept their width while the muscle shortened, so the threads were not getting shorter. They were sliding.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Muscles push as well as pull.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: We do push doors, carts and swings, and it feels as if our arms push.  
  Against: When you push a door, your triceps is pulling on your forearm bone, and the bone pushes the door. Bones and liquids can push. Muscle threads can only slide together.
- **A relaxed muscle is using no energy, and only pulling costs energy.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: Relaxing feels like doing nothing.  
  Against: A myosin arm needs ATP to let go of actin. A signal from a nerve releases calcium inside the fibre, and the calcium switches the muscle on. The cell uses more ATP to pump that calcium away again. A muscle with no ATP cannot relax. It goes stiff.
- **A skeleton has to be hard.** (A common belief, and the usual meaning of the word; *rejected*)  
  For: Our own skeleton is bone, and the word 'skeleton' makes most people think of bones.  
  Against: Earthworms, sea anemones and caterpillars move with a skeleton of liquid. A tongue and a trunk move with a skeleton of muscle. What makes something a skeleton is the job it does for the muscles.
- **Your ring finger moves poorly because its muscle is weak.** (A common belief; *rejected*)  
  For: The ring finger does feel weaker when you try to lift it alone.  
  Against: Studies of hands show that the fingers are tied together by links between their tendons, and that some forearm muscles serve several fingers. The way the brain drives these muscles adds to the effect. A weak muscle is not the main cause.
- **Animals with soft bodies are simpler than animals with bones.** (An old view in many textbooks; *rejected*)  
  For: A worm looks plain beside a cat, and a soft body has fewer parts with names.  
  Against: A bony arm bends only at its joints. An octopus's arm can bend, twist, shorten and lengthen at any point. Controlling it is a harder problem, and engineers who build soft robots study octopus arms and elephant trunks to learn how.
- **Insects are small because oxygen cannot reach the inside of a big insect.** (Many biologists since the 1990s; *debated*)  
  For: Insects breathe through air tubes. A 2007 X-ray study found that bigger beetles give more of the body to tubes. Giant insects lived when the air held more oxygen.  
  Against: A 2026 study of 44 kinds of insect found that the finest tubes take very little room at any size. Moulting, weight, and being hunted by birds and bats may matter as much or more.

## Roots
In this lesson you found that a muscle can only pull, and that a body gets longer when it is squeezed thinner. Here we ask what that tells us about who does the moving when a body moves.

**Deep truth.** A muscle can only shorten, and it cannot make itself long again. In the Dough Worm, the dough grew four times as long when it was squeezed to half its width. An earthworm's ring muscles squeeze their own body like that, and the lengthening stretches the lengthwise muscles for their next pull. Bone, shell and liquid all do this one job: they carry one muscle's pull across to stretch another. So a skeleton is whatever lets muscles undo each other, and it need not be hard.  
*Habit it breaks:* We say 'my biceps bends my arm', as if one muscle did the job alone. We also use the word skeleton to mean bones. Both habits hide the same fact. A muscle is useless without a partner, and a skeleton is defined by what it does for the pair, not by what it is made of.  
*Evidence:* In the Dough Worm, a squeeze across became a push along. An earthworm has ring muscles and lengthwise muscles round liquid, and each set stretches the other. A spider's leg is bent by a muscle and straightened by liquid pressure. Under the microscope, muscle threads slide together and have no way to push apart.

- *who am i:* When you bend your arm, the muscle that bends it was made ready by the muscle that straightens it. No part of you moves you alone.
- *what is life:* Every animal who moves has the same problem, because muscle can only pull. Animals have solved it with bone, with shell, with liquid and with muscle itself.
- *what is this world:* A pull does nothing useful until something answers it. A rope needs a weight, a muscle needs a partner, and a worm's squeeze needs liquid that will not give way.

The threads inside a muscle can only slide together. From that one limit come very different bodies: your arm with two muscles and a bone, a crab with muscles inside a tube, a spider who straightens a leg with liquid, an earthworm whose skeleton is liquid, and a tongue that is its own skeleton. They look unlike, but each is the same arrangement, in which one pull is undone by an opposite pull. One rule shows up as many kinds of body. And inside each body, no muscle acts alone, so every movement is something the whole arrangement does.

- **Heraclitus, fragment 51 (Diels-Kranz B51):** Heraclitus says that people do not understand how a thing that pulls against itself agrees with itself. He calls it a fitting-together that turns back on itself, like that of a bow or a lyre. (A bow works only because the wood pulls one way and the string pulls the other. Your elbow works only because two muscles pull opposite ways. The opposition is what makes the movement possible.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 3.27:** The verse says that all actions are carried out by the qualities of nature. A person confused by the sense of 'I' thinks, 'I am the doer.' (The verse is about a person and their actions, not about muscles. But the science of this lesson shows something of the same shape in the body. We say one muscle bends the arm, when the bending is done by a pair, a bone, nerves and fuel together.)
- **Tao Te Ching 76:** The chapter says that a person is soft and supple while alive, and stiff and hard when dead. It calls the stiff and hard the companions of death, and the soft and weak the companions of life. (A living muscle can let go, and letting go needs energy. A muscle with no energy left locks stiff. And two of the most skilful movers, an octopus's arm and an elephant's trunk, have no hard parts at all.)
- Science (settled): Actin and myosin are far older than muscles. Single-celled beings such as amoebas use the same two proteins to crawl and to divide. Animals built muscle from a tool that cells already had.
- Science (open): Whether muscle arose once or more than once in animals is still being worked out. A 2012 study found that the striped muscle of jellyfish lacks some key proteins that ours has. Its authors argue that such muscle arose separately in jellyfish and in animals like us.
- Other view, Many anatomists and doctors: Each muscle is a separate organ, with its own name, its own nerve and its own job. A doctor can test one muscle, find that it alone is weak, and repair it. On this view it is more useful, and more exact, to say 'the biceps bends the elbow' than to say that no muscle acts alone. And they would keep the word skeleton for hard parts, because a word that covers bone, water and tongue may be too wide to be useful.
- Practice, Pull and let go (3 min): Sit still and rest one arm on your lap. Bend the elbow very slowly, and count to five. Feel the front of your upper arm get firm. Now straighten the arm just as slowly. Feel the front go soft and the back get firm. Do it twice more with your eyes shut. Then let the whole arm hang loose and breathe out. Notice that letting go is something you do.
- Ask: When you bend your arm, which muscle is doing it? Is there one answer?
- Ask: An earthworm's skeleton is liquid. A tongue's skeleton is the tongue. What would you say a skeleton is?
- Ask: A muscle needs energy to let go. Is letting go a kind of doing?
- Ask: If no muscle moves you alone, who or what moves you?

## Story: The Net That Would Not Rise
*Ammu, her mother Sosamma, Thresiamma chechi and the crew are made up, and so is this evening. These facts are real. The big shore nets at Fort Kochi in Kerala are called cheenavala, or Chinese fishing nets. Each one is a frame of teak and bamboo about 10 metres high. The crew lower the net by walking out along the main beam. Large stones hang from ropes on the shore side, and the weight of the stones helps to lift the net again. A crew is usually four to six people. Crews at Fort Kochi are almost always men. A crew led by a woman is imagined here.*

Ammu's mother kept the ropes of the third net at Fort Kochi.

There were six people in the crew, and Sosamma was the only one who never touched a fish. She oiled the pulleys with coconut oil. She checked every knot at low tide. She knew each of the seven stones that hung on the shore side of the net, because she had tied them there herself, each on a rope of a different length.

Ammu was ten, and she had watched the net go down and come up more times than she had eaten breakfast.

It always went the same way. Thresiamma chechi, who led the crew, would call out. Two of the crew would walk along the great beam, out over the water, with their arms spread like people on a wall. Their weight tipped the frame. The net sank into the green water, wide as a house. On the shore side, the seven stones rose into the air, one after another, turning slowly on their ropes.

Then everyone waited. Five minutes, never much more.

Then the two walked back, the crew took hold of the ropes, and the stones came down as the net came up, streaming, with silver things jumping in the bottom of it.

On the evening it went wrong, the sky over the harbour was the colour of a ripe papaya, and a ferry was hooting its way across to Vypin.

The net went down. The stones went up. Ammu was sitting on an upturned boat, eating a boiled egg.

There was a crack like a coconut splitting.

The biggest rope, the one that carried the three heaviest stones, had parted high up near the frame. The three stones dropped straight down and buried themselves to their middles in the wet sand. The frame gave a long groan. It tilted further towards the sea than Ammu had ever seen it tilt, and it stayed there.

"Up!" Thresiamma chechi shouted. "Bring it up!"

The crew threw themselves on the four ropes that were left. They pulled until their feet dug trenches in the sand. The net rose the height of a child, and stopped. It was full. Ammu could see the water inside it shivering with fish.

"It is too heavy," Sosamma said. "Four stones cannot do it."

"Then we push," said Thresiamma chechi.

She and Babu ran under the sea end of the frame with two long bamboo poles. They set the poles against the beam above their heads and pushed. The poles bent like bows. The beam did not move. Babu's pole slipped and went into the harbour like a spear.

"Again!"

They pushed again. Ammu watched their arms shake. She watched the tide, too. It had turned. In half an hour the water would be pulling hard at a full net on a tilted frame, and her mother had told her what happened to the second net in the year Ammu was born.

She looked at the three stones in the sand. She looked up at the four stones still hanging, and at the long beam, and at the net.

It was strange. She had seen it every day of her life, and she had never seen it.

Nothing in the whole frame ever pushed. The crew walking out did not push the net down. Their weight pulled that end down. The stones did not push the net up. They hung on their ropes and pulled the shore end down, and the net end rose because the other end fell. A rope could not push anything. Every rope on the frame pulled, and for every pull one way there was a pull the other way to undo it. Now one of the two pulls was lying in the sand.

Pushing was no use. The frame had never been built to be pushed.

"Amma," she said. "The broken rope. Is the top half still tied to the frame?"

Sosamma looked up. Four metres of rope hung from the beam, swinging, its end frayed like a paintbrush.

"It is."

"Then it does not need the stones," Ammu said. "It only needs to be pulled down. By anything heavy." She was already running. "We are heavy!"

Her mother understood before she had finished the word. Sosamma knotted a loop in the hanging rope as high as she could reach, put her foot in it, and hung there with her whole weight. Ammu jumped and caught the rope above her mother's hands.

The frame creaked.

"More!" Sosamma shouted, not to the crew, but to the sea wall.

There were people on the sea wall, as there were every evening. A woman selling roasted groundnuts. Two college girls with a phone. A man with a child on his shoulders. They had been watching the trouble in the way people watch trouble.

The groundnut seller put down her basket first. She was a broad woman, and when she took hold of the rope and lifted her feet, the beam moved a full hand's width. The college girls came next, laughing and frightened. Then the man, with the child still on his shoulders.

Seven people hung on one rope like fruit on a stalk.

"Now pull!" called Thresiamma chechi, and the crew hauled on the other four.

The net came up out of the harbour, pouring. It came up slowly and all the way, and the frame stood level again against the papaya sky.

Nobody on the rope let go until Sosamma said they could.

Later, when the catch was in baskets and the groundnut seller had been given a seer fish as long as her arm, Ammu went and sat by the three stones in the sand. Her mother was already measuring new rope against her outstretched arms.

"Three days to tie them back," Sosamma said. "You can help. Each on its own length, so they lift one by one."

Ammu put her hand on the biggest stone. It was still warm from the day.

"I always thought the net was the thing that did the work," she said.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the story never mentions muscles, but the net works the way a joint does. A rope can only pull, like a muscle. The crew's weight pulls the net down and the stones pull it up again, so the two are an antagonistic pair with the beam between them, as the biceps and triceps are with the forearm between them. When one pull is lost, pushing fails, because nothing in the frame was built to push. Ammu's turn is seeing that the missing pull can be given by anything heavy. Ask your child which part of the net is like a bone, which is like the biceps and which is like the triceps.

- Ask: A rope can pull but cannot push. Which part of your body works like that?
- Ask: The net went down by one pull and came up by another. Which pair in your arm does the same?
- Ask: Why did pushing on the beam with poles fail?
- Ask: The people on the rope replaced the stones. What could replace a muscle's partner in a body?
- Ask: What did Ammu mean when she said she had thought the net did the work?

### e1 · The Dough Worm: if a body gets half as wide, how much longer does it get? (35 min)
- **question:** An earthworm's ring muscles squeeze a segment thinner, and the segment gets longer. If a roll of dough is squeezed to half its width, how many times as long does it become?
- **predict:** Guess first. A roll of dough is 4 centimetres wide and 4 centimetres long. You roll it until it is 2 centimetres wide. How long will it be? Why do you think so? Write your guess and your reason.
- **fair test:** The same lump is used from start to finish, and nothing is pinched off or added. The roll is kept the same width all along, and the ends are patted flat before measuring. The width is checked in three places with a card gauge. A second, smaller lump is tested in the same way, to see whether the rule holds at another size.
- **safety:** A grown-up helps with the scissors when cutting the card gauge.
- **record:** Fill in the results table. Under it, write three sentences: what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.

  *Parent does:* 1. Mix about 1 cup of atta with water and a few drops of oil. 2. Knead until the dough is firm and does not stick. 3. Cut four notches in a card, 4, 3, 2 and 1 centimetres wide.  
  *Parent says:* This dough will stand in for an earthworm's body. When you squeeze dough, does the amount of dough change?  
  *Child mode:* Make firm dough that does not stick. Cut four notches in a card.
  - Note: Sticky dough will not roll evenly, so add a little dry atta until it is firm. Measure each notch with the ruler before cutting. The notch only needs to be as deep as it is wide.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take a lump of dough the size of a large lemon. 2. Shape it into a roll that just fits the 4 centimetre notch. 3. Pinch off or add dough until it is 4 centimetres long. 4. Ask for a guess and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* This roll is 4 centimetres wide and 4 long. We will roll it to 2 centimetres wide. How long will it be then? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Make a roll 4 centimetres wide and 4 long. Guess its length at 2 centimetres wide. Say why.
  - Ask: Why do you think it will be that long? (good: Half as wide, so twice as long: 8 centimetres.; It gets thinner all round, so maybe more than twice.; The dough has to go somewhere.)
  - Note: Most children, and most adults, say 8 centimetres. Do not correct the guess. Write it in the table with the reason. Also ask for a guess at 3 centimetres wide.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Roll the dough under flat palms until it just fits the 3 centimetre notch all along. 2. Pat both ends flat with the ruler. 3. Measure the length and write it down. 4. Roll on to the 2 centimetre notch and measure again.  
  *Parent says:* Keep it even all along. Check the width at both ends and in the middle. Now measure the length.  
  *Child mode:* Roll it to 3 centimetres wide. Measure the length. Then roll it to 2 and measure again.
  - Note: At 3 centimetres wide the roll is usually close to 7 centimetres long. At 2 centimetres wide it is usually between 14 and 18. The ends of a roll go thin, so push them in and pat them flat before measuring. If the roll cracks, knead in two drops of water and start again from 4 centimetres.
  - Check: In the Dough Worm, why must you never pinch off any dough once you start rolling? → **The amount of dough must stay the same**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Take a small lump, the size of a marble or a little bigger. 2. Shape it to fit the 2 centimetre notch, 4 centimetres long. 3. Ask for a new guess. 4. Roll it to fit the 1 centimetre notch and measure.  
  *Parent says:* This small roll is 2 centimetres wide and 4 long. We will roll it to 1 centimetre wide. Now what do you guess, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Make a small roll 2 centimetres wide and 4 long. Guess again. Roll it to 1 centimetre wide. Measure.
  - Note: This is the same halving at a smaller size. Many children now guess 16, using the pattern from the first lump. A thin roll breaks easily. Roll gently with your fingers spread, and work on a counter if the plate is too small.

  - Ask: Why do you think that finger will lift least? (good: The little finger is the smallest, so it is weakest.; The ring finger always feels stuck.; They should all be the same, because each finger has its own muscle.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Many children pick the little finger as the lowest, because it is the smallest.
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Keep the palm and four fingertips on the table. 2. Lift the thumb as high as it goes and hold for a count of three. 3. The helper measures from the table to the underside of the fingertip. 4. Repeat for each finger, then do the whole hand a second time.  
  *Parent says:* Lift only this finger. Keep the others down. Hold it there while I measure. Did any other finger want to rise?  
  *Child mode:* Lift one finger. Keep the others flat. Hold it for a count of three. Do every finger twice.
  - Note: Stop at the first feeling of stretch. Nobody should force a finger. If a neighbouring finger lifts off the table, start that try again. The ring finger usually lifts least, often only 1 to 2 centimetres. Hands differ, so write what you really find.
  - Check: In the Finger Lift, why is each finger measured twice? → **One try can be a fluke**
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Lay the hand flat again. 2. Lift the ring finger, and this time let the middle and little fingers rise with it if they want to. 3. Measure the ring fingertip. 4. Write it in the last column.  
  *Parent says:* Now let the neighbours come up too, if they want to. How high does the ring finger go this time? What does that tell you?  
  *Child mode:* Lift your ring finger again. This time let its neighbours rise too. Measure it.
  - Note: With its neighbours free, the ring finger usually goes much higher. This points to a link between the fingers and not to a weak finger.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Work out the average of the two tries for each finger. 2. Draw a bar chart of the five averages. 3. Ask your child to say what they thought, what they saw and why. 4. After they have answered, read the note below aloud.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your chart. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why you think it happened.  
  *Child mode:* Find each average. Draw five bars. Say what you guessed, what you saw and why.
  - Ask: The ring finger went higher when its neighbours were free. What does that suggest? (good: The ring finger is tied to its neighbours.; Its muscle is strong enough, but something holds it back.; The fingers share something.)
  - Ask: Where are the muscles that lift your fingers? (good: In the forearm.; Not in the fingers. I can see the cords on the back of my hand.)
  - Note: The reason: the muscles that straighten the fingers are in the forearm, and long tendons carry their pull over the back of the hand. Small bands join the tendons of the middle, ring and little fingers to each other. So when the neighbours are held down, the ring finger's tendon is held down too. The index finger and the little finger each have an extra straightening muscle of their own, and the ring finger has none. Scientists who measured this found that these links are a main cause. The way the brain controls the forearm muscles adds to it.
  - Think: A veena player or a typist trains for years. Do you think practice can change these links, or only the control?

  *Parent says:* Each pair of hands was stretched by the other. What carried the push between them? What carries the pull between your biceps and your triceps?  
  *Child mode:* Each pair of hands stretched the other. What carried the push? What does that in your arm?
  - Note: The water keeps its volume, so a squeeze one way comes out as a push another way. That is how an earthworm's ring muscles stretch the lengthwise muscles, and the lengthwise muscles stretch the ring muscles.   *Child mode:* Hold each stalk out sideways. Which ones droop? Snip the ends and stand them in water.

## Worksheets
### Pairs, joints and three kinds of skeleton (core) (core, 17 questions)
1. (mcq) What can a muscle do?  
   Options: Pull only / Push only / Pull and push / Neither, it only holds  
   **Answer:** Pull only. *Why:* A muscle works by getting shorter, and a thing that gets shorter pulls. The threads inside it have no way to push apart.
2. (mcq) Which two muscles are the antagonistic pair of the elbow?  
   Options: Biceps and triceps / Biceps and calf / Triceps and shin muscle / Biceps and tendon  
   **Answer:** Biceps and triceps. *Why:* The biceps bends the elbow and the triceps straightens it. A tendon is a cord, not a muscle.
3. (fill) A muscle that bends a joint is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['flexor']. *Why:* A flexor bends a joint. An extensor straightens it.
4. (fill) The slippery liquid inside a moving joint is called ____ fluid.  
   **Answer:** ['synovial']. *Why:* Synovial fluid lets the cartilage surfaces slide, and it carries food to the cartilage.
5. (match) Match each joint to its kind.  
   **Answer:** Elbow → Hinge; Shoulder → Ball and socket; Top of the neck, turning the head → Pivot; Base of the thumb → Saddle. *Why:* The shape of the bone ends sets the movement. A hinge swings one way, a ball turns in a cup, a pivot turns round, and a saddle bends two ways.
6. (truefalse) A tendon joins a bone to another bone.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A tendon joins a muscle to a bone. A ligament joins a bone to a bone.
7. (truefalse) The muscles of a crab are inside the skeleton.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A crab has an exoskeleton, which is a skeleton on the outside. The muscles pull from inside the hard tubes.
8. (mcq) How does an animal with an exoskeleton grow?  
   Options: By moulting / By stretching the shell / By adding rings to the shell / It does not grow  
   **Answer:** By moulting. *Why:* A hard exoskeleton cannot stretch. The animal sheds it and swells before the new one hardens.
9. (sort) Sort these animals by the kind of skeleton.  
   **Answer:** frog → Bones inside; crow → Bones inside; prawn → Exoskeleton; ant → Exoskeleton; earthworm → Hydrostatic skeleton; sea anemone → Hydrostatic skeleton. *Why:* A frog and a crow have bones. A prawn and an ant have a hard outer covering. An earthworm and a sea anemone are held firm by liquid inside.
10. (order) Put these in order, from a whole muscle down to its smallest part.  
   **Answer:** ['Muscle', 'Bundle of fibres', 'Muscle fibre', 'Actin and myosin threads']. *Why:* A muscle is made of bundles. A bundle is made of fibres, which are cells. Each fibre is packed with actin and myosin threads.
11. (mcq) An earthworm's circular muscles contract. What happens to the segment?  
   Options: It gets longer and thinner / It gets shorter and fatter / It curls sideways / It gets smaller all over  
   **Answer:** It gets longer and thinner. *Why:* The liquid inside keeps its volume. A segment squeezed thinner must get longer.
12. (mcq) Which of these has no bone in it at all?  
   Options: Your tongue / Your thumb / Your jaw / Your heel  
   **Answer:** Your tongue. *Why:* A tongue is almost all muscle. Thumb, jaw and heel each have bone.
13. (write) Why does every muscle need a partner? Use the words 'pull' and 'long'.  
   **Answer:** A muscle can only pull by getting shorter. It cannot make itself long again. So something else, usually another muscle, must pull it long before it can work again.. *Why:* A strong answer says that a muscle only shortens, and that the partner's job is to stretch it back.
14. (write) In the Dough Worm, a roll was squeezed to half its width. What happened to its length, and why?  
   **Answer:** It became about four times as long. The amount of dough stayed the same. The roll got narrower from side to side and from top to bottom at once, so the dough had to spread along four times the length.. *Why:* A strong answer gives 'about four times' and says that the amount of dough did not change.
15. (draw) Draw an arm twice: once bent and once straight. Colour the muscle that is contracted in each.  
   **Answer:** Bent arm: the biceps, on the front of the upper arm, is short and fat. Straight arm: the triceps, on the back, is short and fat. In each drawing the other muscle is long.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the two muscles on opposite sides of the upper arm bone. Only one is short in each picture.
16. (fill) The smallest bones in the body are inside the ____.  
   **Answer:** ['ear']. *Why:* The three tiny bones of each ear pass the shaking of a sound inward, so you can hear. The longest bone, the thigh bone, is at the other end of the body.
17. (sort) Sort each muscle by whether you choose to move it.  
   **Answer:** fingers writing → Voluntary; feet kicking a football → Voluntary; heart beating → Involuntary; intestines squeezing food along → Involuntary. *Why:* You decide when to write or kick, so those muscles are voluntary. Your heart and intestines keep working without you deciding.
### Inside the muscle and inside the joint (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Under a microscope, what happens to the threads inside a muscle fibre when it contracts?  
   Options: They slide past each other / They shrink / They coil up like springs / They melt together  
   **Answer:** They slide past each other. *Why:* The actin and myosin threads keep their length and overlap more. Scientists once thought they folded or coiled, and in 1954 two teams showed otherwise.
2. (mcq) What does a myosin arm need in order to let go of actin?  
   Options: ATP / Oxygen gas / A push from actin / Nothing  
   **Answer:** ATP. *Why:* ATP must arrive before the arm can release its grip. With no ATP, the arm stays locked on.
3. (truefalse) A body goes stiff some hours after death because the muscles have run out of ATP.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Without ATP the myosin arms cannot let go of actin, so the muscles lock. The stiffness passes when the threads begin to break down.
4. (mcq) You lower a full bucket slowly into a well. Which arm muscle works hardest?  
   Options: The biceps, letting the arm down / The triceps, pushing the arm down / No muscle, because gravity does it / The calf  
   **Answer:** The biceps, letting the arm down. *Why:* Gravity straightens the elbow. The biceps works to let the arm down slowly, so it works while it gets longer.
5. (fill) Cartilage has no ____ vessels, so it takes its food from the fluid in the joint.  
   **Answer:** ['blood']. *Why:* Cartilage has no blood supply of its own. This is one reason why damaged cartilage heals slowly.
6. (match) Match each part to what it does in a joint.  
   **Answer:** Cartilage → Covers the bone ends smoothly; Synovial fluid → Makes the surfaces slippery; Ligament → Ties bone to bone; Tendon → Ties muscle to bone. *Why:* Each part has one main job. Together they let a joint move smoothly without coming apart.
7. (mcq) Where are the muscles that bend and straighten your fingers?  
   Options: Mostly in the forearm / Inside each finger / In the palm only / In the upper arm  
   **Answer:** Mostly in the forearm. *Why:* Long tendons carry the pull from forearm muscles to the finger bones. You can feel the muscles work near your elbow when you wiggle your fingers.
8. (mcq) At two main joints of a spider's leg, what straightens the leg?  
   Options: The pressure of the spider's blood / An extensor muscle / A spring of silk / The weight of the leg  
   **Answer:** The pressure of the spider's blood. *Why:* Those joints have a flexor muscle and no extensor. Liquid pushed into the leg under pressure straightens it, as Parry and Brown showed in 1959.
9. (order) Put the steps of a moult in order.  
   **Answer:** ['A new soft exoskeleton grows under the old one', 'The old exoskeleton splits', 'The animal climbs out', 'The animal swells with water or air', 'The new exoskeleton hardens']. *Why:* The new covering must be ready before the old one is shed. The animal swells while the new covering is still soft, so that it hardens at a larger size.
10. (odd) Three of these are muscular hydrostats. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A tongue / An elephant's trunk / An octopus's arm / A crab's claw  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* A crab's claw has a hard shell with muscles inside. The other three are almost all muscle, with no hard part.
11. (count) A dough roll is 3 centimetres long. It is rolled to half its width. About how many centimetres long is it now?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* Halving the width makes a roll about four times as long. 3 times 4 is 12 centimetres.
12. (write) In the Finger Lift, the ring finger rose much higher when its neighbours were free. What does that tell you?  
   **Answer:** The ring finger is not simply weak. It is linked to its neighbours. Bands join the tendons on the back of the hand, so holding the neighbours down holds the ring finger down too.. *Why:* A strong answer uses the evidence: the same finger went higher when only the neighbours changed.
13. (write) A clam has one muscle to close the shell and no muscle to open it. How can the shell open?  
   **Answer:** A springy band at the hinge is squashed when the muscle closes the shell. When the muscle relaxes, the band springs back and opens the shell. The band is the muscle's partner.. *Why:* A strong answer names something other than a muscle that undoes the pull, and says that it stretches the muscle again.
14. (draw) Draw one segment of an earthworm cut across. Show the circular muscle, the longitudinal muscle and the liquid.  
   **Answer:** A ring. The circular muscle is a thin layer round the outside, under the skin. The longitudinal muscle is a thicker layer inside it, seen as cut ends. The space in the middle is full of liquid, with the gut in the centre.. *Why:* A strong drawing has two muscle layers round a space of liquid. Arrows may show that one layer squeezes round and the other pulls along.
15. (perspective) Is an inside skeleton better than an outside one?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason from the unit. For example: an exoskeleton must be moulted, and the animal is soft and in danger each time. But a tube on the outside is strong for its weight.. *Why:* Each view has something right. A strong answer names a real gain and a real cost, such as armour and moulting.
16. (sort) Sort each joint into movable or immovable.  
   **Answer:** elbow → Movable; knee → Movable; wrist → Movable; between the plates of the skull → Immovable. *Why:* The elbow, knee and wrist bend or turn. The skull plates are locked together to protect the brain.
### Tongues, moults and fair tests (challenge) (challenge, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) A tongue gets a little narrower and thinner. What happens to its length?  
   Options: It grows by a lot / It grows by the same small amount / It stays the same / It gets shorter  
   **Answer:** It grows by a lot. *Why:* The volume stays the same. A small squeeze in two directions gives a large gain in length. That is why hydrostats can reach far and fast.
2. (mcq) Why does a freshly moulted crab move so poorly?  
   Options: Soft tubes bend when muscles pull / The muscles have been shed too / The crab is asleep / The new shell is too heavy  
   **Answer:** Soft tubes bend when muscles pull. *Why:* A muscle needs something stiff to pull against. Until the new exoskeleton hardens, the tubes give way under the pull.
3. (mcq) A plant has no muscles. How does a touch-me-not fold a leaf?  
   Options: Cells on one side let water out / A tendon pulls the leaf / The leaf dries up / Wind pushes the leaf  
   **Answer:** Cells on one side let water out. *Why:* Cells on one side of a swelling at the leaf base lose water quickly and go slack, so the leaf folds. The plant moves by changing a water skeleton.
4. (truefalse) When you hold your arm stiff, the biceps and the triceps are both pulling at once.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. When both partners pull, neither wins, and the joint is held rigid. You can feel both muscles firm.
5. (truefalse) The elephant's trunk has one long bone down the middle.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A trunk has no bone. It is a muscular hydrostat, built from about 90,000 small bundles of muscle.
6. (fill) A joint of the skull that does not move is called a ____.  
   **Answer:** ['suture']. *Why:* The plates of the skull lock together along zigzag sutures. They protect the brain and do not need to move.
7. (sort) Sort each pull by what undoes it.  
   **Answer:** biceps bending the elbow → Another muscle; earthworm ring muscles → Another muscle; crab claw closing → Another muscle; spider leg bending → Something that is not a muscle; clam shell closing → Something that is not a muscle; biceps lifting a tumbler, then lowering it → Something that is not a muscle. *Why:* The triceps, the worm's lengthwise muscles and the claw's opener are muscles. A spider's leg is straightened by liquid pressure, a clam is opened by a springy band, and gravity straightens the arm with the tumbler.
8. (count) A dough roll 2 centimetres wide is 5 centimetres long. Rolled to 1 centimetre wide, about how many centimetres long is it?  
   **Answer:** 20. *Why:* Half the width gives about four times the length. 5 times 4 is 20 centimetres.
9. (mcq) Scientists disagree about why no insect is as big as a dog. What should you say?  
   Options: Several ideas are being tested / It is because of oxygen / It is because of moulting / Nobody has studied it  
   **Answer:** Several ideas are being tested. *Why:* Oxygen supply, moulting, weight and being hunted by birds and bats have all been suggested. A 2026 study argued against the oxygen idea. It is honest to say the question is open.
10. (write) Explain how you stick out your tongue, to someone who knows that muscles only pull.  
   **Answer:** Fibres that run across the tongue and from top to bottom contract. The tongue gets narrower and thinner. Its volume cannot change, so it gets longer and comes forward. A muscle from the chin also pulls the back of the tongue forward.. *Why:* A strong answer has no pushing muscle in it. It uses the idea that the tongue keeps its volume.
11. (write) Design a fair test: does a cold hand lift its fingers less high than a warm hand?  
   **Answer:** Measure the lift of each finger with the hand at room warmth. Hold the hand in cold water for one minute and dry it. Measure again in the same way, with the same ruler and the same helper. Do each measurement twice. Only the warmth of the hand changes.. *Why:* A strong answer changes one thing only, keeps the measuring the same, repeats each measurement and says what will be compared.
12. (write) A friend says, 'My biceps bends my arm all by itself.' What would you add?  
   **Answer:** The biceps can bend the arm only if something has stretched it first, and that is the triceps or gravity. It also needs the bone to pull on, a tendon to join it to the bone, a nerve to switch it on and ATP from food.. *Why:* A strong answer names the partner that stretches the biceps and at least one other thing the movement depends on.
13. (label) Label the parts of this moving joint.  
   **Answer:** ['Bone: the two hard ends that meet', 'Cartilage: the smooth cap on each bone end', 'Synovial fluid: the liquid in the space between the caps', 'Ligament: the band that ties one bone to the other']. *Why:* The caps and the fluid let the bones slide. The ligaments stop them from coming apart.
14. (draw) Draw a crab's leg as a tube. Draw the two muscles that bend and straighten the next joint.  
   **Answer:** A tube of shell with a second tube joined to it by a soft joint. Inside the first tube are two muscles, one fixed to each side of the next tube's base. One bends the joint and one straightens it.. *Why:* A strong drawing puts both muscles inside the tube, on opposite sides of the joint.
15. (perspective) Is the liquid inside an earthworm a skeleton?  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. For example: in the Water Balloon Worm, the water passed one pair of hands' squeeze to the other pair, which is what a bone does for two muscles. But the water did nothing until the balloon shut it in.. *Why:* Scientists do call it a skeleton, because of the job it does. A strong answer says what job a skeleton does and tests the liquid against that.
16. (truefalse) The human body has about six hundred named muscles, and only a few are voluntary.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Most of the muscles you move on purpose, in your arms, legs, hands and face, are voluntary. The heart and the gut muscles are the ones that work alone.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Why can a muscle not push?  
   Options: Its threads can only slide together / It is too soft / Bones get in the way / It has no nerves  
   **Answer:** Its threads can only slide together. *Why:* The myosin arms drag the actin threads inwards. Nothing in the fibre can drive the threads apart again.
2. (mcq) What stretches your biceps back to its long shape?  
   Options: The triceps, or gravity / The biceps itself / The elbow bone / The blood  
   **Answer:** The triceps, or gravity. *Why:* A muscle cannot lengthen itself. The triceps straightens the elbow and stretches the biceps. The weight of the arm can do it too.
3. (mcq) Which joint is a ball and socket?  
   Options: The hip / The knee / The wrist / A finger joint  
   **Answer:** The hip. *Why:* The round top of the thigh bone sits in a cup in the hip bone. The knee and finger joints are hinges.
4. (mcq) Which kind of joint lets you shake your head to say no?  
   Options: Pivot / Hinge / Saddle / Gliding  
   **Answer:** Pivot. *Why:* At the top of the neck, one bone turns round a peg on the bone below. That is a pivot.
5. (mcq) What does cartilage do in a joint?  
   Options: Gives the bone ends a smooth cap / Ties bone to bone / Pulls the bone / Makes blood  
   **Answer:** Gives the bone ends a smooth cap. *Why:* Smooth cartilage on smooth cartilage, wetted by synovial fluid, slides with very little friction. Ligaments tie bone to bone.
6. (mcq) A beetle's leg muscles are where?  
   Options: Inside the hard tubes of the leg / On the outside of the shell / Only in the body, not the legs / Beetles have no muscles  
   **Answer:** Inside the hard tubes of the leg. *Why:* A beetle has an exoskeleton. The muscles are inside it and pull on the inner side of the shell.
7. (mcq) What is the biggest cost of an exoskeleton?  
   Options: The animal must moult to grow / It is always too heavy / It cannot have joints / Muscles cannot pull on it  
   **Answer:** The animal must moult to grow. *Why:* A hard shell cannot grow. Each moult leaves the animal soft, slow and without armour for a time.
8. (mcq) What is an earthworm's skeleton made of?  
   Options: Liquid shut inside each segment / Tiny bones / Chitin / Hard skin  
   **Answer:** Liquid shut inside each segment. *Why:* Each segment is a closed bag of liquid wrapped in muscle. The liquid keeps its volume, so the muscles can work against it.
9. (mcq) A roll of dough is squeezed to half its width. How long does it become?  
   Options: About four times as long / Twice as long / The same length / Half as long  
   **Answer:** About four times as long. *Why:* The roll gets narrower in two directions at once, so the same dough needs about four times the length. If you chose 'twice', remember that it also got thinner from top to bottom.
10. (mcq) How does your tongue come out of your mouth?  
   Options: Muscles pull it thinner, so it gets longer / A muscle behind it pushes / Air pressure pushes it / A bone slides forward  
   **Answer:** Muscles pull it thinner, so it gets longer. *Why:* A tongue is a muscular hydrostat. Its volume stays the same, so when fibres pull it narrower and thinner it must lengthen.
11. (mcq) Which of these is a muscular hydrostat?  
   Options: An octopus's arm / A crab's leg / A bird's wing / A cat's tail  
   **Answer:** An octopus's arm. *Why:* An octopus's arm is almost all muscle, with no bone and no shell. A crab's leg has a shell, and a wing and a tail have bones.
12. (mcq) In a fair test of finger lifting, what must stay the same for every finger?  
   Options: How the height is measured / Which finger is lifted / The height reached / Nothing  
   **Answer:** How the height is measured. *Why:* Only the finger changes. The ruler, the helper, the hand position and the way of measuring stay the same, so the numbers can be compared.
13. (mcq) Which is the longest bone in an adult's body?  
   Options: The thigh bone / The bone of the upper arm / The bone of the spine / The bone of the skull  
   **Answer:** The thigh bone. *Why:* The thigh bone runs from the hip to the knee and is the longest bone we have. The smallest bone is not in the arm or the leg at all. It is deep inside the ear.
14. (mcq) Which of these joints does not move?  
   Options: The plates of the skull / The elbow / The knee / A finger joint  
   **Answer:** The plates of the skull. *Why:* The plates of the skull lock together and do not move, to protect the brain. The elbow, knee and finger joints are movable joints.
15. (mcq) Which muscle keeps working while you are fast asleep, without you deciding?  
   Options: The heart / The muscle that lifts your finger / The muscle that moves your tongue to speak / The muscle that kicks a ball  
   **Answer:** The heart. *Why:* The heart is an involuntary muscle. It works all day and night without being told. The other three are voluntary muscles. They move only when you decide.

## Applications
- A physiotherapist treats a stiff or weak joint by working on both muscles of a pair. Strengthening one and stretching its partner go together.
- Many artificial hands are worked by cords that run through the fingers like tendons. A motor in the palm or forearm pulls each cord, as a forearm muscle pulls a finger.
- Engineers build soft robot arms with no rigid parts, copied from the octopus's arm and the elephant's trunk. Such an arm can wrap round a fragile object without crushing it.
- The arm of an excavator at a building site is moved by oil pushed into cylinders under pressure. Liquid that keeps its volume carries the push, as it does in a spider's leg.
- When the cartilage of a knee or a hip wears away, a surgeon can replace the joint surfaces with smooth metal and plastic.
- Chitin from the shells of prawns and crabs is made into chitosan, a material used in some wound dressings.
- Dancers, bowlers and veena players train both muscles of each pair, because smooth movement needs the partner to let go at exactly the right moment.

## Open questions
- **Did muscle arise once in animals, or more than once?** Jellyfish have striped muscle that looks like ours, but a 2012 study found it is built without some of our key proteins. That suggests it arose separately. Scientists are still comparing the genes of more animals.
- **Why is no land insect as big as a dog?** Oxygen supply, the danger of moulting with a heavy body, and being hunted by birds and bats have all been suggested. A 2026 study argued that oxygen is not the limit. No single answer is agreed.
- **How does an octopus control an arm that can bend at any point?** A bony arm has a few joints to control. An octopus's arm has no fixed joints. Most of her nerve cells are in the arms, and scientists are still working out how much each arm decides for itself.
- **Can worn joint cartilage be made to grow back?** Cartilage has no blood vessels and repairs itself very little. Researchers are trying cells grown in the laboratory and new materials, but no method yet rebuilds cartilage as good as the original.
- **How much can practice free the ring finger?** The links between the finger tendons are part of the hand's build. Musicians do gain finger control with training. How much of that is change in the brain's control, and whether the links themselves change, is not settled.

## Connected maths
- In a Dough Worm test, a roll was 4 centimetres long at the start. At half the width it measured 16 centimetres. How many times as long had it become? **4 times as long.**. Divide the new length by the starting length: 16 ÷ 4 = 4. The roll became 4 times as long. (queues `math.division.division-facts.L5`)
- Each time a dough roll is squeezed to half its width, it gets 4 times as long. A roll starts 4 centimetres wide and 3 centimetres long. How long is it at 1 centimetre wide? **48 centimetres.**. From 4 centimetres wide to 2 is one halving: 3 × 4 = 12 centimetres. From 2 to 1 is a second halving: 12 × 4 = 48 centimetres. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- A child rolled a 4 centimetre long roll to 2 centimetres wide in the middle, and it measured only 12 centimetres, not 16. The ends were still fat. Explain the low result. **The fat ends held extra dough, so less dough was left to make length.**. The rule needs the roll to be 2 centimetres wide all along. The fat ends were wider than that, so they held more than their share of dough. Rolled even, the roll would be longer. (queues `math.measurement-basics.estimation-and-precision.L5`)
- In the Finger Lift, a ring finger lifted 1.4 centimetres on the first try and 1.8 centimetres on the second. What is the average? **1.6 centimetres.**. Add the two tries: 1.4 + 1.8 = 3.2. Halve it: 3.2 ÷ 2 = 1.6 centimetres. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
- A ring finger lifted 1.5 centimetres with its neighbours held flat, and 4.5 centimetres with them free. How many times as high is that? Does it fit the idea of a weak muscle? **3 times as high. It does not fit a weak muscle.**. 4.5 ÷ 1.5 = 3. The same muscle lifted the finger three times as high when only the neighbours changed. So the muscle was strong enough, and something was holding the finger back. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- A baby elephant's trunk has about 90,000 small bundles of muscle. A human body has about 600 named muscles. 90,000 ÷ 600 = 150. Why is '150 times as many muscles' not a fair claim? **Bundles and named muscles are different things, so the two numbers cannot be compared.**. The sum is right: 90,000 ÷ 600 = 150. But one named human muscle, such as the biceps, is itself made of many bundles. The trunk number counts bundles and the human number counts whole muscles. (queues `math.number-sense.big-numbers.L5`)
