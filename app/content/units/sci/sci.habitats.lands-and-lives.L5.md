# India's ecological regions: why each land has its own plants, animals and knowledge
`sci.habitats.lands-and-lives.L5`

**Summary.** Mangaluru and Bengaluru get the same monsoon wind, yet Mangaluru gets more than three times as much rain. A line of hills between them takes most of the water out of the air. Rain, height, salt and soil divide India into ecological regions, each with their own plants, animals and people. Some living beings are found in one region and nowhere else. Your child tests how buttress roots hold up a tall tree on thin soil. They also build paper towers for a still land and a windy land. They find out why knowledge that fits one land can fail in another.

**Intro.** *In Meghalaya, people cross some rivers on bridges that are alive. Nobody cut a single plank for them. Why would anyone grow a bridge when they could build one?* The answer is in the rain. The hills of southern Meghalaya get about 12 metres of rain a year, more than almost any place on Earth. A bridge of cut wood or bamboo rots there in a few years. A living root gets thicker and stronger every year. So Khasi and Jaintia villagers guide the roots of a rubber fig tree across the stream. Then they wait up to 15 years. The bridge fits that land. In this unit you find out what makes one land differ from another. You meet living beings who are found in only one place. You also test whether a good design for one land still works when it is moved to another.

## Terms
- **Ecological region**: A large area of land or sea with its own climate, landforms, plants and animals. *e.g.* The Thar desert is one ecological region. The wet forest of the Western Ghats is another.
- **Climate**: The usual weather of a place over many years: how hot, how cold, how wet and how dry. *e.g.* Ladakh has a cold, dry climate. Kerala has a warm, wet climate.
- **Monsoon**: A wind that changes direction with the season. In India the summer monsoon blows from the sea and brings most of the year's rain. *e.g.* The monsoon usually reaches Kerala around the first of June.
- **Rain shadow**: The drier land on the far side of a mountain range. The air has already dropped most of its rain on the near side. *e.g.* Coimbatore lies in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats. It gets less than a quarter of the rain of Kozhikode.
- **Altitude**: How high a place is above the level of the sea. *e.g.* Leh is at an altitude of about 3,500 metres. Chennai is almost at sea level.
- **Island**: Land with water on all sides. *e.g.* The Andaman and Nicobar Islands lie in the sea, far to the east of Chennai.
- **Endemic**: Found naturally in only one place in the world. *e.g.* The lion-tailed macaque is endemic to the Western Ghats. The sangai deer is endemic to Manipur.
- **Buttress roots**: Wide, flat roots that spread out from the bottom of a tall trunk like the fins of a rocket. They help to hold the tree up. *e.g.* Many rainforest trees of the Andaman Islands stand on buttress roots taller than a person.
- **Mangrove**: A tree who can grow in salty mud that the sea covers twice a day. *e.g.* Mangroves grow in the Sundarbans of West Bengal and at Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu.
- **Breathing roots**: Roots that grow upwards out of wet mud and take in air. Scientists call them pneumatophores. *e.g.* The sundari tree of the Sundarbans sends up hundreds of breathing roots, like pencils stuck in the mud.
- **Coral**: A tiny sea animal with a soft body, who builds a hard cup of stone around herself. Thousands of corals live joined together. *e.g.* One lump of coral the size of a football holds thousands of coral animals.
- **Coral reef**: A rocky ridge under the sea, built over thousands of years from the stony cups of corals. *e.g.* Coral reefs ring the Andaman Islands. The islands of Lakshadweep sit on top of coral reefs.
- **Tsunami**: A series of very large sea waves, most often started by an earthquake under the sea. *e.g.* On 26 December 2004 a tsunami struck the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and the coast of Tamil Nadu.
- **Erosion**: The slow carrying away of soil or rock by water, waves or wind. *e.g.* Waves erode a bare mud bank. A bank held by mangrove roots is eroded far more slowly.
- **Living root bridge**: A bridge grown from the living roots of a rubber fig tree, which people guide across a stream. *e.g.* The Khasi and Jaintia people of Meghalaya grow living root bridges. Some are said to be more than a hundred years old.
- **Shola**: A patch of low, dense evergreen forest in a fold of the high hills of South India. Open grassland lies all round. *e.g.* In the Nilgiris, sholas fill the damp valleys and grass covers the windy slopes.
- **Biodiversity hotspot**: A region with very many endemic plants which has already lost most of its natural plant cover. *e.g.* The Western Ghats, together with Sri Lanka, form one of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots.
- **Protected area**: Land or water where the law protects wild plants and animals. National parks and wildlife sanctuaries are protected areas. *e.g.* Silent Valley in Kerala is a national park. Nobody may cut trees or harm animals there.
- **Traditional knowledge**: What the people of a place have learned about their land over many generations. They learn by trying and watching, and they teach their children. *e.g.* A farmer knows when the salt has washed out of her field, and which rice will stand in a flood.
- **Variety**: One kind of a crop plant, with a taste, size or strength of their own. Farmers keep a variety going by saving seed. *e.g.* Pokkali is a variety of rice. Pokkali plants grow tall and can stand some salt in the water.

## Explanations
### What an ecological region is · *defined*
**Definition:** An ecological region is a large area of land or sea with its own climate, landforms, plants and animals.

India has snowy mountains, dry deserts, wet forests, salty swamps and coral islands. Each of these is an ecological region. Inside one region, the rain, the warmth and the soil are much the same from place to place. So the same kinds of plants grow across the region, and the animals who need those plants live there too. People are animals as well, and the region shapes their food, their houses and their work. A region has no wall around it. Walk from the wet side of the Western Ghats to the dry side, and the forest thins out slowly. The lines on a map of regions are drawn by people. They help us to think about a land that changes little by little.
- e.g. The Thar desert: little rain, sand and thorny trees, with chinkara, desert foxes and camels.
- e.g. The Western Ghats: heavy rain, steep hills and evergreen forest, with hornbills and lion-tailed macaques.
- e.g. The Sundarbans: salty tidal mud and mangrove trees, with crabs, mudskippers and swimming tigers.
### What makes one region differ from another: rain, height, salt and soil · *defined*
**Definition:** Four things decide most of what can live in a region: how much rain falls, how high the land is, how salty the water is, and what the soil is like.

Rain matters most. Evergreen forest needs about 200 centimetres of rain a year or more. Dry thorn forest grows on less than 75 centimetres. Below about 25 centimetres there is desert. Height comes next, because high land is cold land. Salt matters on the coast, where the sea soaks the mud twice a day. Most plants die there, and only mangroves and a few others grow. Soil matters too. The black soil of the Deccan holds water for months, and sand lets water run straight through. These four things work together. Ladakh is dry because mountains block the rain, and cold because Ladakh is high. That is why Ladakh is a cold desert, and the Thar is a hot one.
- e.g. Rain: Mawsynram in Meghalaya gets about 1,190 centimetres a year. Jaisalmer in the Thar gets about 20.
- e.g. Height: Ooty, at 2,240 metres, is about 13 degrees cooler than the plains below.
- e.g. Salt: in the Sundarbans the water in the mud is salty, so only trees who can deal with salt grow there.
- e.g. Soil: rainforest soil keeps its plant food in the top few centimetres, so even giant trees have shallow roots.
### India's ten ecological regions · *defined*
**Definition:** Scientists divide India into ten large ecological regions, which they call biogeographic zones.

In 1988 two scientists at the Wildlife Institute of India, W. A. Rodgers and H. S. Panwar, drew this map. They wanted to know whether every kind of land in India had a protected area. Five zones are the Trans-Himalaya, the Himalaya, the Desert, the Semi-arid zone and the Western Ghats. The other five are the Deccan Peninsula, the Gangetic Plain, the Coasts, the North-East and the Islands. The Deccan Peninsula is by far the largest, with about 42 parts in every 100 of India's land. The Islands are the smallest, with less than 1 part in 100. A small zone can still hold a great deal. The Western Ghats cover about 4 parts in 100 of India. Yet hundreds of plants and animals live there and nowhere else. You draw the ten zones in the activity Ten Regions on One Map.
- e.g. Trans-Himalaya: the cold desert of Ladakh and Spiti, behind the main Himalaya.
- e.g. Semi-arid zone: the dry grasslands and thorn forests of Gujarat, Rajasthan and Punjab, around the desert.
- e.g. Gangetic Plain: the flat, fertile land of the Ganga, from Haryana to West Bengal.
- e.g. Coasts: the beaches, lagoons and mangroves along about 7,500 kilometres of shore.
### Mountains and rain: the wet side and the dry side · *defined*
**Definition:** When wet air is pushed up a mountain range, the air cools. Most of its rain falls on the side facing the wind. The far side stays much drier and is called the rain shadow.

From June to September the monsoon wind blows in from the Arabian Sea, full of water vapour. Water vapour is water as an invisible gas. The Western Ghats stand in the wind's way, so the air has to climb. Higher up there is less air pressing on it, so the air spreads out and cools. Cool air holds less water vapour than warm air. The extra vapour turns into drops, clouds form, and rain pours on the sea side of the hills. When the air crosses the top, most of its water is gone. As the air sinks down the far side it warms again, and the clouds thin out. So Mangaluru, on the coast, gets about 350 centimetres of rain a year. Bengaluru, behind the hills, gets about 100. The same thing happens in Meghalaya. Wet wind from the Bay of Bengal is pushed up the steep Khasi hills. Mawsynram, on those hills, gets about 1,190 centimetres. The Himalaya do it on the largest scale. They stop the monsoon, and Ladakh behind them gets about 10 centimetres. You compare three pairs of towns in the activity Two Sides of One Hill.
- e.g. Wet side: Kozhikode, about 310 centimetres of rain a year. Dry side: Coimbatore, about 70.
- e.g. Wet side: Mumbai, about 240 centimetres. Dry side: Pune, about 75.
- e.g. Wet side: Mawsynram, about 1,190 centimetres. A hundred kilometres north, Guwahati gets about 170.
### Height: every kilometre up is about six degrees cooler · *defined*
**Definition:** The higher the land, the colder the air. On a wet mountain the air cools by about 6 degrees Celsius for every 1,000 metres you climb.

This is why there is snow on the Himalaya in June. The peaks are a little nearer to the Sun than the plains, and still far colder. The Sun warms the ground, and the ground warms the air above. High up, the air is thin and holds little heat. So climbing a mountain is like travelling towards the cold north. In the eastern Himalaya you can pass through four kinds of forest in one day's drive. Sal trees grow in the warm foothills. Higher up come oaks and rhododendrons, then firs, then low bushes and grass, and at last bare rock and snow. Each belt has its own animals. Height also explains two unusual places in South India. Above about 1,700 metres in the Nilgiris, frost and wind keep trees off the open slopes. Grass covers the slopes, and small forests called sholas grow only in the sheltered folds.
- e.g. Chennai, at sea level, has an average temperature of about 28 degrees. Ooty, at 2,240 metres, has about 15.
- e.g. The red panda lives in the cool bamboo forests of Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh, mostly above 2,200 metres.
- e.g. In Leh, at 3,500 metres, winter nights can fall to minus 20 degrees.
### The Andaman and Nicobar Islands: rainforest, buttress roots and coral reefs · *defined*
**Definition:** The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are more than 500 islands in the sea east of the Indian mainland. They are covered in rainforest and ringed by coral reefs.

The islands are the tops of a mountain chain that runs under the sea from Myanmar towards Sumatra. The southern tip of India is here, at Indira Point on Great Nicobar Island. Kanyakumari is only the southern tip of the mainland. The islands are warm and wet all year. Forest covers more than 80 parts in every 100 of the land. Rainforest trees grow 40 metres tall to reach the light. But heavy rain washes plant food out of the soil. What remains lies in the top few centimetres, where fallen leaves rot. So the trees' roots stay shallow. Many trees grow buttress roots, wide flat roots that spread from the trunk like fins. You test what such roots do in the Leaning Tree Test. Around the islands lie coral reefs. A coral is a tiny animal, related to jellyfish. She takes lime from sea water and builds a stony cup around her soft body. Tiny algae live inside her and share the food they make from sunlight. Over thousands of years the cups pile up into a reef, where fish, crabs and turtles feed and hide. Because the islands are far from other land, many of their living beings are endemic.
- e.g. The Andaman padauk, a giant tree with buttress roots, grows wild only on the Andaman Islands.
- e.g. The Nicobar megapode is a bird who buries her eggs in a warm mound of sand and leaves. She lives only on the Nicobar Islands.
- e.g. When the sea stays too warm for weeks, corals push out their algae and turn white. This is called bleaching, and many of them die.
- e.g. Barren Island, in the Andaman Sea, is India's only active volcano.
### The Sundarbans: a forest in salty mud · *defined*
**Definition:** The Sundarbans is the largest mangrove forest in the world. The forest grows on islands of mud where the Ganga and the Brahmaputra meet the Bay of Bengal.

The forest covers about 10,000 square kilometres. About four parts in ten are in West Bengal, and the rest are in Bangladesh. Twice a day the tide covers the mud with salty water. This gives a tree two problems. The first is salt, which pulls water out of roots. Mangrove trees keep most of the salt out at their roots, and some push the rest out through their leaves. The second problem is air. Roots need oxygen, and waterlogged mud has almost none. You measure how much air a flood pushes out of soil in the activity Mud Without Air. Many mangroves answer with breathing roots. These grow up out of the mud like pencils and take in air at low tide. The tangle of roots slows the water, so mud settles and the bank is held against erosion. Tigers here swim from island to island and eat fish and crabs as well as deer. People live around the forest too. Honey collectors go in each April in groups, and fishers work the creeks. Many of them honour Bonbibi, the lady of the forest, whose story tells them to take only what they need.
- e.g. The Sundarbans may be named after the sundari tree, who has both breathing roots and buttress roots.
- e.g. The mudskipper is a fish who walks on the mud at low tide and breathes air through her wet skin.
- e.g. India has other mangrove forests at Bhitarkanika in Odisha, Pichavaram in Tamil Nadu and in the Andaman Islands.
- e.g. The Indian part of the Sundarbans has about 100 tigers.
### North-East India: the wettest hills, living bridges and a floating park · *defined*
**Definition:** North-East India is eight states of steep wet hills and wide river valleys: Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim and Tripura.

The region has more kinds of land than any other part of India of its size. The Brahmaputra floods the Assam valley almost every year, so the Mising people build their houses on bamboo stilts. The Khasi hills of Meghalaya are among the wettest places on Earth. Wooden bridges rot there, so Khasi and Jaintia villagers grow living root bridges from rubber fig trees. Yet in winter the same hills run short of water, because rain runs quickly off the steep, thin soil. In Manipur lies Loktak Lake, with floating mats of plants, roots and soil called phumdis. Some phumdis are thick enough to walk on. Keibul Lamjao National Park is made of them, and is the only floating national park in the world. The sangai, a deer with curved antlers, lives on the phumdis and nowhere else. A count in 1975 found only about 14 sangai. A count in 2016 found about 260. The region also has India's only ape, the hoolock gibbon. India's only pitcher plant grows here too, and traps insects in her leaf jugs.
- e.g. Majuli, in the Brahmaputra in Assam, is one of the largest river islands in the world.
- e.g. The pitcher plant Nepenthes khasiana is endemic to the hills of Meghalaya.
- e.g. In Assam, Purnima Devi Barman and the women of the Hargila Army protect the nesting trees of a rare stork.
- e.g. The Apatani people of Arunachal Pradesh raise fish in the same flooded fields where their rice grows.
### The Western Ghats: rainforest, sholas and protected valleys · *defined*
**Definition:** The Western Ghats are a range of hills about 1,600 kilometres long. They run down the west side of India through six states.

The Ghats are older than the Himalaya. Their western face catches the monsoon, so thick evergreen forest grows there. In the south, above about 1,700 metres, the land opens into grassland with sholas in the folds. The Nilgiri tahr, a wild mountain goat, grazes on those grassy cliffs and lives nowhere else. So many endemic plants and animals live in the Ghats that scientists count the range as a biodiversity hotspot. The purple frog spends almost the whole year under the ground. She comes up for a few days in the monsoon to lay eggs. The neelakurinji bush flowers once in 12 years and turns whole hillsides blue. Many parts of the Ghats are protected areas, such as Periyar, Silent Valley, Bandipur and Eravikulam. Silent Valley was nearly lost. In the 1970s a dam was planned that would have drowned its rainforest. Teachers, scientists and writers in Kerala argued against the dam for years, and the plan was dropped in 1983. The valley became a national park in 1984. India's mainland ends just south of the Ghats at Kanyakumari, where three seas meet.
- e.g. The lion-tailed macaque lives only in the rainforest canopy of the Western Ghats.
- e.g. The neelakurinji flowered in 2018. The next flowering is expected in 2030.
- e.g. Anamudi in Kerala, at 2,695 metres, is the highest peak in India south of the Himalaya.
- e.g. The Kani people of the Agasthya hills know hundreds of forest plants and their uses.
### Two kinds of desert: the hot Thar and cold Ladakh · *defined*
**Definition:** A desert is a region with very little rain. The Thar in the west is a hot desert, and Ladakh in the north is a cold desert.

The Thar gets between 10 and 50 centimetres of rain a year, most of it in a few storms. Plants and animals there save water. The khejri tree sends her roots many metres down. The chinkara, a small gazelle, can live for long periods on the water in the plants she eats. People save water too. Families dig a kund, a covered tank with a wide saucer of hard ground around it. The saucer catches the rain of a few storms and sends it into the tank for the whole year. The Thar is often called the most crowded desert in the world. South of the Thar lies the Rann of Kutch, a flat plain of salt. The Indian wild ass lives there and nowhere else. Ladakh is different. It lies behind the Himalaya at about 3,500 metres and gets about 10 centimetres of rain. Its water comes from snow and ice melting on the peaks. Villagers lead the meltwater to barley fields along channels. One villager is chosen each year to share the water out fairly. Houses have thick mud-brick walls and flat roofs, because the cold is the danger there and rain is not.
- e.g. Thar: khejri trees, chinkara, desert foxes, and the great Indian bustard, of whom perhaps only about 150 are left.
- e.g. Rann of Kutch: the Indian wild ass, and flamingos who nest on the salt flats in wet years.
- e.g. Ladakh: snow leopards, wild asses called kiang, and black-necked cranes who nest beside high lakes.
### Endemic and rare: why unique places have living beings of their own · *defined*
**Definition:** An endemic living being is found naturally in only one place in the world. Places that are cut off, or unlike anywhere else, have the most endemic living beings.

An island is cut off by sea. A cold hilltop is cut off by the hot valleys around it. A salt plain or a floating mat of plants is unlike any land nearby. A group of animals or plants may live in such a place for a very long time. They change slowly in their own way. In the end they differ from all their relatives elsewhere. That is why the Andaman Islands, the sholas, the Rann of Kutch and Loktak Lake each have living beings of their own. Endemic does not mean rare. Nilgiri tahr are common on some hills. But a being with one home is at risk if that home is harmed. A rare being is one with very few members left, such as the great Indian bustard. Scientists are still finding new species in these places. The purple frog was first described by scientists only in 2003. People in the hills of Kerala had long known her.
- e.g. Islands: the Narcondam hornbill lives on one small island in the Andaman Sea.
- e.g. High hills: the Nilgiri tahr and the neelakurinji of the southern Western Ghats.
- e.g. A salt plain: the Indian wild ass of the Rann of Kutch.
- e.g. A floating lake: the sangai deer of Loktak Lake in Manipur.
### Traditional knowledge: what it is and how it is built · *defined*
**Definition:** Traditional knowledge is what the people of a place have learned about their land over many generations. They pass it on by teaching and showing.

It is built slowly. Someone tries something: a seed, a roof, a way of catching rain. If it works, others copy it. If it fails, people remember, and warn their children. Each generation keeps what worked and changes a little. After hundreds of years the result can be very exact. A Pokkali farmer in Kerala knows the week when the monsoon has washed the salt from her field. A Khasi builder knows how to lead a fig root through a hollow palm trunk to the far bank. Much of this knowledge is kept in words, songs and festivals. Some is kept in living things. A rice variety that farmers have saved for centuries carries their choices in the seed. Traditional knowledge can also be wrong, like any knowledge, and it changes when it is tested. You watch knowledge being built in the Two Lands Test, where each tower learns from the one before.
- e.g. Meghalaya: living root bridges, and pipes of split bamboo that carry stream water to pepper and betel plants, drop by drop.
- e.g. Kerala coast: Pokkali rice in the rainy months and prawns in the salty months, in the same field.
- e.g. Thar: the kund, a covered tank with a saucer of hard ground that catches the rain.
- e.g. Nagaland: the zabo system of Kikruma village. Forest is kept at the top of the slope, with ponds below and rice fields at the bottom.
- e.g. Agasthya hills: the Kani people's use of the arogyapacha plant against tiredness.
### Knowledge fits the land where it was tested · *defined*
**Definition:** A way of farming, building or finding water is tested by the land where people use it. So it fits that land, and may fail in a different one.

In the Two Lands Test, the tall tower that won in still air fell in the wind. Real knowledge behaves the same way. A flat mud roof is right for Ladakh, with 10 centimetres of rain a year. In Meghalaya the same roof would wash away in a week. Short, heavy-yielding rice gives far more grain than Pokkali in a field of fresh, shallow water. In a tidal field near Kochi the short rice drowns. This also explains some large mistakes. Foresters trained in Europe saw the grasslands of the Nilgiris as waste land and planted trees on them. In Kutch, a thorny tree from the Americas was planted from the 1960s to hold the soil. These trees now cover more than half of the Banni grassland. Some knowledge does travel well. The way to test an idea fairly works in every land, and so does a vaccine. What changes from land to land is the rain, the height, the salt and the soil. A design has to meet these.
- e.g. A stilt house from the Assam flood plain would also suit the backwaters of Kerala, because both lands flood.
- e.g. A kund works in the Thar and in Kutch, because both get their rain in a few storms.
- e.g. A living root bridge would die in the Thar. The rubber fig needs wet air all year.
### Is traditional knowledge a kind of science? · *debated*
**Definition:** People who study knowledge do not agree on whether traditional knowledge should be called science.

Traditional knowledge and science share a lot. Both watch nature closely, try things, and keep what works. They also differ. A scientist writes down her method so that a stranger in another country can repeat the test. Traditional knowledge is usually taught by doing, inside one community, and often comes with stories and rules about respect. Each has found things that the other missed. Scientists described the purple frog's family tree. People in Kerala already knew when and where the frog comes up. In 1987 Kani guides in the Agasthya hills showed scientists the arogyapacha plant, whose fruit they ate against tiredness. A medicine was made from the plant, and half of the money earned was shared with the Kani people. There have been unfair cases too. In 1995 a patent was given in the United States for healing wounds with turmeric. Indian families had done this for centuries. India's science council challenged the patent, and it was cancelled in 1997.
- e.g. In the Biligiri Rangaswamy hills of Karnataka, Soliga people and scientists have mapped the forest together.
- e.g. India began a library of written traditional medicine in 2001, so that old knowledge cannot be patented as new.
- e.g. Under a law of 2002, a village can keep a People's Biodiversity Register. It records what people know about local plants and animals.
- **Madhav Gadgil, an Indian ecologist:** People who live with a forest or a river know its plants, animals and seasons in great detail. This knowledge should be written down with their names, and used together with science.
- **Meera Nanda, an Indian historian of science:** The strength of science is that anyone can test a claim anywhere. Old practices should be tested in the same way. Some will pass, and some will fail.
- **Fikret Berkes, a Canadian ecologist who wrote Sacred Ecology (1999):** Traditional knowledge is knowledge, practice and belief joined together and handed down. It is a different way of knowing, which overlaps with science and can work beside it.
### Do mangroves protect a coast from a tsunami? · *debated*
**Definition:** Scientists agree that mangroves weaken ordinary waves and storm floods. They still argue about how much mangroves can do against a giant tsunami.

After the tsunami of 26 December 2004, scientists visited villages on the coast of Tamil Nadu. Some reported that villages behind mangroves or other trees had less damage. Other scientists checked the same villages. They pointed out that the safer villages were often farther from the sea, or on higher ground. So the trees may not have been the cause. This is a common problem in science. When two things differ between two villages, you cannot tell which one made the difference. You work through such a case in the activity Two Villages and a Wave. The evidence is stronger for cyclones. In 1999 a great cyclone struck Odisha. The economist Saudamini Das later compared hundreds of villages, and allowed for distance and height. Villages with wider mangroves in front of them had fewer deaths. For a tsunami, everyone agrees on what saves the most lives: a warning, and running to high ground.
- e.g. Mangrove roots and trunks rub against moving water and slow it down.
- e.g. A village 1 kilometre from the sea is safer than a village 200 metres away, with or without trees.
- e.g. India and Bangladesh replant mangroves mainly against cyclones and erosion.
- **Finn Danielsen and his team, writing in the journal Science in 2005:** In Cuddalore district of Tamil Nadu, villages behind coastal trees were damaged less than villages on open shore. Coastal trees should be protected and replanted.
- **Alexander Kerr, Andrew Baird and other critics, from 2006:** The safer villages were also farther from the sea or higher up. Once distance and height are counted, the trees explain little of the difference for a wave this large.
- **Saudamini Das and Jeffrey Vincent, writing in 2009:** In the Odisha cyclone of 1999, villages with wider mangroves had fewer deaths, even after allowing for distance and height. Mangroves do protect against storm floods.
### Why India has so many kinds of land · *defined*
**Definition:** India has many ecological regions for three reasons: where the land lies, how the land was pushed up, and which way the wind blows.

The ground of India rides on a huge slab of rock called a plate. Long ago this plate was joined to Africa, Madagascar and Antarctica. It broke away and drifted north for tens of millions of years. About 50 million years ago the plate began pushing into Asia. The crust crumpled and rose, and that rise made the Himalaya. They are still rising by a few millimetres a year. The Western Ghats are the raised western edge of the same plate. The islands of the Andaman Sea are the top of a ridge pushed up where two plates meet. Then comes the wind. In summer the land heats up faster than the sea. Warm air rises over India, and wet sea air flows in to replace it. That flow is the monsoon. So moving rock built the mountains, and the Sun moves the wind that carries the rain. The mountains then decide where that rain falls.
- e.g. The purple frog's nearest relatives live on the Seychelles islands, which were once joined to India.
- e.g. Sea shells are found in rocks high in the Himalaya, because those rocks formed under a sea.
- e.g. The earthquake of 26 December 2004 happened where the Indian plate slides under its neighbour, near Sumatra.

## Mostly agreed
- Rain is shared out very unevenly across India, and mountain ranges cause much of the difference. *How we know:* Weather offices have measured rain with gauges for more than 100 years. Mangaluru, on the sea side of the Western Ghats, gets about 350 centimetres a year. Bengaluru, behind the hills, gets about 100.
- Air is colder at greater heights, by about 6 degrees Celsius for every 1,000 metres on a wet mountain. *How we know:* Thermometers at hill stations and on weather balloons show the same fall in every country. Ooty, at 2,240 metres, is about 13 degrees cooler than the plains.
- Buttress roots help to hold up tall trees who have shallow roots. *How we know:* In 1997 Martin Crook, Roland Ennos and John Banks pulled rainforest trees over to measure how strongly each held to the ground. Trees with buttresses resisted almost twice as strongly as trees without them.
- Waterlogged mud holds almost no oxygen, and breathing roots carry air down to a mangrove's buried roots. *How we know:* In 1955 Per Scholander and his team measured the gas inside mangrove roots. They showed how air reaches the buried roots through the breathing roots.
- A coral is an animal, and a reef is built from the stony cups of millions of corals. *How we know:* Under a microscope each coral shows a mouth and a ring of arms. Divers saw the corals of Lakshadweep turn white in 1998, when the sea stayed unusually warm for weeks.
- Places that are cut off, such as islands and high hilltops, hold many endemic living beings. *How we know:* Scientists list where each species has been found. Most frog species of the Western Ghats have been found nowhere else. The same is true of many birds of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
- The roots of a living root bridge join where they cross, and grow thicker with the years. *How we know:* In 2019 Ferdinand Ludwig and his team reported on more than 70 living root bridges in Meghalaya. They had surveyed and measured them. Old bridges had roots that had grown into one another.
- The high grasslands of the Nilgiris are natural and very old. People did not make them by cutting forest. *How we know:* In 1993 Raman Sukumar and his team studied the layers of a Nilgiri bog. The layers showed that grass covered those hills about 20,000 years ago.

## How we found out
**Raman Sukumar, R. Ramesh, R. K. Pant and G. Rajagopalan, The plantations began in the 1840s. The study of the bog layers was published in the journal Nature in 1993..** High in the Nilgiri hills, small dark forests called sholas sit in the folds of wide grasslands. In the 1800s British foresters looked at the grass and saw waste land. Many believed that forest had once covered the hills, and that fire and grazing buffaloes had destroyed the trees. So from the 1840s to the 1980s, large parts of the grassland were planted with trees from other countries: wattle, pine and eucalyptus. Some foresters disagreed, but nobody could look into the past. Then a team led by Raman Sukumar of the Indian Institute of Science dug into a Nilgiri bog. A bog keeps dead plants in layers, with the oldest at the bottom. Tropical grasses leave a different kind of carbon in the layers than trees do. In 1993 the team reported that grass had covered these hills about 20,000 years ago. The Earth was colder and drier then. The grasslands were far older than any plantation or herd. By then the planted trees had spread over much of the grass where the Nilgiri tahr feeds.  
*What people believed before:* That the high grasslands of the Nilgiris were ruined forest and waste land, made by fire and grazing. The layers of the bog showed that grass had grown on those hills for about 20,000 years. That is long before any herds or plantations.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Open grassland on a hilltop is waste land, and trees should be planted on it.** (Many foresters in the Nilgiris from the 1840s to the 1980s.; *rejected*)  
  For: Trees give timber and firewood, and a forest looks richer than grass. In other places, cut forest does turn into poor grass.  
  Against: The layers of Nilgiri bogs show grass on those hills about 20,000 years ago. The Nilgiri tahr and many plants live only on that grass. Planted wattle and pine spread over the grass and took their place.
- **A modern method is better than an old local one, in every place.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Modern rice varieties give two or three times as much grain in good fields. Vaccines, pumps and concrete have saved many lives.  
  Against: A method is tested by the land where people use it. Short modern rice drowns in the tidal fields where tall Pokkali stands. In the Two Lands Test, the winner in still air fell in the wind.
- **Traditional knowledge is always right, and always gentle on nature.** (A belief held by some people who admire old ways of life.; *rejected*)  
  For: Sacred groves, root bridges and kunds have lasted for centuries without using a place up.  
  Against: People with long traditions have also made mistakes. The first people to reach New Zealand hunted the giant moa birds until none were left. Old remedies have to be tested like new ones, and some fail.
- **A belt of mangroves will stop a tsunami.** (Some newspapers and campaigners after December 2004.; *debated*)  
  For: Mangroves slow moving water. In the Odisha cyclone of 1999, villages behind wider mangroves had fewer deaths.  
  Against: For the 2004 tsunami, the safer villages were often farther from the sea or higher up. A giant wave can pass through or over a belt of trees. A warning and high ground save the most lives.
- **Scientists discovered the purple frog in 2003.** (Many news reports at the time.; *debated*)  
  For: S. D. Biju and Franky Bossuyt were the first to describe the frog for science. They showed that she belongs to a family of her own, with relatives in the Seychelles.  
  Against: People in the hills of Kerala had seen the frog come up in the monsoon for generations. They had names for her. What was new in 2003 was her family tree.
- **A desert has too little rain for people to live on rain alone.** (A common belief.; *rejected*)  
  For: Jaisalmer gets about 20 centimetres of rain a year. Chennai gets about seven times as much.  
  Against: Each square metre of ground in Jaisalmer still receives about 200 litres of rain a year. A kund with a wide saucer of hard ground collects enough for a family. People have lived this way in the Thar for centuries.

## Roots
In this unit you tilted paper trees and built towers for a still land and a windy land. Here we ask what it means for a way of doing things to be right.

**Deep truth.** In the Two Lands Test, the tall thin tower won in still air and fell under the fan. The short wide tower stood under the fan but lost in still air. Each design had been improved by the tests of one land only. So knowledge that is right for one land can be wrong for another.  
*Habit it breaks:* We rank ways of doing things as modern or backward, as if one list fitted every place. But each tower was right only for the land that had tested it. A ranking leaves out the question 'where?'  
*Evidence:* In the Two Lands Test the materials, the time and the builders were the same, and only the test differed. Short modern rice drowns in the tidal fields where Pokkali stands. Trees planted on the Nilgiri grasslands spread and harmed a place that needed no trees.

- *who am i:* Much of what you know came from people who lived on your land before you. They worked out which foods grow there, which roof keeps the rain out and which months are dry.
- *what is life:* Living beings and their land shape each other. A fig tree sends out roots, people guide them, and a stream gets a bridge that grows.
- *what is this world:* The Earth is one planet with many lands. Rain, height, salt and soil make each land ask different things of those who live there.

One wind brings rain to the whole west coast. One range of hills turns that wind into a wet side and a dry side. From that single difference come different forests, different animals and different roofs. So the many ways of living in India are answers to the same few things: rain, height, salt and soil. Tamil poets said this about 2,000 years ago, and the Two Lands Test shows it with newspaper.

- **Tolkappiyam, Porulatikaram, the chapter Akattinaiyiyal (Tamil, about 2,000 years old, though scholars disagree on the exact date):** The Tolkappiyam divides the Tamil country into five landscapes: hills, forest, farmland, seashore and dry land. Each landscape has its own flower, tree, animals, work and way of life. The hills are named kurinji, after the flower that blooms there. (This is close to the idea of an ecological region. The poets saw that the land, the living beings and the people's lives go together. They used it to write poems, and scientists use it to plan protected areas.)
- **Udana 6.4, the Buddha's story of the blind men and the elephant:** Several men who had been blind from birth each touched one part of an elephant. One said an elephant is like a pot, another like a pillar, another like a broom. Each was sure, and they quarrelled. (The Buddha told the story about people who argue over what is true. It fits this unit in a small way. Each land's knowledge is true of the part of the world that tested it. It goes wrong when it is taken to describe every land.)
- Science (settled): Ferdinand Ludwig's team surveyed more than 70 living root bridges in Meghalaya. Where two roots cross and press together, they grow into each other, so an old bridge becomes one connected frame.
- Science (open): Rain, sea level and temperature are now changing within one lifetime. Nobody knows how much traditional knowledge will still fit, or how quickly new knowledge can be built.
- Other view, Meera Nanda, an Indian historian of science, in her book Prophets Facing Backward (2003), and many working scientists: The laws of nature are the same in every land. A claim that has been tested fairly is true in Ladakh and in Kerala alike. On this view, calling local knowledge 'right for its own place' can protect mistaken beliefs from being tested. The fair course is to test every practice, old or new, in the same way.

Is it warm or cool, damp or dry, moving or still? Now open your eyes. Find one thing people have made that answers this air: a fan, a sloping roof, a thick wall, a shady tree. Ask who first worked that answer out.

## Story: The Corner of New Rice
*Nimmy, Ammachi, Uncle Jose and their field are made up. These facts are real. Pokkali is an old, tall rice of the tidal fields near Kochi in Kerala. Kadamakkudy is a group of islands where farmers grow Pokkali. Farmers build mounds in April and sow sprouted seed on them when the June rain has washed out the salt. After about a month they spread the young plants over the field. At harvest they cut only the heads of grain and leave the stalks standing in the water. From November to April the same fields become prawn ponds, and neither crop needs bought fertiliser. Pokkali gives less grain than modern rice. Rice breeders have used Pokkali to breed new varieties that can stand salt. 'Ammachi' is a word for grandmother in many Kerala Christian families.*

Kadamakkudy is a handful of small islands in the backwaters near Kochi. Nimmy's house stood on a mud bank between two fields. For half of every year the fields were under water.

In May her uncle Jose came home from Dubai. He brought a suitcase, a phone full of videos and a cloth bag of rice seed.

"From a shop in Thrissur," he told Ammachi, his mother. "Short, strong plants. Five tonnes from a hectare. Your Pokkali gives hardly two."

Ammachi did not argue. She was mending the wooden shutter of the sluice. The sluice is the small gate in the bank that lets the tide in and out. "Take the corner by the coconut tree," she said. "Sow yours there. I will sow mine everywhere else. Nimmy will keep the count."

Nimmy was ten, and she liked counting. She took an old school notebook and wrote two headings: NEW RICE and POKKALI.

All through April the family had heaped the mud of the field into mounds, each as high as Nimmy's knee. When the June rain came, it fell on the mounds day and night and ran off them into the channels. Every morning Ammachi scooped a little field water into her palm and tasted it.

On the ninth of June she said, "Now it is sweet." That day they pressed the sprouted seed into the tops of the mounds. Up there the young roots would sit above the last of the salt.

Uncle Jose sowed his corner flat, in neat lines. "Mounds waste half the land," he said.

For three weeks the corner was winning, and Nimmy's notebook said so. New rice: dark green, thick as a mat. Pokkali: thin, pale, leaning. Uncle Jose took a photograph every evening and sent it to his friends.

In July Ammachi broke up the mounds and spread the young Pokkali plants across the whole field. They looked lost in all that mud.

Then came the week of the new moon. The tide pushed in higher than usual, and heavy rain fell on top of the tide. One morning Nimmy stepped off the bank into the field, and the water came up to her waist.

The corner was gone. She could see the new rice under the brown water, like weed on the bottom of a pond. All around her the Pokkali stood with their top leaves a clear hand above the water.

The water stayed for six days. When it went down, the plants in the corner lay flat and yellow, and they smelt. Uncle Jose stood on the bank and looked at them for a long time.

"Bad seed," he said.

"Good seed," said Ammachi. "Good seed for Thrissur."

That evening Nimmy measured. One of the dead plants was as long as her arm from elbow to fingertip. The Pokkali already reached her chest. She wrote both lengths down. Then she looked at the other two numbers in her notebook, the ones from the seed bag. Five tonnes. Two tonnes.

Somebody had weighed those five tonnes. Somebody had stood in a field and counted sacks, just as she was counting. But in which field?

"Uncle," she said, "where did they count the five tonnes?"

He turned the bag over. There was a picture of a flat green field with a tractor in it. The water in the picture was only ankle-deep.

"Not here," he said slowly.

"Then the bag is true," said Nimmy. "It is true for that field. Our field asks a different question."

In October the grain was ripe, and the water still stood knee-deep. Ammachi and the neighbours waded in and cut only the heads of the rice. They left the tall stalks standing. Nimmy counted nine sacks from the field and none from the corner, and wrote 9 and 0.

"Now open the gate," said Ammachi.

The rains had ended. With each high tide, salty water came in through the sluice, and baby prawns came in with the tide. A net across the gate kept them from going out again. All through the dry months they fed among the rotting stalks, and grew.

Uncle Jose watched the tide come in one evening. "So for five months this is a rice field," he said, "and for six months it is a prawn pond."

"The stalks feed the prawns," said Nimmy. "And what the prawns leave behind feeds the next rice. Ammachi has never bought a bag of fertiliser."

"Who worked all this out?" he asked.

Ammachi laughed. "Nobody. Everybody. My grandmother kept seed from the plants that stood through her worst year. Her grandmother did the same."

Uncle Jose did not throw the cloth bag away. He wrote on it with Nimmy's pen: FOR SHALLOW FIELDS WITH SWEET WATER. Then he posted it to a cousin near Palakkad.

Nimmy opened her notebook. Beside NEW RICE and POKKALI she ruled a third column, and at the top she wrote one word: WHERE.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the turn comes when Nimmy asks where the five tonnes were counted. The number on the bag was true for the field where the new rice was tested. That was a shallow field of fresh water. Her family's field floods to the waist and turns salty every year. Pokkali was chosen over many generations by exactly that field. A method is tested by a place, so the question 'which is better?' needs the question 'where?'

- Ask: Why did the family sow the Pokkali seed on top of mounds?
- Ask: The new rice was winning for three weeks. What had not happened yet?
- Ask: Was the number on the seed bag false? Explain.
- Ask: How does one field give two harvests in a year?
- Ask: Ammachi said 'Nobody. Everybody.' What did she mean about where the knowledge came from?

  - Ask: What is different about the new land on this card? (good: It rains a hundred times as much there.; The water there is salty.; Nothing important differs, so the idea should work.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Play the last eight cards the same way. Keep the score on a piece of paper.  
  *Parent says:* Eight more cards. This time, say the deciding fact first, and then put down your token.  
  *Child mode:* Play eight more cards. Say the deciding fact first each time.
- **s4 (2 min)**

### e1 · The Leaning Tree Test: how much do buttress roots help a tall tree stand? (33 min)
- **question:** Three paper trees are the same height. One has no buttress roots, one has two and one has four. How far can the ground tilt before each tree falls? Does it matter which way the buttresses point?
- **predict:** Before you tilt the board, guess. Which tree will stand on the steepest tilt? Will a tree with two buttresses stand equally well whichever way the buttresses point? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Make all three trunks from the same paper, the same height and the same width. Cut all six buttresses the same size. Use the same board, and lift it at the same slow speed each time. Test each tree three times, because one try can be a fluke. Switch off the fan, so that no wind pushes the trees. Only the buttresses change.
- **record:** Draw a table with five rows. Row 1 is tree A. Rows 2 and 3 are tree B, with buttresses in line with the slope and then across the slope. Rows 4 and 5 are tree C, with a buttress pointing downhill and then a gap pointing downhill. For each row write the three heights, and circle the middle one.
- **results table:** Tree | Which way the buttresses point | Height of the board's high end when the tree fell: try 1, try 2 and try 3 (centimetres) | The middle one of the three heights
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the paper, the card and the board. Say that you will make three paper trees and tilt the ground under each. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* A rainforest tree is very tall, and her roots are shallow. We will make three paper trees. One has no buttress roots, one has two and one has four. Then we tilt the ground. Which tree will stand on the steepest tilt? Does it matter which way the buttresses point? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Three paper trees stand on a board. We tilt the board. Which tree stands longest? Does the direction matter? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think that tree will stand longest? (good: She has a wider bottom.; The fins prop her up.; It is like standing with your feet apart.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Roll each sheet the long way round the broomstick, into a tube about 3 centimetres wide. Tape the edge and slide the tube off. Cut 6 right-angled triangles from the card. Each is 5 centimetres along the bottom and 8 centimetres up the side. Leave tree A plain. Tape 2 triangles to the bottom of tree B, on opposite sides. Tape 4 triangles to tree C, evenly spaced round the trunk. The 5-centimetre edge of every triangle must sit flat on the table.  
  *Parent says:* All three trunks are the same paper and the same size. Tree A has no buttresses. Tree B has two, opposite each other. Tree C has four, all round. Check that each tree stands straight and that every buttress touches the table.  
  *Child mode:* Roll three paper trunks. Cut six card triangles. Tree A stays plain. Tree B gets two buttresses. Tree C gets four.
  - Note: Tape each triangle on both of its sides, so that it stands square to the trunk. If a trunk leans, trim its bottom edge flat with scissors.
  - Check: Why are all three paper trunks the same height and width? → **So that only the buttresses differ**
- **s3 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Lay the board flat on a table. Tape the second pencil across the board, about 5 centimetres from one end. That end is the low end. Switch off the fan. Stand tree A on the board, just touching the uphill side of the pencil.  
  *Parent says:* The pencil is a kerb. It stops a tree from sliding down the board, so that the tree can only tip. We will lift the other end slowly and watch.  
  *Child mode:* Tape a pencil across the low end of the board. Switch off the fan. Stand tree A against the pencil.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* One person lifts the high end of the board slowly, about one finger-width each second. The other holds a ruler upright on the table beside the high end. Stop lifting the moment the tree falls. Read how high the end of the board is above the table. Test tree A three times and write each height.  
  *Parent says:* Lift slowly and keep your eyes on the tree. Stop when the tree falls. How many centimetres is the end of the board above the table? We do it three times, because one try can be a fluke.  
  *Child mode:* Lift the high end slowly. Stop when tree A falls. Read the height. Do it three times.
  - Note: Tree A usually falls when the end of a 30-centimetre board is only 2 or 3 centimetres up.
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand tree B against the pencil with one buttress pointing straight downhill and the other uphill. Tilt and measure three times. Then turn tree B a quarter turn, so that both buttresses point across the slope. Tilt and measure three times again.  
  *Parent says:* Now tree B. First the two buttresses point down the slope and up the slope. Then we turn the tree, so that they point to the sides. Watch which way tree B falls each time. Are the two results the same?  
  *Child mode:* Test tree B with a buttress pointing downhill. Then turn the tree sideways. Test again. Compare.
  - Ask: What did you notice when tree B was turned sideways? (good: She fell almost as soon as the plain tree.; The buttresses did not help in that direction.; She fell between the two buttresses.)
- **s6 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Stand tree C against the pencil with one buttress pointing straight downhill. Tilt and measure three times. Then turn the tree a little, so that a gap between two buttresses points downhill. Tilt and measure three times again.  
  *Parent says:* Last, tree C, with four buttresses. First one buttress points straight downhill. Then a gap points downhill. Is tree C equally steady both ways?  
  *Child mode:* Test tree C with a buttress pointing downhill. Then point a gap downhill. Test again.
- **s7 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at the table together and circle the middle height in each row. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your five rows. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. A storm can blow from any side. So how many buttresses does a real tree need, and where?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: Why did a buttress pointing downhill help so much? (good: It made the bottom wider on that side.; The tree had to lift over the tip of the buttress to fall.; The edge she tips over was farther out.)
  - Ask: Why do rainforest trees need buttresses more than a neem tree in a dry town? (good: Their roots are shallow.; They are much taller.; The soil is wet and soft.)
  - Note: In most homes tree A falls at 2 or 3 centimetres. With a buttress pointing downhill, trees B and C stand until about 10 or 12 centimetres. Tree B turned sideways falls as early as tree A. Tree C with a gap pointing downhill falls a little earlier than with a buttress pointing downhill. Write down what you really found. The reason: a standing thing falls when its weight passes beyond the edge of its base. A thin trunk has a base only 3 centimetres wide, so a small tilt is enough. A buttress pointing downhill moves that edge 5 centimetres farther out. A buttress pointing sideways adds nothing in the downhill direction. Wind can come from any side, so real trees grow several buttresses all round. Rainforest soil keeps its plant food in the top few centimetres, so even giant trees have shallow roots. The paper leaves one thing out. Real buttresses are joined to roots in the ground. On the side the wind comes from, they also pull like the ropes of a tent.
  - Think: Tree C fell a little sooner when a gap pointed downhill. How would you place six buttresses to make the gaps smaller?
### e2 · The Two Lands Test: does the best tower for still air also win in wind? (35 min)
- **question:** Two villages build towers from the same newspaper. One village tests its towers in still air, and the other tests them in wind. After three towers each, are the two best towers alike? What happens when they swap lands?
- **predict:** Before you build, guess. After three towers, will the Still Land and the Wind Land build the same kind of tower? Which tower will win when the two swap lands? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every tower gets the same materials: 2 single sheets of newspaper and 30 centimetres of tape. Every tower gets 4 minutes. Both lands build three towers. Every Wind Land tower gets the same 5 fans, from the same notebook and the same distance. Only the test differs between the two lands.
- **record:** Draw a table with two rows, one for the Still Land and one for the Wind Land. Write the score of towers 1, 2 and 3 in each row. Then draw a second table with two rows and two columns. Write what each land's best tower scored in the Still Land and in the Wind Land.
- **results table:** Land | Tower 1: height in centimetres, or 0 if it failed the test | Tower 2 | Tower 3 | What was kept and what was changed
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Explain the two lands. A Still Land tower must stand for 10 seconds. A Wind Land tower must stand through 5 hard fans from a notebook, one big step away. Your child builds for the Still Land and you build for the Wind Land. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down.  
  *Parent says:* We are two villages. Yours is in the Still Land, and mine is in the Wind Land. Each village builds three towers, and each tower learns from the one before. A tower that passes its test scores its height in centimetres. After three towers, will our villages build the same kind of tower? What will happen when we swap? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* You build for the Still Land. A grown-up builds for the Wind Land. Will the towers end up alike? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think the towers will be alike, or different? (good: Wind knocks tall things over.; In still air you can go as high as you like.; Each village only sees its own test.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. A child working alone builds all three Still Land towers first, and then all three Wind Land towers. Allow about 50 minutes.
- **s2 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each builder takes 2 sheets of newspaper and one strip of tape. Set the timer for 4 minutes and build. A tower must stand on the floor by itself, and nothing may be taped to the floor. Then test each tower in its own land. Measure a tower that passes. A tower that fails scores 0. Write the scores.  
  *Parent says:* Four minutes, starting now. Build the tallest tower you can that will pass your land's test. Time is up. Now we test. Yours must stand for ten seconds. Mine must stand through five fans.  
  *Child mode:* Build your first tower in four minutes. Stand it up. Count to ten. Measure it.
  - Note: First towers often fail. That is useful: the next tower can learn from it. Fan as hard as you can each time, so that the wind is the same for every tower.
- **s3 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Each builder looks at their own first tower and its score. Each says one thing that worked and one thing that failed. Keep what worked and change one thing. Build a second tower from 2 fresh sheets and a fresh strip of tape, in 4 minutes. Test it and write the score and the change.  
  *Parent says:* Look at your first tower. What worked? What failed? Keep the part that worked and change one thing. You are the next generation of your village, so you start with what the last builder learned.  
  *Child mode:* Look at your first tower. Keep what worked. Change one thing. Build tower two and test it.
  - Ask: What did you keep, and what did you change? (good: I kept the rolled tube and joined a second one on top.; I kept the height and spread the bottom out.; I made the bottom heavier.)
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Do the same again for tower 3. Each builder keeps what worked in tower 2, changes one thing, and builds from fresh sheets in 4 minutes. Test and write the score and the change. Then stand the best tower of each land side by side.  
  *Parent says:* One more generation. Keep what worked and change one thing. Now put our two best towers side by side. Look at them. How are they different?  
  *Child mode:* Build tower three the same way. Then put the two best towers side by side.
  - Ask: How are the two best towers different? (good: The Still Land tower is taller and thinner.; The Wind Land tower has wide feet.; The Wind Land tower used paper on its base that the other used for height.)
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Now swap. Take the best Still Land tower to the Wind Land and fan it 5 times. Then measure the best Wind Land tower as a Still Land tower. Write the four scores in the second table.  
  *Parent says:* Now the swap. Your best tower comes to my windy land. Five fans. Did it stand? And my best tower goes to your still land. What does it score there?  
  *Child mode:* Swap the best towers. Fan the Still Land tower five times. Measure the Wind Land tower.
  - Note: If your Wind Land tower also beat the Still Land tower in still air, your wind was too gentle. Fan from half a step away, and build one more tower in each land.
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at both tables together. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Look at the four scores. Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. Which tower is the better tower? What would you need to know before you answered?  
  *Child mode:* Say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened.
  - Ask: Which tower is the better tower? (good: It depends on the land.; The tall one in still air, the wide one in wind.; You have to ask where it will stand.)
  - Ask: Where in India do people build differently because of what their land tests? (good: Stilt houses where the river floods.; Flat mud roofs in Ladakh, where it hardly rains.; Steep roofs in Kerala and Meghalaya, where it pours.)
  - Note: In most homes the Still Land towers grow taller and thinner, to about a metre. The Wind Land towers stay under half a metre and grow wide feet. After the swap, the tall tower falls in the wind and scores 0. The wide tower stands in still air but scores less than the tall one. Write down what you really found. The reason: each village improved its tower using only its own land's test. With the same paper, height and a wide base compete, so each land pushed the design a different way. Real knowledge grows the same way, over hundreds of years. Pokkali rice was chosen by tidal fields, kunds by desert storms, and stilt houses by floods. Moved to another land, each may fail, as the tall tower did.
  - Check: A short, wide tower stood in the wind, and a tall, thin tower won in still air. What does this show? → **The best design depends on the land**
  - Think: Suppose the wind came only one year in four. Which tower would a careful village choose? What would the village lose?
### e3 · The Guided Root Test: can a slope lead a growing root across a gap? (37 min, 7 days)
- **question:** A root grows downwards. A wet sloping strip leads away to one side. Will a moong root follow the strip across an 8-centimetre gap? How many centimetres does the root grow in a day?
- **predict:** Before you set up, guess. Will the roots at the top of the strip follow the slope to the other bank, or leave the strip? How many days will they take? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use seeds from the same packet, soaked for the same night. Put 3 seeds at the top of the strip and 3 seeds on the same bank away from the strip. Both groups get the same wet cotton, the same box and the same light. Use three seeds in each group, because one seed may not sprout. Only the strip differs.
- **safety:** A grown-up cuts the strip from the plastic bottle, and trims any sharp corners.
- **record:** Draw a table with one row for each day from day 3 to day 7. Write the length of the longest guided root along the strip. Write yes or no for whether an unguided root has reached the low bank. On day 7, draw the box from the side and show where every root went.
- **results table:** Day | Length of the longest guided root along the strip (centimetres) | Has any unguided root reached the low bank?
- **schedule:** day 1: Guess what the roots will do. Soak 8 moong seeds in water overnight. (5 min); day 2: Build the two banks and the sloping strip in the box. Place 3 guided seeds and 3 unguided seeds. Close the lid. (12 min); day 3: Measure the longest guided root along the strip. Add a few drops of water. Close the lid. (3 min); day 4: Measure, add a few drops of water, and close the lid. (3 min); day 5: Measure, add a few drops of water, and close the lid. (3 min); day 6: Measure, add a few drops of water, and close the lid. (3 min); day 7: Measure for the last time. Work out how fast the roots grew, and explain what you found. (8 min)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the box, the bowl and the strip. Explain that seeds will sprout on a high bank, with a sloping strip leading to a low bank. Ask for the guess and the reason, and write both down. Then put 8 moong seeds in a small bowl of water for the night.  
  *Parent says:* A root grows downwards. We will give some roots a wet sloping path that leads away to one side. Will they follow the path to the other bank, or leave it? How many days will it take? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Roots grow down. Will a sloping path lead them sideways? Guess, and say why. Then soak eight moong seeds.
  - Ask: Why do you think the roots will follow the strip, or leave it? (good: They cannot go through the plastic, so they will slide along it.; Roots go where the water is.; They might fall off the side.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start.
  - Wait: Leave the seeds in water overnight
- **s2 (12 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Stand the steel bowl upside down at one end of the box. Cover its top with wet cotton. This is the high bank. Put a flat pad of wet cotton on the floor of the box, 8 centimetres from the bowl. This is the low bank. Rest the plastic strip from the top of the bowl down to the low bank. Lay the tissue on the strip and wet it. Tuck 3 soaked seeds under a wisp of wet cotton at the top of the strip. Put 3 more on the cotton at the far side of the bowl, away from the strip. Close the lid. Keep the box in a bright place, out of direct sun.  
  *Parent says:* The bowl is one bank of a stream, and the cotton pad is the other bank. The strip is our guide. These three seeds sit at the top of the guide. Those three sit on the same bank with no guide. Everything else is the same for both groups.  
  *Child mode:* Build the high bank and the low bank. Slope the wet strip between them. Place three seeds at the top. Place three away from it.
  - Note: The cotton and the tissue should be wet through but not dripping. The two spare seeds replace any that do not swell. The closed lid keeps the air inside damp, so that the root tips do not dry out.
  - Check: Why do three seeds sit on the high bank away from the strip? → **To compare with the seeds on the strip**
  - Wait: Leave the box closed until tomorrow
- **s3 (12 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On each of days 3, 4, 5 and 6, look through the side of the box. Hold a ruler beside the strip and measure the longest guided root. Write the length. Look at the unguided seeds and write whether any root has reached the low bank. Open the lid, add a few drops of water to the cotton and the tissue, and close it again.  
  *Parent says:* Look, without touching. How long is the longest root on the strip today? Which way is the tip pointing? What are the roots of the other three seeds doing? Add a few drops of water and close the lid.  
  *Child mode:* Each day, measure the longest root on the strip. Check the other seeds. Add a few drops of water.
  - Ask: What are the roots of the unguided seeds doing? (good: They have gone down into the cotton on the bowl.; They are curling round on the high bank.; None has reached the other side.)
  - Note: This step is 3 minutes on each of four days. If a root tip has left the edge of the strip, leave it. Write down what really happened.
  - Wait: Come back at the same time each day until day 7
- **s4 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* On day 7 measure for the last time. Subtract the day 3 length from the day 7 length. Divide the answer by 4, to find the growth in one day. Ask your child to say what they thought would happen, what they saw, and why it happened. After they have answered, read them the explanation in the note below. Then plant the seedlings in a pot.  
  *Parent says:* This is the last measurement. How many centimetres did the root grow in one day? Now say what you thought would happen, what you saw, and why it happened. How is our strip like the hollow palm trunk that Khasi builders use?  
  *Child mode:* Measure one last time. Work out the growth in one day. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Ask: Why did the guided roots cross the gap? (good: They grew down, and the strip turned down into along.; The wet tissue gave them water all the way.; They could not go through the plastic.)
  - Ask: A fig root in Meghalaya must cross 20 metres. Why does a bridge take up to 15 years? (good: The roots must cross first, and that alone takes years.; Then they have to grow thick enough to carry people.; New roots are added every year.)
  - Note: In most homes the guided roots grow 1 to 3 centimetres a day. They reach the low bank by day 6 or 7. The unguided roots stay in the cotton of the high bank. If a root left the strip or dried up, write that down. Then try again with a wider strip and wetter tissue. The reason: the tip of a root senses which way is down. Tiny heavy grains inside the cells of the tip settle to the low side, and the root steers that way. On the strip she cannot go straight down, so she follows the slope. Khasi and Jaintia builders use the same habit of the rubber fig. They lead her hanging roots through a hollowed trunk of the betel nut palm, laid across the stream. The trunk guides the roots and keeps them damp. On the far bank the roots enter the soil and begin to thicken. The builders add more roots every year.
  - Think: The root did the growing and you chose the path. Who made the crossing?

### a3 · Mud Without Air: how much air does a flood push out of soil? (15 min)
- **record:** Two lines in your notebook. Write how many tablespoons of water went into the loose soil before water stood on top. Write the same for the pressed soil.
- **s1 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill one tumbler to the top with dry, loose soil. Do not press it. Stand it on the plate. Ask for a guess and a reason before adding any water.  
  *Parent says:* This tumbler is full of soil. Can any water still go in? How many tablespoons do you think it will take before water stands on top? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* The tumbler is full of soil. How many spoons of water can still go in? Why?
  - Note: Many children guess one or two spoons, because the tumbler looks full.
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Add water to the soil one level tablespoon at a time. Pour slowly near the middle, and wait for each spoonful to sink in. Watch for bubbles. Stop when water stands on top and does not sink. Write the number of spoons.  
  *Parent says:* Pour slowly, and watch the top of the soil. What is coming out as the water goes in? Keep counting. Stop when the water stays on top.  
  *Child mode:* Add water one spoon at a time. Watch for bubbles. Count the spoons. Stop when water stays on top.
  - Ask: What came out of the soil as the water went in? (good: Bubbles of air.; The air that was between the bits of soil.)
  - Note: A 200-millilitre tumbler of loose pot soil usually takes 5 to 7 tablespoons. That is about 75 to 105 millilitres, close to half the tumbler. Very dry soil takes in water slowly, so be patient.
- **s3 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Fill the second tumbler with dry soil. Press the soil down hard with a thumb, add more, and press again until the tumbler is full. Add water by the tablespoon in the same way, and count.  
  *Parent says:* Now we press the soil down hard, the way feet or heavy wheels would. Fill it right up again. Will this tumbler take more water or less? Count the spoons.  
  *Child mode:* Press the soil down hard in the second tumbler. Fill it up. Add water and count again.
  - Note: Pressed soil usually takes 3 or 4 tablespoons. Pressing squeezes out some of the air spaces.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Compare the two numbers. Ask where the air went, and what a root would find in soil that stays full of water. Read the explanation in the note after your child has answered.  
  *Parent says:* The loose soil took all those spoons of water. What was in that space before? Where is that air now? A root needs air as well as water. What would a root find in mud that the sea covers twice a day?  
  *Child mode:* What was in the soil before the water? Where is it now? What would a root find there?
  - Ask: What would a root find in mud that is always full of water? (good: No air.; Water in every space where air used to be.)
  - Note: Nearly half of a tumbler of loose soil is air, in the spaces between the bits of soil. Roots and soil animals breathe that air. Water fills the spaces and pushes the air out as bubbles. In the Sundarbans the tide refills the mud twice a day, so air never returns. Most trees would die there. Mangroves grow breathing roots that stand above the mud like pencils. Each one has tiny openings that take in air at low tide. Rice has another answer: air tubes inside her stems carry air down to her roots.
  - Check: Why do many mangrove trees grow roots that stick up out of the mud? → **To take in air above the wet mud**

  - Ask: Why did farmers in each place keep their own variety? (good: It grew well in their soil and rain.; It stood up to their floods, salt or dry months.; They liked its taste for their own dishes.)
  - Note: India's farmers once grew many thousands of varieties of rice alone. Each was chosen, year after year, by saving seed from the plants that did best on that land. So a variety carries the choices of generations of farmers. Debal Deb is a scientist and farmer in Odisha. He keeps more than 1,000 old rice varieties alive by growing each of them every year. When all farmers grow one variety, one bad year or one disease can harm all the fields at once.
  - Check: Farmers on a salty coast saved seed each year from the rice plants that survived. What happened over many years? → **Their rice became better at standing salt**
### a5 · Two Villages and a Wave: was it the mangroves? (15 min)
- **record:** Your own copy of the table of four villages. Under it, two sentences: which two pairs of villages make a fair comparison, and what those pairs show.
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Copy the table of four villages into the notebook, or read it from the screen. Cover rows C and D with the sheet of paper. Ask your child to compare villages A and B.  
  *Parent says:* The same giant wave struck these villages. Look only at A and B. Which was damaged less? What do you think protected that village? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Look at villages A and B. Which was damaged less? What protected that village? Why?
  - Note: Most people say the mangroves saved village A. Do not correct this yet.
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to read across rows A and B again. They list every difference between the two villages, and not only the trees.  
  *Parent says:* Read the two rows again, slowly. How many things are different between village A and village B? Could any of the other differences explain the damage?  
  *Child mode:* List every difference between A and B. Could another difference explain the damage?
  - Ask: What else differs between villages A and B? (good: A is five times as far from the sea.; A stands 2 metres higher.; Three things differ at once, so we cannot tell which one mattered.)
- **s3 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Uncover rows C and D. Ask your child to find two villages that differ only in having mangroves. There are two such pairs. For each pair, work out the difference in damage.  
  *Parent says:* Now look at all four villages. Find two that are the same distance from the sea and the same height, where only the trees differ. There are two such pairs. What do the trees change in each pair?  
  *Child mode:* Uncover C and D. Find pairs where only the trees differ. What do the trees change?
  - Ask: Which pairs make a fair comparison? (good: A and C: both far and high. 5 houses against 10.; B and D: both near and low. 80 houses against 60.)
- **s4 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask what the fair pairs show, and what mattered most. Read the explanation in the note after your child has answered.  
  *Parent says:* In the fair pairs, how much did the mangroves help? Now compare A with D. Both have mangroves. What made the bigger difference there: trees, or distance and height?  
  *Child mode:* How much did the trees help? What made the bigger difference?
  - Note: In this made-up table the trees helped a little: 5 houses against 10, and 60 against 80. Distance and height helped far more: 5 against 60 for the two villages with trees. This is close to what scientists argued after the tsunami of 2004. The first reports compared villages like A and B. Critics showed that the safer villages were also farther from the sea or higher. Scientists now agree that mangroves weaken storm floods and small waves. How much they help against a giant tsunami is still argued.
  - Check: Village A, behind mangroves, was damaged less than village B. Why does this not prove that the mangroves protected A? → **A was also farther away and higher**

## Challenges
### c1 · Split the Stream: water three plants from one trickle (30 min)
- **problem:** In the Jaintia hills of Meghalaya, farmers lead water from a hill stream to their pepper and betel plants. The water runs along split bamboo. They divide the flow again and again, until each plant gets only a few drops a minute. Build a set of channels that takes one thin stream of water and shares it between three tumblers standing apart.
- **your turn:** Now design your own channels. Draw the plan first, with the cup, the three tumblers and every gutter. Test with 300 millilitres. Measure the depth in each tumbler, change one thing, and test again.
- **constraints:** The water starts from one plastic cup with a single small hole in the bottom.; The channels are made only from kitchen foil or from strips cut from plastic bottles. Books, clothes pegs and tape may hold them up.; The three tumblers are the same size and stand at least 15 centimetres apart from one another.; Pour 300 millilitres of water through the cup, a little at a time. Nobody may touch the channels while the water runs.; Every tumbler must end with water at least 1 centimetre deep. The deepest must be no more than twice as deep as the shallowest.
- **general:** Fold a strip of foil along the middle to make a V-shaped gutter. Give every gutter a gentle slope, about one finger-width down for each hand-length.; Let the end of one gutter pour onto the place where two lower gutters meet. The stream then splits in two.; Split the stream in two first. Then split one of the halves again, and make up the difference by widening or narrowing a gutter.
- **creative:** Cut a small notch in the side of a gutter, so that part of the water leaves early. Bamboo farmers cut holes in their pipes in the same way.; Hang a strip of wet cloth over the edge of a gutter as a wick. It leads a slow, steady share of water away.; Pinch a gutter narrower further down, so that less water passes. The farmers use thinner bamboo as the pipes get near the plants.
- **s1 (5 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Read the problem together. A grown-up makes one hole in the bottom of the cup with the point of a compass or a nail. Hold the cup over one tumbler and pour in a little water, to see the thin stream. Ask how one stream could be shared between three tumblers.  
  *Parent says:* Farmers in Meghalaya share one stream between hundreds of plants, using split bamboo. We have one stream and three tumblers. How could we split a stream of water? List every idea. Then draw your plan.  
  *Child mode:* One stream must fill three tumblers. How can a stream be split? List ideas. Draw a plan.
  - Ask: How could one stream be split into two? (good: Let it fall on a ridge where two gutters meet.; Cut a hole in the side of a gutter.; Lead some away along a strip of cloth.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child builds the channels on a tray or on the bathroom floor. Stand the cup on two pencils laid across a gap between two piles of books. Help to hold things while tape is put on, but let your child decide the design.  
  *Parent says:* Build your channels. Every gutter needs a gentle slope, or the water will sit still or rush over the side. Check each one by letting a few drops run down.  
  *Child mode:* Build your channels. Give every gutter a gentle slope. Test each one with a few drops.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Pour 300 millilitres of water into the cup, a little at a time. Do not touch the channels while it runs. When the cup is empty, measure the depth of water in each tumbler with the ruler. Write the three depths.  
  *Parent says:* Pour in the water, and hands off. Watch where the stream goes at each split. Now measure. How deep is the water in each tumbler? Which got the most, and why?  
  *Child mode:* Pour in the water. Do not touch. Then measure the depth in each tumbler.
  - Note: A first design often sends most of the water one way. That is normal. A split is very sensitive to which way the gutter leans.
- **s4 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child changes one thing to make the shares more equal, and tests again. Then compare the design with the bamboo system of Meghalaya, using the note below.  
  *Parent says:* Which tumbler got too little? Change one thing to send more water there. Test again. In Meghalaya, about 20 litres a minute enter the bamboo. How do you think that becomes a few drops at each plant?  
  *Child mode:* Which tumbler got too little? Change one thing. Test again.
  - Ask: How does a big stream become a few drops at each plant? (good: It is split again and again.; The pipes get thinner each time.; A little water leaves at every plant along the way.)
  - Note: Praise a clear account of what went wrong as much as equal shares. In the bamboo systems of Meghalaya, about 18 to 20 litres a minute enter the first pipe. The water travels hundreds of metres, and the flow is divided at every stage. By the end, each plant receives between 20 and 80 drops a minute. The system needs no pump, because the stream starts above the garden.
### c2 · Pass It On: make your best tower outlive you (28 min)
- **problem:** In the Two Lands Test your village learned how to build its best tower. Now that knowledge is only in your head and your hands. Find a way to pass it on to a new builder who did not see your towers. You may not speak or touch anything while they build. Their tower must score at least three quarters of your best score, in your land's test.
- **your turn:** Now choose your own way to pass the tower on. Test it with a builder and score their tower. Ask them which part of your message was unclear. Change one thing, and try again with another builder.

They get 6 minutes, because they must also read your message.; While they build, you may not speak, point or touch.; Their tower is tested in your land's test: 10 seconds standing for the Still Land, or 5 hard fans for the Wind Land.

  *Child mode:* Your best tower must be built by someone else. How do people pass on what they know?
  - Ask: How do people pass on what they know? (good: By showing, and letting the learner try.; By stories, songs and sayings.; By writing and drawing.; By handing on a seed, a tool or a sample.)
- **s2 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Your child chooses up to two kinds of message and prepares them in 10 minutes. Do not help with the content. Keep the new builder out of the room.

  *Child mode:* Choose up to two kinds of message. Prepare them in ten minutes.
- **s3 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Bring in the new builder. Give them the message, 2 sheets of newspaper and 30 centimetres of tape. Set the timer for 6 minutes. Your child watches in silence. Then test the tower in your child's land and measure it.

  *Child mode:* Give the builder your message. Watch in silence for six minutes. Then test their tower.
  - Note: Three quarters of 100 centimetres is 75 centimetres. Work out the target from your child's own best score before the test.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask the new builder which part of the message was unclear. Ask your child what the builder did differently, and what the message left out. Then talk about the questions below.

## Worksheets
### India's regions: the main ideas (core, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) What is an ecological region?  
   Options: A large area with its own climate / A state with its own language / Any place that has a forest / The capital city of a country  
   **Answer:** A large area with its own climate. *Why:* An ecological region is a large area with its own climate, landforms, plants and animals. Its edges follow rain and height, and state borders do not matter to it.
2. (mcq) Which four things decide most of what can live in a region?  
   Options: Sun, Moon, stars and clouds / Rain, height, salt and soil / Roads, towns, shops and schools / Colour, shape, size and smell  
   **Answer:** Rain, height, salt and soil. *Why:* Rain decides between forest and desert. Height decides how cold the air is. Salt and soil decide which plants can grow in the ground.
3. (fill) The drier land on the far side of a mountain range is called a rain ____.  
   **Answer:** ['shadow']. *Why:* The air drops its rain while climbing the near side. The far side, which gets little, is the rain shadow.
4. (fill) A living being found naturally in only one place in the world is called ____.  
   **Answer:** ['endemic']. *Why:* Endemic means found in one place only. The lion-tailed macaque is endemic to the Western Ghats.
5. (truefalse) Kanyakumari is the southernmost point of India.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Kanyakumari is the southern tip of the mainland. Indira Point, on Great Nicobar Island, lies farther south.
6. (truefalse) A coral is a tiny animal.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. A coral has a soft body, a mouth and a ring of arms. She builds a stony cup, and millions of cups make a reef.
7. (match) Match each region to its kind of land.  
   **Answer:** Ladakh → A cold desert behind the Himalaya; The Thar → A hot desert of sand and rare storms; The Sundarbans → Salty mud covered by the tide; The Western Ghats → Wet hills facing the monsoon wind; The Andaman Islands → Rainforest islands ringed by coral reefs. *Why:* Each region is set by its rain, height and salt. Ladakh is high and dry, the Thar is low and dry, and the Sundarbans is salty and wet.
8. (sort) Sort these. Is each one a plant's answer to the land, or people's answer?  
   **Answer:** buttress roots → A plant's answer; breathing roots → A plant's answer; roots that go many metres down → A plant's answer; a house on stilts → People's answer; a covered rain tank → People's answer; a flat mud roof → People's answer. *Why:* Plants answer with their bodies, over many generations. People answer with things they make, and teach the next generation how.
9. (order) Put these in order, as wet sea air crosses the Western Ghats.  
   **Answer:** ['Wet air blows in from the sea', 'The hills push the air upwards', 'The air cools as it rises', 'Rain falls on the sea side', 'Drier air sinks down the far side']. *Why:* Air must rise before it cools, and cool before it rains. What sinks on the far side has already lost most of its water.
10. (mcq) How do buttress roots help a tall rainforest tree?  
   Options: They reach water deep down / They frighten animals away / They make her base wider / They store rainwater  
   **Answer:** They make her base wider. *Why:* A wide base means the tree must lean much farther before she falls. Her other roots are shallow, because the soil's plant food is near the top.
11. (mcq) A tall paper tube has two buttresses. Which tilt makes the tube fall soonest?  
   Options: A tilt across the buttresses / A tilt along the buttresses / Both are the same  
   **Answer:** A tilt across the buttresses. *Why:* A buttress helps only in the direction in which it points. Tilted sideways, the tube tips between the buttresses, as a plain tube would.
12. (write) Mangaluru gets about 350 centimetres of rain a year, and Bengaluru about 100. Explain why.  
   **Answer:** Mangaluru is on the sea side of the Western Ghats. The wet monsoon air is pushed up the hills, cools, and drops its rain there. Bengaluru is behind the hills, in the rain shadow.. *Why:* A strong answer names the hills and says that rising air cools and rains. It also says that little water is left for the far side.
13. (write) Why does a mangrove tree need roots that stick up out of the mud?  
   **Answer:** The mud is full of water and has almost no air. Roots need oxygen. The breathing roots take in air above the mud and pass it down to the buried roots.. *Why:* A strong answer says that waterlogged mud has no air, and that roots need air as well as water.
14. (draw) Draw a hill range from the side, with wind from the sea. Mark the wet side and the dry side.  
   **Answer:** A hill in side view with the sea on one side. Arrows of wind rise up the sea side, with clouds and rain there. The far side has few clouds and is labelled dry, or rain shadow.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows the air rising, the rain on the side facing the wind, and the dry side behind.
15. (perspective) A village in the wet Khasi hills needs a new bridge across a stream.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. One reason is that cut wood and bamboo rot within a few years in very heavy rain. Another is that a root bridge cannot carry anyone for many years. A strong answer also says what the other view gets right.. *Why:* Each view has something right in it. A strong answer uses the rain of the Khasi hills and the time a root bridge takes. It also takes the children's needs seriously.
### Unique places, and the knowledge that fits them (stretch, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) Why do islands and high hilltops hold many endemic living beings?  
   Options: They are cut off from other places / They get the most rain / They have the most people / They are closest to the Sun  
   **Answer:** They are cut off from other places. *Why:* Living beings who are cut off for a very long time change in their own way. Sea cuts off an island, and hot valleys cut off a cold hilltop.
2. (mcq) Why does a living root bridge suit Meghalaya better than a wooden one?  
   Options: Wooden bridges are too heavy / Cut wood rots fast in heavy rain / Wood is hard to find there / Living roots cross in one month  
   **Answer:** Cut wood rots fast in heavy rain. *Why:* The Khasi hills get about 12 metres of rain a year. Dead wood rots in a few years, and a living root grows thicker each year.
3. (mcq) The sangai deer lives on one lake only. What follows for her safety?  
   Options: She must be very common / She can move to another lake / Harm to that lake harms every sangai / She is safe everywhere else  
   **Answer:** Harm to that lake harms every sangai. *Why:* An endemic being has one home. If the floating mats of Loktak Lake are harmed, there is no second place where sangai live.
4. (truefalse) An endemic living being is always rare.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Endemic means found in one place only. Some endemic beings are common in that one place. Rare means few are left.
5. (truefalse) The wettest hills of Meghalaya can run short of water in winter.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. The rain falls in a few months and runs quickly off the steep slopes and thin soil. Little is stored for the dry months.
6. (match) Match each piece of traditional knowledge to the problem of its land.  
   **Answer:** A kund → Rain comes in only a few storms a year; A house on stilts → The river floods most years; Pokkali rice → Fields turn salty when the tide comes in; Meltwater channels → Almost no rain, with ice on the peaks above; Dripping bamboo pipes → A stream runs above a hill garden. *Why:* Each practice answers what its own land tests: storms, floods, salt, ice or a slope. That is why each one is found where it is.
7. (sort) Sort these living beings by the region where each is endemic.  
   **Answer:** lion-tailed macaque → Western Ghats; Nilgiri tahr → Western Ghats; neelakurinji → Western Ghats; sangai deer → North-East India; pitcher plant of Meghalaya → North-East India; Nicobar megapode → Andaman and Nicobar Islands; Narcondam hornbill → Andaman and Nicobar Islands. *Why:* High wet hills, a floating lake and far islands are all cut off or unlike the land around them. So each has living beings found nowhere else.
8. (odd) Three of these are answers to too much water. Which is the odd one out?  
   Options: A house on stilts / A steep tiled roof / Breathing roots / A kund  
   **Answer:** 3. *Why:* Stilts, a steep roof and breathing roots all deal with floods, heavy rain or soaked mud. A kund deals with too little water.
9. (count) Air cools 6 degrees for every 1,000 metres of height. How much cooler is a town 2,000 metres up?  
   **Answer:** 12. *Why:* 12 degrees. 2,000 metres is 2 lots of 1,000 metres, and 2 times 6 is 12.
10. (order) Put the growing of a living root bridge in order.  
   **Answer:** ['Lay a hollow palm trunk across the stream', 'Lead young fig roots into the trunk', 'The roots reach the far bank and enter the soil', 'The roots thicken and join over many years', 'People walk across']. *Why:* The roots need a guide before they can cross. They must take hold on the far bank before they can thicken. Only then can the bridge carry people.
11. (write) A flat mud roof is right in Ladakh and wrong in Meghalaya. Explain why, using rain figures.  
   **Answer:** Ladakh gets about 10 centimetres of rain a year, so a mud roof stays dry and firm. The Khasi hills of Meghalaya get about 1,190 centimetres. That much rain would wash a mud roof away.. *Why:* A strong answer gives both rain figures. It says that the roof was tested only by the land where people built it.
12. (write) How is traditional knowledge built? Describe the steps in three sentences.  
   **Answer:** Someone tries a way of doing something. If it works, others copy it, and if it fails, people remember and warn their children. Each generation keeps what worked and changes a little.. *Why:* A strong answer includes trying, keeping what works, and passing it on over many generations.
13. (write) Look at the table. Which two villages show most fairly what the mangroves did? Explain.  
   **Answer:** A and C, or B and D. In each pair the villages are the same distance from the sea and the same height. Only the mangroves differ, so any difference in damage comes from the trees.. *Why:* A strong answer chooses a pair that differs in one thing only. It says why A and B do not make a fair pair.
14. (perspective) Scientists described the purple frog in 2003. People in the hills of Kerala already knew her.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer separates the two kinds of knowing. Local people knew where and when the frog appears. The scientists added her family tree.. *Why:* People really disagree about this, so no answer is the only right one. A strong answer says exactly what each side knew.
15. (label) Draw a kund from the side. Label the saucer, the tank and the cover. Write what each does.  
   **Answer:** ['Saucer of hard ground: it catches the rain of a storm and slopes towards the tank', 'Tank: it stores the water under the ground for the whole year', 'Cover: it keeps out the Sun, the dust and animals']. *Why:* A strong answer links each part to the desert's problem. Rain is rare, so a wide area must catch it, and the Sun must not dry it up.
### Testing ideas about lands and knowledge (challenge, 15 questions)
1. (mcq) A new rice gave double the grain in tests. What should a coastal farmer ask first?  
   Options: Where was it tested? / What colour is the grain? / Who sells the seed? / How old is the variety?  
   **Answer:** Where was it tested?. *Why:* A result is true for the land where the test was done. A field of shallow fresh water does not test a rice for salt or deep floods.
2. (mcq) Bog layers showed grass on the Nilgiris 20,000 years ago. Which idea did this overturn?  
   Options: That bogs are wet / That the grasslands were ruined forest / That the Nilgiris are hills / That grass needs rain  
   **Answer:** That the grasslands were ruined forest. *Why:* Foresters had thought the grass was waste land left after people destroyed a forest. The layers showed that the grass was far older than any herd or plantation.
3. (mcq) Why is Ladakh both cold and dry?  
   Options: It has sandy soil / It has few trees / It is high, and behind the Himalaya / It is far from any river  
   **Answer:** It is high, and behind the Himalaya. *Why:* Height makes the air cold. The Himalaya stop the monsoon, so Ladakh lies in a rain shadow and gets about 10 centimetres of rain.
4. (mcq) Corals turn white, and may die, when the sea does what?  
   Options: Has a very high tide / Has too many fish / Becomes fresh after rain / Stays too warm for weeks  
   **Answer:** Stays too warm for weeks. *Why:* In water that stays too warm, corals push out the tiny algae who live inside them and share food. Without the algae they are white, and they slowly starve.
5. (truefalse) A method that was tested in one land is sure to work in every land.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A method is tested by the rain, height, salt and soil of one land. Where those differ, the method may fail, as the tall tower did in the wind.
6. (truefalse) Scientists agree that mangroves weaken storm floods.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Roots and trunks slow moving water. What scientists still argue about is how much mangroves help against a giant tsunami.
7. (count) Jaisalmer gets 20 centimetres of rain a year. How many litres fall on one square metre of ground?  
   **Answer:** 200. *Why:* 200 litres. One centimetre of rain on one square metre is 10 litres. 20 times 10 is 200.
8. (count) A root grows 2 centimetres a day. How many days does she need to cross a 10-metre stream?  
   **Answer:** 500. *Why:* 500 days. 10 metres is 1,000 centimetres, and 1,000 divided by 2 is 500. That is well over a year, before the root has thickened at all.
9. (fill) India's ten ecological regions were mapped in 1988, to check that each kind of land had a ____ area.  
   **Answer:** ['protected']. *Why:* W. A. Rodgers and H. S. Panwar drew the map to see whether every region had national parks or sanctuaries of its own.
10. (order) Put these places in order of yearly rain, from least to most.  
   **Answer:** ['Leh', 'Jaisalmer', 'Bengaluru', 'Mumbai', 'Mawsynram']. *Why:* Leh gets about 10 centimetres, Jaisalmer 20, Bengaluru 100, Mumbai 240 and Mawsynram about 1,190. Mountains explain the two ends of the list.
11. (write) Wattle trees grew very well on the Nilgiri grasslands. Why was planting them still a mistake?  
   **Answer:** The grassland was a natural, very old home for the Nilgiri tahr and many plants found nowhere else. The wattle spread over the grass, and the grass was lost. Growing well in a place is not the same as belonging there.. *Why:* A strong answer says what was lost, and says that the people who planted the trees had misread the land.
12. (write) In the Two Lands Test, which tower was better? Explain why the question needs one more word.  
   **Answer:** Neither was better everywhere. The tall tower was better in still air and the wide tower was better in wind. The question needs the word 'where'.. *Why:* A strong answer gives both results and says that 'better' always means better for some land.
13. (write) Name one kind of knowledge that works in every land, and one that fits one land only. Explain the difference.  
   **Answer:** A fair test, or the fact that rising air cools, works everywhere. A kund or a flat mud roof fits one kind of land. Laws of nature are the same everywhere, but designs must meet the rain, height, salt and soil of one place.. *Why:* A strong answer gives one example of each kind. It says that a design answers local conditions, while a law of nature does not change.
14. (perspective) A company makes a medicine from a plant that forest people have used for generations.  
   **Answer:** Any view with a reason. A strong answer says what each side added: the community knew the plant and what the plant can do, and the scientists tested and made a safe medicine. Many will choose sharing.. *Why:* This question has been argued in courts and parliaments. A strong answer is fair to both kinds of work.
15. (draw) Design a house for the Assam flood plain. Draw the house, label three features, and give the reason for each.  
   **Answer:** A house raised on tall stilts, with the floor above flood level. It has a steep roof for heavy rain and bamboo walls that dry quickly. The steps or ladder can be lifted, and a boat may be tied underneath.. *Why:* A strong design answers what this land tests: yearly floods, heavy rain and damp. Each label should name one of these.

## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which of these is an ecological region?  
   Options: The Thar desert / The city of Chennai / A cricket ground / A school  
   **Answer:** The Thar desert. *Why:* An ecological region is a large area with its own climate, plants and animals. A city or a school is a place people built inside a region.
2. (mcq) Two towns get the same sea wind. One lies behind a range of hills. Which town gets less rain?  
   Options: It changes every day / The town behind the hills / The town on the sea side / Both get the same  
   **Answer:** The town behind the hills. *Why:* The air drops most of its rain while climbing the side facing the wind. The town behind the hills is in the rain shadow.
3. (mcq) Ooty and Coimbatore are close together. Why is Ooty much cooler?  
   Options: Ooty has more people / Ooty is farther south / Ooty is far higher / Ooty is nearer the sea  
   **Answer:** Ooty is far higher. *Why:* Ooty stands at about 2,240 metres. Air cools by about 6 degrees for every 1,000 metres of height.
4. (mcq) What do a mangrove's breathing roots take in?  
   Options: Salt / Sunlight / Mud / Air  
   **Answer:** Air. *Why:* Waterlogged mud has almost no air, and roots need oxygen. The breathing roots take in air at low tide.
5. (mcq) What is a coral reef built from?  
   Options: The stony cups of tiny animals / Sand pressed hard by waves / Dead seaweed / Lava from a volcano  
   **Answer:** The stony cups of tiny animals. *Why:* Each coral animal builds a hard cup of lime. Over thousands of years the cups pile up into a reef.
6. (mcq) Which of these is endemic to the Western Ghats?  
   Options: The snow leopard / The lion-tailed macaque / The house crow / The sangai deer  
   **Answer:** The lion-tailed macaque. *Why:* The lion-tailed macaque lives only in the rainforests of the Western Ghats. The sangai belongs to Manipur, and the snow leopard to the high Himalaya.
7. (mcq) Which place lies farthest south in India?  
   Options: Kochi / Chennai / Indira Point / Kanyakumari  
   **Answer:** Indira Point. *Why:* Indira Point is on Great Nicobar Island. Kanyakumari is the southern tip of the mainland only.
8. (mcq) How is traditional knowledge mostly built?  
   Options: One person invents it in a year / People copy it from a book / It appears by chance, untested / Many generations keep what works  
   **Answer:** Many generations keep what works. *Why:* People try things, keep what works, warn their children about what failed, and change a little in each generation.
9. (mcq) A design wins in one land and fails in another. What is the most likely reason?  
   Options: The two lands test it differently / The builders were careless / The design was never good / The materials were different  
   **Answer:** The two lands test it differently. *Why:* In the Two Lands Test the tall tower won in still air and fell in wind. A design fits the land that tested it.
10. (mcq) A village behind trees had less damage than a village on open shore. What else must you check?  
   Options: The day of the week / Their distance and height from the sea / The names of the two villages / The colour of the houses  
   **Answer:** Their distance and height from the sea. *Why:* If the safer village was also farther away or higher, the trees may not be the reason. A fair comparison changes one thing only.
11. (mcq) Why do Mising families in the Assam valley build their houses on stilts?  
   Options: To watch for tigers / To catch more wind / The river floods most years / Bamboo must not touch soil  
   **Answer:** The river floods most years. *Why:* The Brahmaputra floods the valley almost every monsoon. On stilts, the water passes under the floor.

## Applications
- Planners use the map of ten ecological regions. They check that every kind of land in India has national parks and sanctuaries.
- Rice breeders have used old varieties such as Pokkali, who can stand salt. They breed new rice for coasts where the sea is creeping into the fields.
- Engineers place wide feet and side supports on cranes, pylons and tall cupboards. A wide base works for them as buttress roots work for a tree.
- India and Bangladesh plant mangroves along their coasts, to hold the mud and to weaken the floods that cyclones bring.
- Architects in Ladakh build new schools with thick earth walls and large windows facing south, as old Ladakhi houses have.
- Towns in Rajasthan and Gujarat are repairing old tanks and step-wells, because pipes alone cannot supply a dry land.
- Villages across India write down what they know about local plants and animals in a People's Biodiversity Register.

## Spotted around us
- A banyan or peepal in a city, with roots hanging from the branches. Root bridges are grown from hanging roots like these.
- A sloping tiled roof on an old house in Kerala or coastal Karnataka, beside a flat concrete roof.
- A clay pot of drinking water on a window sill in a dry, hot town.
- Wide feet on a table fan, a crane or a water tank tower. They do what buttress roots do.
- Different kinds of banana or rice at the market, each with its own name and its own district.
- Mist on a cold steel tumbler or a mirror when you breathe on it.
- Clouds piled against the hills on the road up to Munnar, Ooty or Mahabaleshwar, and clear sky beyond.
- Mangrove trees along a creek in Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi or Kolkata, with roots standing out of the mud.

## Open questions
- **How many kinds of living beings in the Western Ghats and the North-East are still unknown to science?** New frogs, fish and plants are described from these regions almost every year. Nobody knows how many remain.
- **How much can a belt of mangroves do against a giant tsunami?** Villages differ in distance, height and shape of shore as well as in trees. Giant tsunamis are rare, so there are few cases to compare.
- **Why exactly do some rainforest trees grow buttress roots?** Support is the best-tested answer. Some scientists think the buttresses also help the tree to gather plant food from thin soil. The two ideas are still being tested.
- **How old are the oldest living root bridges?** A written report describes them in 1844. Villagers say some are much older. The roots grow in a tangle, so they are hard to date.
- **Will traditional knowledge still fit when the rain and the sea level change?** The monsoon is becoming less regular, and the sea is rising. Nobody knows how fast old knowledge will stop fitting, or how fast new knowledge can be built.

## Connected maths
- Mangaluru gets about 350 centimetres of rain a year, and Bengaluru about 100. Mumbai gets about 240, and Pune about 75. In each pair, how many times as much rain does the sea-side town get? **Mangaluru gets 3 and a half times as much as Bengaluru. Mumbai gets a little more than 3 times as much as Pune.**. Divide the larger figure by the smaller. 350 divided by 100 is 3.5. 240 divided by 75 is 3.2. Both pairs give about the same answer. This suggests that the same thing is happening on both stretches of the hills. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- Of every 100 parts of India's land, the Deccan Peninsula has about 42. The Semi-arid zone has 17 and the Gangetic Plain 11. What share is left for the other seven regions together? The Western Ghats have 4 parts in 100. India's land is about 3,300,000 square kilometres. About how large are the Western Ghats? **The other seven regions share about 30 parts in 100. The Western Ghats cover about 132,000 square kilometres.**. 42 plus 17 plus 11 is 70, and 100 minus 70 is 30. For the Western Ghats, one part in 100 of 3,300,000 is 33,000. Four parts are 4 times 33,000, which is 132,000 square kilometres. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L5`)
- In the Leaning Tree Test, tree A fell when the end of the board was 2 centimetres up. Tree B, with a buttress pointing downhill, fell at 11 centimetres. How many times as high is that? The edge that tree A tipped over was 1.5 centimetres from the middle of the trunk. For tree B the edge was 6.5 centimetres from the middle. How many times as far is that? Why might the two answers differ? **The board went 5 and a half times as high. The tipping edge was a little more than 4 times as far out. The answers differ because lifting by hand and reading a ruler are rough.**. 11 divided by 2 is 5.5. 6.5 divided by 1.5 is about 4.3. Both say the same thing: move the tipping edge several times farther out, and the tree stands on a tilt several times steeper. A 1-centimetre mistake in the small figure, 2 against 3, would change the first answer a great deal. This is a reason to test three times. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L5`)
- In the Guided Root Test, the longest root was 1 centimetre long on day 3. On day 7 the same root was 9 centimetres long. How many centimetres did the root grow in a day? At that speed, how many days would a root need to cross a stream 20 metres wide? About how many years is that? **2 centimetres a day. A 20-metre crossing would take 1,000 days, which is about 2 years and 9 months.**. From day 3 to day 7 is 4 days, and the root grew 9 minus 1, which is 8 centimetres. 8 divided by 4 is 2. Twenty metres is 2,000 centimetres, and 2,000 divided by 2 is 1,000 days. A year has 365 days, so 1,000 days is 2 years and 270 days. Real fig roots do not grow at a moong root's speed, and they must also thicken, which takes far longer. (queues `math.time.duration.L5`)
- On a wet mountain, the air cools by about 6 degrees for every 1,000 metres of height. It is 30 degrees at sea level. Make a table for 1,000, 2,000, 3,000, 4,000 and 5,000 metres. At what height does the air reach 0 degrees, where water freezes? **24, 18, 12, 6 and 0 degrees. The air reaches 0 degrees at about 5,000 metres.**. Take away 6 for each 1,000 metres: 30, 24, 18, 12, 6, 0. The pattern reaches 0 at the fifth step. This is why high Himalayan peaks keep their snow in summer, while the plains below are hot. (queues `math.patterns.number-patterns.L5`)
- In the Two Lands Test, a tall tower scores 110 in a still year and 0 in a windy year. A wide tower scores 45 in any year. Add up each tower's score over 4 years, if 1 year is windy. Do the same if 3 years are windy. Which tower should a village choose in each case? **With 1 windy year in 4, the tall tower scores 330 and the wide tower 180. The tall tower is ahead. With 3 windy years in 4, the tall tower scores 110 and the wide tower 180. The wide tower is ahead.**. One windy year: the tall tower scores 110 three times and 0 once, which is 330. Three windy years: 110 once, which is 110. The wide tower scores 4 times 45, which is 180, in both cases. So the choice depends on how often the wind comes. A careful village may still choose the wide tower. One year with a score of 0 can be a disaster. (queues `math.data.reading-data.L5`)
