# Choosing the right material for a job
`sci.materials.using-materials.L3`

**Summary.** Why is a kadai made of metal, but its handle of wood or plastic? Metal carries heat to the food fast, and wood and plastic carry it to your hand only slowly. A property is what a material is like, such as hard, bendy, waterproof or see-through, and we choose a material because its properties suit the job. Most objects are made of several materials, each doing the job it suits best. In this unit you test spoons, woollen socks and clay pots to find out which material does which job, and why.

**Intro.** *Try this. Imagine a kadai made of chocolate, an umbrella made of paper and a chappal made of glass. What would go wrong with each one?* Somebody chose the material of every object in your home. They chose steel for your tumbler, cotton for your kurta and plastic for your bucket, because each suits its job. Some choices are surprising. Water left in a clay pot stays cooler than water left in a steel bottle in the same room. A woollen sweater does not make any heat at all. In this unit you test materials the way an engineer does, and find out why each one is right for its job.

## Terms
- **Material**: What an object is made of. *e.g.* Your tumbler is made of steel. Your veshti is made of cotton.
- **Object**: A thing you can see and touch. One object can be made of one material or many. *e.g.* A pencil is one object made of wood, graphite, paint and sometimes a rubber eraser.
- **Property**: What a material is like, or what it can do. Hard, soft, bendy, waterproof and see-through are all properties. *e.g.* Glass is see-through. Rubber is stretchy. Cotton soaks up water.
- **Use**: The job a material does in an object. *e.g.* Glass is used for windows because you can see through it.
- **Conductor of heat**: A material that lets heat pass through it quickly. Most metals are good conductors of heat. *e.g.* A steel spoon left in hot coffee gets hot all the way up to the end.
- **Insulator of heat**: A material that lets heat pass through it only slowly. Wood, plastic, wool, cork and air are good insulators. *e.g.* A wooden spoon left in hot sambar stays cool at the end you hold.
- **Waterproof**: Does not let water through. *e.g.* A plastic raincoat is waterproof, so you stay dry in the monsoon.
- **Absorbent**: Soaks up water or other liquids. *e.g.* A cotton towel is absorbent. It soaks up the water on your skin.
- **Flexible**: Bends easily without breaking. *e.g.* A rubber chappal is flexible. It bends as you walk.
- **Rigid**: Stiff. It keeps its shape and does not bend easily. *e.g.* A wooden table is rigid, so your plate stays flat on it.
- **Strong**: Hard to break, tear or snap. *e.g.* Steel is strong, so it is used for bridges and almirahs.
- **Brittle**: Hard, but it cracks or shatters when hit or bent. *e.g.* A glass bangle is brittle. It breaks if it falls on a stone floor.
- **Durable**: Lasts a long time, even when used again and again. *e.g.* A steel plate can be used every day for years. A paper plate is used once.
- **Porous**: Full of tiny holes, called pores, that water or air can move through. *e.g.* A clay pot is porous. Water slowly seeps through it to the outside.
- **Evaporation**: When a liquid slowly turns into a gas and goes into the air. Evaporation takes heat away, so it cools things down. *e.g.* Wet clothes dry on the line because the water evaporates. Your wet skin feels cool in a breeze.
- **Composite**: A material made by joining two or more materials, so it has good properties of each. *e.g.* Plywood is thin sheets of wood glued together. Bone is a natural composite of a bendy part and a hard part.
- **Plastic**: A material made by people, mostly from oil and gas. It is made of very long chains of atoms joined together. Plastics can be shaped into almost anything. *e.g.* Buckets, toothbrushes and pen bodies are usually plastic.
- **Atom**: The tiny building block that every material is made of. Atoms are far too small to see, even with an ordinary microscope. *e.g.* Your steel spoon is mostly iron atoms, packed together very tightly.
- **Electron**: An even tinier particle on the outside of every atom. In metals, some electrons are free to move about, which is why metals carry heat and electricity so well. *e.g.* When one end of a steel spoon gets hot, its free electrons carry the heat along to the other end.
- **Engineer**: A person who designs and builds things to solve problems. Choosing materials is a big part of an engineer's job. *e.g.* An engineer chooses steel and concrete for a bridge, and rubber for its joints.
- **Biomimicry**: Copying the clever designs of living things to make new materials and machines. *e.g.* Water rolls off a lotus leaf. Scientists copied it to make paints that clean themselves.

## Explanations
### Properties decide uses · *defined*
**Definition:** We choose a material for a job because its properties suit that job. The job says what properties are needed. Then we look for a material that has them.

Think of a raincoat. Its job is to keep you dry. So it needs a material that is waterproof. It must also be flexible, so you can move your arms. Plastic and rubber-coated cloth have both properties. A towel has a different job. It must soak up water, so it needs an absorbent material, like cotton. The same property can be good for one job and bad for another. Soaking up water is perfect for a towel and terrible for a raincoat.
- e.g. A window is glass because glass is transparent, so light comes in and you can see out.
- e.g. A cricket bat is willow wood because it is strong but not too heavy, and it does not shatter.
- e.g. An electric wire is copper inside, because copper carries electricity well. Its cover is plastic, because plastic stops electricity from reaching your hand.
### One job, many materials. One material, many jobs. · *defined*
**Definition:** The same job can often be done by several materials. And one material can do many different jobs.

Look at spoons. People make them from steel, wood, plastic, silver, coconut shell and even bamboo. Each one works, because each is rigid and can be shaped into a bowl. But each has extra good and bad points. A steel spoon is durable and easy to clean. A wooden spoon does not scratch a non-stick pan. A coconut-shell spoon is cheap and goes back into the soil when it is thrown away. Now look at steel. It makes spoons, almirahs, bridges, cycle frames and needles. Steel is strong, so it suits all of them.
- e.g. Plates can be steel, banana leaf, areca palm leaf, paper, clay or plastic.
- e.g. Cotton makes saris, veshtis, towels, bedsheets, wicks for diyas and the cotton wool in a first-aid box.
- e.g. Bamboo makes houses, baskets, flutes, ladders and scaffolding.
### There is no best material, only the best for this job · *defined*
**Definition:** No material is best at everything. Choosing a material means weighing up what matters most for the job: how well it works, cost, weight, safety, how long it lasts and what happens to it afterwards.

Engineers call this a trade-off. You give up a little of one thing to get more of another. Steel is very strong but heavy. Aluminium is weaker but much lighter, so it is used for aeroplanes and pressure cookers. Glass is wonderfully clear but brittle, so spectacles are often made of plastic instead, which is lighter and does not shatter. A banana leaf makes a lovely plate for a feast, and it rots away after. But you cannot keep it for next week. The best choice depends on the job, the place and the person.
- e.g. Spectacles for a small child are often plastic, because a fall would shatter glass.
- e.g. A bamboo house can bend in an earthquake, while a brick wall with nothing holding it together may crack.
- e.g. A steel tiffin costs more than a plastic box, but it lasts for many years.
### Silly materials, and what goes wrong · *defined*
**Definition:** A material is a silly choice when one of its properties stops the object from doing its job.

Silly objects are a good way to think. A chocolate teapot would melt, because chocolate turns soft when it is warm. A paper umbrella would go soggy and tear, because paper is absorbent and weak when wet. A glass chappal would crack, because glass is brittle. A wool swimming costume would soak up water and sag. Each time, ask two questions. Which property is the problem? Which material has the property we need?
- e.g. A cardboard boat would soak up water and sink, unless it is coated with wax.
- e.g. Ice spectacles would melt on your nose.
- e.g. A steel pillow would be far too hard and rigid.
### Pans and handles: conductors and insulators · *defined*
**Definition:** A conductor of heat lets heat pass through it quickly. An insulator lets heat pass through only slowly. Most metals are good conductors. Wood, plastic, cloth, cork and air are good insulators.

A kadai must carry heat from the flame to the food, fast and evenly. So it is made of a metal, like iron, steel or aluminium. But the handle must keep heat away from your hand. So it is made of wood or a special hard plastic, which are insulators. The Hot Spoon Race in this unit shows the difference. Butter stuck on the handle of a steel spoon standing in hot water melts within a few minutes. Butter on a wooden spoon stays solid. Many pressure cookers have metal bodies and plastic handles for exactly this reason.
- e.g. A dosa tawa is iron. Its handle is often wood.
- e.g. A steel tumbler of hot chai is too hot to hold. A clay kulhad of the same chai is easy to hold.
- e.g. Oven gloves are thick cloth with air trapped inside, which is a good insulator.
### A sweater does not make heat · *defined*
**Definition:** Wool, cotton wool and feathers keep things warm because they trap lots of still air. Still air is a very good insulator. They do not make any heat themselves. They only slow heat down as it tries to escape.

Your body makes heat all the time. Without a sweater, that heat leaks away into the cold air. A sweater is full of tiny pockets of air between its curly fibres. The trapped air cannot move about, so heat passes through it very slowly. So your own heat stays near you. Here is the surprise. Wrap an ice cube in a woollen sock and it melts more slowly than an ice cube left in the open. The sock keeps the room's heat out, just as it keeps your body's heat in. Insulators slow heat down in both directions.
- e.g. An ice cube inside a woollen sock stays frozen longer than one on a bare plate.
- e.g. A quilt, a razai, is thick with cotton and air, so it keeps you warm on winter nights.
- e.g. Birds fluff up their feathers on cold mornings, to trap more air.
### Why a clay pot keeps water cool · *defined*
**Definition:** A clay pot, like a clay pot or a kooja, is porous. Water seeps slowly through its tiny holes to the outside. There it evaporates, and evaporation takes heat away from the pot and the water inside.

When a liquid evaporates, it needs heat to turn into a gas. It takes that heat from whatever it is touching. That is why you feel cool when you come out of a bath and stand in a breeze. A clay pot works the same way. Its outside is always a little damp. As that water evaporates, it takes heat from the pot. So the water inside stays cooler than the air. It works best in dry, breezy weather. In very damp, sticky weather, water evaporates slowly, so the clay pot cools less. A plastic or steel bottle has no pores, so this cannot happen.
- e.g. In hot Indian summers, families have kept drinking water in clay pots, koojas and surahis for thousands of years.
- e.g. A wet cloth wrapped round a steel bottle in a breeze makes the water cooler, because the cloth's water evaporates.
- e.g. Mansukhbhai Prajapati, a potter from Wankaner in Gujarat, used the same idea to make a whole clay fridge that needs no electricity. He called it Mitticool.
### Most objects are made of several materials · *defined*
**Definition:** Most objects are made of several materials. Each material does the job its properties suit best.

No single material can do everything, so designers put materials together. An umbrella has waterproof cloth to keep off rain. It has springy steel ribs to hold the cloth open. It has a plastic or wooden handle that is comfortable to hold. A pencil has soft graphite to leave a mark, wood to hold it and protect it, and paint so the wood does not get dirty. Sometimes two materials are joined so closely that they become a new material, called a composite. In 1823, a Scottish chemist, Charles Macintosh, made waterproof cloth by gluing two layers of cloth together with rubber in the middle. The cloth made it soft. The rubber made it waterproof. In Britain, a raincoat is still sometimes called a mackintosh, or a mac.
- e.g. A bicycle uses steel for the frame, rubber for the tyres, leather or plastic for the seat, and oil to help the chain move.
- e.g. A kurta button can be plastic, wood, coconut shell or metal, sewn on with cotton thread.
- e.g. A school bag uses strong cloth, plastic zips, foam padding for your back and a waterproof lining.
### Deep down, properties come from atoms · *defined*
**Definition:** A material's properties come from what its atoms are and how they are joined together.

Zoom into a steel spoon until you see its atoms. They are packed in neat rows. Some of their electrons, the tiny particles on the outside of atoms, are not stuck to any one atom. They wander freely through the whole metal. When one end gets hot, these free electrons carry the energy along very fast. They also make metals shiny, because they bounce light back. Now zoom into wood or plastic. Their electrons are held tightly in place, so heat creeps through slowly. Plastic is made of very long chains of atoms tangled like noodles. That is why many plastics can bend. Cotton is a mat of fibres with gaps between them. Water creeps into the gaps, so cotton is absorbent.
- e.g. Metals are shiny, carry heat and bend without snapping, all because of their free electrons and their neat rows of atoms.
- e.g. Rubber is stretchy because its long chains are curled up. Pulling straightens them. Letting go lets them curl back.
- e.g. Diamond and pencil lead are both made of carbon atoms. In diamond they hold each other tightly in every direction, so it is the hardest natural material. In pencil lead they form flat sheets that slide, so it is soft.
### Nature's clever materials · *defined*
**Definition:** Living things make materials with amazing properties, at ordinary temperatures, from simple things like water, sugar and minerals. Copying them is called biomimicry.

Bone is a composite. It mixes a bendy material called collagen with a hard mineral. The mineral alone would be brittle like chalk. The collagen alone would bend like rubber. Together, they make bone hard and tough. A lotus leaf stays clean in muddy ponds. Its surface is covered in tiny waxy bumps, so water rolls off in balls and carries the dirt away. In 1997, two German botanists, Wilhelm Barthlott and Christoph Neinhuis, explained this and called it the lotus effect. Now some paints and glass use the same trick. Spider silk is light, stretchy and very tough. Scientists have tried for years to make it in factories, using bacteria, yeast and even goats.
- e.g. The shiny inside of a seashell, called mother-of-pearl, is made of tiny flat tiles of mineral glued with a bendy material, like bricks and cement.
- e.g. Your tooth enamel is the hardest material in your body.
- e.g. Velcro was invented after a Swiss engineer, George de Mestral, looked closely at burrs stuck to his dog's fur.
### Materials for space · *defined*
**Definition:** Space is airless, very hot in sunlight and very cold in shadow. So spacecraft need materials chosen very carefully, each for one job.

Many spacecraft are wrapped in a shiny gold or silver blanket. It is many thin layers of plastic coated with metal, with gaps between them. The shiny layers reflect the Sun's heat. The gaps stop heat from passing through. India's Chandrayaan-3 lander, which landed near the Moon's south pole in 2023, wore a blanket like this. The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, has mirrors made of a light metal called beryllium. Beryllium keeps its shape even when it is extremely cold. The mirrors are coated with a layer of gold far thinner than a hair, because gold reflects the kind of light the telescope looks for. Some spacecraft also use aerogel, a solid so light it is mostly air. It is one of the best insulators ever made.
- e.g. A NASA spacecraft called Stardust used aerogel to catch tiny grains of comet dust and bring them back to Earth in 2006.
- e.g. Space suits have many layers. One holds air in, some reflect heat, and the outer layer stops sharp dust from cutting through.
- e.g. Rocket nozzles are made of materials that can stand fiercely hot gas rushing through them without melting.
### Plastic: wonder material or problem? · *debated*
Plastic is cheap, light, waterproof and can be shaped into anything. That is why it is everywhere. But it is also durable, and that is the problem. Most plastics take a very long time to break down. So thrown-away plastic piles up in fields, rivers and the sea. People disagree about what to do.
- e.g. Plastic drips, gloves and syringes in hospitals keep medicines clean and stop germs spreading.
- e.g. Plastic bags blown into drains can block them and make floods worse in the monsoon.
- **People who want strong bans:** Most plastic things are used once and thrown away. Ban the ones we do not really need, like thin carry bags and straws. India banned many single-use plastic items in 2022.
- **People who want better recycling and reuse:** Plastic does useful jobs that other materials do badly. Keep it, but use it many times, collect it properly and recycle it.
- **Scientists working on new plastics:** Invent plastics that last while we need them, then break down safely. This is hard. Some plastics labelled 'compostable' only break down in hot factory compost, not in a garden.
### Your spoon was made in a star · *debated*
Every material on Earth is made of atoms. Where did the atoms come from? Scientists agree on most of the story. The lightest atoms, hydrogen and helium, were made just after the universe began. Heavier atoms, like the carbon in wood and the oxygen in water, were made inside stars, which squeeze small atoms together. Iron, the main atom in your steel spoon, was made in stars and in the explosions of stars. The explosions scattered these atoms into space. Later, the Sun and the Earth formed from that dust. For the very heaviest atoms, like gold, scientists still argue.
- e.g. The iron in your blood and in your steel tumbler was once inside a star.
- e.g. The calcium in your bones was made in stars too.
- **Many astronomers, for a long time:** Gold was made mainly when giant stars exploded. These explosions are called supernovas.
- **Many astronomers today:** In 2017, telescopes watched two neutron stars crash together. A neutron star is the small, very heavy core left after a giant star explodes. The crash seemed to make heavy atoms like gold. So much of the gold on Earth may come from crashes like this.
- **Other scientists:** Both kinds of event probably make some gold. How much comes from each is still being worked out.
### Materials that are still being invented · *idea*
Some jobs still need materials nobody has made well yet. Scientists have many ideas, and some are working in laboratories. Nobody knows yet which ones will reach our homes.
- e.g. Self-healing concrete: Henk Jonkers, a scientist in the Netherlands, mixed tiny living bacteria into concrete. When a crack lets water in, the bacteria wake up and make limestone that fills the crack.
- e.g. DNA as a building material: Yamuna Krishnan, an Indian scientist who worked in Bengaluru and now works in Chicago, folds DNA into tiny machines. They change shape inside living cells and glow to report what is happening there.
- e.g. Materials that carry electricity with no loss at all, at ordinary room temperature. In 2023, some scientists claimed to have made one, called LK-99. Other scientists tested it carefully, and it did not work. The search goes on.
- **Hopeful scientists:** Materials that heal, grow or change shape could make houses, roads and medicines last longer and waste less.
- **Careful scientists:** Many exciting new materials work in a laboratory but are too costly or hard to make in large amounts. We must test every claim before we trust it.

## Mostly agreed
- We choose materials for jobs because of their properties. *How we know:* Every engineer and maker in the world does this. When people choose badly, the object fails, like a paper umbrella in the rain.
- Metals are good conductors of heat. Wood, plastic, wool and still air are good insulators. *How we know:* Put a steel spoon and a wooden spoon in hot water. The steel one gets hot all the way up. The wooden one stays cool at the top. Scientists have measured this for every common material.
- Trapped air keeps things warm, or keeps them cold. *How we know:* An ice cube wrapped in wool melts more slowly than one left in the open. Flasks, quilts and fur all work by slowing heat down.
- No material is best at everything. *How we know:* Strong materials are often heavy. Clear materials are often brittle. That is why almost every object is made of several materials.
- Most plastic ever made has not been recycled. *How we know:* In 2017, three scientists, Roland Geyer, Jenna Jambeck and Kara Lavender Law, added up all the plastic ever made and thrown away up to 2015. They estimated that only about 9 out of every 100 parts had been recycled.

## How we found out
**Joseph Black, a Scottish chemist and doctor, from the 1760s (his lectures were published in 1803).** For a long time, many people assumed that different materials in the same room held different amounts of cold. It seemed obvious. Touch an iron lock and a woollen blanket on a winter morning, and the iron feels far colder. Joseph Black, a chemist and doctor in Scotland, showed that this is wrong. In his lectures, from the 1760s, he taught that things left together in a room without a fire, such as iron, wood, wool and water, all end up at the same temperature. A thermometer placed on each one shows the same reading. Heat always spreads from warmer things to cooler ones until they are equal. So why does the iron feel colder? Your hand is warmer than the room. Iron carries heat away from your hand quickly, so your skin cools fast. Wool carries heat away slowly, so your hand stays warm. Black died in 1799. His friend John Robison published his lectures in 1803, so that others could learn from them.  
*What people believed before:* Different materials in the same room are at different temperatures: iron is colder than wood or wool. Thermometers show they are all the same. Iron only feels colder because it carries heat away from your hand faster.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Metal is colder than wood.** (Almost everyone, when they touch a steel railing and a wooden bench on a winter morning; *rejected*)  
  For: The metal really does feel much colder to your hand.  
  Against: If both have been in the same place all night, they are the same temperature. A thermometer shows it. Metal feels colder because it is a good conductor. It carries heat away from your warm hand very fast. Wood carries it away slowly, so your hand stays warm.
- **A sweater makes you warm by making heat.** (A very common idea, among children and grown-ups; *rejected*)  
  For: You feel warmer as soon as you put it on.  
  Against: A sweater has no heat of its own. It traps your body's heat. Put a sweater on a cold stone and the stone does not get warm. Wrap an ice cube in a woollen sock and it melts more slowly, not faster.
- **Natural materials are always better than materials made by people.** (Some people who care about the Earth; *debated*)  
  For: Many natural materials, like cotton, bamboo, clay and banana leaves, break down easily when thrown away. Their making often needs less energy.  
  Against: It depends. Growing cotton can use a lot of water. Leather needs chemicals to tan it. And some made materials save lives, like plastic syringes in hospitals or steel in bridges. The best choice depends on the job, how long it is used and what happens to it after.
- **Harry Brearley invented stainless steel, in 1913.** (Many books, and the city of Sheffield in England; *debated*)  
  For: Brearley, working in Sheffield, made steel with chromium in it, which did not rust. He saw it could be used for knives and forks.  
  Against: Scientists in Germany, France and the USA were also making steels with chromium at about the same time. Some patented them first. Like many inventions, stainless steel had several inventors. Each did part of the work.
- **Charles Martin Hall found the cheap way to make aluminium, all by himself.** (Older history books; *debated*)  
  For: In 1886, in the USA, Hall found a way to make aluminium cheaply, using electricity. Before that, aluminium was so costly it was used for fancy jewellery. Paul Héroult found the same way in France in the same year, on his own.  
  Against: Hall's older sister, Julia Brainerd Hall, had also studied chemistry. She helped with his experiments and kept careful records. Historians argue about how big her part was. Her name was left out of the story for a long time.
- **Plastic is a modern material that nature never made.** (A common idea; *debated*)  
  For: The plastics in our buckets and bottles were invented in the last 160 years or so. The first plastic made entirely from chemicals, not from plants or animals, was Bakelite. It was invented by Leo Baekeland in 1907.  
  Against: Nature makes many materials of long chains, just like plastic. Rubber from trees, silk, wool, horn and the shellac made by lac insects are all natural long-chain materials. Indian craftspeople have used shellac to make bangles, toys and varnish for centuries.

## Roots
We have been learning why we choose each material for its job. Now we ask what materials tell us about ourselves and the world.

**Deep truth.** A woollen sock squeezed in your fist shrinks to a small lump, because most of it is air. That still air, caught between the fibres, does most of the work of slowing heat. A clay pot cools water only because its clay has tiny holes that let water seep out and evaporate. So the gaps in a material often do its most important job.  
*Habit it breaks:* We think a material works because of what it is made of, so we call wool warm and clay cool. But much of the work is done by its gaps, such as the still air between wool's fibres and the tiny holes in baked clay.  
*Evidence:* In the sweater test, the ice cube inside a woollen sock lasted about twice as long as a bare one, though wool makes no heat. Squeezed, the same sock shrinks to a small lump, which shows it is mostly air. In the Matka Mystery, the outside of the clay pot is damp, because water seeps out through its pores.

- *who am i:* Your bones are full of tiny spaces too. The spaces make bone lighter, and it stays strong.
- *what is life:* Birds fluff up their feathers on cold mornings to hold more still air. Living things used the trapped-air trick long before people did.
- *what is this world:* The iron in your spoon was made in stars, long before the Earth formed.

A clay pot, a sweater and a flask look like solid things. But each one works because of what lies between its parts: tiny holes, pockets of still air, or an empty gap. Everything rises from one source, and the spaces between things belong to that whole as much as the things do. So a material is more than its fibres or its clay. Its gaps are working too.

- **Tao Te Ching 11:** We shape clay into a pot. But it is the empty space inside that makes the pot useful. (The gaps in wool are not truly empty. They are full of still air. But like the space inside Laozi's pot, they are what make the sweater useful. The tiny holes in a clay pot do the same for cool water.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.1.4:** Uddalaka tells his son that once you know one lump of clay, you know everything made of clay. The pots are only different shapes and names. What is real is the clay. (A clay pot, a kulhad and a diya are different objects with different jobs, yet all are clay. This unit's science goes one step further: clay, steel and wool are all atoms, joined in different ways.)
- Science (settled): Still air carries heat very poorly. Most good insulators, like wool, feathers, foam and the aerogel used in spacecraft, work by holding air still in tiny spaces.
- Science (settled): Iron, and most atoms heavier than helium, were made inside stars. Margaret and Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle showed how, in 1957.
- Other view, Democritus, a Greek thinker who lived about 2,400 years ago: Democritus said there are only atoms and empty space. Hot and cold, sweet and bitter, are only how atoms seem to us. On his view, the gaps are simply empty, and there is no awareness underneath, only atoms moving.
- Practice, Wool and air (2 min): Hold a woollen sock or shawl loosely in your hands. Breathe in slowly. Now squeeze it into a small ball. Feel how little wool there really is. Breathe out, and let it go. Watch it puff back up as air fills it. Hold it against your cheek. The warmth you feel is your own, held near you by the air.
- Ask: Is a sweater mostly wool, or mostly air? Which part keeps you warm?
- Ask: If the gaps do the work, are the gaps part of the material?
- Ask: Can a thing be useful because of the spaces in it?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* Is there a material that is best at everything? Why do you think not? (hint: Think of something very strong. Is it also light? Is it see-through? Is it cheap?)
- *big:* Metal feels colder than wood on a winter morning, but a thermometer says they are the same. Can your senses fool you? How do you know when to trust them? (hint: Put one hand in cold water and one in warm water, then both in the same bowl. What do they each feel?)
- *big:* Plastic lasts a very long time. When is lasting a long time a good thing, and when is it a problem? (hint: Think of a steel plate you use for twenty years, and a plastic straw you use for twenty minutes.)
- *deep:* Why are metals shiny and good at carrying heat, while wood is not? (hint: Zoom in to the atoms. What are the tiny electrons doing in each?)
- *big:* People have used clay pots to cool water for thousands of years, long before anyone knew about evaporation. Can you know how to do something without knowing why it works? (hint: Think of something you can do, like riding a cycle, without being able to explain it.)
- *big:* The person who invents a material often gets the credit. What about the workers who make it, or the people who first noticed the idea? (hint: Think of the potters who made clay water pots long before scientists explained them.)
- *big:* The iron in your spoon was made inside a star. Does that change how you feel about ordinary things? (hint: Pick up a spoon. Imagine where each of its atoms has been.)
- *deep:* Living things make bone, silk and shell without fire or factories. What can engineers learn from them? (hint: Spiders make silk from what they eat, at the temperature of a garden. A steel factory needs huge fires.)
- *big:* If you had to choose the materials for your own house, what would matter most to you: cost, comfort, beauty, how long it lasts, or the Earth? (hint: Would your answer change if you lived in Ladakh, or by the sea in Chennai?)
- *big:* Some 'wonder materials' turn out not to work when others test them. Why do scientists test each other's claims? (hint: In 2023, some scientists said they had made an amazing new material. What happened when others tried to make it too?)

## Story: The Winter the Ink Did Not Freeze
*Made up. Dolma, her mother, Stanzin Ma'am, Abi-le and the village are imagined. The science is real. Ladakh's winters are long and very cold. Sun-warmed buildings like this really exist there, built of thick mud walls with big south-facing windows, for example the SECMOL school campus near Leh.*

In our village in Ladakh, the winter is so cold that the river freezes and the yaks grow coats like carpets. That winter, the ink froze in my inkpot every morning. I had to hold it in my armpit for ten minutes before I could write my name.

Our school was one room with concrete walls and a tin roof. The concrete was always cold, like a stone pulled out of the river. The tin roof was the worst. When I touched the nail heads on the ceiling beam, they bit my fingers with cold. We kept on our caps, our gloves, two sweaters each and our long woollen gonchas, and still our noses ran.

Our teacher was Stanzin Ma'am. She was new, and young, and she came from Leh, where people have heaters. She lit the kerosene stove each morning. It filled the room with smoke and made a small warm circle around itself. The children nearest the stove coughed. The children by the window shivered. Nobody learned anything except how to be cold.

My mother's kitchen was different. It was the warmest room I knew. Its walls were thick mud bricks, as thick as my arm is long. In the afternoon the sun came through the small window and lay across the wall like a sleeping cat. In the evening, after the sun had gone, I would lean my back on that wall. It was still warm. 'How does the wall remember the sun?' I asked my mother. She laughed and said, 'Mud is slow. It takes the heat slowly and gives it back slowly.'

On the bus to the market in Leh, I noticed something else. The windows were shut and the sun poured in. My gloves came off. My cap came off. Outside, the wind was sharp as a knife. Inside the bus, behind the glass, it was almost like summer.

I told Stanzin Ma'am. I told her about the mud wall that remembered the sun, and about the warm bus. She listened with her head on one side. Then she said, 'Let's test it.' We took two of my mother's steel cups and filled them with the same cold water. We put both on a dark flat stone in the sun. Over one cup, we put an upside-down glass jar. After the morning, the water under the jar was much warmer. The water in the open cup was barely warmer at all. 'The glass lets the sunlight in,' said Stanzin Ma'am, 'and stops the warm air from blowing away.'

So we made a plan. It was the biggest plan our village had ever made for a classroom. On the sunny south wall, we would put a big window of glass. Behind it, a thick mud wall, painted dark, to drink the sunlight and store its heat, like my mother's kitchen. And under the tin roof, a deep layer of straw and sawdust.

Old Abi-le, who had lived through seventy winters, shook her head. 'Glass is cold,' she said. 'Touch a window in January. It burns your hand like ice.' She was right. I touched the bus window that week and it was freezing. But the sunlight still came through it. Glass is cold to touch, but it is clear. That was the job we needed it for.

'And straw?' said Abi-le. 'Straw is for the dzo to eat.' Stanzin Ma'am explained that straw is full of air, and still air is like a blanket for a house. 'Like your goncha,' she said to Abi-le. 'Your goncha is not hot. It keeps your own heat in.' Abi-le looked down at her thick woollen robe for a long time. Then she said, 'Hm.' In our village, that means yes.

The whole village came to help. Grown-ups mixed mud and straw with their feet. Others carried the bricks in a long line. We children packed sawdust into sacks for the roof. The glass came from Leh on the roof of the bus, wrapped in old blankets. We all held our breath as it was lifted down.

The first sunny morning after it was finished, I came early. The air outside was so cold that my breath made clouds. I opened the door and stepped inside. The sun was pouring through the glass onto the dark wall. The room smelled of new mud. I took off my gloves. I waited for the cold to bite my fingers. It did not.

But that night, a problem came. In the morning the room was colder than we hoped. Glass lets sunlight in, but it also lets warmth slip out in the dark. So my mother and the neighbours sewed thick curtains from old woollen blankets. At sunset, we pulled them across the glass. At sunrise, we pulled them open. Each material did its own job. The glass let the sun in. The mud kept the heat. The straw kept it from leaking up through the roof. The wool shut the window at night like an eyelid.

That February, on the coldest morning of the year, I opened my inkpot. The ink was blue and liquid and ready. I wrote my whole name, Dolma Angmo, without warming it first. Stanzin Ma'am did not light the stove. Nobody coughed. By the window, where I sat, the sun lay across my desk like a sleeping cat.

Abi-le came to see it. She put her old hand flat on the dark wall and held it there. 'This wall remembers the sun,' she said, as if it were her own idea. I did not mind. It was a good idea. It was everyone's.

**For the parent:** For the parent: the classroom was not warmed by burning more fuel. It was warmed by choosing each material for one job, because of its properties. Glass is transparent, so it lets sunlight in, even though it feels cold to touch. Thick dark mud soaks up heat slowly and gives it back slowly. Straw and wool trap still air, which is an insulator. They do not make heat. They keep heat from leaking away. And glass also lets heat out at night, so a woollen curtain covers it. No one material could do every job. Together they did. The story also notices whose idea it was: a child, a mother's kitchen and an old woman's doubts all helped.

- Ask: Why was the tin roof so cold to touch? What did the village put under it?
- Ask: Abi-le said glass is cold. She was right. So why did the village still use glass?
- Ask: What did the dark mud wall do with the sunlight?
- Ask: Why did they need woollen curtains at night?
- Ask: Dolma's idea came from her mother's kitchen and a bus. Where have you noticed something that could solve a problem?

## Experiments
### e1 · The Hot Spoon Race (30 min)
- **question:** Which spoon carries heat up to your hand fastest: steel, plastic or wood?
- **predict:** Before you start, line up the three spoons. Put the one you think will melt its butter first at the front, and the one you think will melt it last at the back. Then say why you put them in that order.
- **fair test:** To make it fair, change only the spoon's material. Use the same size of butter blob on each spoon, at the same height, one finger-width above the rim of the mug. Stand all three spoons in the same mug of hot water at the same moment. Time them with the same clock.
- **safety:** A grown-up pours the hot water and keeps the mug steady. Do not touch the spoons in the water. Use water that is hot, not boiling, and stand the mug on a flat, steady surface.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Then draw a bar for each spoon, as tall as the minutes it took. If a blob never melted, draw the bar to the top and write 'did not melt'.
- **results table:** Spoon | Minutes until the butter melted and slid | How the top of the spoon felt after 10 minutes
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Put the three spoons on the table. 2. Ask your child to hold each one and say how it feels. 3. Ask them to line up the spoons, from 'will melt its butter first' to 'will melt it last'.  
  *Parent says:* We are going to race these spoons. Not with legs. With heat. Which spoon do you think will carry heat up to its top fastest?  
  *Child mode:* Look at the steel, plastic and wooden spoons. Hold each one. Which will get hot fastest at the top? Line them up.
  - Ask: Why did you put that spoon first? (good: It is metal, and metal pans get hot.; Steel tumblers get hot with hot coffee in them.)
- **s2 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Stand the three spoons in the empty mug, handles up. 2. Put a pea-sized blob of cold butter on each handle, one finger-width above the rim of the mug. 3. Press a dried pea or a bead into each blob. 4. Put one more blob on a plate beside the mug. 5. Check all the blobs are the same size and the same height.  
  *Parent says:* The pea will fall off when the butter melts. That is our finish line. The blobs must match, so the race is fair. The blob on the plate tells us if the room alone is warm enough to melt butter.  
  *Child mode:* Stand the spoons in the empty mug. Put a little butter on each handle, just above the rim. Push a pea into each blob. Make them all the same. Put one more blob on a plate.
  - Note: If the blobs are different sizes, a small blob melts first anyway, and the test stops being fair. Cold butter from the fridge sticks best. Keeping the blobs above the rim keeps them out of the steam. On a very hot day, butter softens even on a plate. If the plate blob melts within 15 minutes, do the race in the coolest room, or in the early morning.
- **s3 (15 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* 1. A grown-up lifts the spoons out. 2. The grown-up pours hot water into the mug until it is one finger-width below the rim. 3. Stand all three spoons back in at the same moment. 4. Start the timer. 5. Write down the time each pea falls.  
  *Parent says:* Go! Now we watch. Call out the moment a pea drops.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to pour the hot water. Stand the spoons in it. Start the timer. Shout when a pea falls! Write down the time.
  - Note: Steel usually wins in 2 to 6 minutes. Plastic and wood both carry heat slowly. Often neither of their blobs melts before 15 minutes. Sometimes one melts late, softened by steam rising from the water. Stop after 15 minutes. Then let the spoons cool a little and feel the handles: the steel is warm, the wood is cool. If even the steel blob has not melted after 8 minutes, the water was not hot enough or the spoon is very thin. Try again with hotter water.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Help your child draw a bar chart, one bar for each spoon.  
  *Parent says:* Let's turn our times into a picture. A taller bar means a longer wait.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each spoon. Make it as tall as the minutes it took. The shortest bar carried heat fastest.
  - Check: The steel spoon's pea fell first. What does that tell us about steel? → **Steel is a good conductor of heat**
- **s5 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Look at a kadai, a tawa or a pressure cooker together. Find the metal part and the handle.  
  *Parent says:* Now look at our kadai. Which part should be like the steel spoon? Which part should be like the wooden one?  
  *Child mode:* Look at a pan in your kitchen. Which part is like the steel spoon? Which part is like the wooden spoon? Why?
  - Think: Why are a chef's spoons for stirring hot sambar often wooden?
- **s6 (2 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What do you think now about steel, plastic and wood? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: This is called claim, evidence and reasoning. A good answer sounds like this. What I think: steel carries heat much faster than plastic or wood. What I saw: the steel spoon's pea fell after 4 minutes, the plastic one after 14, and the wooden one never fell. Why: steel is a metal, and metals are good conductors of heat.
### e2 · Does a Sweater Make Heat? (25 min)
- **question:** If wool makes heat, an ice cube in a woollen sock should melt fastest. Does it?
- **predict:** Guess first. Which ice cube will melt first: the one on a bare plate, the one in foil, the one in a steel tumbler, or the one in a woollen sock? Which will last longest? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use ice cubes from the same tray, so they are the same size. Put them all in the same room, out of the sun, at the same time. Check them all at the same times. Change only the wrapping.
- **safety:** Wipe up melted water so nobody slips.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Draw a bar for each wrapping, as tall as the minutes its ice lasted.
- **results table:** Wrapping | Minutes until fully melted | What it looked like after 30 minutes
- **s1 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Ask your child to put on a sweater or wrap a shawl around their arm. 2. Ask: why does it feel warm? 3. Write down their answer.  
  *Parent says:* Put on your sweater. Does it feel warm? Where does the warmth come from? Is the sweater making heat?  
  *Child mode:* Put on a sweater. Do you feel warm? Where does that warmth come from? Does the sweater make it?
  - Ask: Where does the warmth come from? (good: From my body.; The sweater keeps my own heat in.; Any guess is fine here, if the child gives a reason. The test will check it.)
- **s2 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Squeeze the dry woollen sock in your fist, then let it go. 2. Put one ice cube on a bare saucer. 3. Wrap one in foil. 4. Put one inside the steel tumbler on a saucer. 5. Push one deep inside the woollen sock and lay it on a saucer. 6. Note the time.  
  *Parent says:* Squeeze the sock. See how small it gets? Most of a sock is air, caught between the wool fibres. Now, if the sock makes heat, it should melt its ice cube fastest. Let's find out.  
  *Child mode:* Squeeze the sock in your fist. How small does it get? Most of it is air! Put one ice cube on a plate. Wrap one in foil. Put one in the tumbler. Hide one in the sock. Note the time!
  - Note: Children nearly always predict the sock will melt the ice fastest. Do not correct them. Let the test do it.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Every 10 minutes, look at each ice cube. 2. For the sock, gently peep inside. 3. Write down when each one has fully melted. Keep checking until all four have melted. The sock's ice can take one to two hours.  
  *Parent says:* Let's check. Which ice cube is smallest now? Which is biggest?  
  *Child mode:* Every 10 minutes, check each ice cube. Peep inside the sock. Which one is melting fastest? Which is still big?
  - Note: Usually the bare cube melts first and the sock cube lasts much longer, often twice as long. Foil sometimes helps a little, because it is shiny. If you used a towel instead of wool, the result is the same: the towel cube lasts longer, because a thick towel also traps air.
- **s4 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Draw a bar chart of how long each ice cube lasted.  
  *Parent says:* Let's draw a bar for each wrapping. The tallest bar lasted longest.  
  *Child mode:* Draw one bar for each wrapping. Make it as tall as the minutes the ice lasted. Which bar is tallest?
  - Check: The ice cube in the woollen sock lasted longest. Why? → **Wool traps still air, which slows heat from the room reaching the ice**
- **s5 (3 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. Does a sweater make heat? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a sweater does not make heat. It slows heat down, coming in or going out. What I saw: the ice in the sock lasted 95 minutes, but the bare ice melted in 40. Why: the sock is mostly air, and still air slows heat down.
  - Think: So why do you feel warm in a sweater? Where does that heat really come from?
### e3 · The Matka Mystery (30 min)
- **question:** Does a clay pot really keep water cooler than a plastic or steel bottle? Why?
- **predict:** Guess first. After three hours, which will hold the coolest water: the clay pot, the plastic bottle or the steel bottle? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Use the same water, poured from one jug, at the same time. Put the same amount in each. Leave the lids off all three. Stand them side by side in the same shady, breezy place. Measure them all at the same time with the same thermometer.
- **safety:** A clay pot is brittle, so carry it with two hands. Wipe up spills.
- **record:** Fill in the table. Work out how many degrees each one cooled down. Draw what the outside of the clay pot looked like.
- **results table:** Container | Water temperature at the start (degrees) | Water temperature after 3 hours (degrees) | Did the outside feel damp?
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Show your child a clay pot. 2. Ask them to feel the outside after it has been soaking. 3. Ask why people keep drinking water in clay pots in summer.  
  *Parent says:* In many Indian homes, drinking water is kept in a clay pot in summer. People say it keeps the water cool. Is that true? And if it is, how?  
  *Child mode:* Feel the outside of the clay pot. Is it damp? People say a clay pot keeps water cool. Shall we test it?
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Pour the same amount of water from one jug into the pot, the plastic bottle and the steel bottle. 2. Measure the water in the jug with the thermometer and write it down. That is the start temperature for all three. 3. Stand them side by side in a shady place with a breeze, like a verandah or a window.  
  *Parent says:* All three start with the same water, at the same temperature. Only the container is different. That makes it fair.  
  *Child mode:* Pour the same water into the pot and both bottles. Measure it with the thermometer. Write the number. Put them side by side in the shade.
  - Note: Read the thermometer at eye level. If you have only a fever thermometer, do not use it in water. A cheap kitchen or bath thermometer works well.
- **s3 (10 min)**  
  *Parent does:* 1. Leave the three containers for 3 hours. 2. Then feel the outside of each one. 3. Measure the water in each with the thermometer. 4. Write the numbers in the table.  
  *Parent says:* Feel the outside first. Which one is damp? Now let's measure.  
  *Child mode:* Three hours later, feel the outside of each one. Which is damp? Now measure the water in each one. Write the numbers.
  - Note: Use an unglazed pot. A shiny glazed or painted pot has its pores sealed, so it cools much less. On a dry, warm, breezy day, the clay pot water is often 3 to 8 degrees cooler than the bottles. On a sticky monsoon day, the difference is smaller, because water evaporates slowly into damp air. Either result is good science.
  - Check: The clay pot water was coolest, and the outside of the clay pot was damp. Why is it cooler? → **Water seeped through the clay and evaporated, taking heat away**
- **s4 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Extra test: 1. Wet the cotton hanky and wrap it round the steel bottle. 2. Leave it in the breeze for another hour. 3. Measure again.  
  *Parent says:* Can we make the steel bottle work like a clay pot? Let's give it a wet coat.  
  *Child mode:* Wrap a wet hanky round the steel bottle. Leave it in the breeze for an hour. Is the water cooler now?
  - Think: Soldiers and travellers once carried water in canvas bags that were always a little wet. Why might that keep the water cool?
- **s5 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Ask your child to say or write three short sentences. First, what I think. Second, what I saw. Third, why I think it happened.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. Does a clay pot really keep water cooler? What did you see that shows it? And why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Say or write three things. What I think. What I saw. Why.
  - Note: A good answer sounds like this. What I think: a clay pot keeps water cooler than a plastic or steel bottle. What I saw: after 3 hours, the clay pot water was 24 degrees and the plastic bottle water was 29 degrees. Why: water seeped out through the clay and evaporated, and evaporation takes heat away.

## Worksheets
### Why Is It Made of That? (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Meenakshi, a girl in Class 3, makes two boats. One is folded from newspaper. The other is a plastic box lid. She floats both in a bucket of water. Which boat will still float tomorrow?  
   Options: The plastic lid, because plastic is waterproof / The paper boat, because paper is lighter / Both, because both float at first / Neither, because all boats sink in the end  
   **Answer:** The plastic lid, because plastic is waterproof. *Why:* A strong answer uses a property. Paper is absorbent. It slowly soaks up water, goes soft and sinks. Plastic is waterproof, so it stays dry and keeps floating. Picking 'paper is lighter' is a sensible first guess, but being light only helps until the paper fills with water.
2. (fill) A material that slows heat down, like the wood of a ladle handle, is called an ____ of heat.  
   **Answer:** ['insulator']. *Why:* Wood, wool, plastic and still air are insulators of heat. Metals are the opposite: they are conductors, which let heat pass through quickly.
3. (truefalse) Every material is either a good material or a bad material.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. A material is only good or bad for a job. Glass is perfect for a window, because it is transparent. It is a terrible choice for a cricket ball, because it is brittle. A strong answer gives one job where the material is right and one where it is wrong.
4. (sort) What does each object's material need to do most? Sort them.  
   **Answer:** a window pane → Let light through; a spectacle lens → Let light through; a sponge → Soak up water; a floor mop → Soak up water; a bucket → Keep water in or out; a tent → Keep water in or out. *Why:* Start from the job, not the material. A window and a lens must be transparent. A sponge and a mop must be absorbent. A bucket and a tent must be waterproof.
5. (match) Each of these went wrong. Match each problem to the property that caused it.  
   **Answer:** A paper plate goes soggy under hot sambar → absorbent; A glass tumbler breaks on the floor → brittle; A steel spoon in hot payasam burns your fingers → good conductor of heat; A plastic bucket left by the stove goes out of shape → softens when hot. *Why:* When an object fails, ask which property let it down. Then you know what property the new material needs.
6. (mcq) A cricket bat is made of willow wood, not steel. What is the main reason?  
   Options: A steel bat of that size would be far too heavy to swing / Wood is waterproof / Steel cannot be shaped / Wood carries heat well  
   **Answer:** A steel bat of that size would be far too heavy to swing. *Why:* Steel is strong, but it is very heavy for its size. A bat must be strong and light enough to swing fast. Willow is both. Picking 'steel cannot be shaped' is not right, because steel is shaped into cars and spoons. A strong answer weighs strength against weight.
7. (mcq) Arjun says, 'A clay pot cools water because clay is a cold material.' Which test would show whether he is right?  
   Options: Wrap the wet clay pot in a plastic bag, so no water can evaporate, and see if the water still cools / Paint the pot a nicer colour / Use a bigger pot / Leave the pot in the fridge  
   **Answer:** Wrap the wet clay pot in a plastic bag, so no water can evaporate, and see if the water still cools. *Why:* If clay itself were cold, the pot would cool water even inside a plastic bag. It does not. The water only cools when it can seep out and evaporate. A strong answer changes one thing that would tell the two ideas apart.
8. (write) An old, flat blanket keeps you less warm than a new, fluffy one made of the same cotton. Why?  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the fluffy blanket has more air trapped between its threads. Still air is an insulator, so it slows your body's heat from leaving. The flat blanket has lost most of its trapped air.. *Why:* Both blankets are the same material. The difference is the trapped air, and that is what does most of the work.
9. (truefalse) Cotton is a good choice for a diya wick and a bad choice for an umbrella. Both are true because of the same property.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Cotton is absorbent. That is right for a wick, which must soak up oil and carry it to the flame. It is wrong for an umbrella, which must keep water out. One property can be good for one job and bad for another.
10. (order) Put these in order to explain how a clay pot cools the water inside it.  
   **Answer:** order shown. *Why:* Clay is porous: it has tiny holes. Water reaches the outside and evaporates. Evaporation takes heat away with it. A strong answer gets the evaporation step before the cooling step.
11. (write) Look around the room you are in. Find one object made of two or more materials. Name each material and the job it does.  
   **Answer:** Any real object, with each material linked to a job. For example: my water bottle has a steel body, because steel is strong and easy to clean. It has a plastic lid, because plastic can be made to fit tightly and bend a little.. *Why:* A strong answer names a real object, every material in it, and a property that fits each job.
12. (draw) Draw your school bag. Label three materials it is made of. Next to each label, write the job that material does.  
   **Answer:** For example: cloth or nylon for the body, because it is strong and light. A plastic or metal zip, because it is stiff and slides. Foam in the straps, because it is soft on the shoulders.. *Why:* A strong drawing shows that one object uses several materials, each chosen for one job.
13. (perspective) Three people in Madurai argue about the best pot for keeping drinking water at home in summer.  
   **Answer:** Any choice can be strong. A strong answer uses evidence, for example: the clay pot water was cooler after three hours because water evaporated from its outside. It then admits a real point from another side, for example: clay is brittle and can crack, or plastic is easiest to carry.. *Why:* Each pot is good at something. Clay cools, steel lasts, plastic is light. There is no single right answer, but a strong answer uses the test results and is fair to the other side.
14. (mcq) Glass is very hard. Yet a glass tumbler breaks when it falls, and a steel tumbler only dents. Why?  
   Options: Glass is brittle: it cracks instead of bending / Glass is soft / Glass is heavier than steel / Glass is transparent  
   **Answer:** Glass is brittle: it cracks instead of bending. *Why:* Hard means difficult to scratch. Brittle means it cracks instead of bending. Glass is both. Steel can bend a little and soak up the bump. Picking 'glass is soft' mixes up breaking with softness. A strong answer says hard is not the same as strong.
15. (write) After the Hot Spoon Race, write three sentences. What did you guess before? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
   **Answer:** For example: I guessed the plastic spoon would get hot first. I saw the butter on the steel spoon melt first, after about 4 minutes. 16. (mcq) Kavya puts a steel bowl of hot payasam on the wooden table. Which mat will protect the table best?  
   Options: A thick, folded cotton cloth / A thin steel plate / A copper sheet / No mat  
   **Answer:** A thick, folded cotton cloth. *Why:* The folded cloth is an insulator. It has air trapped between its folds, so it slows heat moving down into the table. Steel and copper are conductors, so they pass the heat straight through. Picking the steel plate is a common guess, because it looks strong. But this job needs an insulator, not strength.
### Testing and Choosing (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Look at the chart from the Hot Spoon Race. The plastic spoon's butter melted after 14 minutes. The wooden spoon's butter had not melted by 15 minutes. What does this tell you?  
   Options: Plastic is an insulator, but here it let heat through a little faster than wood / Plastic is a conductor, like steel / Plastic makes its own heat / The plastic spoon had more butter  
   **Answer:** Plastic is an insulator, but here it let heat through a little faster than wood. *Why:* Plastic took more than three times as long as steel, so it is an insulator. But it was a little quicker than wood, so it is a slightly weaker one. Picking 'a conductor, like steel' misses how big the gap is: 4 minutes against 14. A strong answer compares the numbers.
2. (write) Suppose you added a copper spoon to the Hot Spoon Race. Where would you expect its bar on the chart to be? Explain your guess.  
   **Answer:** A strong answer says the copper bar would be the shortest, under 4 minutes. Copper is a metal and an even better conductor of heat than steel. So heat would reach the butter even faster.. *Why:* This uses the result to predict a new case. The child has not tested copper, so the answer should be a reasoned guess, and say how to check it.
3. (mcq) Two clay pots are filled with the same water. One stands in the breeze of a fan. The other stands in a still corner. Which water will be cooler after two hours?  
   Options: The pot in the fan's breeze / The pot in the still corner / Both the same, because they are the same pot / Neither will cool  
   **Answer:** The pot in the fan's breeze. *Why:* Moving air carries away the water that has evaporated, so more water can evaporate. More evaporation takes away more heat. Picking 'both the same' forgets that the air around the pot matters, not just the clay.

## Applications
- Cooking pots, tawas and pressure cookers are metal, because metal carries heat to the food.
- Pan handles, oven gloves and tea cosies use insulators, like wood, plastic, thick cloth and wool.
- Clay pots, koojas and surahis keep water cool, because clay is porous. A clay kulhad is easy to hold with hot chai inside, because clay carries heat slowly.
- Electric wires have copper inside to carry electricity, and plastic outside to keep it from reaching your hand.
- Windows, spectacles and phone screens use transparent glass or plastic.
- Raincoats, umbrellas and tarpaulins use waterproof materials, often cloth coated with plastic or rubber.
- Towels, mops, nappies and diya wicks use absorbent materials.
- Bridges, buildings and cycle frames use strong steel. Aeroplanes use light aluminium and carbon fibre.
- Helmets have a hard plastic shell outside and squashy foam inside, two materials doing two jobs.
- Packing for glass and phones uses squashy materials, like bubble wrap, foam and crumpled paper.
- Flasks keep tea hot and water cold using two walls with almost no air between them.
- Spacecraft, like India's Chandrayaan-3 lander, are wrapped in shiny layered blankets to block the Sun's heat.
- Mud houses in Ladakh and Rajasthan use thick walls that soak up heat by day and give it back at night.
- Self-cleaning paints and glass copy the bumpy, waxy surface of a lotus leaf.
- Doctors use plastic syringes, steel tools and soft cotton, each chosen for a different property.

## Open questions
- **Can we make a material that carries electricity with no loss at all, at ordinary room temperature?** Some materials do this only when they are extremely cold. Many claims of a room-temperature one, like LK-99 in 2023, failed when others tested them.
- **Can we make plastics that last as long as we need them, then safely break down?** Some new plastics do break down, but often only in special hot compost. Scientists are still searching.
- **How does a spider make silk so strong, light and tough, at garden temperature?** Scientists understand parts of it. Making silk just as good in a factory is still very hard.
- **What exactly is glass?** Its atoms are jumbled like a liquid's but stuck in place like a solid's. Physicists still argue about how best to explain why some liquids turn into glass when they cool.
- **Could buildings one day grow and repair themselves?** Self-healing concrete with bacteria works in tests. Using living materials for whole buildings is still an idea being explored.
- **Where did most of the gold on Earth come from?** Exploding stars and crashing neutron stars both make gold. How much comes from each is still being worked out.
- **Which materials are best for the Earth?** It depends on how a material is made, how long it is used and what happens to it after. Scientists add these up in different ways, and do not always agree.

Read the height of each bar: sock 95, foil 55, steel tumbler 45, bare plate 40. Put them from biggest to smallest: 95, 55, 45, 40. Then 95 take away 40 is 55. So the sock's ice lasted 55 minutes longer. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L3`)

## Warm-up
- What do we call what a material is like, such as hard or waterproof? → A property.
- Why is a kadai made of metal? → Metal carries heat to the food. It is a good conductor.
- Why is a kadai's handle made of wood or plastic? → They do not carry heat well, so the handle stays cool. They are insulators.
- Does a sweater make heat? → No. It traps air, which keeps your own heat in.
- Why does a clay pot keep water cool? → Water seeps through the clay and evaporates, which takes heat away.
- What would go wrong with a paper umbrella? → Paper is absorbent and weak when wet, so it would go soggy and tear.
- Name three materials in an umbrella. → Waterproof cloth, steel ribs, and a plastic or wooden handle.
- A steel railing feels colder than a wooden bench. Is it really colder? → No. Steel just carries heat away from your hand faster.
- What is copying nature to make new materials called? → Biomimicry.
- Where were the iron atoms in your spoon first made? → Inside stars, long ago.
