# A burning groundnut and a food label: what the numbers say about your food
`sci.food.nutrients.L4`

**Summary.** Hold a groundnut in a flame and it keeps burning on its own for about a minute. Yet the school chart calls groundnuts a body-building food, not an energy food. Your child burns a groundnut, a cashew and a piece of dry coconut under a tumbler of water, measures the heat with a thermometer, and works out how much heat was lost. They cook rice and find that 100 grams of cooked rice holds only about a third of the energy in 100 grams of raw rice. They learn to read a food label, including the many names of sugar, and meet Wilbur Atwater, who showed that a living body keeps the same energy accounts as a fire.

**Intro.** *A groundnut burns like a tiny candle, but a grain of rice does not. What does a groundnut have inside that a grain of rice has much less of?* The food on your plate is fuel for your body, the way wood is fuel for a stove. Scientists measure that fuel in kilocalories. In this unit you burn real foods under a tumbler of water and measure the heat they give out. You cook rice and weigh it before and after. Then you read the label on a packet, and find out what its numbers say and what they leave out.

## Learning outcomes
0. I can sort foods by their main job, which is giving energy, building the body or protecting it, and explain why one food can do more than one job.
1. I can explain that a kilocalorie measures the heat in food, and that fat holds about twice the energy of starch or protein in the same weight.
2. I can run a fair test that burns a food under a tumbler of water, measure the heat, and say how much heat was lost.
3. I can read a food label: energy per 100 grams and per serving, the order of the ingredients, the names of sugar, and the green or brown dot.
4. I can explain why the water in cooked food lowers its energy per 100 grams.
5. I can name protective foods and say what vitamins, minerals and fibre do for the body.
6. I can explain how the body releases the energy in food, and why the number on a label is only an estimate.
7. I can describe how scientists showed that a living body follows the same energy rules as a fire.

## Terms
- **Kilocalorie**: A unit for measuring energy. One kilocalorie is the heat that warms one litre of water by one degree Celsius. On a label it is written kcal, and people often call it a calorie. *e.g.* A groundnut kernel holds about 3 kilocalories.
- **Kilojoule**: Another unit of energy that scientists use. One kilocalorie is about 4.2 kilojoules. *e.g.* A packet from another country may show 2,400 kilojoules where an Indian packet shows 570 kilocalories.
- **Energy-giving food**: A food that mostly gives the body fuel. Foods rich in carbohydrates or fats are energy-giving foods. *e.g.* Rice, roti, potato, ghee and oil.
- **Body-building food**: A food that mostly gives the body protein to build and mend muscles, skin and blood. The body can also burn protein for fuel when it needs to. *e.g.* Dal, egg, paneer, fish and curd.
- **Protective food**: A food rich in vitamins and minerals, which help the body fight illness and work properly. *e.g.* Guava, amla, spinach, carrot and papaya.
- **Energy density**: How much energy a food holds in each 100 grams. Foods with little water and a lot of fat have a high energy density. *e.g.* Ghee has an energy density of about 900 kilocalories in 100 grams. Cucumber has about 15.
- **Serving**: The amount of food that the maker of a packet counts as one helping. It can be much smaller than what people really eat. *e.g.* A packet of chips may list 30 grams as one serving, and the whole packet may hold four servings.
- **Ingredient list**: The list of everything a packet food is made of. The ingredients are listed from the largest amount by weight to the smallest. *e.g.* If sugar is the first word, there is more sugar than anything else.
- **Added sugar**: Sugar that a maker puts into a food. It is different from the sugar that is part of a fruit or milk. *e.g.* The sugar stirred into packaged fruit juice.
- **Sodium**: The part of salt that matters for health. Too much sodium over many years can harm the heart and kidneys. Salt is about two and a half times the weight of its sodium. *e.g.* A packet of instant noodles can hold more than half a day's salt in one serving.
- **Fortified food**: A food that has had a vitamin or mineral added to it on purpose. Packets show a small +F sign. *e.g.* Iodised salt and fortified atta or oil.
- **Fibre**: The part of plant foods that the body cannot digest. It keeps the gut moving and feeds the helpful bacteria that live there. *e.g.* Whole wheat, ragi, vegetables and fruit with their skins.
- **Respiration**: The way every cell in a living body uses oxygen to release the energy stored in food. It is not the same as breathing, but breathing brings the oxygen. *e.g.* When you run, your cells use food faster and you breathe faster to bring more oxygen.
- **Calorimeter**: A device that measures the heat that something gives out when it burns or works. *e.g.* Your tumbler of water and thermometer make a simple calorimeter.
- **Per 100 grams**: A label column that gives the numbers for the same weight of every food, so you can compare one food with another fairly. *e.g.* Comparing biscuits and murmura per 100 grams shows which one holds more energy in the same weight.

## Explanations
### Energy in food, and how a kilocalorie is measured · *defined*
**Definition:** A kilocalorie is the heat needed to warm one litre of water by one degree Celsius. We measure the energy in food by how much heat it gives out when it burns.

Food is fuel. When a food burns, it joins with oxygen and gives out its stored energy as heat. If that heat warms water, a thermometer tells us how much energy there was. One kilocalorie warms one litre of water by one degree, so two kilocalories warm it by two degrees. A tumbler of 50 millilitres is one twentieth of a litre. So one kilocalorie would warm it by 20 degrees. A groundnut kernel holds about 3 kilocalories, but a home flame wastes most of the heat into the air, so the water gains only part of it. That is why scientists use a closed, insulated metal chamber called a bomb calorimeter. Your body does not burn food in a flame, but the energy released is the same.
- e.g. A packet that says 570 kilocalories per 100 grams holds enough heat to warm 570 litres of water by one degree.
- e.g. One whole groundnut kernel weighs about half a gram and holds about 3 kilocalories, enough to warm 50 millilitres of water by up to 60 degrees if none of the heat were lost.
- e.g. Indian packets show kilocalories. Packets from some other countries show kilojoules, and one kilocalorie is about 4.2 kilojoules.
### Three jobs: energy-giving, body-building and protective · *defined*
**Definition:** Food does three main jobs. It gives energy, it gives the materials to build and mend the body, and it gives the vitamins and minerals that protect the body and keep it working.

Many schoolbooks sort foods into energy-giving foods, body-building foods and protective foods. Rice, roti and ghee are energy-giving foods. Dal, egg and paneer are body-building foods. Fruit, vegetables and leafy greens are protective foods. The sorting is useful for planning a meal, because a meal with all three jobs covered has what the body needs. But a job is something food does, not a label stuck on the food. A groundnut is called a body-building food, because a quarter of it is protein, yet it burned and heated your water. Your body also burns protein for fuel when it has little else. Curd, milk and eggs do all three jobs at once. So the three groups tell you what a food mostly does in a normal meal. The body decides what it really does.
- e.g. Dal is called a body-building food. About a fifth of dry dal is protein, and more than half is starch, which is fuel.
- e.g. Milk gives energy in its fat and milk sugar, building material in its protein and calcium, and protection in its vitamins.
- e.g. A mango is called a protective food for its vitamins A and C, and it also gives plenty of energy as sugar.
### Carbohydrates and fats: the fuel foods, and why fat holds more · *defined*
**Definition:** Carbohydrates and fats are the body's main fuels. Carbohydrates give about 4 kilocalories in each gram. Fat gives about 9 kilocalories in each gram.

Starch and sugar are carbohydrates, and they are the body's quick fuel. Your body breaks starch into sugar, and your cells burn the sugar. Fat is a slower, longer-lasting fuel. A gram of fat holds a little more than twice the energy of a gram of starch, because fat has more hydrogen atoms for oxygen to burn. This is why a groundnut, which is nearly half fat, burns well, and rice, which is almost all starch with very little fat, burns poorly in a flame. It is also why ghee holds about 900 kilocalories in 100 grams. Fat is how the body stores energy for later. An animal that has to go a long time without food, such as a camel or a migrating bird, stores fat for the same reason.
- e.g. 100 grams of sugar holds about 400 kilocalories. 100 grams of ghee holds about 900.
- e.g. A groundnut is about 45 percent fat. Puffed rice is under 1 percent fat. This is why the groundnut burns for a minute and puffed rice flares and is gone.
- e.g. Migrating birds store fat before a long flight because a gram of fat holds more energy than a gram of anything else they can carry.
### Protein: building first, fuel when needed · *defined*
**Definition:** Proteins are long chains made of small building blocks called amino acids. The body builds its muscles, skin, hair, blood and many other parts from protein.

When you eat protein, your gut breaks it into single amino acids. Your body then joins them again, in a new order, into the proteins it needs. Your body can make some amino acids itself, but there are nine it cannot make, so they must come from food. Different foods carry different amounts of each. Grains such as rice are low in one amino acid called lysine. Pulses such as dal are low in another called methionine. Together, they supply all nine well. Proteins are also a fuel. If a body has too little carbohydrate and fat, it burns protein, at about 4 kilocalories in a gram. Children need protein every day, because they are growing. Adults need it to mend their bodies.
- e.g. Rice with dal, idli with sambar and roti with rajma each join a grain and a pulse, and are old traditional pairings.
- e.g. A growing child needs more protein for each kilogram of body weight than an adult does.
- e.g. Protein from the egg white you eat is taken apart and rebuilt as the muscle in your leg.
### Protective foods: vitamins, minerals and fibre · *defined*
**Definition:** Vitamins and minerals are nutrients needed in small amounts, and fibre is the part of plants we cannot digest. Foods rich in them protect the body.

Vitamins and minerals give no energy, but the body cannot work without them. Vitamin A, from carrots, papaya, drumstick leaves and spinach, protects the eyes and the skin. Vitamin C, from amla, guava and lemon, helps the body heal and helps it take in iron. Iron, from ragi, jaggery, dates and leafy greens, helps the blood carry oxygen. Calcium, from milk, curd and ragi, builds bones and teeth. Iodine in iodised salt protects against goitre. Skin makes vitamin D when sunlight falls on it. Vitamin B12 is made by microbes, so plant foods rarely hold any, and vegetarians get it from milk, curd and eggs, or from a supplement a doctor suggests. Tea drunk with a meal holds on to iron and lowers how much the body takes in. A squeeze of lemon on dal or spinach raises it. Fibre keeps the gut moving and feeds helpful gut bacteria.
- e.g. Amla holds several times more vitamin C than an orange does, weight for weight.
- e.g. Kamala Sohonie, who in 1939 became the first Indian woman to earn a doctorate in science, later studied neera, the fresh sweet sap of the palm, and found it rich in vitamins.
- e.g. Ragi holds about 340 milligrams of calcium in 100 grams, and the same weight of milk holds about 120.
### How your body burns food: respiration · *defined*
**Definition:** Respiration is the way every living cell uses oxygen to release the energy stored in food.

A flame burns a nut fast, in one step, and nearly all the energy comes out as heat and light. A cell releases the same energy from sugar in many small steps, using oxygen. Each step is run by a different molecule that works like a tiny machine. Some of the energy is caught in a small molecule called ATP, which works like a charged battery that the cell uses to move, build and send signals. The rest warms the body, which is why you are warm. The waste products are carbon dioxide, which you breathe out, and water. Because cells in a flame and cells in a body both start with sugar and oxygen and end with carbon dioxide and water, the total energy released is the same. A hot flame would kill a cell, so the slow steps matter. A body cannot burn food all at once without cooking itself.
- e.g. When you run, your cells burn sugar faster, so you breathe faster to bring oxygen and you feel warm.
- e.g. Even asleep, your body uses energy for the heart, breathing, the brain and keeping warm. The brain alone uses about a fifth of an adult's energy, and a larger share of a child's.
- e.g. Yeast in dough get energy from sugar without oxygen, by fermenting it. They get less energy from the same sugar and make gas and alcohol instead.
### Water in food: why energy is counted per 100 grams · *defined*
**Definition:** Water holds no energy for the body. The more water a food holds, the less energy it holds in each 100 grams.

Water is part of the weight of nearly every food, but the body cannot get energy from it. Cucumber is about 95 percent water, so 100 grams hold only about 15 kilocalories. Dry foods such as groundnuts, rice and flour have almost no water, so they hold much more in the same weight. When you cook rice, it soaks up two or three times its own weight of water. Nothing is added to the energy, but it is spread over a much heavier pot of food. So 100 grams of cooked rice holds only about a third of the energy of 100 grams of raw rice, and the same pan of rice still gives the same total. Some labels, on ready mixes for example, give one column for the food as sold and another for the food as prepared.
- e.g. Raw rice holds about 350 kilocalories in 100 grams. Cooked rice holds about 130.
- e.g. A boiled egg and about 13 grams of groundnuts both hold about 75 kilocalories, but the egg weighs four times as much because of its water.
- e.g. A hiker who carries dry food, such as chikki and groundnuts, carries much more energy in the same pack than one who carries idli.
### Reading a food label · *defined*
**Definition:** A food label is a table and a list on a packet, set by law in India by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, that says what is in the food and how much.

Start at the nutrition table. Look for the column per 100 grams, because it lets you compare one packet with another fairly. A second column, per serving, shows what the maker counts as one helping. Check how many servings there are in the pack. Then look at the lines: energy in kilocalories, protein, carbohydrate with the sugars inside it, fat, and sodium. Next read the ingredient list. Ingredients are listed from the most by weight to the least, so the first three tell you most of the story. A green dot in a square means vegetarian, and a brown dot means it holds egg, meat or fish. A +F sign means vitamins or minerals have been added. The date tells you when the food should be used by. Percentages on a label are usually worked out for an adult who needs about 2,000 kilocalories a day, so they can mislead for a child.
- e.g. A biscuit packet shows 480 kilocalories per 100 grams, but only 120 per serving, because a serving is 25 grams.
- e.g. If the first ingredient on a 'fruit' biscuit is sugar, the biscuit holds more sugar than fruit.
- e.g. 'Sodium' is not salt. To find the salt, multiply the sodium by about two and a half.
### Sugar hides under many names · *defined*
**Definition:** Sugar is the plain name for sucrose, but food makers use many other names for sweet carbohydrates that the body treats in much the same way.

On an ingredient list, sugar may appear as sugar, jaggery, honey, glucose syrup, fructose, dextrose, maltodextrin, invert sugar, malt extract or fruit juice concentrate. A packet can list several of these separately, so each one is small and none is first on the list, even though together they add up to the biggest share. Four grams of sugar is about one level teaspoon. A small packet drink of 200 millilitres with 22 grams of sugar holds more than five teaspoons. Labels in India show total sugars and, on many packets, added sugars. Sugar is a fuel and not a poison, but sweet foods are easy to eat quickly and in large amounts. Jaggery has a little iron and a few minerals that white sugar lacks, but it is still mostly sugar, and the body handles it much the same way.
- e.g. Ingredients: wheat flour, sugar, glucose syrup, invert sugar, palm oil. Together, the three sugars could weigh more than the flour. The list cannot tell you, but it tells you to look closer.
- e.g. A teaspoon of sugar is about 4 grams. A 22 gram drink is 5 and a half teaspoons.
- e.g. Honey, jaggery and sugar all give about 3 to 4 kilocalories in each gram.
### Where a label's calorie number comes from, and how exact it is · *debated*
A food label's energy number is usually not measured by burning the packet. Makers work it out by weighing the protein, fat and carbohydrate in the food, and multiplying each by a standard number, about 4 for protein, 9 for fat and 4 for carbohydrate. These standard numbers were worked out in the 1890s by Wilbur Atwater. They are averages. A real body does not get exactly the same from every food, so the number on a label is a good estimate and not an exact measure.
- e.g. In a careful study published in 2012, almonds gave volunteers about a quarter fewer calories than the standard numbers predicted.
- e.g. In 2015, Israeli scientists led by Eran Segal and Eran Elinav watched the blood sugar of about 800 people after the same meals, and found that people reacted very differently to the same food.
- e.g. Cooking changes how much of a food the body can use. Cooked starch is easier to digest than raw.
- **Food scientists who use the standard numbers:** The averages are close enough to compare foods fairly, and every packet uses the same system, so comparing labels is fair. Without a standard, no two packets could be compared.
- **Researchers who study how bodies differ:** Bodies differ in how much of a food they take in. Gut bacteria, how the food was chopped and cooked, and a person's own digestion can all change the answer. The number on a label may be right on average and wrong for you.
### Is the processing the problem, or the nutrients? · *debated*
Some foods are made in factories from many ingredients and then sealed in packets. Scientists call the most heavily factory-made group ultra-processed foods. Everyone agrees that many of these foods are high in sugar, salt and fat. The argument is about why they seem to be linked to ill health when eaten in large amounts. In a well-known test in 2019, the American scientist Kevin Hall housed 20 adults for four weeks in a hospital ward. For two weeks they ate mostly ultra-processed foods and for two weeks mostly unprocessed foods, in meals matched for sugar, fat, fibre and protein. On the ultra-processed diet, people ate about 500 kilocalories more a day and put on weight. Nobody yet knows exactly why.
- e.g. Iodised salt, fortified atta, pasteurised milk and frozen peas are all processed, and all of them protect health.
- e.g. In the 2019 test, people ate faster on the ultra-processed diet. Whether speed is the cause is still unknown.
- e.g. A packet of puffed snack and a bowl of boiled groundnuts can hold similar energy, but most people eat far more of the snack before they feel full.
- **Carlos Monteiro and the team who made the NOVA groups:** How much a food has been changed in a factory matters on its own. These foods are made to be soft, sweet and easy to eat quickly, so people eat more of them.
- **Scientists who focus on nutrients:** Processing is not one thing. What matters is the amount of sugar, salt, fat and fibre in a food, and a fair rule should look at those, as well as at how a food was made.
### How much energy does a child need? · *defined*
**Definition:** The energy a body needs depends on its size, its age and how much it moves. A child of 6 to 10 years usually needs about 1,700 to 2,000 kilocalories a day.

Even sitting still, your body uses energy to keep your heart beating, your lungs breathing, your brain thinking and your body warm. This resting energy is the biggest part of what you need. On top of it comes growing, and moving. A child who runs and plays all day needs more than a child who sits. If you eat more energy than you use, your body stores the extra, mostly as fat. If you eat less than you use, your body burns what it has stored. Both can be unhealthy if they go on for a long time. A child who goes hungry for years may grow more slowly. Needs also vary from child to child, so these numbers are a middle, not a rule for every body.
- e.g. A dancer in training and a child who sits all day may need very different amounts.
- e.g. A growing child needs a lot of energy for their size, because they are also building a bigger body.
- e.g. A packet that says 'based on a 2,000 kilocalorie diet' uses an adult number, so a child's share of each nutrient is usually different.

## Mostly agreed
- The energy in food can be measured as heat, in kilocalories. *How we know:* When a food burns inside a closed, insulated chamber, the heat it gives out warms a known amount of water. Scientists have done this for well over a hundred years, and the numbers repeat.
- Fat holds about 9 kilocalories in each gram, and carbohydrate and protein hold about 4. *How we know:* These averages were worked out by Wilbur Atwater in the 1890s, from burning foods and measuring how much of each food people actually absorbed. Every food label in the world still uses them.
- A living body follows the same energy rules as a fire. Energy is changed from one form to another, and none is made or lost. *How we know:* Atwater and his team measured everything that went into a sealed room with a volunteer in it, and everything that came out as heat and work. The two matched very closely.
- Almost all the energy in our food was first caught from sunlight by green plants. *How we know:* Plants kept in the dark stop making food and use up what they stored. A few living beings near deep-sea vents use chemical energy instead, but they feed only a very small part of life on Earth.
- A body that lacks a vitamin or mineral for a long time can fall ill in a particular way. *How we know:* Goitre fell sharply in places after salt had iodine added to it. Night blindness is linked to too little vitamin A, and anaemia is linked to too little iron.
- On a food label, the ingredients are listed from the largest amount by weight to the smallest. *How we know:* This is the rule for packaged food in India, set by the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India, and in most other countries.

## How we found out
**Antoine Lavoisier, Marie-Anne Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace, then Wilbur Atwater and Edward Rosa, 1780s and 1890s.** People once thought that the warmth of a living body came from a special vital heat that no fire had. In the 1780s Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace shut a guinea pig inside a chamber packed with ice and counted how much ice melted. Then they burned carbon and counted the ice it melted. A breathing animal gave out heat in the same way as a slow fire. Marie-Anne Lavoisier, Antoine's wife and lab partner, kept notes and later drew the respiration experiments. A hundred years later, in the 1890s, the American chemist Wilbur Atwater asked the same question about people. With the physicist Edward Rosa he built an airtight, insulated room. A volunteer lived and worked inside it for days, eating food whose energy Atwater had measured by burning it. The heat and work that left the room matched the energy that went in. A living body keeps the same energy accounts as a fire.  
*What people believed before:* That the warmth and work of a living body came from a special vital force that a fire did not have, so a body might not follow the energy rules of the rest of nature.

## Challenging perspectives
- **Jaggery is a healthy replacement for sugar.** (Many families and some advertisements.; *minority*)  
  For: Jaggery is less refined than white sugar and carries a little iron and a few other minerals.  
  Against: Jaggery is about 95 percent sugar, and the body handles it much as it handles white sugar. The extra minerals are small, so a child would need to eat a large amount to get much of them.
- **A calorie is a calorie. All that matters is how many you eat.** (Some diet advice and many weight-loss apps.; *debated*)  
  For: Energy is never made or lost, so the total energy in and out has to balance.  
  Against: Bodies take in different amounts from the same food, and foods differ in how filling they are and how fast they are eaten. The balance of energy is true, but the number on a label is an estimate of what a body will take in.
- **Protein is the only food that builds muscle, so more protein is always better.** (Advertisements for protein powders and bars.; *rejected*)  
  For: Muscles are mostly protein, and a growing child needs it every day.  
  Against: The body uses what it needs and burns or stores the rest. A child eating dal, curd, milk, eggs or fish gets enough for building. Exercise and enough energy from other foods matter just as much.
- **If the front of a packet says 'multigrain', 'natural', 'low fat' or 'no added sugar', the food is healthy.** (Advertisements.; *rejected*)  
  For: Some of these words describe a real difference, such as less fat.  
  Against: These words are chosen to sell the packet, and they do not tell you the amount of sugar, salt or fat. The nutrition table and the ingredient list at the back give the real numbers.
- **You must eat rice and dal in the same meal, or the protein is wasted.** (A popular book, Diet for a Small Planet, in 1971.; *rejected*)  
  For: Rice is low in the amino acid lysine and dal is low in methionine, so together they fit each other well.  
  Against: The author, Frances Moore Lappé, later wrote that this was stricter than needed. The body keeps a store of amino acids across the day, so a grain and a pulse eaten at different meals still work together.

## Roots
We have burned food under water, cooked rice and read labels. Now we ask what the energy in a meal tells us about what a meal is and who we are.

**Deep truth.** The groundnut you burned is called a body-building food, yet it heated the water. Your body can also burn protein for fuel and turn extra sugar into fat. So energy, building and protection are jobs that food does, not kinds of food. The body gives each bite its job.  
*Habit it breaks:* We sort foods into energy-giving, body-building and protective, as if the job were printed on the food. But a hungry body burns its protein, and a well-fed body stores extra sugar as fat. The groups help us plan a meal, but they describe the usual job, not a fixed one.  
*Evidence:* The burning groundnut heated the water although about a quarter of it is protein. Atwater found that protein gives about 4 kilocalories in a gram, the same as starch. The label of dal, milk or a groundnut shows protein, fat and carbohydrate together, so one food carries material for all three jobs.

- *who am i:* You are built from what you ate. The carbon in your muscle was a gas in the air not long ago, and in time much of it will leave in your breath.
- *what is life:* A living body does three things with food at once. It gets warm, it moves, and it builds new parts. A flame can only make heat.
- *what is this world:* The energy in every meal came from the Sun, was caught by a plant and was passed on through bodies. Food is how sunlight travels from one living being to another.

A plant catches the energy of sunlight and stores that energy in sugar, starch, fat and protein. You eat the plant, and your cells release that energy in many small steps. Part of it moves you, part of it warms you, and part of it builds you. What you breathe out and what you sweat out goes back to the air, and plants take the carbon dioxide in again. The meal is not outside you first and then inside you. It becomes you slowly, and you become air, water and heat just as slowly.

- **Taittiriya Upanishad 3.2 (the Bhrigu Valli):** Bhrigu asks his father Varuna to teach him about Brahman, the root of everything. His father tells him to find out through tapas, which means long, steady effort of thought. Bhrigu's first answer is that Brahman is food. All beings are born from food, they live by food, and when they die they go back into food. (Your muscles were once dal and rice, and one day the atoms in them will be soil, air and water again. The teaching names the cycle that the food label leaves out.)
- **Chandogya Upanishad 6.5.1:** Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu that food, once eaten, divides in three. The coarsest part becomes waste, the middle part becomes flesh, and the finest part becomes mind. (This is an ancient guess, not a modern measurement, but it sorts food into outcomes just as our three jobs do. Modern science agrees that thinking costs fuel, because the brain uses about a fifth of an adult's energy.)
- **Bhagavad Gita 15.14:** The verse speaks of a fire in the body of every living being that digests the four kinds of food. The Ayurvedic tradition calls this digestive fire agni. (Your cells really do burn food, but slowly and in small steps. The fire in the verse and the fire under your tumbler release the same energy.)
- Science (settled): Energy is never made or lost, only changed. In the 1890s Wilbur Atwater showed that this holds for the human body as well as for a fire.
- Science (open): How much of a meal's energy a person takes in may depend on their gut bacteria and their own digestion. Scientists are still working out how large these differences are.
- Other view, The Charvaka school of ancient India: Charvaka thinkers taught that the body is made only of material elements, and that awareness arises from them, in the way the power to make people drunk arises from fermenting grain and jaggery. For them, a meal does not become a person and a mind through anything more than matter.
- Practice, The warmth in your breath (3 min): Sit still. Hold your palm in front of your mouth. Breathe out slowly and feel the warm air on your hand. Say to yourself that this warmth was food a few hours ago. Do this five times.
- Ask: If the energy in your body was sunlight not long ago, are you partly made of sunlight?
- Ask: Is a meal something you have, or something you become?
- Ask: If protein can be fuel and sugar can be stored as fat, what does it mean to call a food by only one job?
- Ask: A calorie number belongs to the food on the packet, yet each body gets a different amount from it. Where does the number really live?

## Deep and big questions
- *deep:* A flame and a cell both burn sugar with oxygen and make the same heat. Why does a flame destroy a nut in a minute while your body takes hours? (hint: Think of a firework and a slowly burning lamp. What would happen to a cell that got all its energy at once?)
- *deep:* A label gives one calorie number for a food. Is the number a fact about the food, or a fact about the food meeting a body? (hint: Remember that different bodies took different amounts from the same almonds, and that cooking changes what a body can take in. Where does the number live?)
- *deep:* If the body can burn protein and store sugar as fat, what does it mean to call a food body-building or energy-giving? (hint: Think about what the groundnut did in your tumbler, and what your body does with a day of dal.)
- *big:* The world grows enough food energy for everyone, according to the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization, yet many people are hungry. How can both be true? (hint: Think about who grows food, who can pay for it, how it travels, and how much is thrown away. M. S. Swaminathan spent his life on this question.)
- *big:* A label counts kilocalories, grams and percentages. What does a label leave out that still matters about a meal? (hint: Think of who grew the food, how far it travelled, how it tastes, and who you ate it with.)
- *deep:* A bird stores fat for a flight over a sea. A camel stores it in a hump. A child stores it too. Why do so many living beings store energy as fat and not as sugar? (hint: Compare the energy in a gram of fat with a gram of sugar, and think about what carrying the extra weight costs.)
- *big:* Almost every calorie in your food began as sunlight. When you run, whose energy are you spending? (hint: Follow the energy back through the groundnut, the plant, the Sun, and ask where you begin and the meal ends.)

## Story: The Heart on the Biscuit Packet
*A made-up story. The science in it is real. Ingredient lists run from the largest amount to the smallest, sugar has many names, and a serving can be far smaller than what a person eats.*

Every evening at five, Ammamma sat on the veranda in Thrissur with her tea and her biscuits. Nandini sat beside her with a glass of milk. They watched the crows argue on the compound wall.

That month, the doctor told Ammamma that her blood held too much sugar. 'Eat less sweet food,' he said. 'And read what you buy.' Ammamma was seventy-one. Her eyes were tired, and the print on packets had turned into a grey blur.

On Saturday she walked with Nandini to Sebastian Chettan's shop at the corner. Nandini was nine and she held the cloth bag. The shelves were full of bright packets. Sebastian Chettan was a big man with a pencil behind his ear, and he knew every packet in his shop.

'For the sugar, you want these,' he said. He lifted down a green packet with a big heart on the front. In gold letters it said, Multigrain. No Maida. Heart Friendly. 'Our best seller for people like you, Ammamma. Only forty-eight calories a biscuit.'

Ammamma reached for her purse.

'Wait,' said Nandini.

That week at school, her teacher Anitha Miss had given every child a packet from home. 'The front of a packet is an advertisement,' she had said. 'The back is where the facts are. Read the back.'

Nandini turned the packet over. The print was tiny, even for her. She stepped to the door and held it in the light.

'Read it to me,' said Ammamma.

'Ingredients,' Nandini read slowly. 'Whole wheat flour. Sugar. Oats. Glucose syrup. Palm oil. Invert sugar syrup. Salt.' She stopped. 'Ammamma, it says multigrain. But the oats are only third. And there is sugar in it three times.'

'Three times?' Sebastian Chettan frowned.

'Sugar, glucose syrup and invert sugar syrup. They have different names, but they are all sweet. Anitha Miss says the list goes from the most to the least. So the flour is first and sugar is second. But if you put the three sugars together, they might be the biggest part of all.'

Sebastian Chettan took the packet and read the back himself, moving his lips. 'Thirty years,' he said quietly, 'and nobody ever told me to turn it over.'

'There is a table too,' said Nandini. 'The front says forty-eight calories. But that is per biscuit, and one biscuit is ten grams. Here, the table says per one hundred grams. It says four hundred and eighty kilocalories, and twenty-two grams of sugar.'

Ammamma did the sum in her head, the way she had done sums at the market for fifty years. 'Ten biscuits make a hundred grams. Forty-eight times ten is four hundred and eighty. So the table is honest.' She tapped her walking stick on the floor. 'And I eat six every evening.'

'Six biscuits is sixty grams,' said Nandini. 'Twenty-two grams of sugar in a hundred grams. So sixty grams has about thirteen grams of sugar.' She asked Sebastian Chettan for the spoon from his tea counter. 'A level spoon of sugar is about four grams. So that is three spoons, Ammamma. Every evening.'

Nobody said anything. A crow landed on the shop's tin roof and the sound rang through the shop.

Ammamma laughed first. It was a short, surprised laugh. 'I used to read you the newspaper,' she said. 'Now you read me the biscuit.'

She did not throw the biscuits away, and Nandini did not ask her to. They bought a small packet of roasted chana from the next shelf. Nandini read its back too. It said roasted chana, salt, and nothing else. Ammamma would have two biscuits with her tea, and a handful of chana, which would keep her stomach full for longer.

As they were leaving, Sebastian Chettan called after them. 'Nandini, if you come on Mondays, will you read me the back of five more packets? I will give you a peppermint for each.'

On Monday, Nandini found a small card beside the green heart, written in pencil. It said, Read the back first. The first three words tell you the most.

That evening on the veranda, Ammamma drank her tea slowly, with two biscuits and a handful of chana, while the crows argued over the wall. Nandini counted the sugar in her head. Some things, she thought, are written in very small print, and they matter the most.

**For the parent:** The big words were on the front of the packet. The facts were at the back, in the order of weight.

- Ask: Why does it help that an ingredient list goes from the most to the least?
- Ask: If three kinds of sugar are listed separately, how could you find out how much sugar there is altogether?
- Ask: Why might a maker choose a small serving size for the front of a packet?

## Experiments
### e1 · Burning Nuts: how much heat does a food give? (40 min)
- **question:** Which gives the most heat to a tumbler of water: a groundnut, half a cashew or a piece of dry coconut? How much of the heat reaches the water?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Which food will warm the water the most, and which the least? How many degrees will the water rise? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** Every trial uses the same 50 millilitres of fresh water from the same bottle, the same tumbler, the same thermometer, and a nut weighing about half a gram. The tumbler is held 2 centimetres above the nut each time, and the nut burns until its flame goes out by itself. Only the food changes. The groundnut is tested twice, so we can see how much two identical trials differ.
- **safety:** A grown-up lights the flame and holds the hot tumbler, every time. Work on a bare counter near the sink, away from curtains and paper, with the window open for the smoke but the fan off. Keep a plate nearby to cover and put out a nut. Use a steel or aluminium tumbler, never plastic or glass. Tie back long hair. Do not touch the nut or the pin until they are cold. Do not eat any food that has been burned. Hairpins and needles are sharp, so keep them away from small children. If anyone in the family has a nut allergy, use only the coconut and do not let that person near the nuts or the smoke.
- **record:** Write the weight, start temperature and end temperature for each trial. Work out the rise in degrees. Then multiply the rise by 0.05 to get the heat in the water in kilocalories. Compare it with the heat the label promises.
- **results table:** Trial | Food | Weight (grams) | Start temperature (degrees) | End temperature (degrees) | Rise (degrees) | Heat in the water (kilocalories)
- **s1 (4 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Show your child the groundnut, the cashew and the coconut. Write down their guess about the most and the least heat, and their reason.  
  *Parent says:* We will burn three foods under a tumbler of water. Which will warm the water most? Which will warm it least? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* We will burn three foods under water. Which gives the most heat? Why do you think so?
  - Ask: Why do you think that food has the most heat? (good: It has the most fat.; It burns longest.; It is oily.; It is the biggest.)
  - Note: Do not correct the guess. Any guess with a reason is a good start. One child with a parent: the child weighs, reads and records, and the parent lights and holds. Two to four children: one weighs, one reads the thermometer, one times the burn, and one writes the table. Swap jobs every trial. Each child writes their own guess first.
- **s2 (6 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Weigh each nut and write the weight down. Pour 50 millilitres of water into the tumbler. Put the thermometer in and read the start temperature.  
  *Parent says:* Weigh each food and write the number. Then measure 50 millilitres of water. Read the temperature before we start.  
  *Child mode:* Weigh each food. Pour 50 millilitres of water. Read the temperature.
  - Note: Water from the room-temperature bottle is usually 26 to 32 degrees. If you have no pocket scale, pick pieces that look the same size and say so in your notes. The comparison is then rough. Stand the nut on the pin so that it does not touch the plate. A damp nut will not burn well in the monsoon. A grown-up can dry the nuts for a minute on a warm tawa and cool them first. Keep draughts away: a breeze from a fan wastes much of the heat.
- **s3 (9 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Fix the hairpin in the dough. Rest a groundnut on the pin. The grown-up lights it and holds the tumbler 2 centimetres above, with tongs.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up lights the nut. The grown-up holds the tumbler just above the flame. Watch the nut and the clock.  
  *Child mode:* Ask a grown-up to light the nut. Watch the flame. Time how long it burns.
  - Ask: What do you see happening to the nut while it burns? (good: It turns black.; It has a small yellow flame.; It crackles.; It gets smaller.)
  - Note: Many nuts light after 10 to 20 seconds in a match flame. If a nut will not light, hold the match to it until it catches. Once it burns, keep the tumbler above it until the flame goes out by itself, about 30 to 90 seconds. Holding a tumbler steady is tiring, so you can rest it on two upturned steel katoris, one on each side of the nut. Stir gently with the thermometer before you read it. Cool the tumbler under a tap and wipe it dry before the next trial. If the water rises by less than 3 degrees, nothing is wrong: too much heat escaped. Repeat with 25 millilitres of water and multiply the rise by 0.025 instead of 0.05, and write that you did.
  - Check: Why do we use fresh water from the same bottle, and a cooled tumbler, for every trial? → **So each trial starts at the same temperature**
- **s4 (6 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Repeat with a second groundnut. Use fresh water and a cooled tumbler. Write down the temperature before and after.  
  *Parent says:* Now we do the very same test again with a second groundnut. Will the answer be exactly the same?  
  *Child mode:* Do the same test again with a new groundnut. Write the numbers.
  - Ask: Are the two groundnut results exactly the same? Why might they differ? (good: They are close but not the same.; The nuts are different sizes.; The flame was different.; Some heat escaped.)
  - Note: The two groundnut trials may differ by a few degrees. That is normal and it is an honest result. It tells you how much to trust the small differences between foods.
- **s5 (8 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* Burn half a cashew and then a piece of dry coconut in the same way. Cool the tumbler and use fresh water each time.  
  *Parent says:* Same test again, with the cashew and then with the coconut. Keep the weight about half a gram.  
  *Child mode:* Burn the cashew. Then burn the coconut. Write each rise.
  - Note: Try a few grains of roasted chana or puffed rice last, if you like. They usually char or flare for a moment and go out, and give little heat. That is a result as well. Do not eat anything that has been burned.
- **s6 (7 min)**  
  *Parent does:* For each trial, subtract the start from the end to get the rise. Multiply the rise by 0.05 to get kilocalories. Compare with the label.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you see? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Work out each rise. Say what you thought, what you saw, and why.
  - Note: A typical result is a rise of 5 to 20 degrees, which means 0.25 to 1 kilocalorie in the water. That is about a tenth to a third of the 3 kilocalories in the nut. Your numbers may differ, and a little black char is left, so not all the nut burned. After they have said what they saw, explain: fat holds about 9 kilocalories in a gram, so the three fatty foods gave similar heat. A real lab catches nearly all of it in a closed chamber. If one result looks odd, say so. Write the result you really got.
  - Check: A nut should give 3 kilocalories, but the water gained only 1. Where did the other 2 go? → **Into the air, the tumbler and the smoke**
  - Think: A groundnut of half a gram should give about 3 kilocalories (a typical label value). How much reached the water, and where did the rest go?
### e2 · Rice Before and After: where does the energy go in the pot? (38 min)
- **question:** Does 100 grams of cooked rice hold more energy, the same or less energy than 100 grams of raw rice?
- **predict:** Before you start, guess. Will 100 grams of cooked rice hold more energy, the same or less than 100 grams of raw rice? Why do you think so?
- **fair test:** We weigh the rice and the water before cooking, and weigh the whole pot again afterwards. Nothing is added to the pan except water, so the total energy in the pan cannot change. The only thing that changes is the weight the energy is spread over. We use the same pan and the same lid, and do not drain any water away.
- **safety:** A grown-up uses the stove and moves the hot pan. Lift the lid away from your face to avoid the steam. Let the pan cool for a few minutes before weighing it. Use a heavy-bottomed small pan and stay beside it, because 50 grams of rice can catch at the bottom.
- **record:** Write the weight of the rice before cooking and after cooking. Work out the energy in 100 grams of each. Then write one sentence about where the extra weight came from.
- **results table:** Stage | Weight of the rice (grams) | Energy in 100 grams (kilocalories) | Total energy in the pan (kilocalories)
- **s1 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Read the energy for 100 grams from the rice packet. Write it down. Ask for a guess about cooked rice and a reason.  
  *Parent says:* Read the packet. How much energy is in 100 grams of raw rice? After cooking, will 100 grams hold more, the same or less? Why do you think so?  
  *Child mode:* Read the rice packet. Will cooked rice hold more, the same or less? Why?
  - Ask: Why do you think cooked rice will hold that much? (good: It stays the same, it is still rice.; It soaks up water so it holds less.; Cooking adds energy.)
  - Note: A typical packet says about 350 kilocalories in 100 grams. Do not correct the guess. Both 'the same' and 'less' are common first guesses. One child with a parent: the child weighs and does the sums, the parent cooks. Two to four children: one weighs, one measures the water, one writes, one does the sums. For a comparison, cook a second pan of a different rice, such as basmati and parboiled, side by side.
- **s2 (8 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Weigh the empty pan. Add 50 grams of rice, then 150 millilitres of water. Write all three weights down.  
  *Parent says:* First we weigh the empty pan. Then we add 50 grams of rice. Then we add the water. Write each weight down.  
  *Child mode:* Weigh the pan. Add 50 grams of rice and 150 millilitres of water.
  - Note: Work out the total energy in the pan before cooking. For 350 kilocalories in 100 grams, 50 grams holds 175 kilocalories. The water adds none.
  - Check: 50 grams of rice from a packet saying 350 kilocalories in 100 grams. How much energy is in the 50 grams? → **175 kilocalories**
- **s3 (20 min) · grown-up needed**  
  *Parent does:* A grown-up boils the water, then turns the heat to low and puts on the lid. Cook for 15 minutes. Turn off the heat. Leave it covered for 5 minutes.  
  *Parent says:* A grown-up cooks the rice on low heat with the lid on, for 15 minutes. No water is poured away.  
  *Child mode:* A grown-up cooks the rice with the lid on. Wait 15 minutes.
  - Ask: What do you think is happening to the water while the rice cooks? (good: The rice is soaking it up.; Some turns to steam.; It is going into the grains.)
  - Note: If water is left at the end, cook uncovered for another minute. If the rice is still hard, add 2 tablespoons of water and cook 3 more minutes, and add that weight to your table. Some water leaves as steam, which is why the cooked weight is less than 200 grams.
- **s4 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* Let the pan cool a little. Weigh the pan with the cooked rice, and subtract the empty pan. That is the weight of the cooked rice.  
  *Parent says:* Weigh the pan with the rice in it. Then subtract the empty pan. How heavy is the cooked rice?  
  *Child mode:* Weigh the pan with the rice. Take away the empty pan. How heavy is the rice?
  - Ask: The rice started at 50 grams. Where did the extra weight come from? (good: From the water.; The rice soaked up water.)
- **s5 (5 min)**  
  *Parent does:* The energy in the pan is still 175 kilocalories. Divide 175 by the cooked weight, then multiply by 100. That is the energy in 100 grams of cooked rice.  
  *Parent says:* Tell me three things. What did you think? What did you find? Why do you think it happened?  
  *Child mode:* Divide 175 by the cooked weight. Multiply by 100. Say what you found.
  - Note: For 150 grams of cooked rice the answer is 175 divided by 150, times 100, which is about 117. A usual result is 110 to 140. After they have said what they found, explain: water adds weight and no energy, so the same energy is spread over a heavier food. This is why 100 grams of cooked rice holds about a third of the energy in 100 grams of raw rice. The cooked weight changes with the rice and how much steam escaped, so your answer will differ. Whatever it is, the pan's energy stayed 175 kilocalories.
  - Think: Why does a label for a ready mix give one column for the food as sold and one as prepared?

### Energy, Building and Protection (core) (core, 16 questions)
1. (mcq) Which job do rice, roti and ghee mostly do in a meal?  
   Options: They give energy / They give vitamins to protect the eyes / They build bones only  
   **Answer:** They give energy. *Why:* Rice and roti are rich in starch, and ghee is almost pure fat. Both are fuels for the body. They do hold some other nutrients, but energy is their main job.
2. (mcq) One kilocalorie is the heat needed to warm what by one degree Celsius?  
   Options: One litre of water / One spoon of water / One kilogram of rice  
   **Answer:** One litre of water. *Why:* A kilocalorie is defined by water. It is the heat that warms one litre of water by one degree. That is why a thermometer and water can measure the energy in food.
3. (truefalse) A gram of fat holds about twice as much energy as a gram of starch.  
   **Answer:** True. *Why:* True. Fat gives about 9 kilocalories in each gram, and starch gives about 4. That is why ghee and groundnuts hold so much energy.
4. (fill) On a food label, the ingredients are listed from the ____ amount to the smallest amount.  
   **Answer:** ['largest', 'biggest', 'most', 'greatest']. *Why:* The law says ingredients go from the most by weight to the least. So the first ingredient is what the food is mostly made of.
5. (match) Match each nutrient to what it does for the body.  
   **Answer:** Iron → helps the blood carry oxygen; Calcium → builds bones and teeth; Vitamin A → protects the eyes and the skin; Fibre → keeps the gut moving and feeds helpful bacteria. *Why:* Each of these is a protective nutrient with its own job. None of them gives energy, but the body cannot work properly without them.
6. (mcq) Why does 100 grams of cooked rice hold less energy than 100 grams of raw rice?  
   Options: Cooked rice has soaked up water, which adds weight and no energy / Cooking destroys the energy in rice / Cooked rice is smaller than raw rice  
   **Answer:** Cooked rice has soaked up water, which adds weight and no energy. *Why:* The pan holds the same energy before and after cooking. The water adds weight, so the same energy is spread over more grams. If you chose 'cooking destroys energy', remember that energy is never lost.
7. (sort) Sort each food by the job it does most.  
   **Answer:** ghee → Energy; egg → Body-building; guava → Protection; rice → Energy; paneer → Body-building; spinach → Protection. *Why:* Ghee and rice are mostly fat and starch. Egg and paneer are rich in protein. Guava and spinach are rich in vitamins and minerals. Many of these foods do a second job too.
8. (truefalse) If a packet does not have the word 'sugar' in its ingredient list, it has no added sugar.  
   **Answer:** False. *Why:* False. Sugar hides under names such as glucose syrup, invert sugar, dextrose, maltodextrin, honey and fruit juice concentrate. They are all sweet carbohydrates.
9. (count) A biscuit packet lists 120 kilocalories in one serving, and the pack holds 5 servings. How much energy is in the whole pack?  
   **Answer:** 600 kilocalories.. *Why:* Multiply the servings by the energy in one serving. 5 times 120 is 600. The front of a packet often shows only the number for one serving.
10. (write) A friend says milk is only good for strong bones. Name two other jobs that milk does for the body.  
   **Answer:** Milk gives energy, through its sugar and fat. It gives protein to build and mend muscle and skin. It also gives vitamins such as vitamin A and B12.. *Why:* A strong answer names at least two of the three jobs, energy, building and protection, and says which nutrient does each. This shows that one food can do all three jobs.
11. (mcq) Which part of a food label lets you compare two different packets fairly?  
   Options: The per 100 grams column / The picture on the front / The colour of the packet  
   **Answer:** The per 100 grams column. *Why:* Per 100 grams gives the numbers for the same weight of each food. A serving can be 10 grams in one packet and 40 grams in another.
12. (count) A girl of nine needs about 1,800 kilocalories in a day. A snack bar holds 180 kilocalories. What fraction of her day's energy is in the bar?  
   **Answer:** One tenth.. *Why:* 180 out of 1,800 is 180 over 1,800, which is the same as 1 over 10. The bar holds one tenth of the day's energy.
13. (draw) Draw a plate of your own lunch. Colour each food by its main job and put a star by any food that does more than one job.  
   **Answer:** A plate with several foods, each coloured for energy, building or protection, and stars by foods such as dal, curd or milk.. *Why:* A strong drawing names every food and shows that at least one food, such as dal or curd, does more than one job. This shows that the groups describe a food's usual job, not its only one.
14. (perspective) A biscuit packet says 'Multigrain' in big letters on the front. The ingredient list begins: wheat flour, sugar, oats.  
   **Answer:** Answers vary. A strong answer picks a view, gives a reason, and names something to check, such as the sugar per 100 grams or the order of ingredients.. *Why:* The claim on the front can be true and still not tell you enough. A strong answer looks at the numbers at the back.
15. (fill) The standard numbers say that fat gives about ____ kilocalories in each gram.  
   **Answer:** ['9', 'nine']. *Why:* Fat gives about 9 kilocalories in each gram. Carbohydrate and protein give about 4. These standard numbers are used to work out the energy on labels.
16. (write) Why does a label show the energy per 100 grams as well as per serving?  
   **Answer:** Per 100 grams is the same weight for every food, so you can compare two packets fairly. Per serving shows only what the maker counts as one helping, which can be small.. *Why:* A strong answer says that per 100 grams is a fair comparison across foods, and that servings differ from packet to packet.
### How the Numbers Work (stretch) (stretch, 16 questions)

   **Answer:** Most of the heat escaped into the air, the tumbler and the smoke. *Why:* Energy is never lost, but a home flame warms the air, the metal and the smoke as well as the water. A closed chamber in a lab catches almost all of it.

9. (count) A packet drink of 200 millilitres lists 11 grams of sugar in each 100 millilitres. About how many level teaspoons of sugar are in the whole drink, if 4 grams is one teaspoon?

10. (count) Meera cooks 40 grams of rice that holds 140 kilocalories. After cooking, the rice weighs 120 grams. How many kilocalories are in 100 grams of the cooked rice, to the nearest whole number?  
   **Answer:** 117 kilocalories.. *Why:* The pan still holds 140 kilocalories. Divide 140 by 120 to get the energy in 1 gram, then multiply by 100. That is about 117.
11. (order) Put these in order, for how the energy in a groundnut gets to your muscle.

13. (write) A friend says: 'The label says 570 kilocalories per 100 grams, so my body will take in exactly 570 kilocalories from it.' What would you tell your friend?  
   **Answer:** The label gives an estimate made from standard numbers. Bodies differ in how much of a food they take in. How the food is cooked and chopped can change it too. ## Quiz
1. (mcq) Which food does all three jobs, giving energy, building material and protection?  
   Options: Milk / White sugar / Plain water  
   **Answer:** Milk. *Why:* Milk has sugar and fat for energy, protein and calcium for building, and vitamins for protection. Sugar mostly gives energy, and water gives none of the three, though the body needs it.
2. (mcq) Which nutrient holds the most energy in one gram?  
   Options: Fat / Starch / Protein  
   **Answer:** Fat. *Why:* Fat gives about 9 kilocalories in each gram. Starch and protein give about 4. So a gram of fat holds a little more than twice as much.
3. (mcq) What does one kilocalorie measure?  
   Options: Heat energy / Weight / Sweetness  
   **Answer:** Heat energy. *Why:* A kilocalorie is a unit of heat energy. It is the heat that warms a litre of water by one degree.
4. (mcq) In a test that burns foods under water, why must each food be weighed first?  
   Options: So the foods are compared by the same weight / So that the nut burns for longer / To find the colour of the nut  
   **Answer:** So the foods are compared by the same weight. *Why:* A heavier piece holds more energy. If the weights differ, the result tells you about the weight and not about the food. Equal weights make the test fair.
5. (mcq) Which change would make a home nut test lose less heat?  
   Options: Shield the flame from draughts and hold the tumbler close / Use a much bigger tumbler with more water / Use a nut that burns with a cooler flame  
   **Answer:** Shield the flame from draughts and hold the tumbler close. *Why:* Less heat escapes when the flame is shielded from moving air and the tumbler is close. A bigger tumbler would only give a smaller rise in temperature.
6. (mcq) On a packet, the first ingredient is sugar. What does that tell you?  
   Options: There is more sugar than any other single ingredient / Sugar was added first in the factory / The packet holds only sugar  
   **Answer:** There is more sugar than any other single ingredient. *Why:* Ingredients are listed from the most by weight to the least. The first one is the biggest part. Other sugars may be listed lower down.
7. (mcq) A pack lists 80 kilocalories in a serving, and holds 6 servings. How much energy is in the whole pack?  
   Options: 86 kilocalories / 480 kilocalories / 80 kilocalories  
   **Answer:** 480 kilocalories. *Why:* Multiply the servings by the energy in one serving. 6 times 80 is 480. If you chose 80, you used the number for only one serving.
8. (mcq) Which food has the least energy in 100 grams?  
   Options: Cucumber / Cooked rice / Raw rice  
   **Answer:** Cucumber. *Why:* Cucumber is about 95 percent water and holds about 15 kilocalories in 100 grams. Cooked rice holds about 130 and raw rice about 350.
9. (mcq) Which pair is a protective food and the nutrient it is rich in?  
   Options: Amla and vitamin C / Ghee and vitamin C / Sugar and calcium  
   **Answer:** Amla and vitamin C. *Why:* Amla is one of the richest sources of vitamin C. Ghee is nearly all fat, and sugar has no calcium.
10. (mcq) Why does a squeeze of lemon on spinach or dal help the body?  
   Options: Vitamin C helps the body take in the iron / Lemon adds protein / Lemon adds energy  
   **Answer:** Vitamin C helps the body take in the iron. *Why:* Vitamin C helps the body absorb the iron in plant foods. Tea drunk at the same meal does the opposite, and holds on to the iron.
11. (mcq) What do your cells use oxygen for?  
   Options: To release the energy in food in small steps / To make food from sunlight / To make bones  
   **Answer:** To release the energy in food in small steps. *Why:* Cells use oxygen to release the energy stored in sugar, in many small steps. Making food from sunlight is what plants do, and bones are built from calcium and protein.
12. (mcq) What did the sealed-room measurements of the 1890s show about a living body?  
   Options: The energy going in matched the heat and work coming out, as with a fire / People make energy from nothing / Food holds no energy  
   **Answer:** The energy going in matched the heat and work coming out, as with a fire. *Why:* The volunteer's food energy matched the heat and work measured. So a living body follows the same energy rules as a fire, with nothing made or lost.

## Open questions
- **Why do two people get different amounts of energy from the same food?** Gut bacteria, cooking, chewing and a person's own digestion all seem to matter. Scientists have not yet shown how much each one counts.
- **Does the harm from ultra-processed foods come from how they are made, or from the nutrients in them?** A careful 2019 test found that people ate more on an ultra-processed diet even when sugar, fat, fibre and protein were matched. Nobody knows the cause yet.
- **Why do many people in India have low vitamin D, even where the sun shines all year?** Time indoors, clothing, air pollution and darker skin making less vitamin D in the same sun have all been suggested. Scientists do not agree on how much each one matters.
- **How does the body know how much to eat?** Hormones that signal hunger and fullness have been found, but they do not explain everything. Why some people feel hungry at different times is not fully understood.
- **Why do animals store extra energy as fat, and plants as starch?** Fat is lighter to carry for an animal that moves, which may be part of the answer. But the full reason is still debated.
- **Is there one best diet for all people?** Healthy families around the world eat very differently, from fish and rice to millet and milk. Scientists are still working out which parts of a diet matter for everyone.

## Connected maths
- Meenakshi, a girl in Class 4 in Madurai, weighs a groundnut kernel at 0.5 grams. The packet says 580 kilocalories in 100 grams. How many kilocalories should the kernel hold? **2.9 kilocalories.**. 0.5 grams is 5 hundredths of 100 grams. Take 5 hundredths of 580, which is 580 times 5 divided by 100, or 29 divided by 10. That is 2.9. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- The water above Meenakshi's groundnut gained 0.7 kilocalories, but the nut held 2.9. About what fraction of the nut's heat reached the water? **About one quarter.**. 0.7 out of 2.9 is close to 0.7 out of 2.8, which is one quarter. So about three quarters of the heat was lost to the air, the tumbler and the smoke. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Ghee holds 900 kilocalories in 100 grams and raw rice holds 350. About how many times more energy does ghee hold in the same weight? **About two and a half times.**. 350 times 2 is 700, and 350 times 3 is 1,050. 900 is between them, a little past halfway, so about 2 and a half times. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
- Aarav cooks 50 grams of raw rice, which holds 175 kilocalories. The cooked rice weighs 130 grams. About how many kilocalories are in 100 grams of the cooked rice, and about what fraction is that of the 350 in raw rice? **About 135 kilocalories, which is a little more than one third of 350.**. 175 divided by 130 is about 1.35, so 100 grams holds about 135. 350 divided by 3 is about 117, and 135 is a little more than that, so a little more than one third. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- A packet has 4 servings of 150 kilocalories each. A girl of nine needs about 1,800 kilocalories in a day. If she eats the whole packet, what fraction of her day's energy is that? **One third.**. 4 times 150 is 600. 600 out of 1,800 is the same as 1 out of 3, because 1,800 divided by 600 is 3. (queues `math.fractions.fractions-of-a-whole.L4`)
- Drink A is a 200 millilitre carton with 12 grams of sugar in each 100 millilitres. Drink B is a 250 millilitre bottle with 11 grams of sugar in each 100 millilitres. Which whole drink has more sugar, and by how much? **Drink B has more, by 3.5 grams.**. A holds 2 times 12, which is 24 grams. B holds 2 and a half times 11, which is 27.5 grams. 27.5 take away 24 is 3.5 grams. B has more sugar even though its number per 100 millilitres is lower. (queues `math.fractions.decimals-and-percentages.L4`)
- On a bar chart of energy in 100 grams, the tallest bar is ghee at 900 kilocalories and is drawn 9 centimetres high. How tall should the groundnut bar be, at 580 kilocalories? **About 5.8 centimetres.**. Each centimetre stands for 100 kilocalories, because 900 divided by 9 is 100. So 580 kilocalories is 5.8 centimetres. (queues `math.data.bar-charts.L4`)
- For the Day Pack, chikki holds 500 kilocalories in 100 grams. How many grams of chikki give 1,000 kilocalories, and what share of the 600 gram limit is that? **200 grams, which is one third of the limit.**. 1,000 divided by 500 is 2, so you need 2 lots of 100 grams, which is 200 grams. 200 out of 600 is one third. (queues `math.fractions.ratio-and-proportion.L4`)
