Homeschooling in India: An Honest Guide for Parents

By DeepKids  ·  November 2026  ·  11 min read

Homeschooling in India has quietly stopped being unusual. Ten years ago a parent who kept a child out of school was explaining themselves at every family gathering. Today there are WhatsApp groups in most big cities, co-ops that meet twice a week, and enough families doing it that the question has shifted from why would you to how does it actually work.

This is an attempt at an honest answer to that second question. Not an argument for homeschooling — it suits some families and genuinely does not suit others — but a description of what it involves in India specifically: the legal position, the boards, the money, the day, and the parts nobody puts in the brochure.

Is homeschooling legal in India?

The short version: there is no law against it, and there is no law that formally recognises it either. Homeschooling in India sits in a space the legislation never explicitly addressed.

The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 places a duty on the state to provide schooling for every child aged 6 to 14, and a duty on parents in some state rules to enrol. It was written to get children into school — it was aimed at children who had no access to education, not at parents choosing to teach at home. Successive education ministries have indicated they do not intend to pursue families who are clearly educating their children well outside a school building, and in practice no Indian family is known to have been prosecuted for homeschooling.

What that means practically: thousands of families do this openly and without difficulty. What it also means: you do not have a statute to point at if someone challenges you, and the position can be read differently in different states. If you want certainty rather than custom, speak to a lawyer in your own state. Do not take a blog post — including this one — as legal advice.

What about certificates and board exams?

This is the question that worries parents most, and it is more manageable than it looks. A child does not need to be enrolled in a school for the first several years to sit a board exam later.

The usual route is the National Institute of Open Schooling (NIOS), a national board set up precisely for learners outside conventional schools. NIOS offers Secondary (Class 10) and Senior Secondary (Class 12) certificates that are recognised for college admission in India, and its Open Basic Education programme covers the primary years for those who want a formal track that early.

For the primary years themselves — roughly ages 6 to 10 — certification is mostly beside the point. No one asks a nineteen-year-old for their Class 3 report card. What matters in those years is that the child is genuinely covering the ground, so that joining a school at Class 5, or sitting NIOS at 14, is a step rather than a cliff. That is a planning problem, not a paperwork problem.

Most families follow one of three syllabuses in the primary years: NCERT (the basis of CBSE and most state boards), Cambridge Primary, or NIOS. We have written separately on how to choose between them.

What a homeschooling day actually looks like

Far shorter than people expect. A classroom day runs five or six hours because thirty children need managing, registers need taking, and a lesson has to move at the pace of the middle of the room. One child with one adult does not need any of that.

For a six to ten year old, two to three focused hours covers what school covers in a day, and most of that is not the child sitting at a table. It is a conversation over breakfast, twenty minutes of maths, a story read aloud, something built or grown or taken apart, and a walk where the questions are better than anything a worksheet would have asked.

The thing that makes or breaks it is not curriculum. It is rhythm — the family knowing what today holds before the day starts. Families who improvise every morning burn out within a term. Families with a predictable shape to the week keep going for years. We have written about building that rhythm for a 6 to 10 year old in more detail.

What it costs

Less than private school, more than nothing. The honest line items:

  • Books and materials: NCERT textbooks are inexpensive and available as free PDFs from the NCERT site. Cambridge Primary books cost considerably more. Add art supplies, a few science odds and ends, and a library membership.
  • Classes you outsource: most families buy in one or two things — music, a sport, a language, swimming. This is usually the biggest line.
  • Outings: museums, farms, workshops, travel. Cheap individually, not nothing over a year.
  • A parent's working hours. This is the real cost and it rarely appears in anyone's list. Homeschooling a primary-age child usually means one parent working reduced or flexible hours. For most families this dwarfs every other expense.

The socialisation question

It will be the first thing every relative asks, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a defensive one.

A school gives a child thirty peers of exactly their own age, five days a week, with no effort from the parent. That is a real thing and losing it is a real loss. Homeschooled children can absolutely be isolated if nobody does anything about it.

They can also end up with a wider social life than schooled children, because their week has room in it: a homeschool co-op, a sport, neighbourhood children after school hours, cousins, adults who are not teachers, younger and older children rather than a single age band. The difference is that it is deliberate. In school it happens to you. At home, someone has to make it happen, every week, including the weeks when it is inconvenient.

Families who treat this as a scheduling commitment do fine. Families who assume it will sort itself out often find, a year in, that it did not.

The parts that are genuinely hard

Four things come up again and again, and they are worth knowing before you start rather than after.

Being parent and teacher at once. A child will argue with a parent about long division in a way they would never argue with a teacher. The relationship you already have gets dragged into the lesson. Most families find a workable separation eventually — a particular table, a particular hour, a particular tone — but the first months can be bruising.

Not knowing if you are behind. There is no class average to measure against and no report card to confirm it is working. This uncertainty is corrosive and it is the single most common reason families give up. A plan that actually tracks what has been covered, and against what a school year would have covered, removes most of it.

The lonely parent. Everyone discusses the child's social life. Nobody mentions that the parent has left a world of school gates and other parents. Find the local group. It matters more than you think.

Subjects you are not confident in. Almost every parent has one — usually maths or science. The answer is rarely to teach yourself to graduate level. It is to use material that tells you what to do and what to ask, so you can learn it half a step ahead of the child, out loud, which is a better model of learning than confident fluency anyway.

How to start without upending everything

Nobody needs to withdraw a child on a Monday to find out whether this suits their family.

  1. Try it over a long holiday. Six weeks of running your own days tells you more than six months of reading about it.
  2. Pick a track and stop researching. The comparison is genuinely interesting and will eat a year if you let it. Choose NCERT if you may return to a school, Cambridge if you want the inquiry-led style, NIOS if you want the open-schooling path. You can change later; almost everyone does at least once.
  3. Plan a week, not a year. Year plans are fiction. The family's actual pace shows up within a fortnight and every year plan has to be rewritten around it.
  4. Write down what you did. Not for an inspector — for you, in February, when it feels like nothing has happened. It will show you that a great deal has.
  5. Find one other family. One is enough to stop it feeling eccentric.
